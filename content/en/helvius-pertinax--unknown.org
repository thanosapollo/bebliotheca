* Chapter 1

The father of Publius Helvius Pertinax was a freedman, Helvius Successus,
who is said to have given his son the name from the stubborn run of his
timber trade, because he carried that business on with pertinacity.

Pertinax was born in the Apennines at his mother's villa. At the hour he
was born a dark colt climbed onto the roof-tiles, lingered there a short
while, then fell and died.

Moved by this, the father went to a Chaldean. When the man had predicted
a great future for him, he said that he had lost his offspring.

As a boy he was taught his letters and arithmetic, then given also to a
Greek grammarian and after that to Sulpicius Apollinaris, after whom
Pertinax himself taught grammar.

But when he made too little profit in that work, through Lollianus
Avitus, a man of consular rank and his father's patron, he sought the
rank of centurion. Then, as prefect of a cohort, he set out for Syria in
the reign of Titus Aurelius. Because he had used the public post without
warrants, the governor of Syria forced him to go on foot from Antioch to
his

own station. In the Parthian war he earned promotion by his energy and
was transferred to Britain and kept there. Afterward he commanded a
cavalry wing in Moesia. Then he served as procurator for distributing
the alimenta on the Via Aemilia. From there he commanded the German
fleet. His mother escorted him as far as Germany and died there, and her
tomb is said to stand there still. Then he was transferred to Dacia at a
salary of two hundred thousand sesterces, and, falling under Marcus's
suspicion through the intrigues of certain men, he was removed.
Afterward, through Claudius Pompeianus, Marcus's son-in-law, he was
taken on to command the vexillations, as if to be his assistant.
Approved in that post, he was enrolled in the senate.

Afterward, when he had again done well, the faction that had been got up
against him was exposed, and the emperor Marcus, to make good the
injury, gave him praetorian rank and put him in command of the First
Legion. At once he recovered Raetia and Noricum from the enemy. For
this, by his outstanding energy and by the emperor Marcus's support, he
was designated consul.

There is still extant in Marius Maximus a speech containing his praises
and all that he did or suffered. And besides that speech, which it would
take too long to include, Pertinax was very often praised by Marcus both
in a military assembly and in the senate, and Marcus openly regretted
that, being a senator, he could not be made prefect of the guard by him.
When Cassius's revolt had been put down, he set out from Syria to the
defense of the Danube, and from there received the government of both
Moesias, then of Dacia. Having governed these provinces well, he earned
Syria.

Pertinax kept himself upright down to the government of Syria, but after
Marcus's death he grew eager for money, and for that he was even
attacked in popular jests. After four consular provinces he entered the
Roman senate-house already a rich man. As a senator he had never seen it
before, because he had held the consulship in absence. He was also
ordered at once by Perennis to withdraw to Liguria to his father's farm,
for his father had kept a felt-maker's shop in Liguria. But after he
came to Liguria, having bought up many fields, he surrounded his
father's shop, leaving its former look, with vast buildings, and he
stayed there three years and traded through his slaves.

When Perennis had been killed, Commodus made amends to Pertinax and
asked him by letter to set out for Britain. Once he had gone, he
deterred the soldiers from every mutiny, though they wanted to have
whatever emperor they pleased, and Pertinax himself in particular. Then
Pertinax incurred a mark of ill-will, because he was said to have
accused before Commodus, of aiming at the empire, Antistius Burrus and
Arrius Antoninus. The mutinies against him he did himself put down in
Britain, but he ran a great danger: in a mutiny of a legion he was
almost killed, or at least left among the dead, a deed which Pertinax
himself punished most fiercely. At last afterward he asked to be excused
from the command, saying that because he had upheld discipline

When Commodus had been slain, Laetus, prefect of the guard, and
Eclectus, the chamberlain, came to him, encouraged him, and led him to
the camp. There Pertinax addressed the soldiers, promised a donative,
and said that the empire was being thrust upon him by Laetus and
Eclectus.

It was pretended, however, that Commodus had died of disease, because
the soldiers too were afraid of being put to the test. At last Pertinax
was first named emperor by a few. He was made emperor when already past
sixty, on the day before the Kalends of January. When he had come by
night from the camp to the senate and had ordered the inner room of the
senate-house opened, and the keeper could not be found, he sat down in
the Temple of Concord, and when Claudius Pompeianus, Marcus's
son-in-law, had come to him and had wept over Commodus's fall, Pertinax
urged him to take the empire, but he refused, because he already saw
Pertinax as emperor. At once, therefore, all the magistrates with the
consul came to the senate-house, and Pertinax, who had entered by night,

they named emperor. Pertinax himself, after his praises had been spoken
by the consuls and after the senate's shouts had shown its abuse of
Commodus, gave thanks to the senate and especially to Laetus, prefect of
the guard, on whose initiative Commodus had been killed and he himself
made emperor.

But when Pertinax had thanked Laetus, Falco the consul said:
/We understand from this what kind of emperor you will be, that we see
Laetus and Marcia, the ministers of Commodus's crimes, behind you./
To which Pertinax answered:
/You are a young consul and do not know the necessities of obedience.
They obeyed Commodus against their will, but when they had the chance
they showed what they had always wanted./

On the same day he was named Augustus, his wife Flavia Titiana was also
named Augusta, in those hours when he was paying his vows on the
Capitol. He was indeed the first of all, on the very day he was named
Augustus, to receive also the name of Father of the Fatherland, and
likewise at the same time the proconsular power, and likewise the right
of the fourth proposal. This stood in the place of an omen for Pertinax.

Pertinax therefore set out for the Palace, which was then empty, because
Commodus had been killed in the Vectilian House. On the first day, to a
tribune who asked for the watchword, he gave
/Let us be soldiers,/
reproaching, of course, the slackness of the former times, a watchword

he had given. The soldiers, however, did not bear that reproach, and at
once began to think of changing the emperor.

On that day he also invited the magistrates and the leading men of the
senate to a banquet, a custom which Commodus had neglected. And indeed
already on the day after the Kalends, when Commodus's statues were being
thrown down, the soldiers groaned, both for that and because the emperor
had given the same watchword again.

Service under an old emperor was feared. At last, on the third day
before the Nones, in the very midst of the vows, the soldiers wanted to
lead Triarius Maternus Lascivius, a noble senator, into the camp, in
order to set him over the Roman state. But he fled naked, came to
Pertinax in the Palace, and afterward left the city.

Forced by fear, Pertinax confirmed all that Commodus had given to the
soldiers and veterans. He also said that he was taking the empire from
the senate, an empire which he had already entered of his own accord.
He wholly abolished trials for treason, with an oath. He also recalled
those who had been banished on a charge of treason, and restored the
memory of those who had been killed. The senate named his son Caesar,
but Pertinax did not accept even his wife's title of Augusta, and of his
son he said:
/when he has earned it./
And when Commodus had mixed the praetorian ranks with countless
adlections, Pertinax passed a decree of the senate and ordered that
those who had not held praetorships but had received them by adlection
should rank after those who had truly been praetors. From this too he
stirred up great hatred among many.

He ordered the census-lists to be reviewed. He ordered convicted
informers 1 to be punished severely, and yet more mildly than former
emperors, fixing a penalty for each rank if it incurred the crime of
informing. He also passed a law that earlier wills should not be void
until others had been completed, and that the treasury should never
succeed on that account.

He himself declared that he would not enter upon any inheritance that
had been left him through someone's flattery, or that was tangled in a
lawsuit, so as to deprive the lawful and necessary heirs. And he added
these words to a decree of the senate:
/It is better, Conscript Fathers, to hold a poor state than to come to a
heap of riches by the tracks of dangers and disgraces./
The donatives and congiaria that Commodus had promised he paid. He
provided for the grain-supply with great care, and when he found the
treasury so empty that he confessed he had found no more than a million
sesterces, he was forced to collect what Commodus had imposed, contrary
to what he had declared. At last Lollianus Gentianus, a man of consular
rank, attacked him for acting against his promise, and received an
account of the necessity.

He held an auction of Commodus's goods, so that he even ordered the boys
and the concubines sold, except those who seemed to have been forced
into the Palace. And of those he ordered sold, many were later brought
back into service and amused the old man; some of them, under later
emperors, even reached senatorial rank. The buffoons who bore the
disgraces of the foulest names he posted for sale and sold. The money
from this trafficking, which

was huge, he gave to the soldiers as a donative. He also exacted from
the freedmen those sums by which they had been enriched when Commodus
was selling.

The auction of Commodus's goods was more notable in these items:
clothing with a silk weft and gold threads, of remarkable work; tunics
and cloaks, mantles and long-sleeved Dalmatian tunics, military cloaks
with curly nap, and purple Greek cloaks and camp cloaks, and Bardaic
hoods, and a toga; and gladiatorial arms set with gems and gold, and
Herculean swords, and gladiators' neck-chains, and vessels of clay
composed with gold, ivory, silver, and citrus-wood. And also
phallovitrobuli of the same material, and Samnite pots for heating resin
and pitch for plucking men and making them smooth. Also carriages of a
new kind of workmanship, with wheels whose rims were interlocked and
divided, and with elaborate seats, now for turning away the sun, now for
catching a breeze by turning; and others that measured the road and
showed the hours, and the rest of the things that suited his vices.

He also restored to their masters those who had gone from private houses
into the court. He brought the imperial banquet back from an immense
scale to a fixed measure. He also cut back all Commodus's expenditures.
By the emperor's example, when he lived more sparingly, cheapness arose
from everyone's self-restraint. For the imperial outlay, after what was
not needed had been driven out, to half of what was usual

he reduced it. He set rewards for those on service. The debt he had
contracted at the beginning of his reign he paid. He restored the
treasury to its proper state and established a fixed outlay for public
works. He contributed money for repairing the roads. He paid the wages
owed to very many from former times. At last he made the treasury equal
to meeting all its duties. He even, with a hardened sense of shame,
cancelled the alimentary grants that had been owing for nine years under
Trajan's institution.

Even as a private man he did not escape the suspicion of greed, when at
Vada Sabatia, after crushing the landowners with interest, he stretched
his own bounds more widely. At last, from a Lucilian verse, he was
called the farming cormorant. Many, moreover, put it in writing that
even in the provinces he governed as a consular he had acted meanly, for
he is said to have sold exemptions and military commissions. At last,
though his parents' property was very small and no inheritance had come
to him, he suddenly became rich.

He did restore their possessions to all from whom Commodus had taken
them, but not without a price. He always attended the regular senate and
always brought some motion. He always showed himself a fellow-citizen to
those who greeted him and those who approached him. Those who had been
attacked by false charges through their slaves he freed, after the
informers had been more severely condemned, such slaves being raised on
the cross. He even avenged some who were already dead.

A plot was prepared against him by Falco the consul, who 2 complained
in the senate, being eager to rule. The senate did believe him when a
certain slave, as if the son of Fabia † setique from the household of
Ceionius Commodus, had ridiculously claimed the Palatine house, and when
he had been found out and ordered to be beaten with whips and restored
to his master. In the punishment of this man those who hated Pertinax
are said to have found an occasion for mutiny.

Yet he spared Falco and asked the senate for his impunity. At last Falco
lived secure in his own affairs and died with his son as heir. Though
many said Falco had not known that the empire was being prepared for
him, others said he had been attacked by false testimony from slaves who
had embezzled the accounts.

But a faction was prepared against Pertinax by Laetus, prefect of the
guard, and those whom Pertinax's strictness had offended. For Laetus had
come to regret that he had made Pertinax emperor, because Pertinax used
to rebuke him as a foolish tattler of certain matters. It also seemed
hard to the soldiers that in Falco's case he had ordered many soldiers,
on a single slave's

testimony, to be killed. Therefore three hundred armed soldiers came
from the camp to the imperial palace, formed in a wedge.

Yet on that same day, while Pertinax was sacrificing, they say no heart
was found in the victim; and when he wished to make that good, he did
not find the head of the entrails. And then indeed all the soldiers were
remaining in camp. When they had gathered from the camp for attendance
on the prince, and Pertinax that day had postponed the procession which
he had prepared to the Athenaeum, in order to hear a poet, because of
the omen of the sacrifice, those who had come for attendance began to
return to camp. But suddenly that band reached the Palace and could
neither be kept off nor announced to the emperor. Indeed, such was the
hatred of all the courtiers for Pertinax that they urged the soldiers on
to the crime.

They came upon Pertinax while he was arranging the palace household, and
entered the porticoes of the Palace as far as the place called Sicily
and the dining-room of Jupiter. When he learned this, Pertinax sent
Laetus, prefect of the guard, to them; but Laetus, turning aside from
the soldiers, went out through the porticoes with his head covered and
made for his own house. But when they burst into the inner rooms,
Pertinax went out to them and calmed them with a long and serious
speech. But when a certain Tausius, one of the Tungri, by his talking
had brought the soldiers to anger and to fear, he thrust a spear into
Pertinax's breast. Then Pertinax, having prayed to Jupiter the Avenger,
covered his head with his toga and was stabbed by the rest. And Eclectus
indeed, after stabbing two, perished with him. The rest of the palace
chamberlains (for he had at once, when he was made emperor, given his
own to his emancipated sons) fled.

Many, indeed, say that the soldiers even broke into the bedchamber and
there killed Pertinax as he fled about the bed.

He was a venerable old man, with a flowing beard, hair combed back, a
rather stout body, a slightly protruding belly, an imperial stature, and
middling eloquence; more smooth than kind, and never believed to be
straightforward. Though affable in words, in fact he was mean and almost
stingy, so that he would serve halved lettuces and artichokes at dinners
in private life. And unless some food had been sent as a gift, however
many friends there were, he set out nine pounds of meat in three
courses; but if something more had been sent, he would even put it off
to another day, though he always invited many to dinner. Even as
emperor, if he was without guests, he dined in the same habit. If he
ever wished to send his friends something from his lunch, he sent two
bits of meat or a piece of tripe, sometimes the loins of a fowl. He
never ate a pheasant at a private dinner or sent one to anyone. When he
dined without friends, he had his wife with him and Valerianus, who had
taught with him, so that he might have literary talk.

He changed none of those whom Commodus had set over affairs, waiting for
the birthday of the city, because he wished that day to be the beginning
of his acts; and for that reason Commodus's attendants even prepared his
death in the baths,

it is said. He so shrank from the empire and all imperial things that
he always showed they displeased him. At last he did not wish to seem
other than he had been. In the senate-house he was most respectful, so
that he saluted the well-disposed senate and, as if still prefect of the
city, shared conversation with all. He even wished to lay down the
empire and return to private life. He would not have his sons reared in
the Palace.

He was so sparing and so eager for gain that as emperor he had carried
on trade at Vada Sabatia through his men, no differently than he used to
as a private citizen. Yet he was not much loved, seeing that all who
talked freely spoke ill of Pertinax, calling him a christologus, one who
spoke well and did ill. For even his own townsmen, who had flocked to
him once he was emperor and had gained nothing from him, called him so.
He also gladly accepted gifts from love of gain.

He left a son and a daughter surviving, and a wife, the daughter of
Flavius Sulpicianus, whom he had made prefect of the city in his own
place. He was less concerned about his wife's chastity, though she
openly loved a lyre-player. He himself, moreover, is said to have loved
Cornificia most infamously. He checked the palace freedmen very harshly,
and from this too he drew great hatred.

These were the signs of his death. Three days before he was killed he
seemed to himself to see in a pool a man threatening him with a sword.
And on the day he was killed they denied that the pupils in his eyes,
with the images they reflect, had been seen by those who looked for
them. And when he was sacrificing at the Lares, the most lively coals
went out, though they usually blaze up; and, as was said above, neither
heart nor head was found in the victims. Stars of the brightest kind
were also seen by day next to the sun the day before he died. And he
himself is said to have given an omen about Julianus his successor; for
when Didius Julianus presented to him his brother's son, to whom he was
betrothing his own daughter, he urged the young man to respect his uncle
and added:
/Regard my colleague and successor./
For Julianus had formerly been his colleague in the consulship and had
succeeded him in the proconsulship.

The soldiers and the courtiers held him in hatred. The people bore his
death with the greatest indignation, because they saw that through him
all the old ways could be restored. The soldiers who had killed him
carried his head, fixed on a pike, through the city into the camp. His
remains, when the head had been recovered, were laid in the tomb of his
wife's grandfather. And Julianus, his successor, buried his body with as
much honor as he could, when he found it in the Palace. He never made
any public mention of him either before the people or before the senate;
but when he himself too had already been deserted by the soldiers,

was enrolled among the gods. Under the emperor Severus, when the senate
had given Pertinax a great testimony, a dummy funeral of censorial rank
was held for him, and he was honored by Severus himself with a funeral
oration. Severus himself, from love of a good prince, received from the
senate the name Pertinax. Pertinax's son was made flamen to his father.
The Marcian sodales, who tended the rites of the deified Marcus, were
called Helviani on account of Helvius Pertinax. Circus-games were also
added for the birthday of his reign, which Severus later abolished, and
birthday-games, which remain.

He was born on the Kalends of August in the consulship of Verus and
Ambibulus, and was killed on the fifth day before the Kalends of April
in the consulship of Falco and Clarus. He lived sixty years, seven
months, and twenty-six days. He reigned two months and twenty-five days.
He gave the people a congiarium of a hundred denarii each; to the
praetorians he promised twelve thousand sesterces but gave six thousand.
What was promised to the armies was not given, because death forestalled
him. That he shrank from the empire a letter shows, which was attached
to his life by Marius Maximus. I was unwilling to insert it on account
of its excessive length.
