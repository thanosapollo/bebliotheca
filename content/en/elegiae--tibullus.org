Let another heap riches for himself in tawny gold and hold many acres of
tilled soil, whom constant toil may frighten with an enemy nearby, whose sleep
the blown Martial trumpets put to flight: let my poverty lead me through an
idle life, so long as my hearth shines with a constant fire. I myself will
plant the tender vines in their ripe season, a countryman, and great fruits
with an easy hand; and may hope not fail, but always offer heaps of grain and
rich must in a full vat. For I worship, whether a neglected stump in the
fields or an old stone at the crossroads holds flowery wreaths, and whatever
fruit the new year raises for me is set as a first offering before the
farmer's god. Golden Ceres, let there be for you from our land a crown of
ears, to hang before the temple doors, and let the red guardian be set in the
orchards, that Priapus may frighten the birds with his savage sickle. You too,
guardians of a field once rich, now poor, you bring your gifts, Lares. Then a
slaughtered heifer purified countless bullocks; now a lamb is the small victim
of a scanty soil. A lamb shall fall for you, around which the country youth
may cry /io, give harvests and good wines/. Now, now may I be able to live
content with little and not always be given to the long road, but avoid the
Dog-star's summer rising under the shade of a tree by the streams of passing
water. Yet let me not be ashamed at times to have held the hoe or to have
chided the slow oxen with the goad, nor let it irk me to carry home in my
bosom a lamb or a kid deserted by a forgetful mother. But you, thieves and
wolves, spare the scanty flock: prey is to be sought from a great herd. Here I
am used to purify my shepherd every year and to sprinkle peaceful Pales with
milk. Be present, gods, and do not scorn gifts from a poor table or from clean
earthenware. The ancient countryman first made himself earthen cups, and
shaped them from easy clay. I do not ask for the riches of my fathers and the
fruits that a stored harvest brought to an ancient grandfather: a small crop
is enough, enough if I may rest on a bed and ease my limbs on the accustomed
couch. How it delights, as I lie, to hear the harsh winds and to have held my
mistress in a tender embrace, or, when the winter South Wind has poured icy
waters, to follow sleep in safety with the fire helping. Let this befall me.
Let him be rich by right who can bear the fury of the sea and the grim rains.
O, however much gold there is, and emeralds, let it rather perish than that
any girl should weep for our journeys. It is for you to wage war by land and
sea, Messalla, that your house may display enemy spoils; the bonds of a
beautiful girl hold me bound, and I sit as doorkeeper before her hard doors. I
do not care to be praised, my Delia; so long as I am with you, I ask to be
called sluggish and idle. Let me look on you when my last hour has come; let
me hold you as I die with failing hand. You will weep, Delia, when I am laid
on the pyre that will burn, and you will give kisses mixed with sad tears. You
will weep: your breast is not bound with hard iron, nor does flint stand in
your tender heart. From that funeral no young man will be able to bring dry
eyes home, nor any maiden. Do not wound my shades, but spare your loosened
hair and spare, Delia, your tender cheeks. Meanwhile, while the fates allow,
let us join our loves: soon Death will come, her head wrapped in darkness;
soon sluggish age will creep on, and it will not be fitting to love, nor to
speak blandishments with a white head. Now light Venus is to be practiced,
while it is no shame to break doorposts and it delights to have joined in
quarrels. Here I am a good leader and soldier: you, standards and trumpets, go
far away, take wounds to greedy men, take wealth too: I, secure with my heap
stored up, will look down on the rich and look down on hunger.

Pour in the unmixed wine and check new griefs with wine, that sleep may seize
the conquered eyes of the weary, and let no one wake him whose temples are
struck with much Bacchus, while unhappy love is at rest. For a savage watch
has been set over our girl, and the firm door is shut with a hard bolt. Door
of a harsh master, let the rain beat you, let thunderbolts sent by Jove's
command seek you. Door, now open for me alone, overcome by complaints, and do
not sound when opened by a secretly turned hinge. And if our madness has
spoken any evils to you, forgive: I pray those words be on my own head. It
befits you to remember how many things I performed with a suppliant voice when
I gave flowery wreaths to the post. You too, Delia, do not timidly deceive the
guards; one must dare: Venus herself helps the brave. She favors, whether some
young man tries new thresholds, or a girl unbars the door with a fixed tooth;
she teaches how to slip secretly from a soft bed, she teaches how to set the
foot with no sound, she teaches how to exchange speaking nods before the
husband and hide bland words in arranged signs. And she does not teach this to
all, but to those whom neither sloth delays nor fear forbids to rise in the
dark night. See, I wander anxious through the whole city in the dark,

and she does not allow anyone to meet me who would wound bodies with iron or
seek prizes by snatching a cloak. Whoever is held by love, let him go safe and
sacred wherever he will: it is fitting not to have feared ambush. The sluggish
cold of a winter night does not harm me, nor does it when rain falls in much
water. This labor does not hurt, if only Delia unbar the posts and call me,
silent, at the sound of her finger. Spare your eyes, whether a man or a woman
meets us: Venus wants her thefts to be hidden. Do not frighten with the noise
of feet, do not ask a name, do not bring lights near with a gleaming torch.
And if anyone has looked on us unawares, let him hide it and deny by all the
gods that he remembers: for whoever has been talkative will feel that Venus
was born of blood, that she is from the rushing sea. Yet your husband will not
believe this man, as a truthful witch promised me by her magic ministry. I
have seen her drawing the stars from the sky; she turns the course of a
rushing river by her charm; she splits the ground with her song and draws the
shades from tombs and calls bones down from a warm pyre; now she holds the
infernal troops with a magic hiss, now she bids them withdraw their foot when
milk is sprinkled. When she will, she drives the clouds from a sad sky; when
she will, she summons snows in a summer circuit. She alone is said to hold
Medea's evil herbs, she alone to have tamed Hecate's wild dogs. She composed
for me songs by which you could deceive: sing them three times, spit three
times when the charms are spoken. He will be able to believe nothing about us
to anyone, not even to himself, if he himself has seen us on the soft couch.
Yet keep yourself from others: for he will see all the rest; about me alone he
himself will feel nothing. What, am I to believe? Surely this same woman said
she could loose my loves with songs or herbs, and she purified me with
torches, and on a clear night a dark victim fell to the magic gods. I was not
praying that love be wholly gone, but that it be mutual, nor would I wish to
be able to do without you. He was iron, who, when he could have you, preferred
in his folly to follow plunder and arms. He may drive the conquered troops of
the Cilicians before him, and plant Martial camps on captured soil, and sit,
all woven with silver, all with gold, to be gazed at on a swift horse: if only
I myself could yoke oxen with you, Delia, and pasture the flock on the
accustomed mountain, and hold you, while it is allowed, in tender arms, let
sleep be soft for me even on untilled ground. What does it profit to recline
on a Tyrian couch without favoring love, when night comes that must be watched
with weeping? For then neither feathers nor painted coverlets could bring
sleep, nor the sound of peaceful water. Have I violated the godhead of great
Venus with a word, and does my impious tongue now pay the penalty? Am I said
to have gone unchaste to the seats of the gods and torn wreaths from the holy
hearths? I would not hesitate, if I have deserved it, to fall before the
temples and give kisses to the consecrated thresholds, nor to crawl the earth
on my knees as a suppliant and beat my wretched head on the holy post. But you
who laugh gladly at our ills, beware soon for yourself: the god will not rage
against one man forever. I have seen one who had mocked the wretched loves of
the young, later as an old man submit his neck to Venus's bonds and compose
blandishments for himself with a trembling voice and wish to arrange his white
hair with his hands; nor was he ashamed to stand before the doors of a dear
girl or to have detained her maid in the middle of the forum. This man a boy,
this man a young man, a close crowd tramples round; each spits into his own
soft bosom. But spare me, Venus: my mind always serves you, dedicated: why,
bitter one, do you burn your own harvests?

You will go without me, Messalla, through the Aegean waves; O would that you
yourself and the cohort be mindful of me. Phaeacia holds me sick in unknown
lands; only keep back, black Death, your greedy hands. Keep back, dark Death,
I pray: here I have no mother to gather the burnt bones into her sad bosom, no
sister to give Assyrian scents to the ash and weep with hair poured out before
the tomb; Delia is nowhere; she, when she sent me from the city, is said to
have consulted all the gods first. She three times took up the sacred lots of
the boy: the boy brought her sure omens from the three. All things granted
return: yet she was never deterred from weeping and looking back at our
journey. I myself, the consoler, when I had already given my charges, kept
seeking, anxious, slow delays. Either I alleged birds or dire omens, or that
Saturn's sacred day held me. O how often, having started the journey, I said
that a foot striking the gate had given me sad signs! Let no one dare to
depart against Love's will, or let him know that he has gone out with the god
forbidding. What now, Delia, does your Isis profit me, what those rattles so
often struck back by your hand, or what, while you piously keep her rites,
that you washed yourself purely (I remember) and lay apart on a pure couch?
Now, goddess, now help me (for many a painted tablet in your temples teaches
that you can heal), so that my Delia, paying her vowed words, may sit before
the sacred doors, covered in linen, and twice a day, with hair unbound, be
bound to speak your praises, marked out in the Pharian crowd. But let it
befall me to celebrate my fathers' Penates and to give monthly incense to the
ancient Lar. How well they lived when Saturn was king, before the earth was
opened into long roads! Not yet had the pine despised the dark-blue waves and
offered a poured-out bosom to the winds, nor had the wandering sailor, seeking
gains in unknown lands, pressed his raft with foreign merchandise. In that
time no strong bull went under the yoke, no horse champed the bit with a tamed
mouth, no house had doors, no stone was fixed in the fields to rule the
ploughlands with sure bounds. The oaks themselves gave honey, and of their own
accord the sheep brought udders of milk to meet the carefree. There was no
battle-line, no anger, no wars, nor had the savage smith drawn the sword with
harsh art. Now under Jove as lord there are always slaughters and wounds, now
the sea, now a thousand sudden ways of death. Spare, father. Perjuries do not
frighten me in my fear, nor impious words spoken against the holy gods. But if
we have already filled our fated years, make a stone stand over my bones with
inscribed marks: 'Here lies Tibullus, consumed by a harsh death, while he
followed Messalla by land and sea.' But because I am always easy to tender
Love, Venus herself will lead me into the Elysian fields. Here dances and song
flourish, and wandering everywhere the birds sound a sweet song from a thin
throat; an untilled crop bears cassia, and through all the fields the kindly
earth flowers with scented roses; and a line of young men mixed with tender
girls plays, and Love constantly mixes battles. There is whoever greedy death
came upon as a lover, and he wears myrtle wreaths on his marked hair. But the
accursed seat lies hidden in deep night, around which black rivers sound: and
Tisiphone, with wild snakes uncombed for hair, rages, and the impious crowd
flees this way and that. Then black Cerberus at the gate hisses from a mouth
of serpents and keeps watch before the bronze doors. There the guilty limbs of
Ixion, who dared to try Juno, are turned on a swift wheel, and Tityos,
stretched through nine acres of earth, feeds the constant birds with his black
vitals. Tantalus is there, and around him the pools, but the water already
now, now about to be drunk, leaves his sharp thirst, and the offspring of
Danaus, because they injured Venus's godhead, carry Lethean waters into hollow
jars. Let him be there, whoever has violated my loves, and has wished slow
campaigns for me. But you, I pray, remain chaste, and let a busy old woman
always sit as guardian of your holy modesty. Let her tell you little tales
and, with the lamp set down, draw long threads from a full distaff, but around
her let a girl fixed to heavy tasks, wearied, little by little let the work go
in sleep. Then let me come suddenly, and let no one announce me before, but
let me seem to be present, sent from heaven to you. Then, Delia, just as you
will be, with long hair disordered, run to meet me with bare foot. This I
pray, that shining Dawn on her rose horses may bring us this Lucifer gleaming.

So may shady coverings befall you, Priapus, that the suns may not harm your
head, nor the snows: what skill of yours has captured the beautiful? Surely
your beard does not shine, your hair is not tended, and naked you draw out the
cold of winter's midwinter, naked also the dry times of the summer Dog-star.
So I; then the rustic offspring of Bacchus, the god armed with a curved
sickle, answered me thus: 'O flee from trusting yourself to the tender crowd
of boys, for they always have a cause of just love. This one pleases because
he checks a horse with narrow reins, this one strikes the calm water with a
snowy breast, this one has captured because strong daring is present; but on
that one a maiden shame stands before the tender cheeks. But lest they capture
you, if perhaps he first refuses, tedium: little by little he will give his
neck under the yoke. A long day has taught lions to obey man, a long day has
eaten rocks with soft water; the year ripens grapes on sunny hills, the year
drives the bright signs in a sure turn. And do not fear to swear: the
perjuries of Venus the winds carry void over lands and the top of the straits.
Great thanks to Jove: the father himself has forbidden that whatever foolish
love has sworn greedily should have force, and Dictynna allows you to affirm
with impunity by her arrows, and Minerva by her hair. But if you are slow, you
will stray: age will pass. How quickly the unsluggish day stands and returns,
how quickly the earth loses its purple colors, how quickly the tall poplar its
beautiful leaves! How he lies, when the fates of weak old age have come, who
was first sent from the Elean barrier, a horse! I have already seen a young
man, when later age pressed him, grieving that the foolish days had gone by.
Cruel gods! The snake newly sheds its years; the fates have given no delay to
beauty. Bacchus and Phoebus alone have eternal youth, for unshorn hair befits
each god. You, whatever it will please your boy to try, yield: by compliance
love will conquer very many things. And do not refuse to go as companion,
though a long road is prepared and the Dog-star scorches the fields with dry
thirst, though the rain-bringing bow, bordering the sky with painted rust,
anticipates the coming water. Or if he will wish to go through the dark-blue
waves by ship, yourself drive the light raft through the straits with the oar.
And do not repent to have undergone hard labors or to have worn unaccustomed
hands with work, nor, if he should wish to close the high valleys with snares,
while you please, let your shoulders refuse to carry the nets. If he will want
arms, you will try to play with a light right hand: often you will give your
bare side, that he may win. Then he will be mild to you; then you may snatch
dear kisses: he will fight, but still he will give them, fitting. He will give
them snatched at first, later he will bring them himself to one asking, later
he may even wish to have wrapped himself on your neck. Alas, now these ages
badly handle wretched arts: already the tender boy has grown used to wanting
gifts. But you who first taught to sell love, whoever you are, let an unhappy
stone press your bones. Boys, love the Pierides and learned poets, and let not
golden gifts outdo the Pierides. By song the purple hair of Nisus is; if there
were no songs, ivory would not have shone from Pelops's shoulder. Whom the
Muses report will live, while the earth bears oaks, while the sky bears stars,
while the river carries waters. But he who does not hear the Muses, who sells
love, let him follow the chariot of Idaean Ops and fill three hundred cities
with wanderings and cut his cheap limbs to Phrygian measures. Venus herself
wants a place for blandishments: she favors suppliant complaints, wretched
weepings.' These things the god uttered with his mouth for me to sing to
Titius, but a wife forbids Titius to remember them. Let him obey his own; you
celebrate me as master, whom a boy, clever with much art, treats badly. Each
has his own glory: let lovers who are scorned consult me: our door lies open
to all. There will be a time when a busy crowd of young men will escort me as
an old man, carrying Venus's precepts. Alas, alas, how Marathus tortures me
with slow love! Arts fail, and tricks fail. Spare, boy, I ask, lest I become a
shameful tale, when they will laugh at my empty teachings.

I was harsh and used to say I bore a separation well, but now that strong
glory is far from me. For I am driven as a swift top is driven over the level
ground by a lash, which a quick boy turns with accustomed art. Burn the wild
one and twist him, that he may not please to say anything magnificent after
this: tame the bristling words. Yet spare, by the pacts of the secret bed, by
our love I ask and by the head laid together. I am he who, when you lay worn
out with a sad disease, am said to have snatched you by my vows, and I myself
purified around you with pure sulphur, when an old woman had sung before with
a magic song; I myself took care that savage dreams could not harm, to be
worshipped three times with holy meal; I myself, veiled with a thread and with
tunics loosened, gave nine vows to Trivia in the silent night. I have paid
all: now another enjoys the love, and that lucky one uses my prayers. Yet I
used to invent a happy life for myself, if you had been safe, madman that I
was, but with the god refusing. I will till the country, and my Delia will be
there as guardian of the crops, while the threshing-floor rubs the harvests in
the hot sun, or she will keep the grapes for me in full troughs and the white
must pressed by a swift foot; she will grow used to counting the flock, the
talkative home-born slave will grow used to playing in the mistress's lap of
the lover. She will know how to carry to the farmer's god a grape for the
vines, ears for the crop, a feast for the flock. Let her rule all, let all
things be her care, and let it delight me to be nothing in the whole house.
Here my Messalla will come, for whom Delia may pull down sweet fruits from
chosen trees; and, having venerated so great a man, let her busily care for
him, prepare for him, and herself as attendant carry the feast. These things I
used to invent, which now the East Wind and the South Wind toss as vows
through the scented Armenians. Often I have tried to drive off cares with
wine, but grief had turned all the unmixed wine into tears. Often I have held
another, but already when I was approaching joys, Venus warned me of my
mistress and deserted me. Then as she left the woman called me accursed, and
she is ashamed, and she tells that my girl knows unspeakable things. She does
not do this with words: our girl curses with her face and tender arms and
yellow hair. Such was the Nereid Thetis, sea-blue, once carried to Haemonian
Peleus on a bridled fish. These things have harmed me, because a rich lover is
here for her; a clever bawd has come for my ruin. Let her eat bloody feasts
and with a gory mouth drink sad cups with much gall; let souls complaining of
their fates always fly around her, and let a violent screech-owl sing from her
roof; let her herself, raging with hunger goading, seek herbs from tombs and
bones left by savage wolves, and run with naked groin and howl through the
cities, and afterward let a harsh crowd of dogs drive her from the crossroads.
It will come: the god gives signs; there are powers for a lover, and Venus
rages, left by an unjust law. But you, as soon as you can, desert the precepts
of the greedy witch, for every love is conquered by gifts. The poor man will
always be at hand, the poor man will approach you first and will be fixed at
your tender side, the poor man, a faithful companion in a narrow line of
crowd, will put his hands under and make a way, the poor man will lead you
secretly to hidden friends and himself draw the bonds from your snowy foot.
Alas, we sing in vain, and the door does not open, conquered by words, but
must be struck with a full hand. But you who are now the more powerful, fear
my fates: light Chance is turned on the swift circle of a wheel. Not in vain
does someone already now stand busy on the threshold and often look out and
shrink back, and he pretends to pass the house, then soon runs back, and alone
before the very doors he keeps clearing his throat. Secret love is preparing I
know not what. Use it, I ask, while it is allowed: your boat swims in liquid
water.

Always, that I may be drawn in, you offer me bland looks, yet afterward you
are sad and harsh to the wretched man, Love. What have you to do with cruelty
toward me? Or is it a great glory for a god to have laid ambush for a man? For
nets are stretched for me: already Delia secretly, clever, warms I know not
whom in the silent night. She indeed denies so many things, but it is hard to
believe: so she also keeps denying utterly about me to her husband. I myself,
wretched, taught her by what pact she could mock the guards: alas, alas, now I
am pressed by my own art. Now she has learned to invent causes that she might
lie alone, now to be able to turn the door on a silent hinge. Then I gave
juices and herbs by which the bruise might leave which mutual love makes with
a pressed tooth. But you, careless husband of a deceitful girl, watch me too,
that she may sin in nothing. Beware lest she frequent young men with much
talk, or lie with her breast open in a loose bosom, lest she deceive you with
a nod, and lest she draw liquor with her finger and lead marks on the circle
of the table. Fear how often she will go out, or if she will say she is
visiting the rites of the Good Goddess, not to be approached by males. But if
you trust me, I alone will follow her to the altars; then there would be no
need for me to have feared for my eyes. Often, as if I were testing her gems
and seal, I remember I touched her hand on a pretext; often I produced sleep
for you with unmixed wine, but I myself drank sober cups, a victor, with water
put under. I have not harmed you on purpose: forgive one confessing; Love
commanded: who would bear arms against the gods? I am he, nor will I now be
ashamed to speak true, against whom your dog was pressing all night. What need
have you of a tender wife? If you do not know how to keep your goods, the key
is in the doors in vain. She holds you, she sighs for other loves when they
are absent, and she pretends that her head has suddenly begun to ache. But you
should trust her to me to be kept: I do not refuse savage blows, I do not
decline bonds of the feet. Then keep far away, whoever tends his hair with
art, and whose loose toga flows with a poured-out bosom, and whoever will meet
her, that he may not be able to have a charge, let him stand far off or on
another ~stet procul~ before the road. So the god himself bids it be done, so
the great priestess prophesied to me with a divine sound. When she is shaken
with Bellona's motion, she fears neither the sharp flame nor, out of her mind,
the twisted blows; she herself, violent, cuts her own arms with a two-edged
axe and, unpunished, sprinkles the goddess with poured-out blood, and she
stands with her side pierced by a spit, stands wounded in the breast, and she
sings the outcomes which the great goddess warns: 'Spare to violate the girl
whom Love guards, lest you later repent to have learned it by a great ill. If
he has touched her, wealth will slip, as blood from our wound, as this ash is
torn by the winds.' And she spoke I know not what penalties for you, my Delia;
yet if you should admit them, I pray that one be light. I do not spare you for
your own sake, but your mother moves me, and the golden old woman conquers my
angers. She leads you to me in the dark and with much fear silently joins our
hands in secret, she waits at the doors by night, fixed there, and from far
off she knows the noise of my feet as I come. Live long for me, sweet old
woman: I would wish to have contributed my own years with you, if only it be
right. I will always love you and your daughter because of you: whatever she
does, she is still your blood. Only teach her to be chaste, though no fillet
binds her tied hair nor a long stola her feet. And let the laws be hard for
me: let me not be able to praise any woman without her attacking my eyes, and
if she thinks I have sinned in anything, let me be led by the hair,
undeserving, and dragged headlong along the sloping ways. I would not wish to
strike you, but if that fury should come, I would wish not to have had hands;
and be chaste not from savage fear but with a faithful mind: let mutual love
keep you for me when I am absent. But she who was faithful to none, afterward,
conquered by old age, poor, draws twisted threads with a trembling hand and
ties firm leashes to hired webs and picks the drawn threads from snowy fleece.
Young men's troops see her with rejoicing mind and recall that the old woman
deservedly bears so many ills; Venus from high Olympus looks at her weeping
and warns how bitter she is to the unfaithful. Let these curses fall on
others; we, Delia, let us both be an example of love with white hair.

The Fates have sung this day, spinning the fatal threads, not to be loosed by
any god, that this man would be who could pour out the Aquitanian peoples,
whom the Atax, conquered by a strong soldier, would tremble at. They have come
to pass: the Roman youth has seen new triumphs and leaders with captured arms
bound; but you, Messalla, wearing the victorious laurels, an ivory chariot was
carrying on shining horses. Not without me was the honor born for you: the
Tarbellian Pyrene is witness, and the shores of the Santonic Ocean, witness
the Saone and the swift Rhone and the great Garonne, and the dark-blue water
of the yellow Carnute, the Loire. Or shall I sing you, Cydnus, who with silent
waves, dark-blue, creep gently through the shallows with calm waters, and how
great cold Taurus, touching the clouds with his heavenly peak, nourishes the
unshorn Cilicians? Why should I tell how the white dove, holy to the
Palestinian Syrian, flies untouched through crowded cities, and how Tyre,
first taught to trust a raft to the winds, looks out from her towers on the
vast plain of the sea, and how, when Sirius splits the dry fields, fertile
Nile overflows with summer water? Father Nile, by what cause could I say, or
in what lands, you have hidden your head? Because of you your earth asks for
no rains, nor does the dry grass supplicate rainy Jove. The barbarian youth
sings you and marvels at you as its Osiris, taught to beat the breast for the
Memphite bull. Osiris first made ploughs with a skilled hand and troubled the
tender ground with iron, first committed seeds to the untried earth and
gathered fruits from trees not known. He taught to join the tender vine to
stakes, he taught to cut the green hair with a hard sickle; to him first the
ripe grape, pressed by untilled feet, gave pleasant flavors. That liquor
taught voices to bend in song, and moved limbs that did not know to sure
measures, and Bacchus gave farmers' breasts, worn with great toil, to be
loosed from sadness. Bacchus also brings rest to afflicted mortals, though the
legs sound struck with a hard fetter. You have no sad cares nor grief, Osiris,
but chorus and song and light love is fitting, but various flowers and a brow
bound with ivy-clusters, but a yellow mantle poured to the tender feet, and
Tyrian clothes and the pipe sweet in song, and the light chest conscious of
hidden rites. Come here and celebrate the Genius with games and the Genius
with dances and pour much unmixed wine on his temples: let ointments drip from
his shining hair, and let him wear soft wreaths on head and neck. So come, you
of this day: let me give you honors of incense, and I will bring cakes sweet
with Mopsopian honey. But let offspring grow up for you, to increase the
parent's deeds and stand venerable around the old man. And let him not be
silent about the monuments of the road, whom the Tusculan land and white Alba
holds with its ancient Lar. For here, by the wealth heaped by you, hard gravel
is laid, here flint is joined with fitting art. The farmer sings you, when he

Macer follows the camp: what will become of tender Love? Should he be a

spoken great things magnificently, closed doors shake out the strong words.

your weapons broken, the arrows, if it is allowed, and the torches

would have finished the ills with death, but credulous hope fosters life and

wreaths soaked with my tears, I will flee to her mound and sit as a suppliant

girl herself is good. The bawd Phryne kills the wretched man and secretly

she is at home: often, when night has been promised me, she announces that the
girl is faint or has feared some threats. Then I die with cares, then my lost
mind invents who holds my girl, or in how many ways he holds her: then I pray
curses on you, bawd: may you live anxious enough, if however small a part of
the vows has moved the gods.

The festive Kalends of Roman Mars have come (this was the year rising for our
grandfathers) and now wandering gifts run everywhere in a sure procession
through the city's streets and through the houses. Say, Pierides, with what
honor she should be gifted, whether mine, or if I am deceived, still dear
Neaera. The beautiful are captured by song, the greedy by a price: let her
rejoice, as she is worthy, in my verses. But let yellow parchment wrap the
snowy little book, and let pumice shear the white hairs before, and let a
letter made indicate your name, to border the thin paper's top edges, and let
the horns between the twin brows be painted: for so it is right to send a
tended work. By you, authors of this song for me, I pray, and by Castalian
shade and Pierian lakes, go home and give her the tended little book, as it
will be: let no color flow down from it. She will bring me back word whether
the care is mutual of us, or less, or whether I have fallen from her whole
breast. But first give her a large greeting, deserved, and say these words in
a lowered sound: 'He who was once your husband, now your brother, chaste
Neaera, sends these things and asks that you receive the small gifts, and he
swears that you are dearer to him than his marrows, whether you will be a wife
to him or a sister; but rather a wife: the pale water of Dis will take from
him, when he is extinguished, the hope of this name.

He who first snatched a dear girl from a young man and a dear young man from a
girl, he was iron; hard also was he who could bear so great a grief, and live
with a wife snatched away. I am not firm in this, this patience is not in our
nature: grief breaks strong hearts; nor am I ashamed to speak true things and
to confess that weariness has been born of a life that has suffered so many
ills. Therefore when I have been changed into a thin shade and black ash will
cover the white bones above, let Neaera come before my pyre, sad, with long
hair uncombed, and weep before my pyre; but let her come accompanied by the
grief of a dear mother: let this one mourn for a son-in-law, let that one
mourn for a husband. Having first addressed my shades and prayed for the soul
and having poured a pious liquor on their hands before, the bones, which will
be the only part of my body that will remain, they, girt in a black garment,
will gather, white, and first they will sprinkle the gathered ones with aged
Lyaeus, soon also they will prepare them with snowy milk, after this they will
take off the moisture with linen cloths and place them dry in a marble home.
There let the merchandises which rich Panchaia sends, and the Eastern Arabs,
and rich Assyria, and tears mindful of us be poured in the same place: so I
would wish to be laid, turned into bones. But let a letter show the sad cause
of death and mark these verses on the famous front: Lygdamus is placed here:
grief and care of Neaera, a snatched wife, was the cause for this man to
perish.

What does it profit to have filled heaven with vows, Neaera, and to have given
bland incense with much prayer, not that I should come forth from the
threshold of a marble roof, marked out and conspicuous in a famous house, or
that many of my acres should be renewed by bulls and a kindly earth give great
harvests, but that I might share with you the joys of a long life and that our
old age should fall in your bosom, then when, having finished the measured
time of light, I should be forced to go naked on the Lethean raft? For what
does a heavy weight of rich gold profit me, and if a thousand oxen should
split rich fields? Or what does a house profit, leaning on Phrygian columns,
or on yours, Taenarus, or on yours, Carystus, and groves in houses imitating
sacred woods and gilded beams and a marble floor? Or what does the shell
gathered on the Erythraean shore help, and wool dyed with Sidonian purple, and
what else the people marvel at? In those things there is envy: the crowd loves
very many things falsely. Men's minds and cares are not lightened by wealth,
nor does Fortune rule times by her own law. Let poverty be pleasant for me
with you, Neaera: but without you I want no gifts of kings. O the snowy light
that will be able to give you back to me! O the day three and four times lucky
for me! But if, whatever is vowed for a sweet return, my god should hear with
an averted ear, neither kingdoms please me nor the Lydian gold-bearing river
nor the wealth which the circle of lands holds up. Let others desire these;
let it be allowed me in a poor cult to be able to enjoy a dear wife, secure.
Be present and favor timid vows, Saturnia, and favor, Cyprian, carried on your
shell. Or if the fates deny return and the sad sisters, who draw the threads
and spin what will be, let pale rich Orcus call me into vast rivers and the
black swamp in sluggish water.

May the gods bring better things, and may the dreams not be true for me which
last night's worst rest brought. Go far away, empty and false, turn away the
visions, cease to want to seek faith in us. The gods warn true things,
entrails approved by Tuscan men, messengers of a coming lot, warn true things;
rash dreams play in a deceitful night and bid frightened minds fear false
things; and the race of men, born into cares, appease the omens of night with
pious spelt and leaping salt! And yet, however it is, whether they will wish
to be warned true things, or to believe a lying sleep, let Lucina make the
night's fears empty and wish that I, undeserving, have feared in vain, if my
mind is not liable to a shameful deed nor has an impious tongue injured the
great gods. Already Night, having measured the heavenly world with black
four-horse team, had washed the wheels in the cerulean river, and the god
useful to a sick mind had not put me to sleep: Sleep fails before anxious
houses. At last, when Phoebus looked out from the top of rising, late rest
pressed the eyes of the fainting. Here a young man, his temples wreathed with
chaste laurel, seemed to set foot in our seat. No former age, nor any house of

led to a young husband is dyed on the face with reddening tender cheeks, and

autumn. The bottom of the mantle seemed to play thus: for this was the garment
on the shining body. A work of rare art, gleaming with tortoise-shell and
gold, a talkative lyre hung on the left side. This, arriving, he first
modulated with an ivory plectrum and gave lucky songs with a sounding mouth;
but after the fingers had spoken with the voice, he uttered these sad words in
a sweet measure: 'Hail, care of the gods: for Phoebus and Bacchus and the
Pierides duly favor a chaste poet; but Bacchus, offspring of Semele, and the
learned sisters do not know how to say what the following hour may bring; but
my father has given me to be able to see the laws of the fates and the
outcomes of future age; therefore receive what I say, a prophet not deceitful,
and what I, the Cynthian god, will bring with a true mouth. As dear to you as
not a daughter to a mother, as not a beautiful girl to an eager husband, for
whom you trouble the heavenly powers with vows, who does not allow secure days
to go for you and, when Sleep has veiled you with a dark cloak, deceives you
empty with nocturnal images, beautiful Neaera, celebrated in your songs,
prefers to be another man's girl, and her impious mind drives different cares
of her own, nor does chaste Neaera as a bride rejoice in a chaste house. Ah
cruel race, and woman a name not faithful! Ah let her perish, if any has
learned to deceive a man! But she will be able to be bent: their mind is
changeable; only stretch your arms with much faith. Savage Love has taught to
try strong labors, savage Love has taught to be able to suffer blows. That I
once pastured Admetus's snowy heifers is not a tale invented for empty jest;
then I could not rejoice in the sounding cithara nor give back with the voice
sounds like the strings, but I practiced song on a translucent oat-pipe, I
that son of Latona and of Jove. You do not know what love is, young man, if
you refuse to bear a harsh mistress and a wild marriage. Therefore do not
hesitate to apply bland complaints: hard breasts are conquered by a soft
prayer. But if oracles sing true things in sacred temples, report these things
said to her in our name: the Delian himself promises you this marriage; lucky
in this, cease to want another man.' He spoke, and sluggish sleep flowed down

The water that flows from Etruscan springs holds you, water not to be
approached under the summer Dog-star, but now nearest to the sacred waters of
Baiae, when the ground relaxes itself in purple spring. But Persephone
announces the black hour to me: spare to harm an undeserving young man,
goddess. I have not, bold, tried to teach the rites of the praiseworthy
goddess that are to be violated by no men, nor has my right hand stained cups
with death-bearing juices nor given ground poisons to anyone, nor have we
moved sacrilegious fires to temples, nor do unspeakable deeds trouble my
heart, nor have we, meditating the quarrels of an insane mind, loosed an
impious mouth against the opposing gods. And white hairs have not yet injured
the black, nor has bent old age come with a slow foot: our parents saw our

times. And would that I be frightened in vain by an empty heat! But my limbs

waters are celebrated and the easy wave is struck by a slow hand. Live happy,

Meanwhile promise black sheep to Dis and cups of snowy milk mixed with unmixed

You, Messalla, I will sing, although known virtue frightens me; though weak
strengths cannot stand under it, still I will begin, and if songs should fail
the deserved praises (let me be a humble founder for such great acts, and no
one except you could weave your deeds into sheets so that greater things would
not remain beyond the words), it is enough for us to have wished; and do not
spit out small gifts: even to Phoebus the Cretan brought most welcome gifts,
and Icarus was a more pleasant guest than all to Bacchus, as the stars in a
pure sky testify, Erigone and the Dog, lest a longer age deny it; nay even
Alcides, a god about to ascend Olympus, set glad footsteps in Molorchus's
roof, and a small grain appeased the heavenly ones, nor does a bull always
fall for them as a victim with gilded horn. Let this small labor also be
welcome, that I may be able from there, mindful, to compose other verses and
in another way. Let another tell the marvelous work of the great world, how
the earth settled in the immense air, and how the sea flowed together into the
curved orb, and the wandering air, where it strives to rise from the lands,
and to this let the fiery aether flow woven everywhere, and how all things are
shut under the hanging sky; but whatever my Camenae will be able to dare,
whether they will be able equal to you or, what hope refuses, beyond, or less
(and surely they will sing less), we vow all this to you, and let not the
sheet lack so great a song for me. For although the praises of an ancient race
remain for you, your glory is not content with ancestors' fame, nor do you ask
what each label says under each image, but you strive to conquer the ancient
honors of the line, how you yourself are a greater glory to those who will be
than your ancestors were to you: but a title under a name will not take your
deeds, but there will be great volumes for you in eternal verse, and they will
come together eager to compose your praises from everywhere, both those who
will sing with bound foot and those with loosed; who rather, there will be a
contest: let me be victor among them, that I may inscribe our name in such
great acts. For who carries greater things than you in camp or forum? Yet here
or here praise is not greater or less for you, just as when a scale is pressed
with equal weight, it does not sit more prone on this side nor rise from that,
such as, if ever an unequal burden presses on both sides, it swims unstable,
lower with alternate disk. For whether the inconstancy of the diverse crowd
roars, no other could settle it; or if a judge's anger must be appeased, it
will be able to grow mild by your words. Not Pylos or Ithaca are said to have
begotten so great, Nestor, or Ulysses, the great glory of a small city,
although that old man lived while Titan ran three ages through the world with
fertile hours, although that one, bold, wandered through unknown cities, where
the earth is shut by the sea's last waves: for he drove back the Ciconians'
bands with opposing arms, nor was the lotus strong to turn aside the courses
begun, and the Neptunian inhabitant of the Aetnaean crag yielded, his eyes
fouled, conquered by Maronian Bacchus; he also carried the Aeolian winds
through peaceful Nereus, he approached the uncultivated Laestrygonians and
Antiphates, whom noble Artacie waters with a cold wave; nor did Circe's cups,
learned, turn him alone, although she was the Sun's offspring, fitted either
with herbs or with song to change old figures; he also approached the dark
citadels of the Cimmerians, to whom day never appeared with a white rising,
whether Phoebus ran above the lands or below; he saw how, subjected to Pluto's
infernal kingdom, the great offspring of the gods ran about as light shades,
and he passed the Sirens' shores with a swift ship; him, swimming between the
confines of twin death, neither Scylla's attack with savage mouth terrified,
when she crept, a wild thing, among the raging waves with dogs, nor did
violent Charybdis consume him in her manner, whether she rose high from the
lowest flood, or if with the whirlpool broken she bared the sea. Let not the
unviolated pastures of the wandering Sun be silent, nor the love and fertile
fields of Atlas's daughter Calypso, and Phaeacia's land, the end of wretched
wandering. And these things, whether they are known among our lands, or
whether the tale has given a new world to these wanderings, let that be his
labor, while your eloquence is greater.

Already no other holds the arts of war more fittingly than you, where it is
fitting to draw a safe ditch before the camp, how to fix opposing stakes
against the enemy, or what place it is better to shut with a drawn rampart,
where the earth bursts forth sweet liquors of a spring, that the approach may
be easy for yours and steep for the enemy, that the soldier may flourish in a
constant contest of praise, who has better thrown a slow stake or a swift
arrow or broken things in the way with a pliant javelin, or who can check a
swift horse with a tight rein and grant the poured-out reins to the slow, and
in turn now contend with a straight pace, or if it please, turn shorter in a
curved ring, who can guard with a small shield, whether he wish with the right
or the left, whether the heavy onset of a spear come this way or that, or
touch marked places more amply with a swift sling. Already at once the
contests of bold Mars will come and the lines prepare to run together with
opposing standards, then let it not fail you to compose the face of the fight,
whether there is need that the line stand in a squared column, that a straight
order run down with equalized fronts, or if it please to discern a double war
separately, that the right-hand soldier hold the left and the left-hand the
right, and that victory be double of a twin event. But my songs do not wander
through doubtful praises: for I sing things tested in wars. Witness for me the
strong soldier of conquered Iapydia, witness also the deceitful Pannonian,
scattered everywhere into the icy Alps, witness also he born poor in the
Arupine fields, whom if anyone should see how old age has not broken him, he
would marvel less at the three ages of Pylian fame: for while the old man
completes the times of a long life, Titan will have renewed a hundred fertile
years, yet he himself, swift, dares to set his body over a swift horse and
sits as a controller with strong reins. With you as leader Domator, turned as
to the back as at no other time, subjected free necks to the Roman chain. Yet
you will not be content with these: greater things than those done press, as
has been found by me from true signs, with which Amythaon's son Melampus could
not contend. For just now you had put on a garment gleaming with Tyrian weft
at the rising day, leader of the fertile year, when the Sun raised his head
more splendid from the liquid waves and the fierce winds, discordant, held
their blasts, and the curved rivers did not drive their accustomed courses,
nay even the rushing sea stood with peaceful waves, nor does any bird glide
through the airy breezes, nor does a rough four-footed beast feed on the dense
woods, nay mute silences have been granted lavishly to your vows. Jupiter
himself, carried through the void on a light chariot, was present and left
Olympus neighboring to heaven and offered himself with an ear intent on your
prayers and nodded to all things with a truthful head: a fire added to the
altars shone out more gladly over the heaped heaps. Nay, with the god urging,
begin to press on great affairs; let triumphs not be the same for you and for
others: neighboring Gaul will not delay you, meeting in nearby war, nor bold
Spain in her broad lands, nor the wild land beset by a Theraean colonist, nor
where either Nile or the royal water Choaspes flows forth, or rushing Gyndes,
Cyrus's madness, or the Ocratian wave is dry on Araccaean plains, nor where
Tomyris bounded kingdoms with the wandering Araxes, nor the Padaean who,
celebrating impious banquets at savage tables, holds the last fields
neighboring to Phoebus, and where Hebrus and Tanais water the Getae and the
Magyni. Why do I delay? Where Ocean contains the world with the sea, no region
will offer itself to you with opposing arms. The Briton, unconquered by Roman
Mars, waits for you, and the other part of the world with the sun interposed.
For the earth stands in poured-around air and is arranged in five parts in the
whole orb. And two are always laid waste by icy cold: there the earth is also
hidden in dense shade, and no wave glides with liquor begun, but grown hard it
stiffens into dense ice and snow, inasmuch as Titan has never driven his
rising above. But the middle is always subjected to Phoebus's heat, whether he
is carried nearer the lands into the summer circuit or, swift, he hastens to
run down the winter lights; therefore the earth does not rise from a pressed
plough, nor do crops offer fruit nor the lands fodder; no god tills the fields
there, neither Bacchus nor Ceres, nor do any animals inhabit the burned-out
parts. Between this and between the stiffening ones a fertile part is placed,
ours, and another part opposite this to our soil, which a similar neighborhood
of sky holding on both sides tempers, and one air kills the strengths of the
other; hence the peaceful year is turned for us through the seasons, hence the
bull has learned to submit his neck to the yoke and the pliant vine to climb
high branches, and the yearly crop is shorn of its ripe births, and the earth
is split with iron, the sea with bronze, nay even towns rise with walls built.
Therefore when your deeds have gone through famous triumphs, you alone will be
called the same great in either world. I am not enough for the proclamations
of so great praise, not if Phoebus himself should prescribe songs for me. You
have one who can gird himself for great affairs, Valgius: no other is nearer
to eternal Homer. Our labor does not complete sluggish leisures, although
Fortune, as is her custom, wearies me with adverse things. For to me, when a
high house shone with great wealth, whose yellow furrows enriching in a row
had been granaries failing at fertile harvests, and for whom the hills
pastured a flock in a dense line, enough for the master and too much for thief
and wolf, now longing remains: for care is renewed, when mindful grief always
warns of the years driven before. But though harsher things fall and I be
spoiled of what is left, our Camenae will not fail to recount you. Nor will
Pierian honors be granted to you alone: for you I would dare even to go
through the rushing waves of the sea, though winter straits swell with
opposing winds, for you even to stand alone against dense squadrons or to
commit a small body to Aetnaean flame. I am, whatever I am, yours. If there
should be a little care of us for you, however much it please, if only there
be, neither Lydian kingdoms nor the fame of great Gylippus would be preferable
to me, nor would I rather be able to conquer Meletean sheets. But if our
verse, all or less, should be well known to you, or should wander on the top
of the mouth, no fates set me an end of singing you. Nay even then when the
mound has covered my bones, whether a ripe day hastens swift death for me, or
a long life remains, still, whether a changed figure will form me a horse
learned to run through the stiff plains, or I be a bull, the glory of the slow
flock, or I be carried a bird through the liquid air on wings, whenever a long
age has received me back as a man, I will weave songs about you onto the
sheets begun.

Sulpicia has been tended for you, great Mars, on your Kalends; if you are
wise, come yourself from heaven to look; Venus will forgive this; but you,
violent one, beware lest your arms fall shamefully as you marvel: from her
eyes, when he wants to burn up the gods, bitter Love lights twin lamps. Her,
whatever she does, wherever she has moved her footsteps, Grace secretly
arranges and follows after; whether she has loosed her locks, it befits her to
be with hair poured out: whether she has arranged them, she is to be venerated
with tended hair. She burns, if she has wished to come forth in a Tyrian
mantle: she burns, if she comes shining in a snowy garment. Such is lucky
Vertumnus on eternal Olympus: he has a thousand adornments, he has a thousand
decently. She alone of girls is worthy that Tyre give her soft fleeces twice
soaked with costly juices, and that she possess whatever the rich Arabian
tiller of a scented crop reaps from well-smelling fields, and whatever gems
the black Indian, nearest to Eastern waters, gathers from the red shore. You,
Pierides, sing her on the festive Kalends, and you, Phoebus, proud on the
tortoise lyre. She will take this solemn rite for many years: no girl is
worthier of your chorus.

Spare my young man, whether you, boar, inhabit the good pastures of the plain
or the out-of-the-way places of a shady mountain, and let it not be yours to
have sharpened hard teeth for battles; let Love as guardian keep this one safe
for me. But Diana's care of hunting leads him far away: o let the woods perish
and the dogs fail! What madness is it, what mind, to want to injure tender
hands while shutting the dense hills with a net? Or what does it delight to
enter the lairs of wild beasts secretly and to mark white legs with hooked
brambles? But still, that it may be allowed to wander with you, Cerinthus, I
myself will carry the twisted nets through the mountains, I myself will seek
the tracks of the swift stag and take off the iron bonds from the swift dog.
Then, then let the woods please me, if, my light, I should be charged with
having lain with you before the very toils: then though the boar come to the
nets, he will go away unharmed, lest he disturb the joys of eager love. Now
without me let there be no Venus, but by Diana's law, chaste boy, touch the
nets with a chaste hand: and whoever creeps secretly under my love, let her
fall to be torn by savage wild beasts. But you, yield the zeal of hunting to
your father, and yourself run swiftly back into our bosom.

Come here and drive out the tender girl's diseases, come here, Phoebus, proud
with unshorn hair; believe me, hurry, nor will it now irk you, Phoebus, to
have applied healing hands to the beautiful. Bring it about that leanness not
occupy the paling limbs, nor an ugly color mark the white members, and
whatever of ill there is and whatever sad we fear, let the river carry out
into the sea with rushing waters. Holy one, come, and bring with you whatever
flavors, and whatever songs, lighten weary bodies; and do not torture the
young man, who fears the girl's fates and makes vows for his mistress scarcely
to be numbered; sometimes he vows, sometimes, because she is faint, he speaks
harsh words against the eternal gods. Lay down fear, Cerinthus: the god does
not harm lovers; only love always: the girl is safe for you; there is no need
of weeping: it will be more fitting to use tears if ever she is sadder to you.
But now she is all yours, the shining one thinks of you alone with herself,
and the credulous crowd sits in vain. Phoebus, favor: great praise will be
granted you in one body saved to have restored two. Already you will be
celebrated, already glad, when each glad one will give back what is owed in
rivalry on the holy hearths; then the pious crowd of the gods will call you
lucky, and each will wish your arts for himself.

The day that gave you to me, Cerinthus, this will be holy to me and must
always be held among the feast days: at your birth the Fates sang a new
slavery for girls and gave proud kingdoms to you. I burn before others: it
delights, Cerinthus, that I burn, if a mutual fire from us is present for you;
let mutual love be present, I ask by you, sweetest thefts, and by your eyes
and by your Genius. Remain, Genius, take incense gladly and favor the vows, if
only, when he thinks of me, he is hot. But if by chance he already now sighs
for other loves, then I pray, holy one, leave the unfaithful hearths. And do
not be unjust, Venus: either let each, bound, serve you equally, or lighten my
bonds; but rather let us both be held by a strong chain, which no day
afterward could have loosed. The young man wishes the same that we do, but he
wishes more covertly: for he is ashamed to say these words openly. But you,
Birthday, since as a god you feel all things, nod: what does it matter whether
he asks secretly or openly?

Birthday Juno, take holy heaps of incense, which a tender girl, learned, gives
you with her hand; she is all yours today, she has arranged herself most
gladly for you, that she might stand to be gazed at before your hearths. She
indeed refers the causes of adorning to you, goddess; yet there is one whom
she secretly wishes to have pleased. But you, holy one, favor, and let no one
tear lovers apart, but, I ask, prepare mutual bonds for the young man. So you
will arrange well: he is not worthier to serve any girl, nor she any man. And
let not a waking guard be able to catch the eager, and let Love furnish a
thousand ways of deceiving. Nod and come shining through in a purple mantle:
three times a cake is made for you, three times, chaste goddess, unmixed wine;
and the eager mother instructs her daughter what she wishes: that one already
asks another thing in her silent mind; she burns, as the swift flames burn the
altars, nor, though it be allowed, would she wish to have been well. Be
pleasing to the young man and, when the next year comes, let this same love,
already old, stand out in the vows.

** Little Elegies of Sulpicia
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h3
:END:

At last love has come, such as the rumor that I covered it would be more for
shame to me than that I laid it bare to someone. Cytherea, prevailed on by my
Camenae, has brought him and set him down in our bosom. Venus has loosed her
promises: let my joys be told, if anyone will be said not to have had his own.
I would not wish to commit anything to sealed tablets, lest no one read it
before my own, but it delights to have sinned, it wearies to compose the face
for rumor: let me be reported, a worthy woman, to have been with a worthy man.

A hated birthday is here, which will have to be spent sad in the troublesome
country and without Cerinthus. What is sweeter than the city? Or would a villa
be fitting for a girl, and a cold river in the Arretine field? Now, Messalla,
too eager for me, be quiet; journeys, kinsman, are often not timely. Here,
carried off, I leave my mind and my senses, though you do not allow them to be
at my own judgment.

Do you know that the sad journey has been lifted from the girl's mind? Now it
is allowed to be at Rome for your birthday. Let that day be spent as a
birthday for us all, which now by chance has come to you not expecting it.

It is welcome that you, secure, already allow yourself much about me, lest I
suddenly fall, badly foolish. Let care of a toga be stronger for you, and a
whore pressed with a wool-basket, than Sulpicia, daughter of Servius: they are
anxious for us, to whom it is a grief that I, the greatest cause, may yield to
an unknown couch.

Have you, Cerinthus, a pious care of your girl, because a heat now vexes my
weary body? Ah, I would not wish to conquer sad diseases otherwise than if I
should think that you also wish it. But what would it profit me to conquer
diseases, if you can bear our ills with a slow breast?

Let me not be, my light, now an equally burning care to you as I seem to have
been a few days before, if I, foolish, have committed anything in my whole
youth of which I would confess I have repented more than that last night I
left you alone, wishing to hide my ardor.

** Of Uncertain Authorship
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h4
:END:

No woman will steal your bed from us: by this pact first our love was joined.
You alone please me, nor now besides you is any girl beautiful in the city to
my eyes. And would that you could seem beautiful to me alone! May you
displease others: so I will be safe. There is no need of envy, let the glory
of the crowd be far away: let him who is wise rejoice in a silent bosom. So I
can live well in secret woods, where no way is worn by a human foot. You are
rest from cares for me, you a light even in a black night, and in lonely
places you are a crowd for me. Now though a girlfriend be sent from heaven to
Tibullus, she will be sent in vain and Venus will fail; this I swear to you by
the holy powers of your Juno, who alone before the other gods is great to me.
What am I doing, madman? Alas! alas! I yield my pledges; I have sworn
foolishly: that fear was profitable. Now you will be strong, now you will burn
me more boldly: a talkative tongue has given birth to this ill for the
wretched man. Now I will do whatever you wish, I will remain yours throughout,
nor will I flee the slavery of a known mistress, but I will sit bound at the
altars of holy Venus: she marks the unjust and favors suppliants.

Rumor says that our girl sins often; now I would wish myself to be of deaf
ears. These charges have not been made without our grief: why do you torture
the wretched man, bitter rumor? Be silent.

You too, Tibullus, unjust Death sent as a young man, Virgil's companion, to
the Elysian fields, lest there be either one who would weep soft loves in
