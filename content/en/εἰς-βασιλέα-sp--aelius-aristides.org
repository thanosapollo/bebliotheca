But it seems to me to be among the fair things, at a festival and in a sacred
month, to mention something and to speak of the divine and humane emperor. For
there is no fear that, having chosen to praise him, we should then be at a
loss for words, but rather that the things belonging to the emperor should be
such that no one could speak

* 57
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

worthily about them. But I, lifted by his good and humane fortune in the other
things and by the speeches about him, have taken courage. Therefore, leaving
aside all the rest, which those who make preludes are accustomed to say and to
make excuses, some the greatness of the deeds, others the time of the speeches
being short, and not even needing the Muses, as some of the poets do, nor any
other help from outside, I shall turn to the praise of the emperor himself,
not from arrogance nor despising the matter, but seeing that those who at the
beginning soothe and excuse themselves are in a way despising the subjects
they make of their speeches. For in saying these things they seem to me to
show nothing else than this: some, speaking offhand, that if they had thought
and prepared in more time they would furnish speeches equal to the greatness
of the deeds; others, granting themselves the power to speak of the greatest
things, make this announcement, and give themselves this excess of praises.
But I see neither a sufficient abundance of time nor any speech worthy of the
emperor, nor anyone who will be able to praise him adequately; yet one must
not lose heart, but try to speak with as much power as one has. For not even
when we sacrifice to the gods do we do this looking to what is worthy, I
think, but we pay them this thanks as far as is possible for us; therefore
those who are about to praise the emperor must not hesitate either, but go
with courage to his praise. For to him perhaps what comes from us will be said
deficiently, but to you not deficiently. This then is one first and greatest
thing that belongs to be said about him, that even before he was established
in the rule he was worthy of the empire. For those who acquire the rule
through others or receive it from those before them, some putting force in
place of justice, others as of some lineage

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3

preserving succession and inheritance, thus acquired the empire; but whoever
himself neither canvassed nor asked, but when all asked him to become so, gave
himself to those who asked and called him, to this man this prize was long
owed even through virtue. And indeed many dispute with those others about the
empire, but with this man no one. And yet whether by fear toward him or by
reverence for the better or, as I said, by the best and most just judgment
they think they must yield, how is it not clear from all these things who it
was necessary should be emperor? And yet it is also possible to judge from
those who reigned before him about them, how much he differed from them at
once in these very things first. For those men entered upon affairs with wars
and many murders, destroying many of those in office, and becoming causes of
incurable disasters to many, so that many subject cities were made desolate,
much land was laid waste, and very many bodies were spent, so that not all
things happened piously for them, nor would they themselves be able to say
this about themselves; but he so piously and fairly took his stand over
affairs that neither while being established nor at the beginning of the
empire did he need any murder; nor, as in a change and settlement of affairs,
did anyone experience anything harsh, nor the many things that are accustomed
and even necessary to happen in such times, none of these was done; nor did
they suffer anything incurable or terrible, not men, not cities, not a nation.
But the gods so cared for him, that he should take his stand over affairs
piously and reverently, that the works of madness and desperation they
assigned to others, but those of justice and humanity and the rest of piety
they kept for this man. And moreover, even when he had the rule, he did
nothing grim, nor imitated, nor emulated any of these things, nor as other
emperors before him, fearing some of those in office as plotting against
themselves

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

having accused them, punished some with exile, others with death, he did none
of these things, nor did he change his character, but he is so far from all
such things and so far from wishing to make slaughters and deaths, that even
some of those who plot and of those clearly convicted, even these survive and
live by this man's humanity; and this is not only a display of gentleness, nor
of his being piously disposed toward all and not enviously toward some of the
honored and preeminent, but also of the rule not being struck down by fears,
nor being troubled toward what happens, nor being quick to anger and passion,
but using a firm and unmoved mind toward all; reasonably. For the man who has
learned what is needful and been educated and neglected none of the fair
things nor become ignorant, but has adorned his soul with all the parts of
virtue, it was likely he should be outside of all error.

This education then the emperor too having received, and having shared in all
the fairest things, some belonging to him even from nature, others coming also
from education, not moderately nor as with some others, but toward excess of
each, was a great benefit to the empire even before he was established in it.
For this too belonged to this man alone of emperors, or among few
outstandingly: what then is this? Those men for the rest of the time do their
own affairs, and when they enter the rule, then they begin to take care of it;
but he was so dedicated to the empire that, appointed at first however he
happened to be appointed, the future being unclear, seeing much of the empire
not well nor piously administered, but much arrogance and insolence and
license having arisen, he did not let it grow nor go further, but as healing
the festering and diseased parts of a great and unhealthy body, or as holding
down the wild and

disobedient part of a harsh and violent horse, being carried away by it, but
often holding and checking the continuous and irrational and violent impulses,
he acted for it and foresaw what was advantageous. Such then he was before he
became emperor. But when the providence that administers and orders all things
seated this man too upon the imperial throne, the most just and most pious of
emperors, what first and greatest thing could one say of those of which we
enjoy from his good fortune and providence? Who, when all things were stirred
and changing, so to speak, into another land, and the empire was tossing as in
a great storm or earthquake, and then as a ship about to sink was being
carried off toward the ends of the earth, where also before some of those who
had been in offices and empires had wandered, and then as in a labyrinth
meeting many hard perplexities, at last gave themselves up, shut off from the
way back, unable to return, seeing these things he did not, as an
inexperienced helmsman, let it be carried however it happened nor run risk,
but as the most experienced of emperors and excelling in judgment he first
checked and stopped the impulse thither, then settled it, bringing it to
harbor. And now it rides in the safest harbor, as a ship come to rest from a
long storm, and he so leads and manages the affair of the empire in the way
that is needful, piety and justice, and further temperance and self-control
and prudence and the rest * * of order at least, virtue. For he began, as is
fitting, from piety, and went through the other parts of virtue as well. For
who has brought forth so many and such proofs of justice, who of humanity, who
of the rest of virtue, as many and such as this emperor has brought forth?

And first let us look at his justice toward money. For when the assessment
assigned for the administration was excessive, and more taxes had been imposed

and not even these sufficing, but the treasuries everywhere emptied, and the
fear about the future always greater, he did not need the more nor seek it nor
become bad because of money, but he remitted and lightened, becoming not only
the most just but also the most humane of emperors in these things. And such
he is toward money. But his justice and humanity toward lawsuits, who could
praise worthily? For what Rhadamanthys or what Aeacus so kept himself a just
judge toward all things? Rather, the just is not always nor everywhere humane,
but he knows the just exactly, as himself lawgiver and discoverer of it, and
doing nothing outside the just, yet by this very thing he confirms humanity,
so that the most exact justice has the same force as the purest humanity. And
the cause of this is that he does not, looking to other interpreters, thus
take the just, but himself having from education knowledge of the truly fair
and of the good things, having added the experience from the laws, so that
nothing of what is written escapes him, thus he makes the judgments about all
things. At any rate there is no one who did not go away having obtained what
was just, nor blamed the things judged before him, neither a prosecutor
defeated nor a defendant convicted, but both go away content and doing homage,
and the defeated and the victor give the same vote about the things decided.
And if it happens that some undergo the punishment, they do not think it
terrible to suffer and pay the penalty, but they count it gain not to suffer
more besides; for the punishments do not mean hatred of the doers for him, but
education, most of all, if somehow possible, of them themselves, and if not,
of those who will hear. And if again to be a friend of the Greeks is fair and
fitting for an emperor, to whom does the praise so belong? For so strongly is

the emperor a friend of the Greeks and so much does he have of this fair thing
to spare, that when the education
of the Greeks had been neglected and despised, and the honors upon it taken
away, and the whole Greek race pushed aside and of no account, the emperor did
not neglect it, but besides the honors already there he added others as well.

And about justice so much. But what humanity is greater than this and more
manifest? Whereby the whole subject people, cowering and enslaved by fear,
many informers going about and eavesdropping through all the cities if anyone
should utter anything, and it being possible neither to think nor to speak
anything free, the temperate and just frankness having been taken away, and
each trembling at a shadow, he released from this fear and freed the souls of
all, giving back to them complete and whole freedom. What then is greatest of
the emperor's humanity and better than every example: for being so great the
emperor has differed from all emperors in gentleness and fairness, so that it
is a small thing to say he is as a father, a small thing to say he is as a
shepherd of the rest, and all that poets have uttered when they make rulers
gentle. For what manner of fairness has he left out? Is not the manner of his
humanity most common toward the whole subject people and those who meet him on
every occasion, and the gentleness of his bearing how humane, and the
temperate quality of his speech, and that one need do nothing for the sake of
access, but it is possible, as if going within the lustral basins, for those
who are conscious of even some small good in themselves, how is this not
beyond all gentleness and humanity? For he does not, by making himself rare
and hard of access, think he will carry off as emperor the reputation of being
great and wonderful and such as no other ever was, but that he would most of
all confirm the title of being such, if he should make the goodwill and
humanity from himself common to all

who need it, thinking that the true emperor must be likened to the emperor of
the universe both in humanity and in providence for all the ruled, and not
before the empire show some good and moderate character of himself, but when
appointed emperor be harsh and violent toward the ruled; for such a thing is
not the form of a fair empire, but the most manifest proof of clumsiness and
lack of education; such also was the general of the Lacedaemonians who led the
Greeks against the barbarians, I mean Pausanias the son of Cleombrotus, who
while remaining at home was able to be temperate, but having sailed to the
Hellespont had forgotten the things at home, and had forgotten himself, and
seemed like anything rather than a Spartiate man, being harsh and violent
toward the allies, unrestrained and insolent in his way of life, and
tyrannical in his character. But this man showed that it is possible for the
same man to be both a private person and an emperor throughout, presenting
himself such before the empire as to be justly thought worthy of it, and in
the empire using the same mind and character, not standing outside himself nor
emulating license, but choosing the temperate life, hating insolence and
lawlessness, and presenting himself as a pattern of temperance, so that those
who till then were wanton and insolent changed their character seeing his
temperance, and both ceased from the desire for money and were disposed toward
the more temperate. And moreover of all the pleasures that rule men we know
the emperor defeated by none. For he so held off from them at once from the
beginning that if there is any self-control hymned among men, this seems to be
trustworthy when said about this man alone. For who is so self-controlled in
the things of the belly, who in those of Aphrodite, who in the other
pleasures? For he did not, as I said just now, have virtue by half, nor as
some other

emperors who seemed to be fairly disposed toward courage and humanity appeared
most unrestrained about pleasures and desires, as Homer, praising the general
of the Achaeans, Agamemnon, says, both a good king and a mighty spearman. But
this man appears in him defeated by Chryseis, and in love with Achilles'
Briseis, and through this desire becoming cause of many and great evils to the
Achaeans. Then Achilles, the son of Thetis and Peleus, was so unrestrained
about pleasures and so petty that when Briseis had gone from him and spent
some time with Agamemnon, at once he was so disposed that he wept looking upon
the wine-dark sea, calling his mother like a little child who has suffered
this from another like himself; and when she came, relating the cause of the
tears and bidding her ask Zeus that becoming an ally to the Trojans he might
work the greatest evils upon the Greeks, and after this seeing many of the
Achaeans falling each time under the Trojans and under Hector, and some of his
own companions and friends dying, not turning nor pitying.

Those men then were deficient in many things toward virtue, but this man is
both a good king and a mighty spearman. For he who so despised pleasures as to
be defeated by none of them, how is this man not the one who is in truth
courageous and temperate and strong? For walls often seeming impregnable even
poor men have already taken, but I see many even of the very good men mastered
by pleasures. And easiness of life and cheapness of diet, who is so courageous
as to be able to bear so easily? And moreover, as to things toward enemies, it
has belonged to many to become courageous, but as to being ruled by their own
soldiers or mastering them: he so easily mastered and settled them that though
many and unlimited

were the things given them, and terrible and fearful if they should not
receive so much, and still more than what was given them, he not only did not
increase their desires, but
having defined what was needful he made the soldiers better toward toils and
the exercise of their bodies, no longer letting them attend to receiving, but
making the practice of warlike things customary for them, nor to spend their
time in luxury and softness, but so that they should have no occasion for such
desire. Having done this he helped the wants of the ruled, and took care of
the discipline of the soldiers, and made the revenue of money more secure. Of
these things there could not be greater proofs of courage together with good
counsel. And moreover one might reasonably make this itself a proof of skill
and wisdom. For to have forced and compelled them and made them content by
fear and not by surplus of judgment, it is possible to have made them so; but
to persuade those insatiably disposed toward money and receiving to be
moderate, he would not, I think, easily have been able, if he had not been
beyond all in thinking and speaking. And of prudence he has furnished many
other works in the empire, but the fairest and most worthy of praise is the
emperor's good counsel and understanding toward the wars, that seeing in the
wars those who seem to be clever and warlike thinking they must win by
fighting, but not by counseling well, he did not imitate those men nor emulate
them, thinking that toward equals one must use arms; for it is fair to defeat
such men by courage; but toward the barbarians by counseling well. For he
knew, I think, also him who led the many myriads against the Greeks, and whom
neither land nor sea contained sufficiently, defeated by one man's judgment.
For where it is possible by counseling to prevail, what need of dangers there?

Further then, to win in battles often even belongs to the poor, but to be able
to master by prudence and wisdom belongs only to those who know how to
counsel. And moreover those who wish to display courage on every occasion and
every pretext, if they succeed they have succeeded, but if defeated they throw
themselves and their friends into the greatest disasters; but those who know
what is needful and calculate, if they succeed they obtain no less of what
they wished, and if they miss they are harmed in nothing. Which things the
emperor too knowing, he does not think he must imitate the foolish and
reckless of men, but by counseling well to be safe toward the barbarians. And
moreover not even in battles and arms did the enemies blame him, but he showed
them that he was able to master them not only by understanding and the rest of
education, but also by courage. For where the Celts, the greatest and most
murderous of those under the sun, having dared many things of every kind, now
do homage to the master, having learned that to keep quiet was better and to
do what is commanded than to make war, and this name alone is left of the
race; and all that dwells beyond Euphrates and Tigris toward the east, having
been stirred, has now been set in order and taught to know their betters; and
the whole continent is at rest, and land and sea crown the protector, and
Greeks and barbarians already utter the same, and the empire like some ship or
wall has been repaired and fortified and has firmly got its own good, what
courage have these things not surpassed, or what settlement could become
better and more profitable than this? Is there not all freedom from fear for
all wherever one wishes,

and all the harbors everywhere in use, do not the mountains have the same
safety for those traveling that the cities have for those who dwell in them,
and grace covers all plains, and every fear through all things has been
loosed? For what passages of rivers have been forbidden to cross? What straits
of the sea have been shut? Now also the assemblies are brighter and the
festivals more dear to the gods. Now also the fire of Demeter is brighter and
more sacred. O light of human happiness. Now all men seem to have found the
true happiness. O you who have surpassed all emperors, in wisdom the wise, in
courage the courageous, in piety those who excel in this, in fortune the most
fortunate; in return for which there could not be worthy thanks to you from
us, neither in praises nor in any other honor. And you, O noble child of
nobles, may it be yours to walk in the track of your father. For this, at
least as in brief, let be said as most.
