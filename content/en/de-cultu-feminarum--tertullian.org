** Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2
:END:

If as much faith lingered on earth as the reward of it is looked for in
heaven, none of you at all, dearest sisters, from the time she had come to
know the living God, and had learned her own condition, that is a woman's,
would have sought a merrier dress, to say nothing of a more splendid one, so
as not rather to live in rags and choose squalor instead, carrying herself
about as Eve in mourning and repentance, that she might the more fully atone,
by every habit of satisfaction, for what she draws from Eve: I mean the
disgrace of the first offense and the odium of human ruin.

In pains and anxieties you bear children, woman, and your turning is toward
your husband, and he rules over you. And do you not know that you are Eve?
God's sentence upon this sex lives on in this world; the guilt must live on
too. You are the devil's gate. You are the one who unsealed that tree. You are
the first deserter of the divine law. You are the one who persuaded him whom
the devil was not strong enough to attack. You brought down God's image, man,
so easily. For your desert, that is death, even the Son of God had to die. And
you have it in mind to be adorned over and above your tunics of skins?

Come now: if from the beginning of things the Milesians had already been
shearing sheep, and the Seres spinning from trees, and the Tyrians dyeing, and
the Phrygians stitching in, and the Babylonians weaving in, and pearls were
gleaming, and thunderstones flashing; if gold itself had already come out of
the earth with greed; if the mirror too already had so much leave to lie: did
Eve, I suppose, already expelled from paradise, already dead, still covet
these things? Then she ought not now to seek or even know them, if she wants
to live again, things she neither had nor knew when she was alive. So all
these are the trappings of a condemned and dead woman, set out as if for the
pomp of a funeral.

** Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3
:END:

For those who instituted them too are assigned, under condemnation, to the
penalty of death: those angels, that is, who rushed from heaven upon the
daughters of men, so that this disgrace also falls to woman. For when they had
betrayed to a world still far more ignorant certain materials well hidden, and
a good many arts not well revealed, if it is true they had uncovered the
working of metals, passed on the powers of herbs, published the force of
spells, and marked out every curious inquiry even to the reading of the stars,
they conferred, as their own special gift to women, that apparatus of female
glory: the lights of little stones with which necklaces are varied, and the
rings of gold with which arms are squeezed, and the dyes from paint with which
wools are colored, and that black powder itself with which the edges of the
eyes are drawn out.

What sort of things these are can already be judged from the quality and
condition of their teachers: that sinners could show or supply nothing toward
integrity, lovers nothing toward chastity, deserter spirits nothing toward the
fear of God. If they are to be called teachings, bad masters must have taught
badly. If they are wages for lust, the wage of nothing shameful is honorable.
But why was it so important to show these things, as well as to confer them?
Was it that women could not please men without materials of splendor and
devices of beauty, women who, still unadorned and unkempt and, so to speak,
raw and rude, had already moved angels? Or would the lovers have looked sordid
and, by a gift given for nothing, insulting, if they had conferred nothing on
the women they had drawn into marriage? But this is not worth weighing. They
who possessed angels could have desired nothing more: they had, to be sure,
made a great match. But those who at times remembered from where they had
fallen, and after the steaming moments of lust sighed for heaven, repaid that
very good of women, their natural beauty, as the cause of the evil, so that
their good fortune would not profit them, but that, drawn down from simplicity
and sincerity, they might come, together with the angels themselves, into
offense against God. They were sure that all glory and ambition and the desire
of pleasing through the flesh is displeasing to God.

These are the angels we are to judge. These are the angels we renounce in
baptism. These, of course, are the things for which they earned being judged
by man. What then are their goods doing among their judges? What commerce is
there between those who will condemn and those who will be condemned? The
same, I suppose, as between Christ and Belial. With what face do we mount that
tribunal to pass sentence against those whose gifts we chase? For you too have
the same angelic substance promised then, the same sex as men, and the same
dignity of judging is pledged to you. Unless therefore we already here
prejudge their goods by condemning in advance what we are going to condemn in
them, they will rather judge and condemn us.

** Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4
:END:

I know that the scripture of Enoch, which assigned this order to the angels,
is not received by some, because it is not admitted into the Jewish chest
either. I suppose they did not think that a work published before the flood
could have survived that wreck of the world, the abolisher of all things. If
that is the reason, let them remember that Noah, Enoch's own great-grandson,
was a survivor of the flood, and that he had of course heard and remembered,
by household name and hereditary tradition, of his great-grandfather's favor
with God and of all his preachings, since Enoch had charged his son Methuselah
with nothing else than to hand the knowledge of them on to his posterity. So
Noah could without doubt have succeeded to the trust of that preaching, if
only because he would not otherwise have been silent both about the ordering
of God his preserver and about the glory of his own house.

If he had not had it so readily, this too would still defend the claim of that
scripture: it could have been remade in the Spirit after being wiped out by
the violence of the flood, just as, after Jerusalem was destroyed in the
Babylonian storming, the whole body of Jewish literature is agreed to have
been restored through Ezra. But since Enoch in the same scripture preached
also of the Lord, nothing at all that belongs to us must be rejected by us.
And we read that every scripture fit for edification is inspired by God. It
may already seem to have been rejected by the Jews for that very reason, as
nearly everything else that sounds of Christ. Nor is it any wonder if they did
not receive some scriptures that spoke of him whom they were not going to
receive even when he spoke in person before them. Add to this that Enoch has a
testimony in the apostle Jude.

** Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5
:END:

Let no brand of prior condemnation now be burned onto women's pomp from the
end of its authors. Let nothing be charged to those angels besides the
repudiation of heaven and marriage of the flesh. Let us examine the qualities
of the things themselves, so that we may also catch the purposes of the desire
for them.

A woman's bearing carries a double aspect, dress and ornament. By dress we
mean what they call a woman's cleanliness; by ornament, what it is fit to call
a woman's uncleanness. The one is counted in gold and silver and gems and
clothes; the other in the care of hair and skin and those parts of the body
that draw the eyes. Against the one we bring the charge of ambition, against
the other of prostitution. So already from here you may look ahead, handmaid
of God, to what of these things suits your discipline, you who are rated by
different rules, namely humility and chastity.

** Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6
:END:

Gold and silver, the chief materials of worldly dress, must be what they come
from, that is earth, earth plainly more glorious, since only after it has been
worn down by penal labor in the deadly workshops of accursed mines, and has
left the name of earth behind in the fire, does it change, a runaway from the
metal, from torments into ornaments, from punishments into pleasures, from
disgraces into honors. But iron and bronze and the other cheapest materials
have the same condition, both of earthly kind and of metal work, so that the
substance of gold and silver cannot be judged a whit nobler by nature. But if
glory comes to gold and silver from the quality of use, then all the more to
iron and bronze, whose usefulness is so ordered that they both perform more
and more necessary services of their own for human affairs and still, from
their own strength, take the place of gold and silver for juster purposes. For
rings too are made of iron, and memory of antiquity still keeps certain
vessels for eating and drinking of bronze. Let it look to itself if a mad
plenty of gold and silver instruments even serves filthy uses. At least the
field is not made ready by work in gold, nor is a ship fastened together by
the strength of silver. No mattock sinks gold into the earth; no nail drives
silver into planks. I say nothing of the needs of all life resting on iron and
bronze, when those rich materials themselves, both to be dug again from mines
and to be forged for any use, cannot do without the working strength of iron
and bronze. So it is already to be judged whence such dignity comes to gold
and silver, when they are preferred to materials that are kin by kind and
stronger by usefulness.

** Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7
:END:

And these little stones too, which join pride to gold, what else am I to take
them for but pebbles and grit and crumbs of the same earth, and yet not needed
for laying foundations, or raising walls, or holding up roofs, or packing a
covering? This is the only building they know how to raise: women's stupor,
because they are slowly worn so that they shine, and cunningly set so that
they bloom, and anxiously pierced so that they hang, and they lend gold a
mutual pandering. But whatever ambition fishes from the British or the Indian
sea is a kind of shellfish, I do not say more pleasant in taste than the
purple-shell or the oyster, but not even than the giant mussel. For I know
shells as the fruit of the sea. But if that shell has some pustule inside,
that ought to be its fault rather than its glory. And though it is called a
pearl, still something else must be understood than a hard, round wart of that
shell. They say gems are also dug from the brows of dragons, as there is a
kind of stoniness in the brains of fishes. This too was wanting to the
Christian woman, that she might grow more adorned from the serpent. Is it thus
she will bruise the devil's head, while she builds ornaments from his head for
her neck, or for her own head?

** Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8
:END:

All these things hold their charm from rarity and foreignness alone. Inside
their own native bounds they are not held so dear. Abundance is always
insulting to itself. Among certain barbarians, because gold is native and
plentiful, they keep men bound with gold in the workhouses, and load the
wicked with riches, the richer as they are the more harmful. At last a way has
truly been found how even gold may not be loved. We have seen at Rome the
nobility of gems blushing, in the presence of our matrons, at the contempt of
the Parthians and Medes and the rest of their own countrymen, except that they
are scarcely worn for show. Emeralds hide in their belts, and only the sword
under the breast knows the cylinders of its sheath, and on their boots pearls
want to rise out of the mud. In short they have nothing so gemmed as what
ought not to be gemmed, if it does not show, or shows only so that its neglect
may be shown too.

** Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9
:END:

In the same way even their slaves wear out the honor of colored clothes. They
even put Tyrian and hyacinth and those royal hangings, which you laboriously
unravel and recast, to use on walls in place of painting. Purple is cheaper
among them than red ochre. For what just honor do clothes have from the
adultery of unjust colors? What God himself did not produce does not please
God. Unless perhaps he could not command purple and sky-blue sheep to be born.
If he could, then he already did not will it. What God did not will is
certainly not lawful to invent. So those things are not best by nature which
are not from God, the author of nature. Thus they are understood to be from
the devil, the tamperer of nature. They cannot belong to another if they are
not God's, because what is not God's must be a rival's. And there is no rival
of God besides the devil and his angels.

For the rest, if the materials are from God, it does not at once follow that
this kind of fruit of them in men is too. It is asked also where shells come
from, and by what arrangement they adorn, and where they place their beauty.
For even all those profane pleasures of worldly shows, as we have published a
volume of their own about them, and idolatry itself, consist of God's things.
Yet a Christian ought not for that reason to be taken with the frenzies of the
circus, or the atrocities of the arena, or the shames of the stage, because
God gave man the horse and the panther and the voice; just as a Christian may
not commit idolatry with impunity because incense and wine and the fire that
feeds on them and the animals that become victims are God's making, since even
the material that is adored is God's. Does the descent of materials from God
therefore excuse their use as well, as something foreign to God, as guilty,
that is, of worldly glory?

** Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h10
:END:

For as certain things have been distributed by God through each several land
and each region of the sea, foreign to one another, they are, among outsiders,
rare by exchange, and among their own people, if anywhere, either neglected or
sought, and rightly, because there is not so great a heat of glory in them
when they are cold among the household. But from the distribution of
possessions, which God ordered as he willed, rarity and foreignness, always
finding favor among strangers, stir, from the simple cause of not having what
God placed elsewhere, a craving to have it. From this another vice stretches
out: immoderate having. Even if perhaps a thing is to be had, still a limit is
owed. This will be ambition, and from this its name is to be read, that
craving is born in a mind going about after a vow of glory: a grand vow, to be
sure, which, as we have said, not nature, not truth, but a diseased passion of
the mind, craving, has commended. And there are other vices of ambition and
glory. So it has also inflated the prices of things, that it might kindle
itself as well. For craving grows as great as the greatness it has made of
what it craved. From the smallest caskets a great patrimony is brought out. On
one thread a million sesterces are set. A tender neck carries about estates
and islands. The thin skins of the ears spend a ledger of interest, and the
left hand plays through each finger with a sack apiece. These are the powers
of ambition: to bear the yield of such great substance on one small body, and
a woman's.

** Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h2
:END:

Handmaids of the living God, my fellow-servants and sisters, by the right in
which I am counted with you, last though I am in that right of fellow-service
and brotherhood, I dare to address you, not as one of affection, but serving
affection in the cause of your salvation. That salvation, not of women only
but of men as well, lies especially in the showing of modesty. For since we
are all the temple of God, once his Holy Spirit has been brought in and
consecrated in us, modesty is the keeper and priestess of that temple, who
lets nothing unclean or profane be brought in, lest the God who dwells there
be offended and leave a polluted seat. But just now we are not speaking of
modesty, for the enjoining and exacting of which the divine precepts pressing
everywhere are enough, but of what belongs to it, that is, how you ought to
walk.

For most women, and may God allow me, in rebuking this in all, to rebuke it in
myself, either in simple ignorance or in bold dissembling walk as if modesty
consisted only in the integrity of the flesh and the shunning of fornication,
and as if nothing outward were needed as well, I mean in the arrangement of
dress and ornament, the pursuit of form and sheen, carrying about in their
gait the same surface as the women of the nations, who have no conscience of
true modesty, because there is nothing true in those who do not know God, the
guardian and teacher of truth. For if any modesty can be believed among the
Gentiles, it is agreed to be so imperfect and unformed that, though it hold to
itself to some degree in the mind, it still dissolves into licenses of dress,
seeking, by Gentile perversity, the thing whose effect it avoids. How few, in
the end, do not even desire to please strangers? Who does not take care to
paint herself, and then deny that she has been sought? And yet this too is
familiar to Gentile modesty: not to sin, yet to be willing; or even to be
unwilling, yet not to refuse. What wonder? All things that are not God's are
perverse. Let those women look to it, then, who by not holding the whole good
easily mix even what they do hold with evil. You must turn from them, as in
the rest, so also in your walking, since you ought to be perfect, as your
Father who is in the heavens.

** Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h3
:END:

You must know that for perfect, that is Christian, modesty, the desire of
oneself is not only not to be sought, but even to be cursed. First, because
the zeal to please by beauty, which we know is by nature an inviter of lust,
does not come from a sound conscience. Why then do you stir that evil in
yourself? Why invite what you profess yourself a stranger to? Next, because we
ought not to open a way to temptations, which sometimes, by pressing,
accomplish what God drives from his own, or at least shake the spirit with a
stumbling-block. We ought indeed to walk so holily and with the whole
substance of faith that we are confident and secure of our conscience, wishing
that this remain in us, yet not presuming. For he who presumes fears less; he
who fears less takes less care; he who takes less care is more in danger. Fear
is the foundation of salvation; presumption is a hindrance to fear. It is more
useful, then, if we hope we can fail than if we presume we cannot. By hoping
we shall fear, by fearing we shall take care, by taking care we shall be
saved. Conversely, if we presume, we shall be saved neither by fearing nor by
taking care. He who acts secure and is not also anxious does not possess a
safe and firm security. But he who is anxious will be able to be truly secure.

And for his own servants may the Lord by his mercy take care that they may
even presume on their good with a happy issue. But why are we a danger to
another? Why do we import craving into another? If God, in enlarging the law,
does not distinguish craving from the act of fornication in the penalty, I do
not know whether the one who has been the cause of another's ruin goes
unpunished. For that other perishes as soon as he has craved your form, and he
has already admitted in his mind what he craved, and you have become a sword
to him, so that even if you are free from the fault, you are not freed from
the odium. As when a robbery has been done in someone's field, the crime does
not touch the owner, yet while the land is branded with disgrace, he himself
too is sprinkled with infamy. Shall we paint ourselves so that others may
perish? Where then is, You shall love your neighbor as yourself? Do not care
for your own only, but for another's? No utterance of the Holy Spirit ought to
be directed and received for the present matter only, and not for every
occasion of usefulness.

Since therefore both our cause and others' is at stake in the pursuit of most
dangerous beauty, you must know that not only the display of feigned and
labored loveliness is to be refused by you, but even natural comeliness is to
be blotted out by concealment and neglect, as equally troublesome to the
inroads of the eyes. For though beauty is not to be accused, as a happiness of
the body, as an addition of the divine modeling, as a kind of good garment of
the soul, still it is to be feared, even for the injury and violence of
pursuers, which even Abraham, the father of the faith, feared in his wife's
appearance, and Isaac, by pretending Rebecca was his sister, bought safety
with insult.

** Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h4
:END:

Now grant that the dignity of form is not to be feared, that it is not heavy
to those who have it, not fatal to those who seek it, not dangerous to those
joined to it, not exposed to temptations, not ringed with stumbling-blocks: it
is enough that it is not necessary to the angels of God. For where modesty is,
there beauty is idle, because the proper use and fruit of beauty is luxury,
unless someone thinks there is another harvest for bodily grace. Are those
women to stretch the beauty given them and hunt for what was not given, who
think that in furnishing to another what is demanded of beauty they are
furnishing it to themselves as well? Someone will say, What then? Once luxury
is shut out and chastity let in, may we not enjoy the praise of form alone and
glory in a good of the body? Let him see to it who likes to glory in the
flesh. For us, first, there is no zeal for glory, because glory is the temper
of exaltation. And exaltation does not suit professors of humility by God's
precepts. Next, if all glory is vain and stupefied, how much more that which
is in the flesh, at least for us? For even if we are to glory, we ought to
wish to please in the goods of the spirit, not in the flesh, because we are
followers of spiritual things. In what we work, in that let us rejoice. From
those things let us pluck glory from which we hope for salvation. Plainly a
Christian will glory even in the flesh, but when it has grown hard, torn for
Christ, so that the spirit may be crowned in it, not so that it may draw the
eyes and sighs of young men after it. So what is idle in you from every side
you may justly disdain if you do not have it, and neglect if you do. Holy
women, if they are naturally fair, should not have so much occasion. Certainly
if there is occasion, it ought not to help, but even to hinder.

** Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h5
:END:

As if I were speaking to Gentiles, addressing you with a Gentile precept
common to all: you ought to please your husbands only. And you will please
them so far as you have not taken care to please others. Be without care,
blessed ones: no wife is ugly to her husband. She pleased enough when she was
chosen, commended either by form or by character. Let none of you think that
if she holds back from composing herself, hatred and aversion from her husband
will follow. Every husband is an exactor of chastity; but beauty a believing
husband does not demand, because we are not taken by the same goods the
Gentiles think goods. An unbelieving one, on the other hand, even holds it
suspect, from that wicked opinion the Gentiles have of us. For whom then do
you nurse your beauty? If for a believer, he does not require it; if for an
unbeliever, he does not trust it unless it is simple. Why are you eager to
please either one who suspects or one who does not desire it?

** Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h6
:END:

These things are not suggested to you to be worked into a wholly crude and
wild habit, nor are we urging the good of squalor and filth, but the measure
and hinge and justice of cultivating the body. One must not go beyond what
simple and sufficient cleanliness desires, beyond what pleases God. For they
sin against him who press the skin with medicaments, stain the cheeks with
red, draw out the eyes with soot. The modeling of God displeases them, I
suppose. In themselves, I suppose, they accuse and blame the craftsman of all
things. For they blame when they correct, when they add, taking those
additions from the rival craftsman. That is the devil. For who would show how
to change the body, if not he who also transfigured man's spirit with malice?
He beyond doubt put together devices of this kind, that in you it might appear
you are in a way laying hands on God.

What is born is God's work. What is plastered on, then, is the devil's
business. How wicked it is to overlay Satan's devices on a divine work! Our
slaves borrow nothing from our enemies; soldiers covet nothing from the foes
of their commander. To demand something for use from the adversary of him in
whose hand you are is a transgression. Shall a Christian be helped in anything
by that evil one? I do not know whether this name will still belong to him.
For he will belong to him whose teachings he is eager to be schooled in. How
alien they are from your disciplines and professions, how unworthy of the
Christian name, to wear a made-up face, you to whom all simplicity is
enjoined; to lie in the countenance, you who may not lie with the tongue; to
seek what is not yours, you to whom abstinence from another's is handed down;
to practice adultery in appearance, you whose study is modesty! Believe me,
blessed ones: how will you keep God's precepts, if you do not keep his
lineaments in yourselves?

** Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h7
:END:

I see some turning their hair with saffron. They are ashamed even of their own
nation, that they were not born to Germany and Gaul, so they already transfer
their hair. Ill, and most ill, they augur for themselves with a flame-colored
head, and think graceful what they stain. And yet the force of the drugs burns
harm into the hair, and the constant use even of any clean moisture stores
ruin for the brain; then the sun's heat, so desirable for giving life to the
hair and drying it, does harm. What grace is there with injury? What beauty
with uncleanness? Shall a Christian woman heap saffron on her head as on an
altar? For whatever is commonly burned to an unclean spirit, unless it is put
to honest and necessary and healthful uses, the uses for which God's creature
was provided, can seem a sacrifice. But God says, Which of you can make a
white hair black, or white out of black? And so they refute the Lord. See,
they say, instead of white or black we make it yellow, easier in charm. And
yet they even try black from white, those who repent of having lived on to old
age. What rashness! The time of life prayed for with vows blushes; a theft is
done; youth, in which we sinned, is sighed for; the occasion of gravity is
falsified. Far be such folly from the daughters of wisdom. The more old age
tries to hide itself, the more it will be uncovered. This is the true
eternity, from the youth of the hair. This is the incorruption we have to put
on for the new house of the Lord, which his sole rule promises. Well do you
hurry to the Lord, well do you hasten to leave this most wicked world, you to
whom it is unseemly to draw near the end.

** Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h8
:END:

What again does so much labor in arranging the hair supply toward salvation?
Why may your hair not rest, now bound, now loosed, now raised, now pressed
down? Some are eager to force it into curls, others that it may slip out
wandering and flying, not with a good simplicity. Besides you fasten on I know
not what enormities of fine and woven hairpieces, now in the manner of a cap,
as a sheath of the head and a cover of the crown, now a pile built back on the
neck. It is a wonder there is no striving against the Lord's precepts. It has
been pronounced that no one can add to his measure. You, however, add to the
weight certain rolls, or shield-bosses to be built onto the neck. If you are
not ashamed of the enormity, be ashamed of the pollution, lest you fit onto a
holy and Christian head the spoils of someone else's head, perhaps unclean,
perhaps guilty and destined for hell. No: drive all this slavery of ornament
from a free head. In vain you labor to seem adorned; in vain you call in the
most skilled builders of hair. God bids you be veiled. I believe, so that the
heads of some may not be seen. And would that I, most miserable, on that day
of Christian exultation, may lift my head even below your heels! I shall see
whether you rise with white lead and rouge and saffron and in that circuit of
the head; whether angels lift women so painted into the clouds to meet Christ
in the air. If these things are now good and of God, they will then also meet
the rising bodies and recognize their places. But nothing can rise except
flesh and spirit, sole and pure. What does not rise in spirit and flesh is
therefore condemned, because it is not of God. Abstain today from things
condemned. Let God see you today such as he will see you then.

** Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h9
:END:

Of course now I, a man, as a rival of the sex, am driving women from their
own. Are there not also things taken from us with a view to the gravity we
must keep for the fear due to God? If indeed in men for the sake of women, as
in women for the sake of men, the will to please is inborn by a fault of
nature, and this sex too knows its own tricks of form: to cut the beard too
sharply, to pluck it here and there, to shave around, to arrange the hair and
discolor gray, to strip the first down of the whole body, to streak it with
womanly pigment, to smooth the rest with the roughness of some powder, then to
consult the mirror on every occasion, to inspect it anxiously: yet once God is
known, the will to please being taken away through the occasion of luxury, all
those things are refused as idle, as hostile to modesty. For where God is,
there is modesty, there is gravity her helper and ally. How then shall we
handle modesty without her instrument, that is without gravity? And how shall
we apply gravity for the administering of modesty, unless severity is carried
about in the face and in the dress and in the whole look of the man?

** Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h10
:END:

For this reason, with clothing too and the rest of the baggage of your
composing, you must likewise care for the cutting off and striking away of too
redundant sheen. For what good is it to show a face sober and unencumbered and
a simplicity worthy of the divine discipline, but to occupy the rest of the
body with the fringed follies of pomps and delicacies? How close these pomps
are to the business of luxury, and how they shout down the disciplines of
modesty, is easy to tell, because they prostitute the grace of beauty by the
partnership of dress: so much so that if they cease, they render it vain and
thankless, as if disarmed and wrecked. On the other hand, if form fails, the
support of sheen supplies a grace as if from its own. Times of life already at
rest and drawn into the harbor of modesty the splendor and dignity of dress
call away, and they unsettle severity with cravings, the provocations of dress
making up, of course, for the chill of the years. First then, blessed ones, do
not admit into yourselves clothes and dress that are pimps and prostitutors;
then, if the account of riches or birth or former dignities compels some to go
out so pompous that they seem not to have attained wisdom, take care to temper
an evil of this kind, lest under the pretext of necessity you seize the
license with full reins.

For how will you be able to fulfill the humility our people profess, if you do
not recut the use of your riches or elegances, which make so much for glory?
And glory is used to exalt, not to humble. Shall we not use what is ours, that
we have acquired? Who forbids using it? Yet according to the apostle, who
warns us to use this world as if we did not abuse it. For the fashion of this
world is passing. And those who buy, let them act as if they did not possess.
Why so? Because he had said before, The time is shortened. If then he shows
that even wives themselves are to be had as if they were not had, because of
the straits of the times, what would he think of these vain instruments of
theirs? Do not many also so act, and seal themselves to eunuchhood for the
kingdom of God, laying down of their own will a pleasure so strong and
certainly permitted? Do not some forbid themselves even God's creature,
abstaining from wine and from animals for food, whose enjoyment lies next to
no danger or anxiety, but they sacrifice the humility of their soul to God in
the chastening of diet as well? You too, then, have used riches and delicacies
enough; you have cut the fruit of your dowries enough before the knowledge of
saving disciplines. We are those upon whom the ends of the world have come
down. We were destined by God before the world in the reckoning of time. So by
chastening and, so to speak, castrating the world, we are trained by God. We
are the circumcision of all things, spiritual and fleshly. For both in spirit
and in flesh we circumcise worldly things.

** Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h11
:END:

No doubt God showed how to boil wools with the juices of herbs and the spit of
shells. It had escaped him, when he was bidding the universe to be born, to
command purple and scarlet sheep. God also invented the workshops of those
very clothes which, light and thin, are heavy in price alone. God also
produced such great works of gold for clasping and parting little stones. God
also dealt the ears those painstaking wounds, and thought so much of the
vexation of his own work and the torture of infancy, then first in pain and
innocent, that from those scars of a body born to the steel some grains I know
not of should hang, which the Parthians plainly set into their boots as well
in place of studs. And yet gold itself, whose glory occupies you, serves a
certain people for chains, so Gentile literature reports. So true is it that
they are not good by truth but by rarity. And the labor, brought in by arts
through the sinner angels who also betrayed the materials themselves, joined
with rarity, stirred costliness, and from that a lust in women of possessing
costliness.

But if the same angels who uncovered both materials of this kind and their
lures, gold I mean and bright stones, and handed over the working of them, and
taught among the rest even eyelid-paint itself and the dyeing of fleeces, have
been condemned by God, as Enoch reports, how shall we please God while we
rejoice in the things of those who for that very reason provoked God's anger
and punishment?

Now grant that God foresaw these things, that God permitted them, that Isaiah
rebukes no garment of purple-shell, strikes down no clustered ornament,
rejects no little moons: still let us not, as the Gentiles do, flatter
ourselves, thinking God only the founder and not also the overseer of what he
founded. How much more usefully and cautiously shall we act if we presume that
all things were indeed provided by God then and placed in the world, so that
there might now be things in which the discipline of his servants could be
proved, so that through the license of using, trials of continence might go
forward? Do not wise heads of households purposely offer and permit certain
things to their slaves, to try whether and how they use what is permitted, if
honestly, if moderately? How much more praiseworthy is the one who has
abstained altogether, who has feared even the Lord's indulgence? So too the
apostle: All things, he says, are lawful, but not all are expedient. How much
more easily will he fear the unlawful who reveres the lawful?

** Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h12
:END:

And what causes have you for going out more elaborately, when you are removed
from those things that need such? For you neither go round the temples, nor
demand shows, nor know the feast days of the Gentiles. It is for those
gatherings, and for much seeing and being seen, that all pomps are brought out
in public, either that luxury may do business or that glory may swell. You,
however, have no cause for going out that is not grave. Either some weak
brother is visited, or the sacrifice is offered, or the word of God is
administered. Any of these is a business of gravity and holiness, which has no
need of extraordinary dress, both arranged and loose. And if the necessity of
Gentile friendships and offices calls you, why not go out clad in your own
armor, all the more as you go to those outside the faith? that there may be a
difference between the handmaids of God and of the devil; that you may be an
example to them; that they may be built up in you; that, as the apostle says,
God may be magnified in your body. And he is magnified in the body through
modesty, and of course through a habit fitting to modesty.

But some say, lest the Name be blasphemed in us if we take anything from our
former dress and cultivation. Let us not then take away the old vices: let us
be the same in manners if we are the same on the surface, and then truly the
nations will not blaspheme. A grand blasphemy, from which it is said: Since
she became a Christian she walks more poorly. Will you fear to seem poorer
from the time you were made richer, and more sordid from the time you were
made cleaner? Ought Christians to walk according to the Gentiles' pleasure, or
according to God's?

** Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h13
:END:

Let us only wish that we may not be a cause of just blasphemy. But how much
more open to blasphemy is it if you who are called priestesses of modesty go
out dressed and painted in the manner of the immodest? Or what less have those
most unhappy victims of public lusts? Though certain laws used to restrain
them from marital and matronly ornaments, now at least the world's wickedness,
rising daily, has equaled them with all the most honorable women, even to the
error of telling them apart. And yet the allurements of form, never unjoined
from a prostituted body and owed to it, the scriptures also suggest. That
strong city which sits over seven mountains and very many waters earned from
the Lord the name of prostitute. But in what dress was she matched to her
name? She sits, to be sure, in purple and scarlet and gold and precious stone.
How accursed are the things without which an accursed prostitute could not
even be described!

Tamar, because she had painted and adorned herself, seemed to Judah's
suspicion to be sitting as a harlot, and so, hidden under a veil, her dress
feigning that she was a woman for hire, he both wanted her as a woman for
hire, and addressed her, and struck a bargain. From this we learn that we must
in every way provide against immodest meetings and suspicions. Why is the
integrity of a chaste mind stained by another's suspicion? Why is what I shun
hoped for in me? Why does my dress not announce my manners in advance, lest
the spirit be wounded through the ears by shamelessness? Let it be allowed to
seem modest; it is certainly not allowed to seem immodest.

** Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h14
:END:

Someone perhaps will say: It is not necessary for me to be approved by men;
for I do not seek the testimony of men; God is the inspector of the heart. We
all know it, provided we remember what the same God said through the apostle:
Let your honesty appear before men. For what, if not that malice may have no
access to you at all, or that you may be an example and testimony to the evil?
Or what is, Let your works shine? And why does the Lord call us the light of
the world? Why did he compare us to a city set on a mountain, if we do not
shine in the dark and stand out among those sunk down? If you hide your lamp
under a bushel, left in the dark you must of necessity be run into by many.
These are the things that make us lights of the world, our goods, of course.
And good, at least true and full good, does not love darkness; it rejoices to
be seen and exults even in the very pointing out. For Christian modesty it is
not enough to be so; it must also be seen to be so. For its fullness ought to
be so great that it flows from the mind into the dress and wells from the
conscience onto the surface, so that even from outside it may inspect, as it
were, its own furniture, such as suits the keeping of faith forever. For the
delicacies whose softness and looseness can unman the strength of faith must
be shaken off.

For the rest, I do not know whether a hand used to being ringed with a
bracelet can bear to stiffen into the hardness of a chain. I do not know
whether a leg that has rejoiced in an anklet will suffer itself to be squeezed
into the sinew. I fear the neck, occupied with nooses of pearls and emeralds,
will not give room to the sword. Therefore, blessed ones, let us meditate on
harder things, and we shall not feel them; let us leave gladder things, and we
shall not miss them. Let us stand unencumbered for every violence, having
nothing we fear to leave. These things are the tethers of our hope. Let us
cast off earthly ornaments if we desire heavenly. Do not love gold, in which
all the offenses of the people of Israel are branded. You ought to hate what
ruined the fathers, what was adored by those leaving God. Even then gold is
food for fire. For the rest, times for Christians are always, and now most of
all, spent not in gold but in iron: the robes of martyrdoms are being
prepared; the angels who will carry us are being awaited. Go out already built
up with the medicaments and ornaments of prophets and apostles, taking
whiteness from simplicity, redness from modesty, painting the eyes with
shamefastness and the mouth with silence, inserting into the ears the words of
God, fastening to the necks the yoke of Christ. Submit the head to husbands,
and you will be adorned enough. Occupy the hands with wool, plant the feet at
home, and you will please more than in gold. Clothe yourselves with the silk
of honesty, the fine linen of holiness, the purple of modesty. So painted, you
will have God as a lover.
