* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:
The discourse entitled On the Generation of the Soul in the Timaeus reports
whatever Plato and the Platonists have argued with some zeal. It also brings
in certain geometrical analogies and likenesses that, as he thinks, bear on
the theory of the soul, and musical and arithmetical theorems as well.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
He says that matter was given shape by the soul. He grants a soul to the
universe, and to each living creature the soul that governs it. In one respect
he presents this soul as ungenerated, in another as subject to coming to be.
Matter he holds everlasting, and shaped by the divine through the soul. Vice,
he says, has come about as an offshoot of matter, so that the divine may not
be thought the cause of evils.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
The circle of Posidonius did not set the soul far from matter. Taking the
substance of limits to be called divisible in relation to bodies, and mixing
this with the intelligible, they declared the soul to be the form of what is
extended in every direction, constituted according to number that contains
harmony. For mathematical objects, they say, have been ranked between the
first intelligibles and the sensibles; and since the soul has from the
intelligible its everlastingness and from the sensitive its capacity to be
affected, it is fitting that its substance stand in the middle. For it escaped
them too that God used the limits of bodies later, when the soul was already
finished, for the shaping of matter, bounding and enclosing its scattered and
unbound character by surfaces fitted together from triangles. Still more out
of place is it to make the soul a Form. For the soul is ever-moving, the Form
unmoved; the one is unmixed with the perceptible, the other yoked together
with body. Besides this, God became an imitator of the Form as of a pattern,
but of the soul he was the craftsman as of a finished work. That Plato does
not set number as the substance of the soul, but the soul as ordered by
number, has been said already.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
Common to both these views is this: neither in limits nor in numbers is there
any trace of that power by which the soul is fitted by nature to judge the
perceptible. For intellect and the power of intellection have been put into it
by participation in the intelligible principle; but opinions and beliefs, and
the imaginative and the passible, come from the qualities about the body,
which no one would think simply arise from units or lines or surfaces. And not
only the souls of mortals have a cognitive power of the perceptible, but also,
he says, "the soul of the cosmos, revolving upon itself, whenever it touches
something that has a scattered substance and whenever it touches the
indivisible, is moved through its whole self and speaks of that with which
anything is the same and that from which it is other, in relation to what
especially and where and how it happens, and how the things that come to be
are each toward each and what they undergo." In these words he at the same
time sketches the ten categories,
and he makes it still clearer in what follows. "For true discourse," he says,
"whenever it comes to be about the perceptible and the circle of the Other,
going straight, reports it through the whole of its soul, opinions and beliefs
come to be that are firm and true; but when in turn it is about the rational
and the circle of the Same, running well, discloses them, knowledge is of
necessity produced. And if anyone ever calls that in which these two come to
be among the things that are by any name other than soul, he will say anything
rather than the truth." From where, then, did the soul get this motion that
apprehends the perceptible and forms opinion, a motion other than that
intellective one that ends in knowledge? It is hard to say, unless we firmly
lay down that here he is not composing soul simply, but the soul of the
cosmos, from a substrate of the better, indivisible substance and of the
worse, which he has called divisible about bodies: not something other than
the opinative and imaginative motion that sympathizes with sensibles, not
generated but existing everlastingly, like the other. For nature, having the
intellective, also had the opinative. But the former is unmoved and unaffected
and seated about the substance that always remains; the latter is divisible
and wandering, inasmuch as it touches matter that is carried about and
scattered. For the perceptible had not obtained order, but was shapeless and
unbounded; and the power stationed about it had neither articulate opinions
nor movements all in order, but most of them dreamlike and deranged and
disturbing the bodily, except so far as by chance it fell in with the better.
For it was in the middle of both and had a nature sympathetic and akin to
both, clinging by its sensitive side to matter and by its judging side to the
intelligibles.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
Something like this Plato also makes clear by his names. "For this account,"
he says, "as computed by my vote, let it be granted in summary: that being and
space and becoming were three, and three in kind, even before the heaven came
to be." For he calls matter space, as a seat and sometimes a receptacle; being
he calls the intelligible; and becoming, while the cosmos had not yet come to
be, he means no other than the substance in changes and motions, ranked
between that which stamps and that which is stamped, transmitting here the
images from there. For these reasons, then, it was named divisible, and also
because what perceives must be distributed along with the perceptible, and
what imagines along with the imaginable, and must extend together with them.
For the sensitive motion, being proper to soul, is moved toward the
perceptible outside; but intellect itself was, on its own, stable and unmoved,
yet once it has come to be in the soul and gained mastery it turns the soul
toward itself and completes the circular course, which, about what always
remains, most of all touches upon being. That is why their partnership has
been hard to blend, mixing the divisible with the indivisible and what is
carried every way with what is nowhere movable, and forcing the Other to come
together into the Same. But the Other was not motion, just as the Same was not
rest, but a principle of difference and unlikeness. For each comes down from a
different principle: the Same from the One, the Other from the Dyad; and they
are mixed first here about the soul, bound by numbers and ratios and harmonic
means. The Other, arising in the Same, produces difference; the Same in the
Other produces order, as is clear in the first powers of the soul, which are
the judging and the motive. Motion shows itself at once about the heaven:
otherness in sameness by the circuit of the fixed stars, sameness in otherness
by the order of the planets. For in the former the Same prevails, and in the
things about the earth the contrary. Judgment has two principles: intellect
from the Same toward universals, and sense from the Other toward particulars.
Discourse is mixed from both, becoming intellection among intelligibles and
opinion among sensibles, using as instruments in between imaginations and
memories, of which some produce the Other in the Same and others the Same in
the Other. For intellection is a motion of the thinker about what remains;
opinion is a rest of the perceiver about what is moved. Imagination is a
weaving of opinion with sense: the Same sets it at rest in memory, while the
Other in turn moves it in the difference of before and now, touching otherness
and sameness together.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
We must take the fusion that came to be about the body of the cosmos as an
image of the analogy by which he fitted the soul together. There the extremes
were fire and earth, whose nature is hard to blend with one another, or rather
wholly unblendable and unable to hold together. So placing in the middle of
them air before fire and water before earth, he first blended these with each
other, then through them mixed and fitted those extremes both to these and to
one another. Here again he brought together the Same and the Other, contrary
powers and rival extremes, not through themselves, but with another substance
in between: the indivisible before the Same, and before the Other the
divisible, assigning each as belonging to each, then blending them further
once those had been mixed. In this way he wove together the whole form of the
soul, as far as it could be done, making like from different and one from
many.
