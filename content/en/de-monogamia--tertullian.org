* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Heretics take marriages away; the psychics pile them on. The former do not
marry even once; the latter do not marry only once. What are you doing, law of
the Creator? Between alien eunuchs and your own grooms you complain as much of
household over-service as of strangers' disdain. Those who abuse you injure
you just as those who do not use you. But neither is that kind of continence
to be praised, because it is heretical, nor is the license to be defended,
because it is psychic. The one blasphemes, the other runs riot; the one
destroys the God of marriages, the other confounds him. Among us, however,
whom the recognition of spiritual gifts justly makes to be called spiritual,
continence is as religious as license is modest, since both are with the
Creator. Continence honors the law of marriage, license tempers it; the former
is not forced, the latter is ruled; the former has free choice, the latter has
a limit. We know one marriage, as we know one God. The law of marriage pays
more honor where it has shame as well. But to the psychics, who do not receive
the Spirit, the things that are of the Spirit are not pleasing. So, while the
things of the Spirit are not pleasing, the things of the flesh will please, as
being contrary to the Spirit. The flesh, he says, lusts against the Spirit,
and the Spirit against the flesh. But what will the flesh lust after except
what is more of the flesh? For that reason also in the beginning it was made
alien to the Spirit. My Spirit, he says, shall not remain in these men
forever, because they are flesh.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

And so they taunt the discipline of monogamy as a heresy; nor are they forced
to deny the Paraclete by any cause more than this, that they think him the
institutor of a new discipline, and a very harsh one for them. So we must
first take our stand on this in a general review: whether it is possible that
the Paraclete has taught anything of the kind that can be counted new against
catholic tradition, or burdensome against the Lord's light burden. On both
points the Lord himself has spoken. For in saying, I still have many things to
say to you, but you cannot bear them yet; when the Holy Spirit has come, he
will lead you into all truth, he plainly enough holds out that the Spirit will
do things that can be thought new, as never published before, and at times
burdensome, as for that reason not published. So, you say, by this argument
anything new and burdensome can be ascribed to the Paraclete, even if it comes
from an adversary spirit. Not at all. For an adversary spirit would appear by
the diversity of his preaching, first adulterating the rule of faith, and so
adulterating the order of discipline, because the corruption of what holds the
prior rank comes first, that is, of faith, which is prior to discipline. A man
must first be a heretic about God, and then about his institution. But the
Paraclete, having many things to teach which the Lord deferred to him,
according to the predefinition, will first bear witness to Christ himself as
we believe him, with the whole order of God the Creator, and will glorify him,
and will bring him to remembrance, and so, recognized from the principal rule,
he will reveal those many things that belong to disciplines, the integrity of
his preaching speaking for them: new, because they are now revealed,
burdensome, because even now they are not borne, yet of no other Christ than
the one who said he had many other things as well which were to be taught by
the Paraclete, no less burdensome to men of our day than to those by whom they
were not yet then able to be borne.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But whether monogamy is burdensome, let the still shameless infirmity of the
flesh look to that; whether it is new, let this be settled for now. For we say
something broader: even if the Paraclete had today determined a total and
solid virginity or continence, so as not to let the heat of the flesh foam
itself down even in a single marriage, even so he would seem to be introducing
nothing new, since the Lord himself opens the kingdoms of the heavens to
eunuchs, being himself a eunuch as well, looking to whom the apostle also, and
himself for that reason c(??)strated, prefers continence. But, you say, saving
the right of marrying. Saving it, plainly, and we will see how far; yet
already destroyed in that part where he prefers continence. It is good, he
says, for a man not to touch a woman. Therefore it is evil to touch her. For
nothing is contrary to good except evil. And so it remains that even those who
have wives should be as if they had them not, all the more that those who do
not have them ought not to have them. He also gives the reasons why he so
advises: that the unmarried think about God, but the married about how each
may please his partner in marriage. And I can contend that what is permitted
is not purely good. For what is purely good is not permitted, but is lawful of
itself. Permission has a cause sometimes even of necessity. In this case, in
short, it is not the will of the one who permits marrying. For he wills
something else: I will, he says, that you all be as I also am. And when he
shows that it is better, what does he confirm that he wills if not what he has
first said is better? And so if he permits something other than what he
willed, permitting not by will but by necessity, he shows that what he
unwillingly indulged is not purely good. Finally, when he says, It is better
to marry than to burn, what kind of good is that to be understood, which is
better than a penalty? which cannot seem better except when compared with the
worst? That is good which holds this name by itself, without comparison, I do
not say with evil, but even with another good, so that even if it is
overshadowed by comparison with another good, it still remains in the name of
good. But if it is forced to be called good by comparison with evil, it is not
so much good as a kind of lesser evil which, obscured by a higher evil, is
driven to the name of good. Take away the condition, then, so that you do not
say, It is better to marry than to burn, and I ask whether you dare to say, It
is better to marry, without adding than what it is better. It then becomes no
longer better, and while not better, not good either, once the condition is
removed which, while making it better than another thing, so forces it to be
held good. It is better to lose one eye than two. If, however, you leave the
comparison of either evil, it will not be better to have one eye, because it
is not even good.

What now, if he grants all indulgence of marrying from his own, that is from
human, sense, out of the necessity we have named, because it is better to
marry than to burn? In short, turning to the other case by saying, But to the
married I announce, not I but the Lord, he shows that what he had said above
was not of the Lord's authority but of human judgment. But when he turns their
minds back to continence, I will, however, that you all be so, I think, he
says, that I also have the Spirit of God, so that if he had indulged anything
out of necessity, he might recall it by the authority of the Holy Spirit. John
too, warning that we ought to walk as the Lord also walked, surely warned us
to walk according to holiness of the flesh as well. So more plainly: And
everyone, he says, who has this hope in him, purifies himself, as he also is
pure. For elsewhere also: Be holy, as he also was holy, namely in the flesh.
For he would not have said it of the Spirit, because the Spirit is recognized
as holy of himself and does not wait for an admonition to holiness, which is
his proper nature. But the flesh is taught holiness, which was holy in Christ
as well. Therefore if all these things blot out the license of marrying, both
by inspection of the condition of the license and by the preference of
continence imposed, why could not the same Spirit, coming after the apostles
to lead the discipline into all truth through the stages of the times
(according to what Ecclesiastes says, A time for every thing), now put a last
clasp on the flesh, no longer calling away from marriage sideways, but openly,
since the time is now more than ever gathered in, about 160 years having been
added since then? Would you not of yourself think this over: This discipline
is old, already shown then in the Lord's flesh and will, and then in the
counsels as well as the examples of his apostles? We were long ago destined to
this holiness. The Paraclete introduces nothing new. What he warned of, he
defines; what he deferred, he exacts. And now, thinking these things over, you
will easily persuade yourself that it was much more fitting for the Paraclete
to preach single marriages, who could have preached none, and that he is more
to be believed to have tempered what it would have become him even to have
taken away, if you understand what Christ wills. In this too you ought to
recognize the Paraclete as advocate, that he excuses your infirmity from total
continence.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Let the mention of the Paraclete now stand aside, as of some author of ours.
Let us unroll the common instruments of the ancient scriptures. This very
thing is shown by us: that the discipline of monogamy is neither new nor
foreign, but ancient and proper to Christians, so that you may feel the
Paraclete to be its restorer rather than its institutor. As for antiquity,
what older form can be brought forward than the very origin of the human race?
God fashioned one woman for the male, one rib of his having been taken, and
that from among several. And in the preface of the work itself, It is not, he
says, good for the man to be alone; let us make a help for him. For he would
have said helpers if he had destined him for several wives. He also added a
law about the future, if indeed it was said prophetically, And they shall be
two into one flesh; not three, nor more: otherwise they are no longer two, if
there are more. The law stood. In short, the union of marriage lasted in the
authors of the race to the end, not because there were no other women, but
because there were none for this reason, that the first-fruits of the race
might not be stained by a double marriage. Otherwise, if God had willed, there
could have been others; he could at least have taken from the number of his
own daughters, having Eve no less from his bones and from his flesh, if this
could have been done piously. But when the first crime, homicide, was
dedicated in fratricide, no crime was so worthy of the second place as two
marriages. For it does not matter whether a man has had two wives one by one,
or whether two at the same time have made a pair. The number of those joined
and of those separated is the same. Yet God's institution, once it had
suffered violence through Lamech, stood afterward to the end of that race. No
second Lamech arose, married as he was to two. Scripture denies what it does
not note. Other iniquities provoked the flood, avenged once, of whatever kind
they were, yet not seventy times seven, which is what two marriages deserved.

But the reformation of the second human race is also enrolled with monogamy as
its mother. Again two into one flesh undertake to grow and overflow, Noah and
his wife and their sons in single marriages. Even in the animals themselves
monogamy is recognized, lest even beasts be born of adultery. Out of all
beasts, he says, out of all flesh you shall lead two into the ark, that they
may live with you, male and female: they shall be of the flying animals
according to kind, and of all the creeping things of the earth according to
their kind: two of all shall enter to you, male and female. In the same form
he also commands sevens to be chosen from pairs of male and female, one and
one. What more shall I say? Even to the unclean birds it was not allowed to
enter with two females.

So much for the testimony of the beginnings, and the advocacy of our origin,
and the prejudgment of the divine institution. Which is of course a law, not a
memorial; since if it was so done from the beginning, we find ourselves
directed to the beginning by Christ; as in the question of divorce, saying
that it was permitted by Moses because of their hardness, but from the
beginning it was not so, he without doubt recalls the undividedness of
marriage to the beginning. And therefore those whom God from the beginning
joined, two into one flesh, man shall not separate today. The apostle also,
writing to the Ephesians, says that God had purposed in himself, for the
dispensation of the fulfillment of the times, to gather to the head, that is
to the beginning, all things in Christ, things above the heavens and above the
earth in him. So also the Lord put on himself the two letters of Greece, the
first and the last, figures of beginning and end meeting in him, so that, just
as Α rolls on as far as Ω and again Ω is rolled back to Α, catur, he might
show that in himself there is both the course of the beginning down to the end
and the return of the end to the beginning, so that every arrangement, ending
in him through whom it began, through the Word of God who was made flesh, may
end as it also began. And so truly are all things recalled in Christ to the
beginning, that faith too has returned from circumcision to the integrity of
that flesh, as it was from the beginning, and freedom of foods and abstinence
from blood alone, as it was from the beginning, and the undividedness of
marriage, as it was from the beginning, and the restraint of divorce, which
was not from the beginning, and lastly the whole man is recalled into
paradise, where he was from the beginning. Why then should Adam not even be
brought back there as a monogamist, who cannot be presented as entire as he
was when he was sent out from there? So far then as the restoration of the
beginning is concerned, the reason both of the arrangement and of your hope
exacts from you what was from the beginning according to the beginning, which
is counted for you in Adam and recounted in Noah. Choose in which of them
you reckon your beginning. In both the censure of monogamy claims you for
itself. But if the beginning also passes on to the end, as Α to Ω, just as the
end sends back to the beginning, as Ω to Α, and so our origin is transferred
to Christ, the animal to the spiritual, because what is spiritual is not
first, but what is animal, then what is spiritual: let us likewise see whether
you owe this same thing to this second origin as well, whether the last Adam
also meets you in the same form as the first, since the last Adam, that is
Christ, was wholly unwedded, which the first Adam also was before exile. But
by the gift to your infirmity of the example of his own flesh, the more
perfect Adam, that is Christ, more perfect also by this name as he is more
entire, meets you if you will as a eunuch in the flesh. If however you are not
equal to it, he meets you as a monogamist in the spirit, having one Church as
spouse, according to the figure of Adam and Eve, which the apostle interprets
into that great sacrament, into Christ and the Church, that carnal monogamy
answered through the spiritual. You see therefore how, even renewing your
origin in Christ, you cannot carry it down without the profession of monogamy,
unless you are in flesh what he is in spirit, though you equally ought to have
been what he was in the flesh.

But let us still seek some other chief men of our origin. For to some the
monogamist parents Adam and No(??) are not pleasing, perhaps not Christ
either. They appeal at last to Abraham, though forbidden to acknowledge
another father besides God. Let Abraham now be our father; let Paul be too. In
the gospel, he says, I have begotten you. Show yourself a son of Abraham as
well. For your enrollment in him is not indiscriminate. There is a definite
time at which he is your father. For if we are counted sons of Abraham from
faith, as the apostle teaches, saying to the Galatians, You know, surely, that
those who are of faith, these are sons of Abraham, when did Abraham believe
God, and it was counted to him for righteousness? I suppose while still in
monogamy, because not yet in circumcision. But if afterward he was changed in
both, into digamy through concubinage with the handmaid, and into circumcision
through the seal of the covenant, you cannot acknowledge him as father except
at that time when he believed God, if indeed you are his son according to
faith, not according to flesh. Or if you follow the later Abraham as father,
that is the digamist, receive him circumcised as well. If you reject the
circumcised, then you will refuse the digamist too. You will not be able to
mix his two arrangements, diverse from each other in two ways. He began to be
a digamist with circumcision, a monogamist with uncircumcision. You receive
digamy: admit circumcision too. You keep uncircumcision: you are bound to
monogamy as well. And so truly are you a son of the monogamist Abraham, as
also of the uncircumcised, that if you are circumcised, you are no longer a
son, because you will not be of faith, but of the seal of a faith that was
justified in uncircumcision. You have the apostle; learn with the Galatians.
Likewise if you bring digamy upon yourself, you are not a son of that Abraham
whose faith went before in monogamy. For even if afterward he is named father
of many nations, it is of those who, from the faith that preceded digamy, had
to be counted sons of Abraham. From there let the facts look to themselves.
Figures are one thing, forms another. Images are one thing, definitions
another. Images pass away when fulfilled; definitions remain to be fulfilled.
Images prophesy; definitions govern. What that digamy of Abraham portends, the
same apostle teaches, interpreter of both testaments, just as he also
determines that our seed is called in Isaac. If you are of the free woman,
belonging to Isaac, he at any rate bore one marriage.

These then, I suppose, are they in whom I am enrolled. The rest I do not know.
If I look around at their examples, of some David even thrusting marriages on
himself through blood, of some Solomon rich even in wives, having been ordered
to follow the better things, you have Joseph as well, once yoked, and on this
name I dare to call him better than his father; you have Moses, God's arbiter
from near at hand; you have Aaron, the chief priest. The second Moses too of
the second people, who led our image into the promise of God, in whom first
the name of the Lord was dedicated, was not a digamist.

After the ancient examples of original persons, let us pass equally to the
ancient instruments of the legal scriptures, that we may review in order all
our equipment. And since some at times say they have nothing to do with the
law, which Christ did not dissolve but fulfilled, and at times seize on what
they want of the law, we too say plainly that the law has passed away in this
sense: that its burdens, according to the sentence of the apostles, which not
even the fathers were able to bear, have been dropped, but those things that
look to righteousness not only remain reserved, but are even enlarged, so that
our righteousness may be able to overflow above the righteousness of the
scribes and Pharisees. If righteousness, then chastity too. If then, because
it is commanded in the law that a man take in marriage the wife of his brother
who has died without children, to raise up seed to his brother, and this can
happen more than once to one person according to that crafty question of the
Sadducees, they therefore think a frequency of marriages is permitted in other
cases as well, they ought first to understand the reason of the precept
itself, and so they will know that that reason, now ceasing, is among those
things of the law that have been emptied. It was necessary to succeed into the
marriage of a brother who died without children. First, because that old
blessing, Increase and overflow, still had to run; then, because the faults of
the fathers were exacted even from the sons; third, because eunuchs and the
barren were held ignominious. So, lest those who had died childless not by a
guilt of nature but by being overtaken by death should be judged equally
accursed, a vicarious and, so to say, posthumous offspring was supplied them
from their own kind. But when Increase and overflow has been emptied by the
extremity of the times, the apostle bringing in, It remains that even those
who have wives be as if they had them not, because the time is gathered in,
and the sour grape eaten by the fathers has ceased to set the sons' teeth on
edge, for each will die in his own fault, and eunuchs have not only lost
ignominy but have even earned grace, being invited into the kingdoms of the
heavens, the law of succeeding into a brother's marriage being buried, its
contrary has obtained: not succeeding into a brother's marriage. And so, as we
said before, what has ceased to be valid, its reason ceasing, cannot furnish
an argument to another. Therefore a wife will not marry when her husband is
dead: she would of course be marrying a brother, if she married. For we are
all brothers. And she who is going to marry has to marry in the Lord, that is
not to a heathen but to a brother, because the old law too takes away marriage
with foreigners. And since it is also provided in Leviticus, Whoever shall
have taken his brother's wife, it is uncleanness, a shame, he shall die
without children, without doubt, while that man is forbidden to marry again,
she too is forbidden, having no one to marry except a brother. How then this
will agree with the apostle and with the law, which he does not assail in its
entirety, will be shown when we have come to his own epistle. Meantime, so far
as the law is concerned, its arguments belong more to us. In short, the same
law forbids priests to marry again. It also commands the daughter of a priest,
if widowed or cast out, if she have no seed, to return into her father's house
and be fed from his bread. Therefore if she have no seed, not so that if she
have she may marry again (how much more will she not marry, if she have
sons?), but so that if she have, she may be fed by her son rather than by her
father, that the son too may carry out the precept of God, Honor father and
mother. But Jesus, the highest priest and great priest of the Father, clothing
us from his own store (because those who are dipped in Christ have put on
Christ), has made us priests to God his Father, according to John. For he also
recalls that young man hurrying to his father's funeral, to show that we are
called priests by him, whom the law forbade to be present at the burial of
parents: Over every dead soul, it says, the priest shall not enter, and over
his father and over his mother he shall not be defiled. Must we then observe
this interdict too? Not at all. For our only father, God, lives, and our
mother the Church, and we are not dead, who live to God, nor do we bury the
dead, because they too live in Christ. At any rate we are priests called by
Christ, debtors to monogamy, from the ancient law of God, which then
prophesied of us in its own priests.

Now, turning to the law properly ours, that is to the gospel, by what examples
are we met, until we come to the sentences? See, at once as on the threshold
two priestesses of Christian holiness meet us, monogamy and continence, one
modest, in Zechariah the priest, one entire, in John the forerunner, one
appeasing God, one preaching Christ, one proclaiming a complete priest, one
presenting more than a prophet, namely him who not only preached or pointed
out in person, but even baptized Christ. For who more worthily would initiate
the Lord's body than flesh of the kind that also conceived it and bore it? And
a virgin bore Christ, about to marry once after the birth, so that each title
of holiness might be discharged in Christ's origin, through a mother both
virgin and wife of one husband. But when the infant is shown in the temple,
who takes him into his hands? who first recognizes him in the spirit? A just
and cautious man, and of course not a digamist, even so that Christ might not
presently be more worthily preached by a woman, an old widow and wife of one
husband, who, devoted to the temple, already portended sufficiently in herself
what sort ought to cling to the spiritual temple, that is the Church. Such
witnesses the infant Lord found; he had no others when grown. Peter alone I
find a husband, through his mother-in-law: I presume him a monogamist through
the Church, which, built upon him, was going to set every grade of her order
from monogamists. The rest, since I do not find them husbands, I must
understand either as eunuchs or as continent. Nor, if among the Greeks women
and wives are classed under a common name from the ease of custom (otherwise
there is a proper word for wives), shall we therefore so interpret Paul as if
he shows that the apostles had wives. For if he were disputing about
marriages, which he does in what follows, where the apostle could better have
named some example, he would seem to say rightly, Have we not power to lead
about wives, as the other apostles and Cephas? But when he subjoins those
things that show his abstinence from a food-allowance, saying, Have we not
power to eat and drink? he does not show wives led about by the apostles, whom
even those who do not have them still have power to eat and drink, but simply
women who ministered to them by the same custom as those who accompanied the
Lord. And now, if Christ reproves the scribes and Pharisees, sitting in Moses'
chair and not doing what they taught, what is it that he himself should set
upon his own chair men who remembered rather to command holiness of the flesh,
not also to undergo it, which he had in every way impressed on them both to be
taught and to be done? first from his own example, then from the other
arguments, when he says the kingdom of the heavens is of children, when he
makes others after marriage children as their partners, when he calls them to
the simplicity of the dove, a bird not only harmless but also modest, which
one male knows as one, when he denies a husband to the Samaritan woman, to
show that a numerous husband is an adulterer, when in the revelation of his
glory, from so many saints and prophets, he prefers Moses and Elijah with him,
the one a monogamist, the other a eunuch. For Elijah was nothing else than
John, who came in the power and spirit of Elijah, while that man, a devourer
and a drinker, a frequenter of lunches and suppers with publicans and sinners,
dines once at one marriage, though many of course were marrying. For he willed
to celebrate them only as often as he willed them to be.

* Chapter 9

:CUSTOM_ID: h9

But let these arguments be thought rather forced from conjectures, if
sentences too have not stood by them, which the Lord uttered in the treatment
of divorce, which, once permitted, he now forbids, first because from the
beginning it was not so, as the number of marriage was not, then because those
whom God has joined, man shall not separate, namely lest he act against the
Lord. For he alone will separate who also joined. He will separate, however,
not by the hardness of divorce, which he reproaches and restrains, but by the
debt of death. If indeed one of two sparrows does not fall to the earth
without the Father's will. Therefore if those whom God has joined, man shall
not separate by divorce, it is equally consistent that those whom God has
separated by death, man should not join by marriage, being about to join the
separation against God's will just as if he had separated the joining. So much
for not destroying God's will and for rebuilding the form of the beginning.
But another reason also conspires, or rather not another, but that which
imposed the form of the beginning and moved God's will to the prohibition of
divorce, because he who has dismissed his wife except for the cause of
adultery makes her commit adultery, and he who has married one dismissed by a
husband is of course an adulterer. Can a repudiated woman not also marry
legally, and if she has committed something of the kind without the name of
marriage, does it not take the charge of adultery, seeing that adultery is a
crime in marriage? God has judged otherwise than men, so that in the whole,
whether through marriage or commonly, the admission of another man is
pronounced adultery by him. For let us see what marriage is with God, and so
we shall know what adultery equally is. Marriage is when God joins two into
one flesh, or, finding them joined in the same flesh, has sealed the
conjunction. Adultery is when, the two having been disjoined in any way, other
flesh, or rather alien flesh, is mixed, of which it cannot be said, This is
flesh of my flesh, and this bone of my bones. For this, once done and
pronounced, as from the beginning, so now too, cannot apply to other flesh. So
you will say without cause that God does not will a repudiated woman to be
joined to another man while her husband lives, as if he willed it when he is
dead, when if she is not bound when he is dead, she is equally not bound when
he is living. As much by divorce breaking the marriage as by death, she will
not be bound to him from whom that by which she was bound has been torn away.
To whom then will she be bound? It makes no difference to God whether she
marries while her husband is living or dead. For she does not offend against
him, but against herself. Every fault that a man has committed is outside the
body: but he who commits adultery offends against his own body. And he commits
adultery, as we laid down above, who mixes other flesh with himself over that
former flesh which God either joined into two or found joined. And therefore
he took away divorce, which was not from the beginning, that he might fortify
what was from the beginning, the perseverance of two into one flesh, lest the
necessity or occasion of a third enfleshment break in, permitting divorce to
one cause only, if perhaps that has come first against which precaution is
taken. And so truly was divorce not from the beginning, that among the Romans
this kind of hardness is noted as committed after the six hundredth year of
the city's founding. But they even without divorcing mix adulteries: to us,

I see us already from this point challenged to the apostle. For the easier
perception of his meaning, it must be the more earnestly pressed that a woman
is more bound when her husband is dead, so as not to admit another man. For
let us think it over: divorce either comes of discord or makes discord, but
death happens from the law of God, not from a man's offense, and it is the
debt of all, even of the unmarried. Therefore if a repudiated woman, who
through discord and anger and hatred and their causes, injury or insult or any
complaint, has been separated in soul and body, is bound to an enemy, not to
say a husband, how much more will she who, by neither her own fault nor her
husband's, but by an event of the Lord's law, has not been separated from
marriage but left behind, belong even to the dead man, to whom even when dead
she owes concord? From him from whom she has not heard a divorce, she has not
turned away; to him to whom she has not written a divorce, she is with him;
him whom she did not wish to have lost, she keeps. She has with her the
license of the mind, which represents to a man in imaginary enjoyment all
things that he does not have. I ask the woman herself, last of all: Tell me,
sister, did you send your husband ahead in peace? What will she answer? In
discord? Then she is the more bound to him with whom she has a case before
God. She has not departed who is bound. But in peace? Then she must persevere
in it with him whom she will no longer be able to divorce, not even then about
to marry, if she had been able to divorce. Indeed she also prays for his soul,
and asks refreshment for him in the meantime, and fellowship in the first
resurrection, and offers on the yearly days of his falling asleep. For unless
she has done these things, she has truly divorced him, so far as in her lies,
and the more unjustly, the more she has done it as she could, because she
could not, and the more unworthily, the more unworthily already, if because he
did not deserve it. Or shall we be nothing after death, according to some
Epicurus, and not according to Christ? But if we believe the resurrection of
the dead, we shall of course be bound with those with whom we are to rise,
about to render account of one another. But if in that age they will neither
marry nor be given in marriage, but will be equal to angels, is it therefore
that we shall not be bound to dead spouses, because there will be no
restoration of marriage? On the contrary we shall be the more bound, because
we are destined to a better state, about to rise into a spiritual fellowship,
about to recognize both ourselves and our own. Otherwise how shall we sing
thanks to God forever, if sense and memory of this debt will not remain in us,
if we shall be reformed in substance, not in conscience? Therefore we who
shall be with God shall be together, while all are with the one God (though
the wage varies, though there are many mansions with the same Father), having
worked for one denarius of the same wage, that is of eternal life, in which
God will still less separate those whom he has joined than he forbids them to
be separated in this lesser life. This being so, how will she have room for
another man who is occupied with her own even for the future? We speak to
either sex, even if the speech is to one, because one discipline governs. She
will have one in the spirit, another in the flesh. This will be adultery, the
consciousness of one woman toward two men. If the one is disjoined from the
flesh, but remains in the heart, there, where even thought without congress of
the flesh already completes both adultery from desire and marriage from will,
he is still a husband, possessing that very thing through which he was also
made one, that is the mind: if another shall dwell in it as well, this will be
the crime. For the rest he is not shut out, if he has withdrawn from the viler
commerce of the flesh. He is a more honorable husband, the purer he has been
made.

So then, that you may marry in the Lord according to the law and the apostle
(if indeed you even care about this), what sort are you, asking for that
marriage which those of whom you ask it are not allowed to have, of a
monogamist bishop, of presbyters and deacons of the same sacrament, of widows,
whose way you have refused in yourself? And they, plainly, will give husbands
and wives just as they give morsels. For this is with them, You shall give to
everyone who asks you. And they will join you in a virgin church, the one
spouse of the one Christ. And you will pray for your husbands, the new and the
old. Choose to which you will show yourself an adulteress. I think, to both.
But if you are wise, be silent toward the dead. Let your silence be a divorce
to him, already written in another's dowry. In this way you will earn the new
husband, if you forget the old. You ought rather to please him for whose sake
you have not preferred to please God. These things the psychics will have it
that the apostle approved, or did not think over at all, when he wrote: A
woman is bound for so long a time as her husband lives; but if he has died,
she is free: let her marry whom she will, only in the Lord. For from this
chapter they defend the license of a second marriage, or rather of very many,
if of a second; for what has once ceased to be is open to every number. But in
what sense the apostle wrote will shine out, if it is first established that
he did not write in the sense the psychics use. And it will be established if
one recalls those things that are contrary to this chapter, both in doctrine
and in will and in Paul's own discipline. For if he permits second marriages,
which were not from the beginning, how does he affirm that all things are
gathered back to the beginning in Christ? If he wants us to repeat unions, how
does he defend our seed in Isaac, once a husband as author? How does he
arrange the whole order of the Church from monogamists, if this discipline
does not come first in the laity, from whom the order of the Church proceeds?
How does he call those still placed in marriage away from the fruit of
marriage, saying the time is gathered in, if he calls those who have slipped
from marriage by death back into marriage again? If these things are contrary
to the chapter in question, it will be established, as we have said, that he
did not write in the sense the psychics use; because it is easier that that
one chapter should have some reason that agrees with the rest, than that the
apostle should seem to have taught things contrary to one another. That reason
we shall be able to recognize in the matter itself.

What was that matter for the apostle of writing such things? The first
training of a new church, then especially rising, which he was of course
feeding with milk, not yet with the solid food of stronger doctrine, so much
so that before that infancy of faith they did not yet know what they ought to
do about the necessity of flesh and sex. Whose very kinds we understand from
the replies, when he says, But concerning the things you write: It is good for
a man not to touch a woman; but because of fornications let each have his own
wife. He shows there were some who, caught in marriage by the faith, feared it
might not be lawful for them thereafter to use their marriage, because they
had believed into the holy flesh of Christ. And yet he grants it according to
pardon, not according to command, that is indulging, not ordering that it be
so. For the rest he preferred all to be what he also was. Likewise, answering
about divorce, he shows that some had thought about this too, especially
because they did not think they should persevere after the faith even in
heathen marriages. They also asked counsel about virgins (for there was no
precept of the Lord), that it is good for a man if he so remain, of course as
he was found by the faith. You are bound to a wife: do not seek to be loosed.
You are loosed from a wife: do not seek a wife. But if you have taken a wife,
you have not sinned, because to one loosed from a wife before the faith a
second wife after the faith will not be counted, who is the first after the
faith. For even our life itself is counted from the faith. But here he says he
is sparing them; otherwise pressure of the flesh would follow, from those who,
because of the straits of the times, refuse the hindrances of marriage; that
care should rather be had of the Lord, by earning him, than of a husband. And
so he recalls what he permitted. Thus then in that same chapter in which he
defines that each should remain in the calling in which he was called, adding,
A woman is bound as long as her husband lives; but if he has fallen asleep,
she is free: let her marry whom she will, only in the Lord: from this too he
shows that she is to be understood who was herself found loosed from a
husband, as the man also loosed from a wife, the loosing having been made by
death of course, not by divorce, because he would not permit the divorced to
marry against the former precept. Therefore the woman, if she has married,
will not sin, because here too a second husband will not be counted who is the
first from the faith, and so it is, that for this reason he added, Only in the
Lord: because it was about her who had had a heathen and, having lost him, had
believed, lest she should presume she could marry a heathen even after the
faith; though the psychics do not care about this either. Let us know plainly
that it is not so in the authentic Greek as it has gone into use by a clever
or simple overturning of two syllables: But if her husband shall have fallen
asleep; as if it sounded of the future, and thereby should seem to belong to
her who has already lost a husband in the faith. If this were so, license sent
out without limit would have given a husband as often as he was lost, with no
shame of marrying even agreeing with the heathen. But even if it were so, as
of the future, If anyone's husband shall have died, the future equally would
belong to her whose husband will die before the faith. Take it as you will, so
long as you do not overturn the rest. For since those sentences also yield:
You were called a slave, do not care; You were called uncircumcised, do not be
circumcised; You were called circumcised, do not draw it on, with which
agrees: You are bound to a wife, do not seek to be loosed: you are loosed from
a wife, do not seek a wife, it is plain enough that these things belong to
those who, placed in a new and recent calling, were asking about the things in
which they had been caught by the faith. This will be the interpretation of
that chapter, to be tested by this: whether it agrees with the time and the
cause, and as much with the preceding examples and arguments as with the
following sentences and senses, and first of all whether it agrees with the
apostle's own counsel and institution; for nothing is so to be guarded as that
no one be found contrary to himself.

But writing also to Timothy he wants young women to marry, to bear children,

you goes. Therefore, he says, my brothers, you also were made dead to the law

rose from the dead, that we may bear fruit to God. For when we were in the

members to bear fruit to death: but now we have been emptied from the law,

in oldness of the letter. Therefore if he bids us be made dead to the law

Now if the apostle had also absolutely permitted marrying in the faith when a
marriage was lost, he would have done just as he did the other things which,
against the form of his own rule, he carried out for the condition of the
times, circumcising Timothy because of the superinduced false brothers, and
bringing certain shaven men into the temple because of the Jews' observance,
he who chastises the Galatians wanting to live in the law. But so the case
required, that he might become all things to all, that he might gain all,
travailing with them until Christ should be formed in them, and warming, as a
nurse, the little ones of the faith, by teaching some things by pardon, not by
command (for it is one thing to indulge, another to order), likewise
permitting a temporary license of marrying again because of the infirmity of
the flesh, as Moses of divorcing because of the hardness of heart. And here
too we will render the supplement of this sense. For if Christ took away what
Moses commanded, because from the beginning it was not so, and Christ will not
therefore be thought to have come from another power, why should the Paraclete
not also have taken away what Paul indulged, because a second marriage too was
not from the beginning, and not therefore be held suspect, as if an alien
spirit, so long as what is superinduced is worthy of God and of Christ? If it
was worthy of God and of Christ to restrain hardness of heart when the time
was fulfilled, why should it not be worthier both of God and of Christ to
shake off the infirmity of the flesh when the time is now more gathered in? If
it is just that a marriage not be separated, it is of course honorable that it
not be repeated. In short, in the world both are counted in good discipline,
the one under the name of concord, the other of modesty. Hardness of heart
reigned until Christ; let the infirmity of the flesh also have reigned until
the Paraclete. The new law took away divorce (it had something to take away);
the new prophecy a second marriage, no less a divorce of the former. But
hardness of heart yielded to Christ more easily than infirmity of the flesh.
This defends Paul for itself more than that Moses; if indeed it defends him
when it catches at him indulging and refuses him prescribing, which eludes his
stronger sentences and lasting wills, which does not let us render to the
apostle what he prefers. And how long will this most shameless infirmity
persist in storming the better things? Its time, until the Paraclete should
work, was that to which were deferred by the Lord the things that then could
not be borne, which it now belongs to no one not to be able to bear, because
he through whom the power to bear is given is not lacking. How long shall we
plead the flesh, because the Lord said, The flesh is weak? But he also
premised, The Spirit is ready, that the Spirit may conquer the flesh, that
what is weak may yield to the stronger. For also, He who can receive, let him
receive, he says, that is, he who cannot, let him depart. That rich man also
departed, who had not received the precept of dividing his substance to the
poor, and was dismissed to his own sentence by the Lord. Nor will hardness
therefore be imputed to Christ from the vice of anyone's free choice. Behold,
he says, I have set before you good and evil. Choose what is good; if you
cannot, because you will not (for he shows that you can if you will, because
he set both before your choice), you must depart from him whose will you do
not do.

another man, washed away the stain of the flesh with her own blood, that she

continence. Blush, flesh, who have put on Christ! Let it suffice you to marry

recalled from the end. Return into Adam, even the former, if you cannot into

better. Produce a third Adam, and him a digamist, and then you will be able to
be what, between the two, you cannot.
