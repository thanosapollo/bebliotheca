* That One Ought Not to Cling to Worldly Things

:CUSTOM_ID: h1

HOMILY 21. On not being fastened to the things of this life, and on the fire
that occurred outside the Church.

I for my part thought, beloved, that by applying the sting of the word more
sharply each time I would seem to you some harsh person, as displaying a
certain excessive frankness, fitting neither for a stranger nor for a man
seized by like charges; but you, from the rebukes, were stirred to goodwill,
and have made the blows from the tongue a kindling for greater longing. And
this is nothing strange. For you are wise in the things of the Spirit. "Rebuke
a wise man, and he will love you," says Solomon somewhere in his own words.
For this reason, brothers, I have come even now to the same exhortation,
wishing, as far as I am able, to lead you away from the nets of the devil. For
the enemy of the truth, beloved, brings against us each day a great and varied
war. And he brings it, as you know, making our own desires arrows against us,
and always taking from us the strength to harm us. For since the Master bound
the greater part of that one's power with unbreakable laws, and did not allow
him, having rushed once for all, to wipe out the race from the earth, the
malignant one thereafter steals the victory against us from our own folly. And
just as the wicked and grasping among men, whose work and choice =31.541= it
is to grow rich from what belongs to others, but who have not the power to use
force with frankness, are accustomed to lie in wait on the roads; and if they
see some place about them, either broken with deep ravines, or shaded with
density of plants, sinking into this, and cutting off the travelers' view from
far off by such coverings, they leap upon them all at once, so that no one is
able to see the snares of the danger before falling into them; so the one
hostile to us from of old, our enemy, running under the shadows of worldly
pleasures, which have grown up along the road of this life, terrible at hiding
a robber and making the plotter escape notice, from there unsuspectedly sows
the traps of destruction for us.

We must therefore, if we should wish to run the road of life that lies before
us safely, and present to Christ both soul and body free from the shame of
wounds, and receive the crowns of victory, always carry about the eyes of the
soul awake in every direction and suspect everything that is pleasant, and
pass it by at once, and fix the mind on nothing, not even if gold should
appear lying in heaps, ready to come into the hands of those who wish (For "If
wealth," he says, "should flow, do not set your heart upon it"); nor if the
earth should sprout every luxury and display costly pavilions (For "our
citizenship is in heaven, from which we also await a Savior, Christ"); nor if
dances, and revels, and drunkenness, and tables played upon by flutes (For
"Vanity," he says, "of vanities, all things are vanity"); nor if beauties of
bodies should be held out, inhabited by wicked souls (For "From the face of a
woman as from the face of a serpent flee," says the Wise one); nor if powers
and tyrannies, and swarms of bodyguards or flatterers; nor if a throne high
and splendid, having yoked nations and cities into willing slavery (For "All
flesh is as grass, and all the glory of man as the flower of grass; the grass
has withered, and the flower has fallen"). For under all these things so
pleasant the common enemy lies in wait, waiting if ever, baited by the things
seen, we should turn aside from the

straight road, and incline ourselves toward his ambush. And there is much fear
lest, running toward them unguardedly, and thinking the pleasure from
enjoyment no harm, we should swallow the hook of deceit hidden in the first
taste; then by this, some things willingly, some unwillingly, we should be
bound to them, and toward the fearful lodging of the robber, death, we should
be dragged unawares by the pleasures. =31.544=

So that it is necessary and profitable for all, brothers, that we, having
equipped ourselves like travelers or runners, and having devised from every
side lightness for our souls toward this race, should press on without turning
back toward the end of the road. And let no one suppose me to be a maker of
new names, because I have now called human life a road, since David the
prophet also named life thus; now saying somewhere: "Blessed are the blameless
in the way, who walk in the law of the Lord"; and now crying to his own
Master: "Remove from me the way of injustice, and have mercy on me by your
law." And sometimes hymning the swift help of God against those who abuse, and
bringing it to the lyre with pleasure, "And who is God except our God?" he
said; "The God who girds me with power, and has made my way blameless";
thinking it reasonable everywhere to call the sojourn of men on earth, whether
admired or base, by this name. For just as those accomplishing some intense
journey, exchanging the steps of their feet competing with one another into a
run, and always showing the former foot planted on the earth to be second by
the swift transfer of the other, easily come to the end of the road; so those
brought forth to live by the Creator, immediately at the beginning stepping
upon the segments of time, and always leaving the former behind as later,
arrive at the end of life. Or does it not seem to you also that the present
life is some continuous road laid out, and a journey divided by the ages as by
stations, furnishing to each as the beginning of the travel the birth-pangs of
mothers, and pointing out as the end of the race the tents of the tombs, and
leading all toward those, some more quickly, some more slowly, and some having
traveled through all the intervals of time, others not even having lodged at
the first stations of life? Now the other roads, as many as lead from city to
city, it is possible to turn aside from, and not to travel, for the one who
does not wish; but this one, even if we should wish to postpone the race,
having seized us by force, drags those upon it toward the end appointed by the
Master; and it is not possible, beloved, for the one who has once run out
through the gate that leads into this life, and has set foot on this road, not
also to come to its end; and each of us, after fleeing the maternal bosom,
immediately bound to the streams of time is swept along, always leaving behind
the day that has been lived, and never able, even if he should wish, to return
to yesterday.

But we take pleasure in being carried forward, and exchanging the ages, we
rejoice as if gaining something; and we judge it blessed, whenever from a
child a man, from =31.545= a man someone becomes an old man; but we do not
know, then, that we lose each time as much of life as we have lived, and we do
not perceive that life is being spent, although we always measure it from what
has run past and flowed by, nor do we consider that it is unclear how much
time the one who sent us out on this journey will wish to grant for the race,
and when he will open to each of the runners the gates of the entrance, and
that we must be prepared each day for the departure from here, and wait for
the Master's nod with eyes standing ready. For "Let your loins," he says, "be
girded, and your lamps burning; and you like men waiting for their own lord,
when

he shall return from the wedding, so that, when he comes and knocks, they may
open to him at once."

Nor do we wish to examine carefully which loads are light for such a race and
can also change place with those who gathered them, and make life there joyful
for us, becoming the property of those who acquired them; and which are heavy
and difficult and nailed to the earth, and not by nature able to be made our
own by men once for all, nor allowed to follow those who have them through
that narrow gate.

But as many things as we ought to gather, we have left behind; and those which
it is fitting to overlook, these we gather; and those which can be united to
us and truly become an ornament connatural to soul and body, to these we do
not even attend; but those which remain alien continually, imprinting on us
only the shame, these we attempt to heap up, toiling in vain, and undergoing a
toil, as if someone, deceiving himself, should wish to draw water into a
pierced jar. For this I think is known even to all infants, that none of the
pleasant things in life, over which the majority are mad, is truly ours, or is
by nature able to become so; but equally all appear alien, both of those who
seem to enjoy them, and of those who do not even approach them. For not even
if some should gather boundless gold in life, does it remain their own
continually; but either while they still live, squeezing it from every side,
it runs away, having flowed over to the more powerful, or, when they have
already come to death, it leaves them, and does not wish to emigrate with
those who acquired it. But they, being dragged by the one who by force
separates souls from this wretched flesh toward the necessary road, turning
often toward the money, weep for the sweats from youth spent upon it; but the
wealth looks toward other hands, having rubbed off on those men only the toil
of the gathering and the charge of greed.

Nor if someone should acquire on earth countless acres, and magnificent
houses, and herds of animals of every kind, and should wrap himself in all the
power among men, does he enjoy =31.548= them forever; but for a little,
having been named from these, he will again yield the abundance to
others, himself having gone under a little earth, and often even before the
tomb, and before the departure from here, he will see prosperity passing over
to others, and perhaps even to enemies. Or do we not know how many fields, how
many houses, how many nations and cities, even while those who acquired them
still lived, have put on the names of other masters? and how those who of old
were slaves have gone up to the throne of rule, while those called lords and
masters have been content to stand with the ruled, and have bowed to their own
slaves, as in turns of dice, when things were suddenly shifted for them? As
for the things devised for us for eating and drinking, and all that wealth,
insulting beyond need, has contrived for the service of the thankless belly
that holds nothing, when would they become ours, even if they should be poured
in continually? Things which, having deposited only some small pleasure in the
taste in passing, we immediately find burdensome and superfluous and throw
them out with haste, as if, should they linger in the inward parts, we should
run the greatest danger concerning life. At any rate satiety has brought death
to many, and has become the cause of enjoying nothing any longer. And
licentious beds, and unholy couplings, and all the works of a raging and
deranged soul, are they not a loss altogether obvious to nature, and a
conspicuous harm, and an alienation and diminution of each person's truly own
things, the body being lessened in the mixings, and being robbed of the most
genuine and most cohesive nourishment of the members? Therefore each of those
who have wallowed in licentious beds, immediately after the act, when the goad
of the flesh has withered, and the mind has come to the disgusting end of what
it attempted, as from a drunkenness or some storm, when it should take leisure
to consider where it is,

a violent repentance of incontinence enters. For he perceives that the body
has become more powerless, and sluggish toward the work of necessities, and
altogether weak.

This then the trainers also, having understood, have written the law of
temperance in the wrestling-schools, keeping the bodies of the young men
inviolate from pleasures, and not permitting them, when competing, even so
much as to look upon shining forms, if they should wish the head to be
crowned, since incontinence brings laughter in wrestling, not a crown. These
things, as altogether alien and superfluous, and able to become the property
of no one, it is good to pass by, having closed the eyes; but of the things
that are truly ours it is fitting to take much forethought. And what is truly
=31.549= ours? Soul, by which we live, being something fine and intellectual,
and needing none of the things that weigh down; and body, the vehicle given to
it for life by the Creator. For this is man: mind bound in fitting and
suitable flesh. This is formed by the all-wise craftsman of all things in our
maternal bosoms. This the time of birth-pangs leads into light from those dark
chambers. This was appointed to rule the things on earth. For this creation
has been laid out as a gymnasium of virtue. For this a law is set, to imitate
the Fashioner as far as we are able, and to sketch on earth the good
order in the heavens. This, being called from here, departs. This stands
before the tribunal of the God who sent it. This is examined; this
receives the requital of what has been lived here. And the virtues too one
would find becoming our
possessions, whenever they are woven together with nature from care; and they
do not wish to leave us while we toil on earth, unless we ourselves willingly
drive them away by bringing in worse things; and when we
hasten there they run ahead, and rank the one who acquired them with the
angels, and shine forever under the eyes of the Creator.

But wealth, and power, and distinction, and luxury, and all such a crowd,
increasing each day from our folly, neither came forth with us into life, nor
has gone away with anyone, but what was said of old to the righteous man
stands and prevails over every man: "Naked I came out of my mother's womb,
naked also I shall go away." He therefore who deliberates best will care for
the soul as much as possible, and will try through all things to keep this
pure and genuine; but of the flesh, whether wasting by hunger, or wrestling
with frost and heat, or pained by diseases, or suffering something violent
from some, he will make little account, crying at each of the hardships the
word of Paul, and uttering: "Even if our outer man is being destroyed, yet the
inner is being renewed day by day." And neither, seeing the dangers for life
coming on, will he appear afraid, but will say to himself with courage: "We
know that if our earthly house of the tent should be dissolved, we have a
building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal in the heavens." But
if someone should wish to spare also the body, as the only possession
necessary to the soul, and cooperating with it toward living on earth, he will
turn a little toward its needs, and so as only to hold it together, and by
moderate care keep it strong for the service of the soul, not however
permitting it to leap under satiety. But if he should ever see it inflamed
with desires of more and beyond what is profitable, he will cry toward it the
lawgiving of Paul: "We brought nothing =31.552= into this world; it is clear
that we can carry nothing out. And having food and coverings, we shall be
content with these." For by chanting these things to it continually and crying
out, he will make it both obedient and always light toward the heavenly
journey, and will acquire it more as a fellow-worker for the things set before
us; but if we should allow it to grow insolent, and to be filled each day

with all things, like some untamed beast, later, dragged down together by its
violent pullings toward the earth, he will lie groaning without profit. And
having been brought to the Master, and being asked for the fruit of the
journey on earth that was granted him, then not having it to give, he will
lament at length, and will dwell in continual darkness, blaming much the
luxury and its deceit, by which he was robbed of the time of salvation; but
gaining nothing any longer from the tears.

"For in Hades who will confess to you?" says David. Let us flee then as
quickly as we can, lest we choke ourselves willingly. But if someone, having
been baited even long ago, has either heaped upon himself the dust of wealth
from injustice, and bound the mind with its cares, or has attached to nature a
stain of licentiousness hard to wash out, or has filled himself with the other
charges; let this man, while there is still time, before coming to complete
destruction, put off the greater part of the loads, and before the ship goes
under water, make a jettison of the cargo which he gathered improperly, and
let him imitate the workers of the sea. For those men, even if they should
happen to be carrying something of the necessities on the ship, but a boiling
swell should rise against them from the sea, and threaten to cover the ship
pressed by the freight, as quickly as they can they throw off the greater part
of the weight, and pump the merchandise unsparingly into the sea, so as to
make the ship higher than the wave, and, if possible, to give flight from the
danger to the souls alone, and to the bodies. And much more than those men,
surely, it is fitting that we both deliberate these things and do them. For
whatever those men throw away, they have immediately lost, and thereafter the
force of poverty surrounds them; but we, as much as we pump out the wicked
freight, will heap up more and better wealth for the souls.

For fornication, and all such things, when thrown away, are destroyed, and go
toward not even existing, being dissolved by tears; but holiness and
righteousness thereafter move into their place, things light, and not knowing
how to be covered by any waves. Money, at any rate, when well thrown out, is
not lost to those who throw it out and cast it; but, as into some other more
secure merchant-ships, transferred to the =31.553= bellies of the poor, it is
saved and arrives having come to harbors, and is kept for those who throw it
out, an ornament, not a danger. Let us therefore, beloved, counsel something
humane concerning ourselves, and the burden of abundance, if we should
altogether wish to make it our own gain, let us distribute to many, who will
both carry it with great joy, and will shut it up in the Master's bosom,
inviolable storehouses, "Where moth does not destroy, nor do robbers dig
through, nor steal." Let us give authority to wealth, when it wishes to
overflow, toward those in need. Let us not pass by the Lazaruses still even
now lying in our eyes, nor, when the crumbs of our table suffice them to
satiety, let us begrudge; nor, having emulated that untamed rich man, let us
go over to the same flame of Gehenna as he. Since then we shall need Abraham
much, and much each of those who have lived well; but there will be no gain
for us from the cry; "For a brother does not redeem, shall a man redeem?" And
each of those will say to us, crying out: "Do not seek kindness which you
yourself ignored toward others; nor wish to receive things so great when you
spared the lesser. Enjoy these things which you gathered in life. Weep now,
since then, seeing your brother weeping, you did not pity." These things they
will say, and justly, to us. And I fear that they may also strike us with
words more bitter than these, since we even surpass that rich man, as you
know, in wickedness.

For we do not, sparing wealth once for all, pass by the brothers thrown on the
ground, nor, keeping the abundance for the children or the other household,

shut our ears to those in need; but transferring the expense onto worse
things, and making munificence a whetstone of wickedness for those who
practice it. For how many women or how many men stand around the table of
some! some charming the host with shameful words, others kindling the fire of
incontinence with unseemly looks and gestures; others wishing to furnish
laughter to the one who invited them by jests against one another; and others
deceiving him with false praises. And they do not gain only being feasted so
splendidly, but they also bring down their hands filled with costly gifts, and
they learn from us that it profits them more to pursue and practice such
things than the virtues. But if a poor man should stand by us, =31.556=
scarcely even speaking from hunger, we turn away from the one of the same
nature, we loathe him, we run off with haste, as if fearing that by walking
more slowly we might even share in the very misfortune. And if he should bow
toward the ground, ashamed of the calamity, we say he is a worker of
hypocrisy; but if he should look at us with frankness because of the heavy
sting of hunger, we call him again shameless and violent. And if he should
happen to be covered with sound clothes, someone having given them, we drive
him away as insatiable, and we swear that he is pretending poverty; but if he
should be covered with rotten rags, we chase him again as stinking, and
neither mixing the name of the Creator into his supplications, nor continually
adjuring us not to fall into such sufferings, is he able to bend the unpitying
choice. For these reasons I suspect a fire of Gehenna heavier than that rich
man had. If then the time had granted it, and sufficient power were present, I
would have gone through all the things concerning that man, as the Book
showed, and would have given you the working of the words; but now it is the
hour to dismiss you who have toiled.

But you yourselves, even if we have omitted something through weakness of
thought and tongue together, fashioning it for yourselves, apply it as some
medicines to the wounds of the souls. For "Give occasion to a wise man, and he
will be wiser," says the Scripture. And God is able to make all grace abound
toward you, that, having in everything always all self-sufficiency, you may
abound unto every good work. But already, as you see, the discourse having
been brought down for us toward harbors, some brothers lead it back again into
the course of counsel, bidding us not to pass by the wonders worked yesterday
by the Master, nor to keep silent the trophy which the Savior set up against
the rage of the devil; but to give you occasion for the leapings of hymnody.
For again, as you know, the devil displayed his own rage against us, and
having armed himself with a flame of fire, he made war upon the precincts of
the Church. But again the common mother conquered, and overturned the device
upon the enemy, and nothing was his except to make public the enmity. Grace
blew against the onsets of the enemy; the temple remained unharmed. The storm
brought on by the enemy was not able to shake the rock on which Christ built
the fold of his own flock. He who of old also quenched the furnace in Babylon
was stationed with us even now. How do you think the devil groans today, not
having enjoyed from the attempt that which he wished? For the hostile one
kindled a pyre neighboring the Church, that he might mar our prosperities; and
from every side the =31.557= flame, being fanned by those violent breaths of
his, was pouring over what lay under it, and was feeding on the air nearby,
being forced to touch the sanctuary, and dragging us down into a share of the
calamity. But the Savior made it flow upon the one who kindled it, and ordered
him to draw his madness back again into himself. And the enemy prepared the
bow of the plot; but he has been prevented from sending the arrow; or rather
he did send it, but it has been turned back upon his own head. He himself
enjoys those bitter

tears which he prepared for us.

But let us make, brothers, the wound more burdensome for the enemy, let us
stretch his mourning. And how, I will say, but you do it. There are some
snatched
from the mastery of the fire by the Creator, and having no remaining resource
of life left to them any longer, but having escaped the danger with soul and
body alone. Let us then, as many as have remained without tasting the
experience of the hardships, set forth our own abundance as common to them.

Let us embrace the brothers barely saved, let each of us say to each: "He was
dead, and has lived again; he was lost, and was found"; and let us cover the
kindred body. Let us set our consolation against the abuses of the enemy, so
that even while harming he may seem to have harmed nothing great, and having
made war, may show no one conquered, and having stripped the abundance of the
brothers, may appear conquered by our munificence. But you who have escaped
the danger, brothers, do not be greatly distressed at the evils that have
happened, nor be shaken in thought, but shake off the mist of grief, and
strengthen the souls, using more manly reasonings, and make what has happened
an occasion of crowns. For if you remain unshaken, you will both appear more
proven in the faith, having shone forth like well-born gold from the fire, and
you will increase the shame for the enemy still more, as not even having been
able to move a tear in you by the things he plotted. Remind yourselves of the
endurance of Job. Say to yourselves what that man said: "The Lord gave, the
Lord has taken away; as it seemed good to the Lord, so also it has come to
pass." And let no one from what he has suffered be persuaded to reason and to
say that no providence at all orders the things concerning us, nor let him
accuse the economy and judgment of the Master; but let him look to that
athlete, and make him a counselor of the better. Let him reckon up all the
contests in order, in which that man was champion; and with how many arrows,
having been struck by the devil, he did not receive a mortal blow. For he
stripped him of the prosperity of his household, and planned to bury him with
the successive reports of evils.

For while =31.560= the former was still announcing such a calamity, another
messenger was coming, bringing the dejection of worse things; and the terrible
things were joined to one another, and the calamities rivaled the onrush of
waves, and before the former tear had ceased, the occasion of another was
brought on. But the righteous man stood like some breakwater, receiving the
assaults of the storm, and changing the force of the waves into foam, and he
sent forth the well-disposed voice toward the Master: "The Lord gave, the Lord
has taken away; as it seemed good to the Lord, so also it has come to pass";
and he thought none of the things that happened worthy of tears. But when
someone came announcing that a violent wind had shaken the chamber of gladness
upon the sons and daughters as they were feasting, then only he tore the
garment, displaying the sympathy of nature, and testifying by what he did that
he was an affectionate father. Yet even then setting a limit and measure to
the griefs, and adorning what was happening with those pious voices, he said:
"The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it seemed good to the Lord, so
also it has come to pass," all but crying such things: I was called father for
as long a time as the one who made me this wished. He has planned again to
take away from me the crown from offspring; I do not contend with him about
what is his own. Let what seems good to the Master prevail; he is the
fashioner of the race, and I am an instrument. Why should I, being a slave, be
distressed without profit, and blame a verdict which I cannot dissolve? With
such words the righteous man was shooting down the devil. But when the hostile
one saw him conquering again, and able to be shaken by none of these things,
he brought the device of the trial against the

flesh itself, and having scourged the body with unspeakable blows, he prepared
springs of worms to be given forth from it; and having brought the man down
from the thrones of kingship, he seated him on a dunghill. But he, even being
struck with such sufferings, remained unmoved; and while the body was being
torn, he kept inviolate the treasure of piety in the hidden place of the soul.
The enemy therefore, not having anything left to do, comes to the memory of
the ancient plot, and having dragged the mind of the woman into an impious and
blasphemous judgment, through her he was trying to shake the athlete.

And she, having given up at the long time, stood by the righteous man, bowed
down, and striking her hands together at the things seen, and reproaching him
with the fruits of piety, and recounting the old plenty of the household, and
pointing out the present evils, and what sort of life he had obtained from
what sort, and what wage he had received from the Master for the many
sacrifices, and she was always saying words worthy of the faint-heartedness of
women, =31.561= but able to trouble every man, and to overturn even a noble
mind; "for I go about as some wanderer and hired servant, I the queen am a
slave, and I have been forced to look toward the hands of my own household
servants, and I who of old fed many am now contentedly fed from what belongs
to others"; and that it would be good and useful, using impious words, to cut
himself out of the earth, having whetted the sword of the Creator's wrath,
rather than, enduring the terrible things, to prolong the toil in the contests
for himself and for her who dwells with him.

But he, having been distressed at these words as at none of the former evils,
and having filled his look with anger, and having turned toward the woman as
toward an enemy, what does he say? "Why have you spoken as one of the foolish
women?" Put down, he says, O woman, the counsel. Until when do you insult the
common life in what you say? You have also lied about my manner of life, as I
would not have wished; you have slandered even my life in what you have
spoken. I have now thought myself to have been impious by half, since marriage
has made us both one body, but you have fallen into blasphemy. "If we received
the good things from the hand of the Lord, shall we not bear the evil?" Remind
yourself of the good things that have gone before. Weigh the better against
the worse. The life of no man is blessed throughout. To fare well through all
belongs to God alone. But you, if you are pained at the present things,
console yourself from those that went before. Now you weep, but you laughed
before; now you are poor, but you were rich before. You drank the clear stream
of life, and drinking this turbid one, endure. Not even the streams of rivers
appear pure throughout. And a river, as you know, is our life, flowing
continually and being filled with successive waves. For one part of it has
already flowed forth, and another is still going; and one has just now peeked
out from the springs, and another is about to; and we all hasten toward the
common sea of death. "If we received the good things from the hand of the
Lord, shall we not bear the evil?" Do we compel the judge to furnish like
things to us forever? Do we teach the Master how he must conduct our
life? He himself has the authority of his own verdicts. As he wishes he orders
the things concerning us. And he is wise, and he measures out what is
profitable to the household servants.

Do not be over-curious about the Master's judgment. Love only the things being
ordered by his wisdom. Whatever he should give you, receive this with
pleasure. Show in the painful things that you were also worthy of the former

life blossomed again with all good things, and twofold upon the house =31.564=
wealth flowed together from every side, so that he might have the one as
having lost nothing, and the other might exist as a wage of endurance for the
righteous man. And why did he receive horses, and mules, and camels, and
sheep, and farmlands, and all the luxury of abundance from the double, but the
number of the children sprouted equal to those who had died? Because the
irrational cattle, and all the wealth being destroyed, came to complete
destruction; but the children, even having died, were living in the fairest
part of nature. Therefore, having been adorned again with other sons and
daughters by the Creator, he had this possession also double. For the ones
were present furnishing the gladness in life to those who begot them, and the
ones who had gone before were awaiting the one who planted them; then all
would stand around Job, when the judge of human life gathers the Church of all
the people; when the trumpet, signaling the arrival of the king, having cried
more strongly into the tombs, shall demand the deposit of the bodies. Then
also those who now seem to be dead will stand more quickly than the living
before the craftsman of all things. For this reason, I think, having measured
out to him the other wealth double, he judged him to be content with children
equal in number. Do you see how many good things the righteous Job gathered
for himself from endurance? You too then, if some hardship has approached you
from the fire kindled yesterday from the plot of the demons, endure this, and
lull the distress from the suffering with better reasonings; and according to
what is written, "Cast your care upon the Lord, and he himself will nourish
you." To him belongs the glory unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
