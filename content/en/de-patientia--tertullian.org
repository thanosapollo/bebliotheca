* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I confess to the Lord God that I have been rash enough, if not even shameless,
to dare write on patience, a thing I am wholly unfit to practice, being a man
of no goodness. Those who set out to prove and commend a thing ought first to
be found practicing it themselves, and ought to steer the firmness of their
warning by the authority of their own way of life, so that their words may not
blush at the failure of their deeds.

And would that this blushing might bring a remedy, so that shame at not
showing what we go to recommend to others might become a school for showing
it; except that some goods, like some evils, are of a size too great to bear,
so that only the grace of divine inspiration can take them in and practice
them. For what is most good rests most with God, and no one else dispenses it
but he who owns it, as he sees fit for each. So it will be a kind of comfort
to argue about what one is not given to enjoy, after the manner of the sick,
who, being empty of health, do not know how to keep quiet about its goods.

So I, most wretched, always sick with the heats of impatience, must sigh after
and call for and plead for that health of patience which I do not possess,
when I recall and, looking at my own weakness, work out that the good health
of faith and the soundness of the Lord's discipline come easily to no one
unless patience sits beside him. So far is she set over the things of God that
no one can keep any precept, or carry out any work pleasing to the Lord, if he
is a stranger to patience.

Even those who live outside her honor her good with the name of highest
virtue. The philosophers, who are counted animals of some wisdom, set so high
a seal on her that, though they quarrel among themselves with the various
lusts of their schools and rivalries of their opinions, yet, remembering
patience alone in common, they have entrusted the peace of their studies to
this one thing. They conspire toward her, they make league toward her, they
pursue her with one mind in their pretension to virtue, and they display all
their show of wisdom from patience. It is a great testimony to her when she
even advances the empty disciplines of the world to praise and glory. Or is it
rather an injury, when a divine thing is rolled about among worldly arts? But
let them look to it, who will soon be ashamed of their wisdom, destroyed and
disgraced together with the world.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

To us the warrant for practicing patience is not a human pretension of canine
evenness of mind, shaped by numbness, but the divine arrangement of a living
and heavenly discipline, holding up God himself as the first example of
patience: who scatters the bloom of this light equally over just and unjust;
who suffers the offices of the seasons, the services of the elements, the
tributes of all created nature, to come alike to the worthy and the unworthy;
bearing with most ungrateful nations, who adore the toys of the arts and the
works of their own hands, who persecute his name together with his family,
with luxury, greed, injustice, and malice growing insolent every day, so that
by his own patience he lessens himself. For many do not believe in the Lord
for this reason, that they have so long not known him angry with the world.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And this form of divine patience may perhaps be judged, as if from far off, to
belong to things above. But what of that patience which among men, openly on
earth, has in a way been taken in hand? God suffers himself to be born in a
mother's womb and waits; once born he bears growing up; once grown he is not
eager to be known, but is even insulting to himself and is baptized by his own
servant; and he drives off the tempter's assault with words alone. When from
Lord he becomes Teacher, teaching man to escape death, trained, of course, in
the full pardon of offended patience.

He did not strive, he did not cry out, nor did anyone hear his voice in the
streets; he did not break the bruised reed, he did not quench the smoking
flax. For the prophet had not lied, or rather the witness of God himself,
placing his own Spirit in the Son with all patience. He refused no one who
wished to cling to him; he despised no one's table or roof; on the contrary he
himself served at the washing of the disciples' feet. He did not scorn sinners
or tax collectors; he was not even angry with that city which had refused to
receive him, though the disciples had wished heavenly fires to be shown to so
insulting a town. He cared for the ungrateful; he yielded to those who lay in
wait for him.

This would be little if he had not also kept his own betrayer with him and
steadily refused to point him out. But when he is handed over, when he is led
as a beast to the victim (for so he no more opens his mouth than a lamb under
the shearer's power), he to whom, had he willed it, legions of angels would
have been present from the heavens at one word, did not even approve the
avenging sword of a single disciple. The Lord's patience was wounded in
Malchus. And so he cursed the works of the sword for the time to come, and by
restoring health he made amends to him whom he himself had not hurt, through
patience, the mother of mercy.

I say nothing of his being nailed; for he had come for this. Yet did the death
that had to be undergone need insults as well? But he wished, as he was about
to depart, to be filled with the pleasure of patience. He is spat upon,
beaten, mocked, clothed in foul things, crowned with fouler. Wonderful is the
faithfulness of his evenness. He who had proposed to hide in the figure of a
man imitated nothing of man's impatience. From this, Pharisees, you ought most
of all to have recognized the Lord; no man would have carried out patience of
this kind. Such and so great proofs, whose greatness is among the nations a
rejection of the faith, but among us its reason and its building, prove
plainly enough, not only by words in teaching but also by the Lord's
sufferings in bearing, to those who have been given to believe, that patience
is the nature of God, the working and the excellence of a certain inborn
property.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Therefore if we see all honest servants of good mind living according to their
master's character (since the art of earning favor is obedience, and the
discipline of obedience is yielding subjection), how much more ought we to be
found living after the Lord, servants of the living God, whose judgment on his
own does not turn on a fetter or a freedman's cap, but on eternity of
punishment or of salvation? To turn aside this severity, or to invite this
generosity, there is need of as much care in obedience as the things
themselves which severity threatens or generosity promises.

And yet we wring obedience not only from men bound in slavery or debtors of
obedience by some other right, but even from cattle, even from wild beasts,
understanding that they have been provided and handed over by the Lord for our
uses. Will those things which God has put under us then be better than we are
in the discipline of obedience? In the end, those that obey recognize it. Do
we hesitate to listen to him to whom alone we are subject, namely the Lord?
But how unjust it is, how ungrateful too, not to pay back to him through whom
you obtain it, from yourself, the same thing which by another's kindness you
obtain from others!

I need not say more about the showing of the obedience we owe the Lord God.
Knowledge of God is enough to understand what lies upon it. Yet lest we seem
to have thrown in something foreign about obedience, obedience itself is drawn
from patience. Never does an impatient man obey, or a patient man fail to take
pleasure in it. Who then will argue at length about that good which the Lord
God, who both shows and receives all goods, has carried about in himself? Who
likewise doubts that every good, because it belongs to God, is to be pursued
with the whole mind by those who belong to God? By these things both the
commendation and the exhortation concerning patience have been set in
readiness, and as it were in the short form of a rule.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Still, it is not idle to let the argument go on concerning the necessities of
faith, because it is not fruitless either. Talkativeness in building up is
never ugly, if it is ever ugly. So if the discourse is about some good, the
matter also demands a review of the contrary of that good. For you will throw
more light on what is to be pursued if you likewise set out what is to be
avoided.

Let us then consider impatience, whether, as patience was born and found in
God, so its adversary was born and found in our adversary, that from this it
may appear how principally it is opposed to faith. For what was conceived by
God's rival is certainly no friend to the things of God. There is the same
discord of things as of their authors. Further, since God is best and the
devil on the contrary worst, they testify by their very difference that
neither does the work of the other, so that it cannot seem to us that anything
good has been brought forth by the evil one any more than anything evil by the
good.

I therefore catch the birth of impatience in the devil himself, already then
when he could not patiently bear that the Lord God had subjected all the works
he had made to his own image, that is, to man. For he would not have been
pained if he had borne it, nor would he have envied man if he had not been
pained. So he deceived him because he had envied; he had envied because he had
been pained; he had been pained because he had certainly not borne it
patiently. Whether that angel of perdition was first evil or impatient I
refuse to ask, since it is plain that either impatience began with malice or
malice from impatience, and that they then conspired together and grew up
undivided in the one father's bosom.

But that which he had first felt, by which he had first entered into sin,
instructed by his own experiment in what would help toward sinning, he called
in to drive man into crime. The woman, as soon as she was accosted, was, I may
say without rashness, inspired through that very conversation with a spirit
infected by impatience. So far is it that she would never have sinned at all
if she had kept patience toward the divine prohibition. What? She did not even
bear to be accosted alone, but before Adam, not yet a husband, not yet owing
her his ears, she is impatient even of silence, and makes him a transmitter of
what she had drawn from the evil one.

So another man also perishes through another's impatience; soon he himself
perishes through his own impatience, committed on both sides, both toward
God's warning and toward the devil's snare, not bearing to keep the one and
refuse the other. Hence the first judgment, from the same place as the origin
of the offense; hence God began to be angry, from where man was led to offend.
From there, in God, the first patience, from where the first indignation: he
then, content with a curse alone, held back from the rush of punishment
against the devil.

Or what crime before this of impatience is charged against man? He was
innocent and a friend of God from near at hand and a tiller of paradise. But
once he fell under impatience, he ceased to be wise toward God, he ceased to
be able to bear heavenly things. From then on man, given to the earth and cast
from God's eyes, began to be easily seized by impatience into everything that
would offend God. For at once she, conceived of the devil's seed by the
fruitfulness of malice, brought forth anger as a son, and trained the child
she had borne in her own arts. For what had itself plunged Adam and Eve into
death taught the son also to begin from murder.

I would ascribe this to impatience in vain if that Cain, the first murderer
and first fratricide, bore with evenness and without impatience the refusal of
his offerings by the Lord, if he was not angry with his brother, if in short
he killed no one. Since then he could not kill unless angry, nor be angry
unless impatient, he shows that what he did through anger is to be referred to
her from whom anger was suggested, through these, as it were, the infant
cradles of impatience at that time. But how great were her increases soon
after? No wonder. If she was the first to offend, it follows that, because she
was first, she is also the sole womb of every offense, pouring from her own
spring the various veins of crimes.

Of murder, indeed, it has been said. But anger, brought forth from the
beginning, refers even whatever causes it later found for itself to impatience
as to its origin. For whether a man commits that crime for enmities or for
plunder, first he becomes impatient of hatred or of greed. Whatever compels,
without impatience of itself, cannot be carried through. Who has gone into
adultery without impatience of lust? And if in women that sale of chastity is
forced by a price, it is certainly arranged by impatience of despising gain.
These are the principal offenses before the Lord. For, to put it shortly,
every sin is to be ascribed to impatience. Evil is impatience of good. No one
is unchaste who is not impatient of chastity, nor dishonest of honesty, nor
impious of piety, nor restless of rest. For anyone to become evil, he will not
have been able to remain good.

Why then should such a hydra of offenses not offend the Lord, the disapprover
of evils? Or is it not plain that Israel too always offended against God
through impatience? From the time when, forgetting the heavenly arm by which
he had been drawn out of the afflictions of the Egyptians, he demands of Aaron
gods as leaders for himself, when he pours the contributions of his gold into
an idol; for he had impatiently taken the delays, so necessary, of Moses
meeting with the Lord. After the edible rain of manna, after the watery
following from the rock, they despair of the Lord, by not bearing a thirst of
three days. For this impatience too is reproached to them by the Lord. And
lest we wander through each case, they never failed to perish by offending
through impatience. How did they lay hands on the prophets except through
impatience of hearing? And on the Lord himself, through impatience even of
seeing. But if they had entered on patience, they would have been set free.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

She herself is the one that both follows faith and goes before it. Abraham
believed God and was counted to him for righteousness; but patience proved his
faith, when he was ordered to sacrifice his son, not, I would say, as a trial
of faith, but as a typical witness. For the rest, God knew whom he had counted
to righteousness. So heavy a precept, which it did not even please the Lord to
have carried out, he both heard patiently and, if God had willed, would have
fulfilled. Rightly then blessed, because also faithful; rightly faithful,
because also patient.

So faith, lit by patience, when it was to be sown among the nations through
the seed of Abraham, which is Christ, and was to overlay the Law with grace,
set her own helper, patience, over the enlarging and fulfilling of the Law,
because she alone had been lacking before to the teaching of righteousness.
For of old they used to demand an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth, and
they lent evil for evil. For patience was not yet on earth, because neither
was faith. Impatience, of course, in the meantime was enjoying the occasions
of the Law. It was easy, with the Lord of patience and her teacher absent.

After he came and composed the grace of faith with patience, it is no longer
lawful even to provoke with a word, or even to say a foolish thing, without
the danger of judgment. Anger is forbidden, spirits are reined in, the wanton
hand is checked, the poison of the tongue is taken out. The Law has found more
than it lost, Christ saying, Love your enemies, and bless those who curse you,
and pray for your persecutors, that you may be sons of your Father in heaven.
Do you see what Father patience wins for us? This chief precept binds up the
whole discipline of patience, since not even to do evil deservedly is allowed.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Now as we run through the causes of impatience, the rest of the precepts too
will answer in their places. If the mind is stirred by loss of household
goods, it is warned in almost every place by the Lord's scriptures that the
world is to be despised; nor does any greater exhortation to contempt of money
lie to hand than that the Lord himself is found in no riches. He always
justifies the poor, he condemns the rich in advance. So he has furnished in
advance, for patience under loss, a disgust at wealth, showing by the casting
off of riches that injuries to them too are not to be counted. What then we
have no need to seek, because the Lord did not seek it, we ought not to bear
ill when it is cut down or even taken away.

The Lord's Spirit through the apostle has declared greed the root of all
evils. Let us not take it as consisting only in lust for what is another's;
for even what seems ours is another's. Nothing is ours, since all things are
God's, whose we ourselves also are. So if, when we have suffered loss, we feel
it impatiently, we shall be caught as kin to greed, since we are not grieving
over what was our own. We are seeking what is another's when we bear ill the
loss of what is another's. He who is stirred by impatience at loss, putting
earthly things before heavenly, sins against God from close at hand. For he
shakes the spirit which he received from the Lord for the sake of a worldly
thing. Let us then lose earthly things gladly, and keep the heavenly. Let the
whole world perish, so long as I gain patience.

Now he who has resolved not to bear with constancy something taken from him by
theft or force or even by sloth, I do not know whether he could easily, or
even from the heart, lay his own hand on his property in the cause of alms.
For who, not bearing at all to be cut by another, himself draws the iron
through his own body? Patience in losses is an exercise in giving and sharing.
He is not slow to give who is not afraid to lose. Otherwise how will a man who
has two tunics give one of them to the naked, unless he is the same man who
can offer even his cloak to him who takes the tunic? How shall we make friends
for ourselves of mammon, if we have loved it so much that we cannot bear its
loss? We shall perish with what is lost. What do we find here where we have
something to lose?

It belongs to the Gentiles to apply impatience to every loss, who perhaps put
money before the soul. For they also do it when, with lusts of gain, they run
the profitable hazards of merchandise on the sea; when for money's sake they
do not hesitate even in the forum to attempt what ought to fear condemnation;
when at last they hire themselves to the games and to the camp; when they rob
along the road in the manner of beasts. But we, according to the difference by
which we stand over against them, ought to lay down not the soul for money but
money for the soul, whether freely in giving or patiently in losing.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

We carry the soul itself, and the body itself, exposed in this world to injury
from all, and we bear that injury patiently: shall we be hurt by a tasting of
lesser things? Far be such a stain from a servant of Christ, that patience
prepared for greater temptations should fall in trifles. If anyone tries to
provoke you by the hand, the Lord's warning is at hand: To him who strikes you
on the face, he says, turn the other cheek as well. Let wickedness be worn out
by your patience. Whatever that blow may be, bound in pain and insult, he is
beaten more heavily by the Lord. You strike that wicked man more by bearing
it. For he will be beaten by him for whose sake you bear it.

If the bitterness of the tongue has broken out in curse or abuse, look to the
saying: When they curse you, rejoice. The Lord himself is cursed in the Law,
and yet he alone is blessed. Therefore let us servants follow the Lord and be
cursed patiently, that we may be able to be blessed. If I hear with too little
evenness some bold or worthless word against me, I must either pay back the
turn of bitterness myself, or I shall be tortured with silent impatience. When
therefore I have struck, being cursed, how shall I be found to have followed
the Lord's teaching, by which it has been handed down that a man is defiled
not by the uncleannesses of vessels but by those things which are brought out
of the mouth? Likewise, that the guilt of every vain and idle word remains
upon us. It follows then that what the Lord forbids us, he also warns us to
bear with evenness from another.

I will add concerning the pleasure of patience. For every injury, whether
struck by tongue or by hand, when it has met patience, will be paid off with
the same end as some weapon flung against a rock of most constant hardness and
blunted. For it will fall there, a vain and fruitless work, and sometimes,
struck back, it will rage with a return blow upon him who sent it. Surely
someone hurts you in order that you may feel pain, because the fruit of the
one who hurts is in the pain of the one hurt. Therefore when you have
overturned his fruit by not feeling pain, he himself must feel pain by the
loss of his fruit. Then you not only go away unhurt, which even by itself is
enough for you, but besides you are delighted by your adversary's frustration
and defended by his pain. This is the use and the pleasure of patience.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Not even that form of impatience is excused in the loss of our own, where some
claim of pain pleads for it. For the regard of the apostle's warning is to be
set first, who says: Do not be saddened at anyone's falling asleep, as the
nations who have no hope. And rightly. For believing the resurrection of
Christ, we believe in ours too, for whom he both died and rose. Therefore
since the resurrection of the dead is established, the grief of death is
empty, and the impatience of grief is empty too. Why would you grieve, if you
do not believe he has perished? Why bear impatiently one taken away for a time
whom you believe will return? It is a setting out that you take for death. He
who goes before is not to be mourned, but plainly to be longed for. That
longing too must be tempered by patience. Why would you bear beyond measure
that he has gone whom you will soon follow?

For the rest, impatience in such things both bodes ill for our hope and
betrays the faith. And we injure Christ when we do not receive with evenness
those he has called away, as if they were to be pitied. I desire, says the
apostle, to be taken now and to be with Christ. How much better does he show
the wish? If then we impatiently grieve that others have attained the wish of
Christians, we ourselves do not wish to attain it.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

There is also another supreme goad of impatience, the lust of vengeance, doing
the business either of glory or of malice. But glory is everywhere empty, and
malice is never otherwise than hateful to the Lord, most of all in this place,
when, provoked by another's malice, it sets itself higher in carrying out
vengeance and, paying back the wicked, doubles what was done once. Vengeance,
in error's keeping, seems a comfort of pain; in truth's keeping it is proved,
on the contrary, a thing of malice. For what is the difference between the one
who provokes and the one provoked, except that the former is caught first in
the evil deed, the latter later? Yet both are guilty before the Lord of the
injured man; he both forbids and condemns every wicked thing. There is no
reckoning of order in an evil deed, nor does place separate what likeness
joins. Absolutely, then, it is commanded that evil is not to be repaid with
evil. A like deed has a like due.

How shall we keep this if we, who are scornful, will not hold vengeance in
scorn? And what honor shall we offer the Lord God if we have claimed for
ourselves the judgment of defense? We rotting ones, earthen vessels. When our
little slaves take vengeance on their fellow-servants we are heavily offended,
and those who have offered us their own patience, as mindful of humility and
of servitude, loving the right of the master's honor, we not only approve, but
we make them a larger satisfaction than they would have presumed for
themselves. Is that at risk for us in the Lord, so just to judge, so powerful
to accomplish? What then do we believe that Judge, if not an avenger too? This
he promises us, saying: Vengeance is mine, and I will avenge, that is,
Patience is mine, and I will repay patience. For when he says, Do not judge,
lest you be judged, does he not demand patience? For who will not judge
another, except one who will be patient of not defending himself? Who judges
in order to pardon? And if he will pardon, still he has not guarded against
the impatience of one judging, and he has taken away the honor of the one
Judge, that is, of God.

How many mishaps has impatience of this kind been accustomed to run into? How
often has it regretted defense? How often has its urgency been found worse for
its own causes? Because nothing undertaken by impatience knows how to be
finished without impulse, nothing done by impulse fails either to offend or to
collapse or to go headlong. If you are defended too lightly, you will go mad;
if more fully, you will be burdened. What have I to do with vengeance, whose
measure I cannot govern through impatience of pain? But if I lean on patience,
I shall not feel pain; if I do not feel pain, I shall not desire to avenge.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

After these principal matters of impatience, gathered as we could, why wander
among the rest, which at home, which abroad? Broad and spread out is the
working of the Evil One, hurling the many goads of the spirit, now little, now
very great. But you may despise the little ones for their smallness, and yield
to the greatest for their excess. Where the injury is smaller, there is no
need of impatience. But where the injury is greater, there the remedy of
injury, patience, is more necessary. Let us then strive to bear what is
inflicted by the Evil One, so that the rivalry of our evenness may cheat the
enemy's zeal. But if we ourselves bring some things upon us either by
imprudence or even of our own will, let us likewise go through patiently what
we charge to ourselves. But if we believe some things are struck by the Lord,
to whom should we offer patience more than to the Lord? Nay, he even teaches
us to congratulate and rejoice at the dignity of divine chastening. I, he
says, chasten those I love. O that blessed servant, whose amendment the Lord
presses, with whom he deigns to be angry, whom he does not deceive by
pretending not to warn!

From every side, then, we are bound to the duty of administering patience,
from whatever part we come upon either our errors or the snares of the Evil
One or the Lord's warnings. Great is the reward of that duty, namely
happiness. For whom did the Lord name blessed except the patient, saying:
Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven? No one
surely is poor in spirit except the humble. And who is humble except the
patient? Because no one can subject himself without the first patience of
subjection itself. Blessed, he says, are those who weep and mourn. Who bears
such things without patience? And so to such both comfort and laughter are
promised. Blessed are the meek. By this name the impatient cannot be counted
at all. Likewise when he marks the peacemakers with the same title of
happiness and names them sons of God, are the impatient kin to peace? A fool
would think so. But when he says, Rejoice and exult, as often as they curse
you and persecute you, for your reward is very great in heaven, he certainly
does not promise that to the impatience of exultation, because no one will
exult in adversities unless he has first despised them. No one will despise
them unless he has practiced patience.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

As for the discipline of peace, most pleasing to God, who that is born of
impatience will forgive his brother even once, I do not say seven times, but
seventy times seven? Who, taking his case to judgment against his adversary,
will settle the business by agreement, unless he has first cut away pain,
hardness, bitterness, the poisons of impatience? How will you forgive and it
be forgiven you, if you have been clinging to the injury through absence of
patience? No one with a torn mind against his brother will complete a gift at
the altar, unless he has first returned to patience by reconciling with his
brother. If the sun has gone down on our anger, we are in danger. We are not
allowed to remain one day without patience.

But since she governs every form of saving discipline, what wonder that she
also serves repentance, which is accustomed to come to the help of those who
have fallen? When a marriage has been parted (yet from that cause by which it
is allowed for either man or woman to be borne on to perseverance in
widowhood), she waits, she longs, she prays for repentance in those who will
at some time enter on salvation. How much good she confers on both! She does
not make the one an adulterer, and she amends the other.

So also in those examples of the Lord's parables she stands by the saints in
patience. The shepherd's patience seeks and finds the wandering sheep. For
impatience would easily despise one. But patience takes up the labor of the
search, and moreover the patient bearer carries on his shoulders the sinful
one that was left. The father's patience also receives that prodigal son and
clothes him and feeds him and excuses him before the impatience of the angry
brother. So he is saved who had perished, because he entered on repentance.
Repentance does not perish, because it has found patience.

For love, the highest sacrament of faith, the treasure of the Christian name,
which the apostle commends with all the forces of the Holy Spirit, by whose
disciplines is she trained if not those of patience? Love, he says, is
long-suffering: so she takes on patience. She is kind: she does no evil while
she is patient. She does not envy: that indeed is proper to impatience. She is
not rude: she has drawn modesty from patience. She is not puffed up, she does
not trample: for that does not belong to patience. She does not seek her own,
if she offers her own, so long as it may profit another. She is not provoked:
otherwise what would she have left to impatience? Therefore, he says, love
bears all things, endures all things, surely because she is patient. Rightly
then she will never fall away; for the rest will be emptied and finished.
Tongues, knowledge, prophecies are exhausted; faith, hope, love remain: faith,
which the patience of Christ brought in; hope, which the patience of man
awaits; love, which patience, with God as teacher, accompanies.

Thus far of patience, simple and of one form and seated only in the mind,
though I labor in many ways at the same patience even in the body for earning
the Lord's favor, inasmuch as she was also put forth by the Lord himself in
the virtue of the body, since the ruling mind easily shares what the spirit
has brought in with its dwelling. What then is the business of patience in the
body? First, affliction of the flesh, a victim to appease the Lord by the
sacrifice of humiliation, when she offers to the Lord squalor with scantiness
of food, content with simple fare and a pure drink of water, when she joins
fasts, when she grows into ash and sackcloth. This patience of the body
commends prayers, confirms petitions; this opens the ears of Christ God,
scatters severity, draws out clemency. So that Babylonian king, when the Lord
was offended, after he had been an exile from human form by seven years of
filth and stench, having sacrificed the patience of his body, both recovered
his kingdom and, what is more to be wished for a man, made satisfaction to
God.

Now if we set out the higher and happier grades of bodily patience, she also
procures for holiness the continence of the flesh. She both keeps the widow,
and assigns the virgin, and lifts the voluntary eunuch to the kingdoms of
heaven. What comes from the virtue of the mind is completed in the flesh. The
patience of the flesh, last of all, does battle in persecutions. If flight
presses, the flesh serves in the hardships of flight. If prison also comes
first, the flesh is in bonds, the flesh on the wood, the flesh on the ground,
both in that poverty of light and in that want of the world. When she is
brought out to the trial of happiness, to the occasion of a second baptism, to
the very ascent of the divine seat, nothing there is more than the patience of
the body. If the spirit is willing, but the flesh without patience is weak,
where is the salvation of the spirit and of the flesh itself? But when the
Lord says this of the flesh, declaring it weak, he shows what it needs to be
made firm, namely patience against every preparation for overthrowing or
punishing the faith, so that it may most constantly bear stripes, fire, cross,
beasts, the sword, which the prophets, which the apostles overcame by bearing.

By these forces of patience Isaiah is sawn and does not keep silent concerning
the Lord; Stephen is stoned and asks pardon for his enemies. O most happy that
man too, who discharged every form of patience against every force of the
devil! whom neither flocks driven off, nor those riches in cattle, nor sons
taken away in one rush of ruin, nor lastly the torment of his own body in the
wound, shut out from patience and faith given over to the Lord; whom the devil
struck with all his forces in vain. For he was not called away from regard for
God by so many pains, but he has stood for us as an example and a witness of
patience to be carried through, as much in spirit as in flesh, as much in mind
as in body, so that we may not sink under losses of worldly things, nor under
losses of those dearest, nor even under conflicts of the body.

What a trophy did God build in that man for the devil? What a banner did he
raise from the enemy of his glory, when that man at every bitter message
brought nothing from his mouth except Thanks be to God, when he cursed a wife
already worn out by evils and urging crooked remedies? What then? God was
laughing; what then? the Evil One was being torn, when Job with great evenness
scraped off the unclean overflow of his ulcer, when he playfully called the
worms breaking out from there back into the same caves and pastures of the
dug-up flesh? So that workman of God's victory, having blunted all the darts
of temptations with the breastplate and shield of patience, both soon
recovered the integrity of his body from God and possessed doubled what he had
lost. And if he had wished his sons also restored, he would have been called
father again. But he preferred that they be given back to him on that day. He
put off so much joy, being thus sure of the Lord. He already bore a voluntary
bereavement, lest he should live without some patience.

So God is a quite sufficient trustee of patience. If you have deposited injury
with him, he is the avenger; if loss, he is the restorer; if pain, he is the
physician; if death, he is the raiser of the dead. How far is it granted to
patience, that she should have God as her debtor? And not without cause. For
she keeps all that pleases him, she takes part in all his commands. She
fortifies faith, she governs peace, she helps love, she trains humility, she
waits for repentance, she assigns confession, she rules the flesh, she keeps
the spirit, she bridles the tongue, she holds the hand, she tramples
temptations, she drives off scandals, she completes martyrdoms; she comforts
the poor, she tempers the rich, she does not stretch the weak, she does not
consume the strong, she delights the faithful, she invites the Gentile; she
commends the slave to the master, the master to God; she adorns the woman, she
approves the man; she is loved in the boy, praised in the youth, looked up to
in the old man; in every sex, in every time of life she is beautiful.

Come now, if we may also grasp her figure and dress. Her face is quiet and
calm, her brow clean, drawn into no wrinkle of grief or anger; her eyebrows
equally relaxed toward a glad manner, her eyes cast down by humility, not by
unhappiness; her mouth sealed with the honor of silence; her color such as
belongs to the secure and the harmless; a frequent motion of the head against
the devil and a threatening laugh; for the rest a garment white about the
breast and fitted to the body, as one who is neither puffed up nor restless.
For she sits on the throne of that most mild and gentle Spirit of hers, who is
not rolled into a whirlwind, not livid with cloud, but is of a tender
clearness, open and simple, whom Elijah saw the third time. For where God is,
there also is his foster-child, namely patience. When therefore the Spirit of
God comes down, undivided patience accompanies him. If we have not admitted
her with the Spirit, will he always tarry in us? Nay, I do not know whether he
would remain any longer. Without his companion and handmaid he must of
necessity be straitened in every place and time. Whatever his enemy has
inflicted, he will not be able to bear alone, lacking the instrument of
bearing.

This is the reason of patience, this the discipline, these the works of the
heavenly and true, that is, the Christian, not like that patience of the
nations of the earth, false and shameful. For that the devil might rival the
Lord in this too, as if plainly on equal terms (except that the very diversity
of evil and good is equally a match in magnitude), he has taught his own a
patience of their own: that, I mean, which subjects to the power of their
wives husbands up for sale for a dowry or trading in pandering; which, to
snare the childless, bears every labor of forced compliance with feigned
affections; which binds the workmen of the belly by insulting patronages in
the subjection of liberty to the gullet. Such pursuits of patience the nations
have known, and they take the name of so great a good for foul works. They
live patient toward rivals and the rich and those who invite them, impatient
of the living God alone. But let them look to their own patience and that of
their chief, which the fire below awaits. For the rest, let us love the
patience of God, the patience of Christ; let us pay back to him what he
himself paid out for us. Let us offer the patience of the spirit, the patience
of the flesh, we who believe in the resurrection of flesh and spirit.
