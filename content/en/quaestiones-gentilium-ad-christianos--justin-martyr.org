Of Saint Justin the Philosopher and Martyr
Greek Questions to the Christians
concerning the incorporeal and concerning God and concerning
the resurrection of the dead.
1. From where is it clear whether there is something incorporeal, and if there
is something incorporeal? 2. From where is it clear that it is able to be by
itself apart from body, and if it is itself by itself incorporeal? 3. From
where is it clear whether the incorporeal is better than the body, and if the
incorporeal is not better than the body? 4. What is the incorporeal, whether
soul or something better than soul, such as God? 5. And in what does soul
differ from God? . And from where is it clear whether there is a God at all?
7. And if there is a God, it must be inquired whether he makes, or ever made,
or is also going to make, whatever it may be? 8. And whether he made, or
makes, or will make? 9. And what he makes and how he makes, whether with
counsel or also without counsel? 10. And if with counsel, using an instrument
or without an instrument? 11. And if without counsel, whether he makes
irrationally without thought and examination, or with some judgment? 12. And
if with judgment, from lack of what does he need judgment? But if without
judgment, by what reason must it be investigated. 13. And if we should grant
that something comes to be from him, whether what comes to be is incorruptible
or corruptible? And if incorruptible, we are at a loss whether what is
generated at all can be incorruptible, and by what reason? And if it is
corrupted, in what do the works of the immortal differ from human works? For
often many of these remain for a much longer time, the maker himself having
been corrupted, they themselves being incorruptible, as one can see in the
case of buildings and such things. 14. And if what comes to be from God is
corrupted, by whose vice is it corrupted, that of the maker, or of what has
come to be, or of something from outside becoming contrary to the maker?
Whichever of these we suppose, it is clear that the vice is the maker's. For
if what has come to be ought to be corrupted through some innate vice of its
own, the maker is the cause, because he made it such that some evil is left
remaining in it; or if there is something from outside contrary to the maker,
in this way too it is by the vice of the one who has made it, because of not
being able to prevail over the opposites; or if the vice is from the maker, it
is obvious that he himself is the evil one. These things then, for now, a few
out of many concerning these; for if we should wish, it must also be inquired
what body is and from what things the body is, and the difficulties upon
these. But leaving these aside for now, as contributing nothing of the
difficulties, we inquire one of the more noble things, thought worthy of
wonder, from the arguments concerning the resurrection; for this too is one of
the not cheap

difficulties, of those stirred among the stone-hearted.
15. For if, he says, those who have died must rise whole, how, if it should
happen that a man die in the sea, then, having been eaten by fishes, should in
turn be eaten by other men through the fishes in between, how would he take
back the flesh that has been consumed into other men? For either this one must
rise without the flesh which the other men ate through the fishes in between,
as has often been said, or those, being demanded the members of his own flesh,
must put them off and become incomplete, in order to fill up what is lacking
of those unjustly eaten by them. And these things the stone-hearted say as if
laughing. But I ask to learn, apart from difficulty, a constructive argument
of the resurrection, one that from necessity demonstrates that there must be a
resurrection through certain demonstrative and true arguments. For when the
demonstrations are strong with the truths, I will, you well know, make no
account of the difficulties; for it is also absurd and contentious to wish to
quarrel against a strong demonstration.
Christian answers to the aforesaid questions from the piety of natural
reasonings.
These arguments are not rational difficulties, but inartistic questions. For
the argument that constructs rational difficulties gathers the absurd in the
things said as following somehow; for example, man alone is able to laugh,
every thing able to laugh is an animal, therefore man alone is an animal;
which are following premises, but the conclusion is absurd. But in the
arguments set forth nothing absurd has been gathered by consequence; therefore
they were not reasonably called difficulties.
Question. From where is it clear whether there is something incorporeal?
Answer. If it is impossible for the same both to be ignorant of and to know
the same things (for in every case either the affirmation or the negation must
be true), how does the one who is ignorant whether there is an incorporeal
know both will and judgment, both thought and examination, both mind and
reason, which are activities of the incorporeal? For to know the activities of
the incorporeal while being in ignorance of the existence of the incorporeal
belongs to those who do not know what they say.
Another. To construct a difficulty and to solve a difficulty, this is not of
sense, nor is it of body. And what is not of body, this must of necessity be
of the incorporeal.
Another. From where do we seek the manifestation of the existence of the
incorporeal? For if seeking concerning something, whether it is or is not,
belongs to reason, and reason is an activity of the rational, and body by
itself is irrational, it is clear that there is something incorporeal, to
which seeking and judging belong as its own.
Another. To the one who does not confess that there is something incorporeal,
it is unfitting to seek and judge by reason whether there is something
incorporeal;

for it is irrational to use the activities of the incorporeal, but to be
ignorant of the existence of the incorporeal.
Another. There are two powers in us that grasp things, both sense and
intellection; whose activities differ from one another by so much as the
things grasped by the one cannot be grasped by the other. But since all powers
are powers of substances, for this reason there must of necessity be two
substances, of which it is proper to the one to sense, and to the other to
think. But if this is so, there is then some incorporeal substance, to which
thinking belongs as its own, just as sensing belongs to the body.
Another. If there is something ensouled, there must of necessity be the soul;
for by participation in the soul the ensouled is ensouled. If knowledge is not
by itself, but in the soul, there must then of necessity be the soul by
itself, in which the knowledge exists.
Another. If the body needs the soul for living and sensing, but the soul does
not need the body, either for living or for thinking, the soul is then
something other besides the body.
Another. If the soul commands the body, and the body serves the commands of
the soul, the soul is then something other besides the body.
Another. If the soul holds the place of a craftsman, and the body that of an
instrument, and the instruments are not the same as the craftsman, the body is
then not the same as the soul.
Another. If reason is first and later that which is according to reason (for
reason is in the soul, and that which is according to reason is in the
matter), matter is then something other besides the soul. And what is other
than matter, this is also other than body.
Another. If what is not body is not even substance, how is substance divided
into body and incorporeal, when that which is divided must of necessity exist
in the things into which it is divided?
Question. If there is something incorporeal, from where is it clear that it is
able to be by itself? Answer. That by whose presence the body by itself
becomes participant of life and of sense and of thought, and by whose absence
again becomes dead and without sense and without thought, this must of
necessity be by itself, as being incorruptible by its own nature.
Another. To construct scientifically the rational difficulties, and likewise
to solve them scientifically, is not a work of sense; for it has been agreed
that sense cannot touch knowledge of substance and of truth. And if this is
so, and it belongs to knowledge to touch knowledge of substance and of truth,
but it does not belong to sense, and what is not of sense is not of body
either, there must then of necessity be something incorporeal by itself, in
which the knowledge exists.
Another. If substance must be by itself, and substance is divided

according to the first division into body and incorporeal, how is the
incorporeal not a substance existing by itself?
Another. That which is able to separate itself from being affected along with
a body and not be corrupted is able also to be separated from the body and not
be corrupted; therefore it is better and more honorable than the body, as
being the cause of the body's being beautiful by its own presence. Such we say
the rational soul is, being an intellectual spirit, vital and cognitive and
self-moved, of which we say both the angels and the demons are consubstantial.
And, to speak generally, every thing that is in a substance, which is able to
be held by something, is a body to that which holds it. And we say that the
divine is incorporeal, not because it is incorporeal (for God is beyond, by
his own substance, both the body and likewise the incorporeal, as being
creator of each of these; for God did not make the things which he himself
is), but, just as we are accustomed always to honor the divine among the more
honorable material things among us, so also among the names, not as if God
needed these, but as we show our conception concerning him by them. In this
way then we name him incorporeal, although knowing that he exists beyond the
incorporeal, as creator of it. And let this too be set down altogether in the
argument as being indicative that there is something incorporeal. And if there
were nothing incorporeal among the things that are, there would not even be
body. But since there is something incorporeal, just as there is something
that is body, for this reason it is necessary to distinguish things contrary
to one another by contrary names. For we also call God uncreated,
distinguishing him from created things by the name of the uncreated. And if
the created things were not, God would not be distinguished from them by the
name of the uncreated. According to this distinction then that comes about by
the name of the uncreated, God is shown to exist beyond both bodies and
incorporeals. For if the created name contains all together both the bodies
and the incorporeals, it is clear that the one distinguished from created
things by this name is of necessity distinguished also from both bodies and
incorporeals. And what belongs to the other incorporeals, not being held by
bodies, this belongs also to God, not as to the incorporeal but as to the one
beyond the incorporeal. And since not having a creator and master is more
honorable than having a creator and master, for this reason we call God
uncreated. For what exists uncreated can have neither creator nor master.
Likewise, since not being held by something is more honorable than being held,
for this reason we call him incorporeal.
Question. From where is it clear whether there is a God at all? Answer. From
the constitution and continuance of the things that are. For the things that
are would not be, if God were not pre-existing them, who substantiated all the
parts of creation usefully toward the benefit of the whole creation,

who has been made known to us through prophecy and teaching of prophets and of
our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ and of his apostles by words witnessed by
divine powers.
Question. Whether God makes, or made, or is going to make? And if he makes,
with counsel or without counsel? Answer. God made and makes and will make
through his own will. For he made the creation that was not formerly, himself
having willed it, which by his own providence he keeps in being, which is "he
makes," which he is also going to re-create and bring into the better
condition through the re-creation, which is "he will make," in order to purify
it from every absurdity that has happened to it from the indolence of the
rational beings, not having found the better later from judgment and
examination, but from above and before the making of the world this had been
resolved by him to do. For it is not possible for there to be added later to
God, either toward knowledge or toward power, what he did not have formerly.
And a proof that God makes the world by will is this: that, although God was
able to make more suns, he did not make more, but made one only; for the one
who is not able to make more suns is not able to make even one, and the one
who is able to make the one sun is of necessity able to make more also. How
then did God not make more suns, which he was able to make, unless indeed he
did not will to make more suns? And if the suns which he did not make he did
not make by will, it is then clear that the one which he made he also made by
will, and just as the sun, so also all the remaining parts of creation, both
the incorruptible and the corruptible, having both their being and their being
of such a kind from the will of God.
Question. Whether the incorporeal is better than the body? Answer. If the body
is made alive and becomes capable of thought, not by the presence of another
body but by that of the incorporeal, how is the incorporeal not better than
the body?
Another. If every bodily substance is from matter and form, and the form does
not come to the matter from the matter but from elsewhere, how is the
incorporeal not better than the matter, from which the form comes into the
matter?
Another. If it is not possible for the equal to be known when the unequal is
unknown, how is it possible for the body to be known when the incorporeal is
unknown?
Another. If all bodily things exist in body after the manner of an image, but
the soul rationally, how is the soul not incorporeal?
Another. If it is possible for the soul to act according to mind, and the soul
acts according to mind when it separates itself from all sense,

how is the soul not by itself?
Question. In what does God differ from soul? Answer. By that by which being
creator and master differs from having a creator and master, and by that by
which that which is above being differs from being.
Question. If God makes with counsel, using an instrument or without an
instrument? Answer. God is in need of nothing outside his own nature; and just
as he counsels without an instrument, so also he makes without an instrument.
For if at the same time as he wills something to come to be, it is
substantiated of the one who has willed, how is the use of instruments of the
things that come to be not superfluous?
Question. If God makes without counsel, whether he makes irrationally without
thought and examination, or with some judgment? And if with judgment, from
lack of what does he need judgment? But if without judgment, by what reason?
Answer. If his works are known to God, and the known things are known by
reason, how does God not know by reason the things he makes? But to make
something with thought and examination and judgment is proper to the craftsmen
among us, who by these arrive at a better end of the things that come to be,
but God, as neither taking on knowledge nor casting off knowledge, needs none
of these.
Question. If what is generated at all can be incorruptible, by what reason?
Answer. Those whose incorruptibility depends on the will of another cannot be
incorruptible without being generated; therefore we say that God alone has
immortality, because these things belong to him from his own nature and not
from the will of another.
Another. If God is above the incorruptibles as maker of them, but is not above
the ungenerated (for he is not creator of the ungenerated things), the
ungenerated does not then follow upon the incorruptible.
Another. If God is incorruptible by his own nature, and the soul also is
incorruptible by the will of the creator, how, in so great a difference of
incorruptibility, does the ungenerated follow, according to you, upon the
incorruptible?
Another. If then something contributes toward the constitution of the
corruptible things, how are the incorruptibles not ministerial of the
perishable things, the cause of their incorruptibility leading them to such a
ministry? But if this is so, it is clear that one can have incorruptibility

through some useful cause, but ungeneratedness no longer; for the ungenerated
must exist ungenerated without any useful cause.
Another. If the first body is the element, and the second body is that from
elements, and the body from elements cannot be ungenerated (for nothing
ungenerated can be later than a generated thing), how, if the bodies from
elements are incorruptible, will they not be generated incorruptibles?
Another. If all the elements are generated, how is it not nonsense to say that
the things from elements are ungenerated? For if matter by itself is neither
element nor substance, but having taken on quality and quantity becomes
element and substance, how can the things completed from such elements be also
ungenerated, if it should happen that they are incorruptible?
Another. If the body is one thing and the incorruptible body another and the
corruptible body another, and according to the difference of incorruptibility
and corruption the body is divided into incorruptible and corruptible body,
how is it not necessary that the generated or the ungenerated be proper to the
body that is divided? And if neither of these is proper to the body that is
divided, how will the body that is divided be neither generated nor
ungenerated? And if this is impossible, that the body that is divided be
neither, it is then necessary to set down that the body that is divided is
this, the incorruptible body and the corruptible, into which the body that is
divided is divided.
Question. If what comes to be by God is corrupted, by whose vice is it
corrupted, that of the maker, or of what has come to be, or of something from
outside becoming contrary to the maker? Answer. The things that are corrupted
are corrupted neither from the vice of the one who has made them nor from that
of the contrary, but by the decree of the creator, who works corruption in the
particulars, and incorruptibility in the universals. For if the things that
are corrupted were corrupted from the vice of some one of those mentioned, the
whole genus would be corrupted, both the particular and the universal; for if
we grant that the corruption is from some vice, the genus of the things that
are corrupted would not have been preserved incorruptible among the things
that come to be, the vice mastering the universal just as the particular. And
if it is impossible for the universal to be corrupted by someone's vice,
neither again is the particular corrupted by someone's vice, but by the decree
of God. For if there is not among the things that are some vice able to hinder
generation, neither then is there among the things that are some vice that
corrupts the genus.
Answers concerning resurrection toward the aforesaid questions concerning it.
1. For a man to be or to become fish-eaten and the fish man-eaten neither
dissolves the man into a fish nor the fish into a man, but the dissolution of
each

comes to be into the elements from which they were composed at the beginning.
Even if the manner of the dissolution of the parts comes about through being
eaten by one another, nevertheless of every dissolution that comes about in
whatever manner the end is that the things dissolved go into the elements. One
must not then, because of the eating of man and fish by one another, construct
a sophistical difficulty destructive of resurrection, but look to the power of
God, who not only promises to make the resurrection of the dead, but has also,
through the resurrection of our Savior Christ that has already begun,
furnished us with firm faith in it. But if the elements underlie the creation
of the things that come to be and the re-creation of the things that have been
corrupted, how, if one thinks the re-creation of the things that have been
corrupted to be unbelievable and impossible, will not also the creation of the
things that come to be at the beginning be unbelievable and impossible? Which
is absurd.
2. The Greeks believe neither the creation of the world nor its re-creation.
But if they speak truly, the first would become clear thus. For they say that
time is eternal. But if time is eternal, the parts of time must then of
necessity also be eternal, that is both the hours and the days, both the
months and the year. And if these are not eternal, because there are in them
some things earlier and some later, neither then can the world, being in time,
be ungenerated.
3. One must disbelieve the resurrection of the dead for two things, either
because God is not able to raise the dead, or because it is not beneficial for
those who rise to be incorruptible. And of these the one is impious, and the
other ridiculous. But if this is so, how is the argument of those who
disbelieve the resurrection of the dead not composed of impiety and laughter?
4. The things God makes by his own working, these he makes by command; and
contemporaneous with the command of God is the existence of the things made by
him. "You thought," he says, "and it is present." But if this has been said
fittingly concerning God, how does the one who disbelieves the resurrection
not also disbelieve that God has such power? For those who rise from the dead
need nothing else except the command of God.
5. If it is true that "God found a way for the unexpected," as indeed it is
also true, how does the one who disbelieves the resurrection of the dead not
make the argument false?
. If, when the re-creation comes about, of necessity all the discord among men
concerning the creator of the world according to his works is taken away, how
will the re-creation of the world not be better than the creation of the
world? For through the re-creation the creation also is beautiful. And if that
is disbelieved as impossible, how will the creation of the world not be most
evil?
7. One thing is the altogether impossible, and another the impossible for
someone. The altogether impossible is, as for the diagonal to be commensurate
with the side; and the impossible for someone is, as for nature to make an
animal without seed. To which of these impossibles do those who disbelieve it
subject

the resurrection? But if to the first, the argument is false; for the diagonal
does not become incommensurate with the side according to re-creation, and
those who rise rise according to re-creation. And if to the impossible for
someone, yet to God all things are possible that are impossible for someone.
8. If in the present life the pious differs from the impious by hope alone,
how does the one who takes away the hope of the things to come not take away
the difference of pious and impious?
9. If without the particular it is impossible for the universal to exist, how,
if the particular is created, will the universal not also be created? But if
this is so, how is it not false that man and the remaining parts of creation
are co-eternal with God? For example, if Plato is created, how is man not
created?
10. How have the Greeks, having supposed that the world is eternal, dogmatized
the things that follow of necessity from the supposition? But if according to
them the world were eternal, they would reasonably say that there will not be
a re-creation of the world; for the re-creation belongs to the created world
and not to the uncreated. And that the world is not eternal is clear from
man's not being co-eternal with God. For if the universal is co-eternal with
God, of necessity the particular also; and if not the particular, neither the
universal.
11. To transfer all men at once through the re-creation into being
incorruptible is much more divine in magnitude than to bring them into this
mortal condition in which we now are. And he kept for us the fitting and more
divine for later, as the end of our subsistence lying in it. Of which if we
had been deprived according to those who disbelieve the resurrection, how much
more would it have been better not to be than for us to be? For everything
deprived of its proper end is useless.
12. If it is impossible for someone to prefer death to his own life for the
sake of a thing that is not, how then, the doctrine concerning the
resurrection not being true according to you, did the martyrs prefer their own
death to their own life for the sake of this? And of what other religion was
the doctrine confirmed by manifold tortures and deaths, or of their own
religion, I mean of the Christians concerning the resurrection of the dead?
13. If the things the Greeks do not believe neither are nor will be, then the
existence of the things that are lies in the knowledge of the Greeks. How then
in most of the most sovereign doctrines are they shown fighting both with
themselves and with one another? For such a fight does not allow the existence
of the things that are to lie in the knowledge of the Greeks.
14. If they disbelieve the tradition of those who saw the pioneer of the
resurrection risen from the dead into immortal life, they are too weak to
dissolve the doctrine of the resurrection by an insoluble demonstration.

For for a man to become fish-eaten has a difficulty, but not a demonstration.
For it is impossible for the same thing also to fall under divinity as having
come to be; and when the thing has been demonstrated to the substance by
power, no syllogism can make the overturning.
15. The sun, when it is above the earth, is not under the earth, and when it
is under the earth, is not above the earth. How then is this eternal according
to you, not having its being above the earth or under the earth eternally? And
if the Greeks take as eternal that which is not eternal, how will they be
trustworthy lawgivers of there not being a resurrection of the dead?
. That which is at one time above and at another time below belongs to the
things that at some time are neither above nor below. How then is the sun
eternal, which before being above or below was not? How then is it not absurd
to believe these, who do not understand that the sun is generated according to
its own nature, concerning the dead not rising, of whom the pioneer of the
resurrection has already become firstborn of them from the resurrection?
17. When the power of the maker is strong, then the matter also is suitable
toward the making of what comes to be. But if some dead man is suitable to God
toward raising, of necessity every one also; and if not every one, no one. And
one must not measure the works of God by our own thoughts; for the works of
God are above mind and sense and reason, among which is also that the
fish-eaten dead man must of necessity come into being again, even if it
happened that he was corrupted by someone.
18. How is it true that the Lord is lord of living and dead, according to you
not having a power that transforms the one into the other?
19. If God is maker of the worse things, but is not of the better, how is this
not a proof of the weakness of God? Which it is impious to say. And if the
power of God is equal toward making of each, why do you disbelieve the
resurrection of the dead, who rise into incorruptibility?
20. If for God it is not impossible toward making, and for us it is beneficial
toward coming to be, that he bring us from the much-groaning and corruptible
life into a blessed and incorruptible condition through the resurrection,
rather than to bring the philosophizing Greeks up from the labor-loving of the
philosophers into the labor-loving of ants through transmigration of souls,
what is more ridiculous than to disbelieve that which has been witnessed, but
to believe and dogmatize this without foundation, so that Plato the
philosopher who once atticized later ant-izes?
21. If according to the Greeks God makes men by being and not by willing, how
is Plato at one time a rational animal and man, and at another time an
irrational animal and an ant through transmigration of souls? If it follows
upon God that Plato come to be a man, of necessity, when Plato is transferred
from

being a man into coming to be an ant, God also is transferred from being what
he is. And if Plato is transferred, but God is not transferred from being what
he is, how does God make men by being and not by willing?
22. If the work of God is not corrupted, but man is corrupted, man is then not
a work of God according to those who say this.
23. If our genus is brought into being through generation, and is brought out
of being through corruption, how is it possible for God to be cause of being
and of not being, not by willing but by his being?
24. If the things from the elements are again elements, what absurd or
impossible appears in the things from the elements being again?
25. If God is not under the decree, but is master of every decree, how does
God master men's coming to be and being corrupted, but does not master our
coming to be and not being corrupted?
. If God, re-creating us into incorruptibility, does this from repentance
according to you, how did he not rather from repentance grant us to be always?
27. If the gifts of God are without repentance, and he granted us that we be,
how does God not fulfill the purpose concerning us from the beginning,
re-creating us into incorruptibility?
28. According to those who believe the resurrection of the dead we both die
and are made alive again by the just judgment of God, the former by the
disobedience of the man, the latter by the obedience of the man. Among those
then to whom the cause of death is unknown, among these the creator of the
nature of man is also unknown. And they being ignorant of the cause of death
and of the creator of the nature of man, how do they not say from ignorance
that there is no resurrection of the dead?
29. One thing is the altogether not-being, and another the not-being of such a
kind; for example, one thing is not to have become a man at the beginning, and
another, after he has become, to be corrupted. And since there is not for God
a work more laborious than a work, how is it not irrational to believe God
concerning the making of the things that altogether are not, but to disbelieve
him concerning the transformation of the things that have been corrupted?
30. If, just as before the man came to be the man was potentially in the
elements from which he has come to be, so also after he has been corrupted he
is potentially in the elements, how do the Greeks disbelieve God, who has
promised to raise the dead, as upon an impossible thing, the man being
potentially in the elements, just as when he was created of old, so also now
when he is re-created?
31. If God was not able from the beginning to make men incorruptible, it is
clear that he is not able now either. And if he both had from the beginning
power to make us incorruptible and has not

cast this off, how is it not irrational to disbelieve God as upon an
impossible thing, who has promised, according to the likeness of the one
raised by him from the dead into incorruptible life, to make the remaining men
also incorruptible?
32. The animal is potentially in the seed by nature, the bed is potentially in
the wood by art, those who rise are potentially in the elements by God. But if
this is so, how is it not absurd to believe nature for the natural things, and
art for the artificial things, but to disbelieve God for the divine things?
33. To the divine authority nothing is disobedient. If the sea and the earth
of old gave to the divine command what they did not receive, how will they not
now rather give what they received, when God has commanded?
34. If, just as nature cannot make the animal from the corrupted seed and art
cannot make the bed from the corrupted wood, so neither can God make an
incorruptible man from the corrupted man, the power of God will then be
measured, just as that of nature and of art. And if it is absurd to say this,
it is then also absurd to disbelieve God making an incorruptible man from the
corrupted man.
35. If, just as it appears impossible to unbelieving men for the resurrection
to come to be, so it is impossible for God to make the resurrection, God then
differs in nothing according to this from man. And if he differs infinitely
many times, as indeed he also differs, how is it not absurd to disbelieve God
the making of the things of which he has the power to make?
. Of the things of which God is strong for the making above nature, how is it
not absurd to disbelieve him the transformation above nature of these?
37. If of that which does not come to be according to nature one must take the
making as unbelievable and impossible, how will the making of the first man
who came to be not also be unbelievable and impossible, he not having come to
be according to nature? For it is impossible for a man to come to be from a
man according to nature, unless first the man comes to be above nature.
38. If I do not come to be again what I was, how do I receive back the
recompenses of virtue or of vice, which I did not obtain in the present? For
if the resurrection of the dead does not come to be, how will those who enact
the martyr contests and those who endure not be equal to one another? And if
this is unjust, how is it not unjust that the resurrection of the dead not
come to be, in which alone it is possible for the distinction of those who
have done the unjust things and of those who have endured to come to be,
according to the difference of honor and of punishment?
39. If that which the man was also is, how, having died, did he go forward
from the knowledge of God? That he be again what he was has not departed. For
the works of God are known to him and possible; and if they are not possible
to him, they are not known to him either.

40. If it is good for us to be mortal in the present, and better for us to be
immortal in the future, how is it not absurd to say that God is able toward
making of the good, but unable toward making of the better?
41. If it is impossible for God to make the mortal body immortal, how will
death not be stronger than God, he not being able to deliver from it those
subject to it?
42. If God has a life-making power of the dead, how are those who rise not
suitable toward resurrection? And God being able toward raising the dead, and
those who rise being suitable toward rising, what place does the unbelief of
those who disbelieve the resurrection have?
43. If God masters our being and our not being, how will our becoming immortal
not be from the same mastery of his? And if our becoming immortal has been
established outside the mastery of God, how will he not be a God not mastering
our not being?
44. If the nature that the immortals and the mortals have, they have from the
will of God, how is it not possible for the dead to be made immortal, God
having willed it?
45. Of those things whose opposing things are weak, the constitution is firm.
But if the Greeks, having used every manner of opposition toward destruction
of the doctrine of the resurrection, were not strong to destroy this, the
doctrine being kept unshaken in the manifold oppositions by the power of God
who makes the resurrection, according to what is the resurrection of the dead
unbelievable, the doctrine concerning it being shaken neither by hands nor by
tongue?
. If the greatest proof of the unconquered and unbounded power of God is to
know of all men, both living and dead, both the forms and the deeds, both good
and base, how is the resurrection of the dead not a demonstration of the
unbounded power and knowledge of God, through which he restores men into being
again? But if this is so, how will the one who deprives the dead of the
resurrection not be depriving God of such a demonstration?
47. If the Greeks, having hoped to destroy through their own opposition the
doctrine concerning the resurrection, failed of the hope, how will they not
again fail of the hope, hoping again that there will not be a resurrection of
the dead?
48. The Greeks, having fought against the doctrine concerning the
resurrection, ought either not to be defeated in the fight or, having been
defeated, not to disbelieve the resurrection; and if not, their first vain
labor will be a refutation of their irrational unbelief.
