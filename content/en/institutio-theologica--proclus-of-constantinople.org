OF PROCLUS THE SUCCESSOR, ELEMENTS OF THEOLOGY. 1 Every multitude participates
the One in some way. For if it should participate in no way, neither the whole
will be one nor each of the many from which the multitude is, but each of
those too will be a multitude, and this to infinity, and each of these
infinites will again be an infinite multitude. For that which participates no
one in any way, neither according to its whole self nor according to each of
the things in it, will be infinite in every way and in every respect. For each
of the many, whichever you take, will be either one or not-one; and if
not-one, either many or nothing. But if each is nothing, that which is from
them is also nothing; and if many, each is from infinitely infinite things.
And these are impossible. For neither is any of the beings from infinitely
infinite things (for there is nothing more than the infinite, but that which
is from all of each is more) nor is it possible for anything to be composed
from nothing. Therefore every multitude participates the One in some way. 2
Everything that participates the One is both one and not-one. For if it is not
the One itself (for it participates the One, being something else besides the
One), it has suffered the One according to participation and has undergone
becoming one. If then there is nothing besides the One, it is only one; and it
will not participate the One, but will be the One itself. But if there is
something besides that, which is not one, [the participant of the One is both
not-one and one, not the very One but a being that is one, as participating
the One] by this, then, it is not-one, nor the very One; but being one at once
and participating the One, and for this reason not being one in itself, it is
one and not-one, being something else besides the One; by that in which it has
exceeded, not-one; by that which it has suffered, one. Therefore everything
that participates the One is both one and not-one. 3 Everything that becomes
one becomes one by participation of the One. For it itself is not one, but
insofar as it has suffered the participation of the One, it is one. For if
things that are not one in themselves should become one, by coming together,
surely, and communicating with one another they become one, and they undergo
the presence of the One, not being the very One. Therefore they participate
the One in this respect, in which they suffer becoming one. For if it is
already one, it does not become one; for that which is does not become what it
already is. But if it becomes from the not-one formerly, it will have the One
when some one has come to be in them. 4 Everything unified is other than the
One itself. For if it is unified, it would participate the One in some way, in
that respect in which it is also called unified; and that which participates
the One is both one and not-one. But the One itself is not both one and
not-one. For if this too were both one and not-one, the one in it again would
have the both-together, and this to infinity, there being no One itself at
which it is possible to stop, but every one being also not-one. Therefore the
unified is something other than the One. For if it were the same as the
unified, the One will be an infinite multitude, and each of those from which
the unified is, likewise. 5 Every multitude is secondary to the One. For if
there is multitude before the One, the One will participate multitude, and the
multitude before the One will not participate the One, if indeed, before it
becomes one, that is multitude; for it does not participate what is not; and
because that which participates the One is at once both one and not-one, and
one has not yet come to subsist, the first multitude existing. But it is
impossible for there to be a multitude that participates the One in no way.
Therefore multitude is not before the One. And if it is together with the One,
and they are coordinate with one another by nature (for nothing prevents this
in time), neither is the One in itself many, nor is the multitude one, as
being divided against one another, existing together by nature, if neither is
prior or posterior to the other. The multitude then in itself will not be one,
and each of the things in it not-one, and this to infinity; which is
impossible. Therefore it participates the One according to its own nature, and
it will not be possible to take anything of it that is not one; for if it were
not one, it will be infinite from infinites, as has been shown. Therefore it
participates the One in every way. If then the One, being one in itself,
participates multitude in no way, the multitude will be in every way posterior
to the One, participating the One, but not participated by the One. And if the
One also participates multitude, according to existence


as subsisting as one, but according to participation not-one, the One will
have been multiplied, just as the multitude is unified through the One.
Therefore the One has communicated with multitude and the multitude with the
One; and things that come together and communicate with one another in some
way, if they are brought together by another, that is before them, but if they
themselves bring themselves together, they are not opposed to one another; for
opposites do not hasten toward one another. If then the One and the multitude
are divided against one another, and the multitude as multitude is not-one,
and the One as one is not multitude, neither coming to be in the other, it
will be one and two at once. But if there is to be something before them that
brings them together, it is either one or not-one. But if not-one, either many
or nothing. But neither many, lest there be multitude before one; nor nothing;
for how will nothing bring together? Therefore one only; for this One too is
not many, lest it go to infinity. Therefore there is the One itself; and every
multitude is from the One itself. 6 Every multitude is either from unified
things or from henads. For that each of the many will not itself also be
multitude only, and each of this again multitude, is clear. And if it is not
multitude only, it is either unified or a henad. And if participating the One,
unified; but if from those of which the primarily unified is, a henad. For if
there is the One itself, there is that which primarily participates it and is
primarily unified. And this is from henads; for if from unified things, again
the unified things are from some things, and to infinity. There must then be
the primarily unified from henads; and we have found what was from the
beginning. 7 Everything productive of another is better than the nature of the
thing produced. For it is either better or worse or equal. Let it first be
equal. Then that which is produced from this either itself also has a power
productive of something else, or it is altogether barren. But if it should be
barren, in this very respect it has been diminished from the producer, and is
unequal to that, which is fertile and has the power of making, while it is
without force. And if it itself is also productive of others, either it too
produces equal to itself, and this likewise in all cases, and all the beings
will be equal to one another and nothing better than another, the producer
always bringing to subsistence the next equal to itself; or unequal, and it
would no longer be equal to that which produces it; for it belongs to equal
powers to make equal things; but the things from these are unequal to one
another, if the producer is equal to that before it, but that after it is
unequal to it. Therefore the produced must not be equal to the producer. But
neither will the producer ever be less. For if it gives substance to the
produced, it also furnishes power to it according to the substance. And if it
itself is productive of all the power for that after it, it would also be able
to make itself such as that is. And if this, it would also make itself more
powerful. For neither does not being able prevent, the productive power being
present; nor not wishing, for all things by nature reach after the good; so
that if it is able to work another more complete, it would also complete
itself before that after it. Therefore the produced is neither equal to the
producer nor better. Therefore in every way the producer is better than the
nature of the produced. 8 Of all things that in any way participate the good,
the primarily good leads, that which is nothing else than good. For if all
beings desire the good, it is clear that the primarily good is beyond the
beings. For if it is the same as some one of the beings, either being and the
good are the same, and this being would no longer be desiring the good, being
itself the good; for that which reaches after something is in need of that
after which it reaches, and [other than and] estranged from the object of
appetite; or the one is other, the other other; and the one will participate,
being, and the other will be participated in this, the good. It is therefore a
particular good, being in some one of the participants, and that which the
participant alone desires, but not the good simply and that which all beings
desire. For this is the common object of desire of all beings; but that which
has come to be in some one is of that participant alone. Therefore the
primarily good is nothing else than good. For if you add anything else, you
have diminished the good by the addition, making a particular good instead of
the good simply; for that which was added, not being the good but less than
that, has diminished the good by its own companionship. 9 Everything
self-sufficient either according to substance or according to


activity is better than that which is not self-sufficient but is hung upon
another substance as the cause of its completeness. For if all beings by
nature reach after the good, and one is furnishing the well to itself, while
the other is in need of another, and one has the cause of the good present,
while the other has it separate, then by so much as this is nearer to that
which furnishes the object of appetite, by so much would it be better than
that which is in need of the separated cause and receives from elsewhere the
completeness of existence or of activity. Since then [that it is both like and
diminished] the self-sufficient is also more like the good itself and
diminished by participating the good and not being itself the good primarily,
it is in some way akin to that, insofar as it is able to have the good from
itself; but that which participates and participates through another stands
more greatly apart from the primarily good and that which is nothing else than
good. 10 Everything self-sufficient is inferior to the good simply. For what
else is the self-sufficient than that which has acquired the good from itself
and in itself? And this is already full of the good and participating, but not
itself the good simply. For that is better than both participating and being
full, as has been shown. If then the self-sufficient has filled itself with
the good, that from which it has filled itself would be better than the
self-sufficient and above self-sufficiency. And the good simply is neither in
need of anything. For it does not desire another (for it would be lacking in
good according to the desire); nor self-sufficient; for it would be full of
good, and not the good primarily. 11 All beings proceed from one cause, the
first. For either there is a cause of none of the beings, or the causes go in
a circle, all things being limited, or the ascent is to infinity and one is
cause of another and nowhere will the pre-subsistence of the cause come to a
stand. But if there should be a cause of none of the beings, there will be
neither an order of second and first things, of those completing and those
completed, of those ordering and those ordered, of those generating and those
generated, of those making and those undergoing; nor science of any of the
beings. For the knowledge of causes is the work of science, and we say we know
scientifically when we have come to know the causes of the beings. And if the
causes go around in a circle, the same things will be prior and posterior,
both more powerful and weaker; for everything productive is better than the
nature of the produced. And it makes no difference whether one joins the cause
to the caused through more or through fewer intermediates and makes from that;
for it will be better than all the intermediates of which it is cause, and by
so much as the intermediates are more, by so much more greatly is it cause.
And if the addition of causes is to infinity, and always another before
another, again there will be science of nothing. For of infinites there is
knowledge of none; and if the causes are unknown, there will not even be
science of the things that follow. If then there must both be a cause of the
beings, and the causes are distinguished from the caused, and the ascent is
not to infinity, there is a first cause of the beings from which, as from a
root, each things proceed, some being near that, others farther; for that the
principle must be one has been shown, because every multitude has come to
subsist second after the One. 12 Of all beings the principle and first cause
is the good. For if all things proceed from one cause, one must call that
cause either the good or better than the good. But if that is better than the
good, does something also come from that into the beings and the nature of the
beings, or nothing? And if nothing, it is absurd; for we would no longer keep
it in the rank of a cause, it being necessary that something from the cause be
present everywhere to the caused, and especially from the first, on which all
things hang and through which each of the beings is. And if there is a sharing
of that too for the beings, just as also of the good, there will be something
better than goodness in the beings, having come from the first cause; for
surely, being better and above the good, it does not give to the second things
something inferior to what that after it gives. And what could become better
than goodness? Since even the better itself we say is that which has more
greatly partaken of good. If then that which is not good would not even be
called better, it is in every way second to the good. And if all beings also
desire the good, how is it still possible for something to be before this
cause? For whether they


desire that too, how do they desire the good most of all? Or if they do not
desire it, how do they not desire the cause of all things, having proceeded
from it? But if the good is that from which all beings hang, the good is
principle and first cause of all things. 13 Every good is unitive of the
things that participate it, and every unification is a good, and the good is
the same as the One. For if the good is preservative of all the beings (which
is why it is also an object of desire for all), and that which is preservative
and holding-together of the substance of each is the One (for by the One all
things are preserved, and scattering removes each from its substance), the
good, to whichever things it is present, works these into one and holds them
together according to the unification. And if the One is gathering and
holding-together of the beings, it completes each according to its own
presence. And therefore to be unified is a good for all. And if unification is
also a good in itself and the good is one-making, the good simply and the one
simply are the same, both unifying and making-good the beings at once. Whence
also the things that have in some way fallen from the good are at the same
time deprived of the participation of the One; and those that have become
without share of the One, being filled with division, are also deprived of the
good in the same way. Therefore goodness is also unification, and unification
goodness, and the good is one, and the One is primarily good. 14 Every being
is either unmoved or moved; and if moved, either by itself or by another; and
if by itself, it is self-moved; and if by another, other-moved. Therefore
everything is either unmoved or self-moved or other-moved. For it is
necessary, there being other-moved things, that there also be the unmoved, and
between these the self-moved. For if every other-moved thing is moved by
another that is moved, either the motions are in a circle or to infinity; but
neither in a circle nor to infinity, if indeed all beings are bounded by the
principle and that which moves is better than that which is moved. There will
therefore be something unmoved that is first mover. But if these things, it is
necessary that the self-moved also be. For if all things should stand still,
what ever will be that which is primarily moved? For neither the unmoved (for
it is not of such a nature) nor the other-moved (for it is moved by another);
it remains therefore that the self-moved is that which is primarily moved;
since this also is that which joins the other-moved things to the unmoved,
being in some way a mean, both moving and being moved at once; for of those,
the one only moves, the other is only moved. Therefore every being is either
unmoved or self-moved or other-moved. And from these this also is clear, that
of things moved the self-moved is first, and of things that move the unmoved.
15 Everything revertive toward itself is incorporeal. For none of the bodies
is of such a nature as to revert toward itself. For if that which reverts
toward something is joined to that toward which it reverts, it is clear that
all the parts of the body too will join to all, of that which has reverted
toward itself; for this was to revert toward itself, when both have become
one, that which has reverted and that toward which it has reverted. And this
is impossible in the case of body, and of all divided things generally; for
the divided does not join whole to whole with itself because of the separation
of the parts, different ones lying in different places. Therefore no body is
of such a nature as to revert toward itself, so as to have reverted whole
toward whole. If then anything is revertive toward itself, it is incorporeal
and without parts. 16 Everything revertive toward itself has a substance
separable from every body. For if it should be inseparable from any body
whatsoever, it will not have any activity separable from body. For it is
impossible, the substance being inseparable from bodies, that the activity
from the substance be separable; for thus the activity will be better than the
substance, if the one is in need of bodies, while the other is
self-sufficient, being of itself and not of bodies. If then something is
inseparable according to substance, it is likewise inseparable according to
activity, or even still more. And if this, it does not revert toward itself.
For that which reverts toward itself, being other than body, has an activity
separated from body and not through body nor with body, if indeed both the
activity and that toward which the activity is need nothing of the body.
Therefore that which reverts toward itself is in every way separable from
bodies. 17 Everything that moves itself primarily is revertive toward


itself, and the mover and the moved are one at once. For either it moves with
a part and is moved with a part, or it moves as a whole and is moved, or it
moves as a whole and is moved with a part, or the reverse. But if one part is
the mover, and another part the moved, it will not be self-moved in itself,
having come to subsist from things not self-moved, but seeming self-moved, yet
not being such according to substance. And if it moves as a whole, and a part
is moved, or the reverse, there will be some part in both that at once
according to one thing is moving and being moved, and this is the primarily
self-moved. And if one and the same moves and is moved, it will have the
activity of moving toward itself, being motive of itself. And toward that at
which it is active, toward this it has reverted. Therefore everything that
moves itself primarily is revertive toward itself. 18 Everything that
furnishes to others by being is itself primarily this, of which it gives a
share to those that are furnished. For if it gives by its very being and makes
the giving from its own substance, what it gives is inferior to its own
substance, and what it is, it is more greatly and more completely, if indeed
everything that brings something to subsistence is better than the nature of
that which is brought to subsistence. Therefore that which pre-exists in the
giver itself is better than what was given; and it is what that is, but not
the same as that; for it is primarily, and the other secondarily. For it is
necessary either that each be the same and there be one account of both, or
that there be nothing common nor the same in both, or that the one be
primarily, the other secondarily. But if the same account, no longer would the
one be cause, the other effect; nor the one in itself, the other in the
participant; nor the one making, the other becoming. And if it should have
nothing the same, the one would not by being bring the rest to subsistence,
communicating nothing toward the being of that. It remains therefore that the
one is primarily what it gives, and the other secondarily what the giver is,
in those cases in which by its very being the one is furnished from the other.
19 Everything that primarily inexists in some nature of the beings is present
to all things ranked according to that nature according to one account and in
the same way. For if not to all in the same way, but to some, and to others
not, it is clear that it was not primarily in that nature, but in some
primarily, and in others secondarily, those that participate at some time. For
that which exists at one time, and at another time not, does not exist
primarily nor in itself, but is adventitious and has come from elsewhere, to
whichever things it so exists. 20 Beyond all bodies is the substance of soul,
and beyond all souls the intellective nature, and beyond all the intellective
subsistences the One. For every body is movable by another, and is not of such
a nature as to move itself, but by a sharing of soul it is moved from itself,
and lives through soul; and when soul is present it is in some way self-moved,
and when absent other-moved, as having this nature in itself, and as soul
having been allotted the self-moved substance. For to whatever it comes, it
gives a share of self-motion; and that of which it gives a share by its very
being, this it itself is far more formerly. Therefore it is beyond bodies, as
self-moved according to substance, of those that become self-moved according
to participation. And again soul, being moved by itself, has a second rank
from the unmoved nature and that which subsists unmoved according to activity;
because of all things moved the self-moved leads, and of all things that move
the unmoved. If then soul, being moved by itself, moves the others, there must
be before it that which moves unmovedly. And intellect moves, being unmoved
and always active according to the same things. For soul too through intellect
participates always thinking, just as body through soul participates moving
itself. For if always thinking were primarily in soul, it would belong to all
souls, just as also moving itself. Therefore this does not belong to soul
primarily; there must therefore be before it that which is primarily
intellective; therefore before souls is intellect. But also before intellect
the One. For intellect, even if unmoved, is yet not one; for it thinks itself
and is active about itself. And of the One all things that in any way are
participate, but of intellect not all; for to whichever a sharing of intellect
is present, these must participate knowledge, because intellective knowledge
is principle and first cause of knowing. Therefore the One is beyond
intellect. And no longer is there another beyond the One. For one and the good
are the same; therefore principle of all things, as has been shown. 21 Every
order


beginning from a monad proceeds into a multitude coordinate with the monad,
and the multitude of every order is led up to one monad. For the monad, having
the account of a principle, generates the multitude proper to itself; which is
why there is also one series and one order, which as a whole has from the
monad the descent into the multitude; for it is no longer an order nor a
series, if the monad remains barren in itself. And the multitude is led up
again to one, the common cause of all the things of like rank. For the same in
all the multitude did not have its procession from one of the things in the
multitude; for that which is from one only of the many is not common to all,
but exclusive of that one's property alone. Since then according to each order
there is some both communion and continuity and sameness, through which these
are called of like rank, and those of other rank, it is clear that the same
has come from one principle to the whole order. There is therefore one monad
before the multitude according to each order and chain, furnishing the one
account to the things ranked in it both toward one another and toward the
whole. For let one of the things under the same series be cause of another;
but that which is cause of the series as one must be before all, and from it
all must be generated as of like rank, not as each being this particular thing
but as belonging to this order. And from these it is clear that both the one
and the multitude belong to the nature of body, and the one nature has the
many natures hung together with it and the many natures are from one, the
nature of the whole, and it is present to the order of souls both to begin
from one soul, the first, and to descend into a multitude of souls and to lead
the multitude up to the one, and to the intellective substance there is both
an intellective monad and a multitude of intellects that has proceeded from
one intellect and reverts toward that, and to the One before all things the
multitude of henads and to the henads the stretching-up toward the One. After
the first One, then, henads, and after the first intellect intellects, and
after the first soul souls; and after the whole nature the many natures. 22
Everything that is primarily and as a principle according to each order is
one, and neither two nor more than two, but every such thing is unique in
kind. For let there be, if possible, two; for the same is impossible also if
there are more. Either then each of these is what it is called primarily, or
that from both. But if that from both, the first things would again be one and
not two. And if each, either the one from the other, and neither is first; or
both equally. But if equally, neither will still be primarily. For if the one
is primarily, and this is not the same as the other, what will that of that
order be? For that which is nothing else than what it is called, this is
primarily; but each of these, being other, both is and is not what it is
called. If then these differ from one another, but they do not differ insofar
as they are what they are called primarily (for this they have suffered as the
same primarily), both will not be primarily, but that, of which both having
participated are said to be primarily. And from these it is clear that the
primarily being is one only, but not two or more the primarily beings; and the
first intellect is one only, but not two the first intellects; and the first
soul is one; and in the case of each of the forms, as the primarily beautiful,
the primarily equal, and in all similarly; and thus also the form of animal is
one, the first, and that of man; for the demonstration is the same. 23
Everything unparticipated brings to subsistence from itself the participated
things, and all the participated subsistences stretch up to unparticipated
existences. For the unparticipated, having the account of a monad as being of
itself and not of another and as exempt from the participants, generates the
things that are able to be participated. For either it will stand barren in
itself, and would have nothing of worth; or it will give something from
itself, and that which received has participated, and that which was given has
come to subsist as participated. And every participated thing, having become
of some one by which it is participated, is second to that which is present to
all similarly and has filled all from itself. For that which is in one is not
in the others; but that which is present to all in the same way, in order that
it may shine upon all, is not in one, but before all. For it is either in all
or in one of all or before all. But that which is in all, having been divided
into all, would again need another that unifies what has been divided; and no
longer would all participate the same, but one another, another another,


of the One having been divided. And if in one of all, it will no longer be of
all, but of one. If then it is both common to the things able to participate
and the same of all, it will be before all; and this is unparticipated. 24
Everything that participates is inferior to the participated, and the
participated to the unparticipated. For the participant, being incomplete
before the participation, and having become complete by the participation, is
in every way second to the participated, insofar as it is complete having
participated. For in that it was incomplete, in this it is inferior to that
which was participated, which makes it complete. And the participated, being
of some one and not of all, has again been allotted an existence inferior to
that which is of all and not of some one; for the one is more akin to the
cause of all, the other less akin. Therefore the unparticipated leads the
participated things, and these the participants. For to speak concisely, the
one is one before the many; the participated is in the many, one and not-one
at once; and every participant is not-one and one at once. 25 Everything
complete proceeds into generations of the things it is able to produce, itself
imitating the one principle of the wholes. For as that through its own
goodness is unitarily the bringer-to-subsistence of all the beings (for the
good and the One are the same, so that also the good-formedly is the same as
the unitarily), so also the things after that, through their own completeness,
are eager to generate other things inferior to their own substance. For
completeness is some portion of the good, and the complete, as complete,
imitates the good. And that was bringer-to-subsistence of all; so that the
complete too is by nature productive of the things it is able. And the more
complete, by so much as it is more complete, is cause of more things. For the
more complete more participates the good; and this, nearer the good; and this,
more akin to the cause of all; and this, cause of more things. And the more
incomplete, by so much as it is more incomplete, is cause of fewer things. For
being farther from that which produces all, it is bringer-to-subsistence of
fewer things. For to the bringing all to subsistence or ordering or completing
or holding together or making live or demiurgically making, acting each of
these upon more things is akin, and upon fewer more alien. And from these it
is clear that that which is farthest from the principle of all is barren and
cause of nothing; for if it generates something and has something after
itself, it is clear that it would no longer be farthest, but what it produced
is farther from that, while it itself is nearer by producing and because it is
other, imitating the cause productive of all the beings. 26 Every productive
cause, itself remaining upon itself, produces the things after it and the
next. For if it imitates the One, and that unmovedly brings to subsistence the
things after it, every producer likewise has the cause of producing. But also
the One brings to subsistence unmovedly. For if through motion, either the
motion is in it, and being moved it will no longer even be one, changing from
the one; or if the motion is after it, this too will be from the One, and
either to infinity, or the One will produce unmovedly. And every producer will
imitate the One and the cause productive of the wholes. For from the primarily
everywhere is that which is not primarily; so that also from that which is
productive of all is that which is productive of some. Therefore every
producer, remaining upon itself, produces the next. Therefore, the producers
remaining undiminished, the second things are produced by them; for that which
is in any way diminished is unable to remain such as it is. 27 Everything
productive is productive of the second things through completeness and excess
of power. For if not through the complete, but producing while deficient
according to power, it would not even be able to keep its own rank unmoved.
For that which through deficiency and weakness furnishes being to another
furnishes subsistence to that by its own turning and alteration. But every
producer remains such as it is; and while it remains, that after it proceeds.
Therefore being full and complete, it brings the second things to subsistence
unmovedly and undiminishedly, itself being what it is and neither changing
into those nor being diminished. For the produced is not a portioning-off of
the producer; for this does not even belong to generation, nor to the
generative causes. Nor a transition; for it does not become matter of that
which proceeds; for it remains such as it is, and the


produced is other besides it. Therefore the generator is seated unalterable
and undiminished, multiplying itself through a fertile power and furnishing
from itself second subsistences. 28 Everything productive brings to
subsistence the things like itself before the unlike. For since the producer
is of necessity better than the produced, they would never be simply the same
and equal according to power. And if they are not the same and equal, but
other and unequal, they are either in every way distinguished from one another
or both unified and distinguished. But if in every way distinguished, they
will be incompatible, and in no way will that from it be sympathetic with the
cause. Nor then will the one participate the other, being in every way other;
for the participated gives a communion to the participant toward that of which
it has participated. But it is necessary that the caused participate the
cause, as having the substance from there. And if the produced is in some way
distinguished, and in some way unified with the producer, if it has suffered
each equally, it would equally both participate it and not participate; so
that it would both have the substance from it and not have it in the same way.
And if it should be more distinguished, the generated would be more alien from
the generator than akin, and more unfitted toward it than fitted, and more
unsympathetic than sympathetic. If then the things from the causes are also
akin to the causes according to being itself and sympathetic, and hang upon
them by nature, and desire conjunction with them, reaching after the good and
attaining the object of appetite through the cause, it is clear that the
produced things are more unified with the producers than distinguished from
them. And things more unified are more like than unlike those with which they
are most unified. Therefore every productive cause brings to subsistence the
like before the unlike. 29 Every procession is accomplished through likeness
of the second things to the first. For if the producer brings to subsistence
the like before the unlike, likeness from the producers brings the produced to
subsistence; for like things are accomplished as like through likeness, but
not through unlikeness. If then the procession in the descent preserves the
same of the generated toward the generator, and such as that is primarily,
such it manifests that after it secondarily, it has the subsistence through
likeness. 30 Everything produced from something immediately both remains in
the producer and proceeds from it. For if every procession both comes to be
while the first things remain and is accomplished through likeness, the like
being brought to subsistence before the unlike, the produced also remains in
some way in the producer. For that which has proceeded in every way would have
nothing the same toward that which remains, but is in every way distinguished;
and if it will have something common and unified toward it, it too would
remain in that, just as that also was remaining upon itself. And if it should
only remain, not proceeding, it will not differ from the cause nor will it be
something else that has come to be while that remains; for if other, it is
distinguished and is apart; and if apart, while that remains, this has
proceeded from it in order to be distinguished while it remains. Insofar then
as the produced has something the same toward the producer, it remains in it;
and insofar as other, it proceeds from it. And being like, it is in some way
the same and other at once; therefore it remains and proceeds at once, and
neither without the other. 31 Everything that proceeds from something
according to substance reverts toward that from which it proceeds. For if it
should proceed, but not revert toward the cause of this procession, it would
not reach after the cause; for everything that reaches after has reverted
toward the object of appetite. But all things desire the good, and the
attainment of that is through the proximate cause for each; therefore each
things also reach after their own cause. For through that through which being
is for each, through this also the well; and through that through which the
well, toward this the appetite first; and toward that toward which the
appetite first, toward this the reversion. 32 Every reversion is accomplished
through likeness of the things reverting toward that toward which they revert.
For everything that reverts hastens to be joined to all and desires communion
and binding with it. And likeness binds all things, just as unlikeness
distinguishes and sets apart. If then reversion is some communion and
conjunction, and every communion and every conjunction is through likeness,
therefore every reversion would be accomplished through likeness. 33
Everything that proceeds


from something and reverts has a circular activity. For if it reverts toward
that from which it proceeds, it joins the end to the beginning, and the motion
is one and continuous, one coming from that which remains, the other toward
that which has remained; whence all things proceed in a circle from the causes
upon the causes. And there are greater circles and lesser, some reversions
coming to be toward the things lying immediately above, others toward the
higher and as far as the principle of all; for from that are all things and
toward that. 34 Everything that reverts according to nature makes the
reversion toward that from which it also had the procession of its proper
subsistence. For if it reverts according to nature, it has acquired the
appetite according to substance toward that toward which it reverts. And if
this, all its being too is hung upon that toward which it makes the
substantial reversion, and it is like that according to substance; which is
why it is also sympathetic with that by nature, as akin by substance. And if
this, either the being of both is the same, or the one from the other, or both
have been allotted the like from one other. But if the being of both is the
same, how has the one reverted toward the other according to nature? And if
both from one, the reverting according to nature would belong to both toward
that. It remains therefore that the one has being from the other. And if this,
the procession too is from that toward which the reversion according to nature
is. And from these it is clear that intellect is also an object of appetite
for all, and all things proceed from intellect, and the whole cosmos has its
substance from intellect, even if it is everlasting. And it is not for this
reason that it does not proceed from intellect, because it is everlasting; nor
for this reason that it has not reverted, because it is always ordered; but it
both proceeds always and is everlasting according to substance, and has
reverted always and is indissoluble according to the order. 35 Every caused
thing both remains in its own cause and proceeds from it and reverts toward
it. For if it should only remain, it will not differ from the cause, being
undistinguished; for with distinction is procession. And if it should only
proceed, it will be unjoined to it and unsympathetic, communicating with the
cause in no way. And if it should only revert, how does that which does not
have the substance from it make according to substance the reversion toward
what is alien? And if it should remain and proceed, but not revert, how is
there for each the appetite according to nature toward the well and the good
and the stretching-up toward that which generated? And if it should proceed
and revert, but not remain, how, having stood away from the cause, does it
hasten to be joined to it, while it was unjoined before the standing-away? For
if it was joined, it remained in every way according to that. And if it should
remain and revert, but not come forth, how is it possible for that which has
not been distinguished to revert? For everything that reverts is like one
resolving into that from which it has been divided according to substance. And
it is necessary either only to remain or only to revert or only to proceed or
to bind the extremes with one another or the mean with each of the extremes or
all together. It remains therefore that everything both remains in the cause
and proceeds from it and reverts toward it. 36 Of all things multiplied
according to procession the first are more complete than the second, and the
second than those after them, and next likewise. For if the processions
distinguish the produced things from the causes, and there are descents of the
second toward the first, the first that have proceeded are more joined to the
causes, sprouting from those themselves, and the second are farther from the
causes, and next similarly. And the nearer and the more akin to the causes are
more complete (for the causes too are of the caused); and the farther more
incomplete, being made unlike the causes. 37 Of all things that subsist
according to reversion the first are more incomplete than the second, and the
second than those next; and the last are most complete. For if the reversions
come to be according to a circle, and toward that from which the procession,
toward this the reversion, and the procession is from the most complete, the
reversion therefore is toward the most complete. And if, toward that which is
last the procession, from this first the reversion, and the procession is
toward the last which is most incomplete, the reversion too is from the most
incomplete. Therefore first among the things according to reversion are the
most incomplete, and last the most complete. 38 Everything that proceeds from
several causes reverts through as many as it proceeds; and every reversion
through the same, through which also the


procession. For since each comes to be through likeness, that which has
proceeded immediately from something has also reverted immediately toward it
(for the likeness was immediate); and that which needs a mean in proceeding
needs a mean also according to the reversion (for each must come to be toward
the same), so that it will first be reverted toward the mean, then toward that
which is better than the mean. Therefore through as many as being is, through
so many also well-being is for each; and conversely. 39 Every being reverts
either substantially only, or vitally, or also cognitively. For either it has
acquired being only from the cause, or living with being, or it has also
received from there a cognitive power. Insofar then as it only is, it makes a
substantial reversion; insofar as it also lives, also a vital one; insofar as
it also knows, also a cognitive one. For as it has proceeded, so it has
reverted, and the measures of the reversion are bounded by the measures
according to the procession. And the appetite therefore for some is according
to being itself only, being a fitness toward the participation of the causes;
for others according to life, being a motion toward the better things; for
others according to knowledge, being a co-perception of the goodness of the
causes. 40 Of all things that proceed from another cause, those that bring
themselves to subsistence and have acquired a self-constituted substance lead.
For if everything self-sufficient either according to substance or according
to activity is better than that which is hung upon another cause; and that
which produces itself, being furnishing of being to itself, is self-sufficient
toward substance, while that which is produced from another only is not
self-sufficient; and the self-sufficient is more akin to the good; and the
more akin and more like to the causes have come to subsist from the cause
before the unlike; therefore the things produced from themselves and
self-constituted are senior to those that have proceeded into being from
another only. For either there will be nothing self-constituted, or the good
is such, or the first things that have come to subsist from the good. But if
nothing self-constituted, the self-sufficient will be in nothing according to
truth. For neither in the good (for that, being one and the good itself, is
better than self-sufficiency, but not having the good); nor in the things
after the good (for everything will be in need of another, only that before it
<being>). And if the good is self-constituted, producing itself it will not be
one; for that which proceeds from the one is not-one. For it proceeds from
itself, if it is self-constituted; so that the One is one and not-one at once.
Therefore the self-constituted must be after the first; and it is clear that
before those that have proceeded from another cause only; for it is more
authoritative than those and more akin to the good, as has been shown. 41
Everything that is in another is produced from another only; and everything
that is in itself is self-constituted. For that which is in another and needs
a substrate would never be generative of itself; for that which is of such a
nature as to generate itself does not need another seat, being held together
by itself and preserved in itself without the substrate. And that which is
able to remain in itself and be seated is productive of itself, itself
proceeding into itself, and being holding-together of itself, and thus being
in itself, as the caused in a cause. For not as in a place, nor as in a
substrate; for the place too is other than that which is in a place, and that
which is in a substrate is other than the substrate; but this is the same as
itself. Therefore self-constitutedly and as that from a cause in a cause, thus
it is in itself. 42 Everything self-constituted is revertive toward itself.
For if it proceeds from itself, it will also make the reversion toward itself;
for from that from which the procession is for each, toward this also the
reversion coordinate with the procession. For if it proceeds from itself only,
but should not revert as proceeding into itself, it would never reach after
its proper good and what it is able to furnish to itself. And every cause is
able to give to that from it, together with the substance which it gives, also
the well of the substance which it gives, yoked together; so that also itself
to itself. This then is the proper good for the self-constituted. And that
which is unreverted toward itself will not reach after this; and not reaching
after, it would not even attain, and not attaining, it would be incomplete and
not self-sufficient. But if to anything else, it belongs also to the
self-constituted to be self-sufficient and complete. And it will therefore
attain the proper and will reach after and will turn toward itself. 43
Everything revertive toward itself is self-constituted. For if it


has reverted toward itself according to nature and is complete in the
reversion toward itself, it would also have the substance from itself; for
toward that toward which the reversion according to nature is, from this also
the procession according to substance is for each. If then it furnishes
well-being to itself, it will surely also furnish being to itself, and will be
master of its own subsistence. Therefore that which is able to revert toward
itself is self-constituted. 44 Everything revertive toward itself according to
activity has also reverted toward itself according to substance. For if it is
able to be reverted toward itself by activity, but should be unreverted by
substance, it would be better according to the activity rather than according
to the substance, the one being revertive, the other unreverted; for that
which is of itself is better than that which is of another only, and that
which is preservative of itself more complete than that which is preserved by
another only. If then something is revertive toward itself according to the
activity from the substance, it has also been allotted a revertive substance,
so as not only to be active toward itself, but also to be of itself and to be
held together and completed by itself. 45 Everything self-constituted is
ungenerated. For if generated, because generated it will be incomplete in
itself and in need of completion from another; and because it produces itself,
complete and self-sufficient. For every generated thing is completed by
another that furnishes generation to it when it is not; for generation is a
road from the incomplete to the contrary complete. And if something produces
itself, it is always complete, always being together with its own cause, or
rather existing in it, toward that which completes the substance. 46
Everything self-constituted is incorruptible. For if it will be corrupted, it
will leave itself and will be apart from itself. But this is impossible. For
being one, it is at once both cause and caused. And everything that is being
corrupted is corrupted when it has stood away from its own cause; for so long
as each hangs from that which holds it together and preserves it, it is held
together and preserved. And the self-constituted never leaves the cause,
inasmuch as it does not leave itself; for it is cause to itself. Therefore
every self-constituted thing is incorruptible. 47 Everything self-constituted
is without parts and simple. For if divisible, being self-constituted, it will
bring itself to subsistence as divisible, and it will turn whole toward itself
and will be all in all itself. And this is impossible. Therefore the
self-constituted is without parts. But also simple. For if composite, there
will be the worse in it, and the better, and the better will be from the worse
and the worse from the better, if the whole proceeds from its whole self; and
further it is not self-sufficient, being additionally in need of its own
elements, from which it has come to subsist. Therefore everything that is
self-constituted is simple. 48 Everything that is not everlasting is either
composite, or has come to subsist in another. For either it is dissoluble into
these from which it is, and is in every way composed from those into which it
is dissolved; or needing a substrate, and leaving the substrate it goes into
not-being. And if it should be simple and in itself, it will be indissoluble
and unscattered. 49 Everything self-constituted is everlasting. For there are
two ways according to which something must not be everlasting, that from
composition and that from things that are in another. And the self-constituted
is neither composite, but simple; nor in another, but in itself. Therefore it
is everlasting. 50 Everything measured by time either according to substance
or according to activity is generation in this respect, in which it is
measured according to time. For if it is measured by time, it would belong to
it to be or to be active according to time, and the was and the will-be
differing from one another; for if the was and the will-be are the same
according to number, it has suffered nothing from time going and always having
the prior other and the posterior. If then the was is other and the will-be
other, it is therefore becoming and never being, but it travels together with
the time by which it is measured, being in becoming and not standing in the
same being, but always receiving being other and other, as the now according
to time is always other and other through the going of time. Therefore it is
not all at once whole, being in the scattered of the temporal extension, and
being coextended; and this is to have being in not-being; for that which
becomes what it becomes is not. Therefore that which is thus is generation. 51
Everything


self-constituted is exempt from the things measured by time according to
substance. For if the self-constituted is ungenerated, it would not be
measured by time according to being; for generation is about the nature
measured by time. Therefore none of the self-constituted things has come to
subsist in time. 52 Everything everlasting is all at once whole; whether it
has the substance only everlasting, having it all present at once, and not one
part of it already having come to subsist, another about to come to subsist
hereafter, which is not yet, but as much as it is able to be, so much it has
already acquired whole, undiminishedly and unintensifiedly; or the activity in
addition to the substance, having this too gathered and standing in the same
measure of completeness and as it were frozen according to one and the same
bound unmovedly and unchangeably. For if that which is everlasting is (as the
name also shows) the always-being, and being at some time and becoming are
other than the always-being, there must not be the prior, and the posterior;
for there will be generation, and not being. And where there is neither the
prior and posterior nor the was and the will-be, but only the being which it
is, each is all at once whole what it is. And the same also in the case of
being active. And from this it is clear that eternity is cause of being
wholes, if indeed everything everlasting either according to substance or
according to activity has the whole substance or the activity present to it at
once. 53 Before all everlasting things eternity pre-exists, and of all things
according to time time has pre-subsisted. For if everywhere before the
participants there are the participated and before the participated the
unparticipated, it is clear that the everlasting is other, and the eternity in
the everlasting other, and the eternity in itself other, the one as
participating, the other as participated, and the other as unparticipated; and
the temporal is other (for participating), and the time in this other (for
participated), and the time before this, being unparticipated. And each of
these unparticipated ones is everywhere and in all the same; but the
participated is in those only by which it is participated. For many too are
the everlasting and the temporal, in all of which there is eternity according
to participation and time divided; but the undivided eternity and the one time
are before these, and the one is eternity of eternities, the other time of
times, being bringers-to-subsistence of the participated. 54 Every eternity is
a measure of the everlasting, and every time of the things in time; and these
two only are measures among the beings of life and of motion. For everything
that measures either measures according to a part or fitted all at once as a
whole to the measured. That which measures according to a whole then is
eternity, and that according to parts time; therefore two only the measures,
one of the everlasting, the other of the things that are in time. 55
Everything that has come to subsist according to time either is the
always-time or has acquired the subsistence at some time in a part of time.
For if all processions are through likeness, and before the things in every
way unlike there come to subsist continuously with the first the things more
like them than unlike, and it is impossible to join to the everlasting the
things becoming in a part of time (for both as becoming they stand apart from
those that are, and as at some time from those that have come to subsist
always), and means between these and those are the things in some way like
those, in some way unlike, therefore of the things becoming at some time and
of the always-beings a mean is either the always-becoming or the at-some-time

the at-some-time being is not a mean. It remains therefore that the
always-becoming is the mean of both, joining by becoming to the worse, and by
the always imitating the everlasting nature. And from these it is clear that
everlastingness was twofold, one everlasting, and another according to time;
the one a standing everlastingness, the other a becoming one; and the one
having being gathered and all together, the other poured out and unfolded
according to the temporal extension; and the one whole in itself, the other
from parts, each of which is apart according to the prior and posterior. 56

from more are more composite, those from fewer of the same more simple.
Therefore everything produced by more causes is more composite, and that by
fewer more simple; for of what the one participates, the other also; but not
conversely. 59 Everything simple according to substance is either better than

of the beings is most simple, just as the first, because it proceeds from the
first only; but the simplicity is one according to the better than every

same. 60 Everything that is cause of more things is better than that which has

of more, and the ones are parts of the others, what the one makes, the rest
too will make, that which is bringer-to-subsistence of the more; but what this
produces, of not all of these is that productive. Therefore more powerful and

proceeded,

participate and by those that at some time secondarily according to the same
order. 64 Every principal monad brings to subsistence a twofold number, one of
self-complete subsistences, the other of illuminations that have acquired the
subsistence in others. For if the procession according to descent is through
the things proper to the hypostatic causes, from the all-complete the complete
and through these as means the incomplete proceed in good order; so that some
will be self-complete subsistences, others incomplete. And these already
become of the participants (for being incomplete they need the substrates for
their own existence); but the others make the participants of themselves (for
being complete they fill those with themselves and seat them in themselves,
and need nothing of the inferior for their own subsistence). The self-complete
subsistences then, diminished from their principal monad through the
distinction into multitude, are in some way made like that through the
self-complete existence; but the incomplete both by being in others stand away
