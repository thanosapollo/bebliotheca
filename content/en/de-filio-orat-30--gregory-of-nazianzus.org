FOURTH THEOLOGICAL ORATION ON THE SON

1. Since we have sufficiently shaken for you, by the power of the Spirit, the
twists and weavings that come from reasonings, and the objections and
counter-statements from the divine Scriptures, by which those who profane the
letter and steal the sense of what is written appropriate the many and disturb
the way of truth, we have already solved them in summary, and not dimly, as I
persuade myself, for the more well-disposed: assigning the loftier and more
God-befitting expressions to the Godhead, and the humbler and more human ones
to the new Adam for our sake and to the God who suffered against sin. But we
have not gone through each in detail, since the discourse was pressing on. And
you seek also of these the solutions in brief, so as not to be swept away by
arguments of plausibility. We too will summarize these, dividing them into
numbers for ease of memory.

2. For there is one thing among them, and very ready to hand: "The Lord
created me a beginning of his ways for his works." How shall we meet this?
Shall we not accuse Solomon? Shall we not set aside what went before because
of the last transgression? Shall we not say that the saying belongs to Wisdom
herself, as it were knowledge and the craftsman's reason, according to which
all things were constituted? For Scripture knows how to personify many things,
even the lifeless, as: "The sea said such and such"; and, "The abyss said, It
is not in me"; and, "The heavens telling the glory of God." And again a sword
is commanded something, and mountains and hills are asked words of leaping. We
say none of these things, even if they have been set down as strong by some
before us. But let the saying be of the Savior himself, the true Wisdom. Let
us consider a little together. What of the things that are is without cause?
Godhead. For no one has a cause of God to name; or that would be older than
God. And what is the cause of the humanity which God underwent for us? That we
should altogether be saved. For what else? Since then here we clearly find
both "He created" and "He begets me," the argument is simple. Whatever we find
with the cause, let us assign to the humanity; whatever is simple and without
cause, let us reckon to the Godhead. Is not "He created" said with the cause?
For "He created me," it says, "a beginning of his ways for his works." And the
works of his hands are truth and judgment, for the sake of which he was
anointed with Godhead. For this is the anointing of the humanity. But "He
begets me" is without a cause; or show something attached to this. What
argument then will contradict, that Wisdom is called a creature according to
the lower generation, but an offspring according to the first and more
incomprehensible?

3. To this follows also his being called a servant, serving well many, and
that it is a great thing for him to be called a child of God. For in truth he
served flesh, and generation, and our passions, for the sake of our freedom,
and all those whom he has saved who were held under sin. And what is greater
for the lowliness of man than to be blended with God, and to become God from
the mixture, and to be so visited by the dayspring from on high, that even the
holy thing born is called Son of the Most High, and the name above every name
is granted to him? And what else is this but God? And that every knee should
bow to him who emptied himself for us, and mixed the divine image with a
servant's form, and that all the house of Israel should know that God has made
him both Lord and Christ? For these things have come about by the energy of
the Offspring, and by the good pleasure of the Begetter.

4. And second, what of their greatest and unconquerable points? For he must
reign until such and such a time, and be received by heaven until the times of
restoration, and have the seat at

the right until the overpowering of the enemies. And what after this? To cease
from the kingdom, or to be thrust from the heavens? Who will make him cease?
Or for what cause? How bold an interpreter you are, and exceedingly unkingly.
And yet you hear that of his kingdom there is no end. But you suffer this from
not knowing that "until" is not altogether set over against what is to come,
but it sets down what is up to this point, and does not refuse what is after
this. Or how will you understand, not to mention the rest, "I will be with you
until the consummation of the age"? As though after this he will no longer be?
And what is the sense? And not only this, but also from not distinguishing the
meanings. For he is said to reign in one way as Almighty, king both of those
who will it and of those who will it not; and in another as working the
subjection and placing us under his own kingdom, as we willingly accept being
ruled. Of the kingdom understood in that former way there will be no end. And
of the second, what? Our being taken under his hand and being saved. For why
must he work the subjection when they have been subjected? After which he
rises judging the earth, and dividing what is saved and what is lost; after
which God stands in the midst of gods, of those being saved, distinguishing
and setting apart of what honor and abode each is worthy.

5. Join to this also the subjection, by which you subject the Son to the
Father. What, do you say, as if he is not now subjected? And does God, being
God, altogether need to be subjected? You make the argument as about some
robber, or one opposed to God. But consider it thus: that just as he who
looses my curse was called a curse for me; and he who takes away the sin of
the world was called sin; and instead of the old Adam he becomes a new; so
also he makes my insubjection his own, as head of the whole body. So long then
as I am insubject and factious, both by denial of God and by the passions,
Christ too is said to be insubject as far as it concerns me. But when all
things have been subjected to him, and they will be subjected both by
knowledge and by transformation, then he too has fulfilled the subjection,
bringing me the saved. For this is the subjection of Christ, according at
least to my account, the fulfillment of the Father's will. And both Son
subjects to Father, and Father to Son; the one working, the other
well-pleased, which we said also before. And thus he who subjected presents
what has been subjected to God, making ours his own. Of such a kind also seems
to me "My God, my God, look upon me, why have you forsaken me?" For he himself
has not been forsaken, either by the Father, or by his own Godhead, as some
think, as though it feared the passion, and for that reason withdrew from the
one suffering. For who compelled him either to be born below in the first
place, or to go up upon the cross? But in himself, as I said, he types what is
ours. For we were the ones forsaken and overlooked before, then now taken up
and saved by the passions of the Impassible; just as, making our folly and our
fault his own, he says the things that follow through the psalm; since the
twenty-first psalm is clearly referred to Christ.

6. Of the same contemplation also is his learning obedience from the things he
suffered, and the cry, and the tears, and the supplicating, and the being
heard, and the piety. These are dramatized and woven together wonderfully for
us. For as Word he was neither obedient nor disobedient. For these belong to
those under the hand, and to the secondary; the one of the more well-disposed,
the other of those worthy of punishment. But as the form of a servant, he
condescends to fellow-servants and servants, and takes the form of what is
alien, bearing in himself all of me with what is mine, that in himself he may
consume the worse, as fire wax, or as the sun a mist of earth, and that I may
partake of what is his through the blending. For this reason he honors
obedience in deed, and makes trial of it from suffering. For the disposition
is not sufficient, just as it is not for us, unless we also go through the
things themselves. For deed is a demonstration of disposition. And perhaps it
is not worse to suppose also that he tests our

obedience, and measures all things by his own passions by a craft of
loving-kindness, so as to have knowledge of ours by his own, and how much is
demanded of us, and how much is conceded, weakness being reckoned together
with the suffering. For if the light was persecuted because of the screen,
shining in the darkness, in this life, by the other darkness, I mean the evil
one and the tempter, how much the darkness, as weaker? And what wonder if,
when that one escaped altogether, we should also in some measure be overtaken?
For it is greater for him to be persecuted than for us to be overtaken, among
those who reckon these things rightly. And I will still add to what has been
said that, having considered "For in that he himself has suffered being
tempted, he is able to help those who are tempted," clearly bearing toward the
same sense. And God will be all in all in the time of the restoration; not the
Father, the Son having been altogether dissolved into him, as a torch that has
been for a time pulled away into a great pyre, then joined again, let not even
the Sabellians be corrupted by this saying, but whole God, when we are no
longer many, as now by movements and passions, bearing nothing at all of God,
or little, in ourselves, but wholly Godlike, capable of whole God and of him
alone. For this is the perfection toward which we hasten; and Paul himself
especially proves it. For what he says here of God indefinitely, elsewhere he
clearly limits to Christ. Saying what? "Where there is not Greek, nor Jew,
circumcision and uncircumcision, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free; but Christ
is all and in all."

7. Count as third "greater"; as fourth "my God and your God." If then he were
called greater, but not equal, perhaps this would be something for them; but
if we clearly find both, what will these brave fellows say? What is their
strong point? How will the incompatible things agree? For the same thing of
the same to be similarly greater and equal is among the impossibles; or it is
clear that the greater is of the cause, and the equal of the nature? And this
we confess of much good will. And perhaps someone else, wrangling with our
argument, would say that to be from such a cause is not less than the
uncaused. For he would share in the glory of the Unoriginate, because he is
from the Unoriginate; and the generation is added, so great a thing, at least
to those who have a mind, and so worshipful. For to say that he is greater
than what is understood according to the man is true, but not a great thing.
For what wonder if God is greater than man? Let these things then be said by
us against those who boast of the greater.

8. And he would be called God, not of the Word, but of the one seen; for how
would he be God of the one who is properly God? Just as also Father, not of
the one seen, but of the Word. For he was double; so that the one is properly
of both, and the other not properly, conversely than it is with us. For of us
he is properly God, but not properly Father. And this is what makes the error
for the heretics, the yoking of the names, the names being interchanged
because of the blending. And a sign: when the natures are distinguished, the
names also are divided together with the conceptions. Hear Paul saying: "That
the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory." God of Christ, but
Father of glory. For even if the both-together is one, yet not by nature, but
by the coming-together of these. What could be more recognizable?

9. Let fifth be said his receiving life, or judgment, or inheritance of
nations, or authority of all flesh, or glory, or disciples, or whatever is
said. And this belongs to the humanity. And if you should give it also to God,
it is not absurd. For you will not give them as acquired, but as coexisting
from the beginning, and by reason of nature, not of grace.

10. Let sixth be set that the Son can do nothing from himself, unless he sees
the Father doing something. And this is of such a kind: "to be able" or "not
to be able" is not among things said in one way; it is many-signed. For one
thing is said according to

a lack of power, and at a time, and toward something, as that the child cannot
compete, or the puppy see, or contend against this one. For perhaps he will
compete sometime, and will see, and will contend against this one, even if he
is powerless against another. And another, as for the most part, as: "A city
cannot be hidden lying upon a mountain." For perhaps even some one might be
hidden, a greater thing standing in front. And another, as not reasonable:
"The sons of the bridechamber cannot fast, so long as the bridegroom is
present"; whether the one seen bodily, for the time of the sojourn is not a
time of hardship, but of gladness; or as the Word understood. For why must
those being purified by the Word fast bodily? And another, as unwilling, as
his not being able to do signs there, because of the unbelief of those
receiving. For since there is need of both together for the healings, both of
the faith of those being healed, and of the power of the healer, it was not
possible, the other of the pair being lacking. And I do not know if this too
should not be added to the reasonable; for healing is not reasonable for those
who will be harmed from unbelief. And of the same account also: "The world
cannot but hate you"; and, "How can you speak good things, being evil?" For
how is any of these impossible, except that it is unwilling? And there is also
something of such a kind in the things said, which is impossible by nature,
but possible to God if he wills, as that the same one cannot be born a second
time; and a needle not receiving a camel. For what would prevent these things
from coming to be if God willed?

11. And apart from all these is the altogether impossible and inadmissible, as
what we are now examining. For as we say it is impossible for God to be evil,
or not to be; for this would be rather of weakness of God than of power; or
for the non-being to be, or for twice two to be both four and ten; so it is
impossible and unallowable for the Son to do any of the things the Father does
not do. For all things whatever the Father has are the Son's; as conversely
the things of the Son are the Father's; nothing then is private, because they
are common. Since even being itself is common and of equal honor, even if for
the Son it is from the Father. According to which is said also: "I live
because of the Father." Not as though the living and the being were held
together for him from there, but as existing from there timelessly and without
cause. And how does he see the Father doing, and so do? As those who draw
forms and letters, because they cannot otherwise hit upon the truth, except
looking toward the archetype, and being led by the hand from there? And how
does Wisdom need one who will teach, or will he do nothing unless he is
taught? And how does the Father do, or has he done? Did he first constitute
another world instead of the present, and will he constitute the future, and
looking toward those the Son constituted the one, and will constitute the
other? Four worlds then according to this account, some works of the Father,
others of the Son. O the unreason. And he cleanses leprosies, and delivers
from demons and diseases, and makes the dead live, and walks upon the sea, and
does the other things he has done, upon what or when of the Father having
first worked these? Or is it clear that the Father impresses the types of the
same things, and the Word completes them, not slavishly, nor ignorantly, but
scientifically and as master, and to speak more properly, paternally? For thus
I receive that whatever is done by the Father, these the Son also does
likewise; not according to the likeness of the things done, but according to
the equal honor of the authority. And this would be that both the Father works
until now, and the Son; and not only this, but also the economy and
preservation of the things they have made, as "making his angels spirits"
shows; and the earth's being founded upon its security; things once
established and come to be; and thunder being made firm, and spirit being
created, of which the Word once constituted the existence, but the energy is
continuous even now.

12. Let seventh be said that the Son has come down from heaven, not that he
may do his own will, but that of the one who sent him. If then these things
were not said by

the one who had come down himself, we would have said that the saying is typed
from the man, not the one understood according to the Savior, for that one's
willing is not even contrary to God, being wholly from God, but from the one
according to us; as the human will does not altogether follow the divine, but
resists, as for the most part, and wrestles against it. For we also understood
in this way: "Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me; yet not
what I will, but let your will prevail." For it is not likely that he is
ignorant whether it is possible or not, nor that he brings in a will against
the will. But since the saying is as from the one who assumed, for this is
what came down, not of the assumption, we will meet it thus. Not as though
there is a private will of the Son besides that of the Father, but as though
there is not, is the saying; so that the conclusion is of such a kind: "Not
that I may do my will," for neither is mine separated from yours, but the
common of both me and you, of whom as there is one Godhead, so also will. For
many of the things said thus are said from a common, and not positively, but
negatively, as: "For God does not give the Spirit by measure"; for he neither
gives, nor a measured one, for God is not measured by God; and: "Neither my
sin, nor my lawlessness"; for the saying is not as of something that is, but
as of something that is not; and again: "Not because of our righteous deeds,
which we did"; for we did not do them. And this is clear even in what follows;
for what, he says, is the will of the Father? That everyone who believes in
the Son may be saved, and attain the last resurrection. Is this then the will
of the Father, and of the Son in no way? Or does he unwillingly evangelize and
is believed? And who would believe this? Since also that the word which is
heard is not of the Son, but of the Father, has the same force. For how what
is common is private to someone, or of him alone, I cannot see, looking at
many things; and I think no one else either. If you think thus about the
willing, you will understand rightly and very piously, as my account has it,
and that of every well-disposed person.

13. Eighth for them is: "That they may know you the only true God and Jesus
Christ whom you sent"; and: "No one is good, except one, God." And this seems
to me to have an altogether easy solution. For if you set the only true upon
the Father, where will you set the Truth itself? For if you understand "To the
only wise God," or "To him who alone has immortality, dwelling in light
unapproachable," or "To the King of the ages, incorruptible, invisible, And
only wise God" thus, the Son will go from you, condemned to death, or
darkness, or not being wise, nor king, nor invisible, nor God at all, which is
the sum of the things said. And how will he not also lose with the others the
goodness, which belongs especially to God alone? But I think "That they may
know you the only true God" is said for the abolition of those who are not
gods, but are called so. For "And Jesus Christ whom you sent" would not have
been added, if the only true were set over against that one, and the saying
were not of the Godhead in common. And "No one is good" has an answer toward
the tempting lawyer, as having borne witness of goodness to a man. For the
extreme good, he says, belongs to God alone, even if a man also is named this,
as: "The good man brings forth the good from the good treasure"; and, "I will
give the kingdom to the one good above you," God saying to Saul about David;
and: "Do good, O Lord, to the good"; and whatever else of such a kind is said
about those praised among us, upon whom the outflow of the first beautiful
reached also according to a second account. If then we persuade this, it is
best; if not, what will you say to those who say elsewhere that the Son alone
has been called God according to your hypotheses? In what words? In these:
"This is your God, another shall not be reckoned beside him"; and after a
little: "After this he was seen upon the earth, and consorted with men." For
that the saying is not about the Father but about the Son, the addition
clearly presents. For this is the one who conversed with us bodily, and came
to be with those below. And if it should prevail

that this is said against the Father, not of the supposed gods, we have
defeated the Father, by the things we were eager about the Son. And what could
be more wretched or more damaging than this victory?

14. Ninth they will say that: "Always living to make intercession for us."
Well and very mystically and lovingly. For to make intercession does not, as
the custom of the many, have the seeking of vengeance; for this is somehow
even of lowliness; but the advocating for us by the account of the mediation;
as the Spirit also is said to intercede for us. For there is one God, and one
mediator of God and men, the man Jesus Christ. For he still even now
advocates, as man, for my salvation, because he is with the body which he
assumed, until he makes me God by the power of the incarnation, even if he is
no longer known according to flesh, I mean the fleshly passions and, apart
from sin, ours. And thus we also have Jesus Christ as advocate, not as rolling
before the Father for us, and falling down slavishly. Away with the truly
slavish suspicion, and unworthy of the Spirit. For neither is it just for the
Father to seek this, nor for the Son to suffer it, nor to think thus about
God; but by the things he has suffered, as man, he persuades us to endure, as
Word and counselor. This is how I understand the advocacy.

15. Tenth for them is the ignorance, and that no one knows the last day or
hour, not even the Son himself, except the Father. And yet how does Wisdom
ignore any of the things that are, the maker of the ages, the one who brings
them to completion and transforms them, the end of the things that have come
to be? He who so knows the things of God, as the spirit of man the things in
him? For what is more perfect than this knowledge? And how does he accurately
know the things before the hour, and the things as it were at the skin of the
end, but ignore the hour itself? For the thing is like a riddle, as if someone
should say he accurately knows the things before the wall, but ignores the
wall itself; or well knowing the end of the day, not knowing the beginning of
the night; where the knowledge of the one necessarily brings in the other. Or
is it quite clear to all that he knows, as God, but says he is ignorant, as
man, if one separates the appearing from the understood? For that the
appellation of the Son is absolute and unattached, the "of whom" not being
added to the Son, gives us this suspicion, so as to take the ignorance in the
more pious way, reckoning it to the human, not to the divine.

16. If then this account is sufficient, we will stop here, and let nothing
more be sought; if not, at least the second, just as each of the other things,
so also the knowledge of the greatest things, let it be referred to the cause
in honor of the Begetter. And it seems to me that someone, even if he had not
read it in that way, as one of the philologists among us, would think a
little, that neither does the Son know the day or the hour otherwise than as
that the Father does. For of what kind is the conclusion? Since the Father
knows, for this reason the Son also, as it is clear that this is known and
graspable by no one, except the first nature. It remained for us to treat of
having been commanded, and having kept the commandments, and having always
done the things pleasing to him; and further of perfection, and exaltation,
and of learning obedience from the things he suffered, and of high-priesthood
and offering, and betrayal, and of the petition to him who was able to save
him from death, and agony, and clots, and prayer, and if anything else of such
a kind; if it were not quite clear to all that such names belong about the one
who suffers, not the unchangeable nature and higher than suffering. The
account then concerning the opposing texts is so much as to be some root and
reminder for the more examining of the more complete working-out. And it is
perhaps worthy, and following upon what has been said, not to pass by the
appellations of the Son uncontemplated, being many, and lying upon

many of the things understood about him, but to present of each of them
whatever it means, and to show the mystery of the names.

17. And we must begin from here. The divine is unnameable; and this not
reasonings only show, but also the wisest and most ancient of the Hebrews, so
far as they have given to conjecture. For those who honored the divine with
their own characters, and did not even endure that anything after God and God
should be written with the same letters, as the divine ought to be unshared
even as far as this with our things, when would they accept that the unloosed
and peculiar nature be shown by a loosed voice? For no one has ever breathed
all air, nor has mind altogether contained the essence of God, or voice
encompassed it. But sketching the things according to him from the things
about him, we collect some dim and weak and other-from-other imagination. And
this is the best theologian among us, not he who found the whole, for neither
does the bond receive the whole, but he who has imagined more than another,
and gathered more in himself of the image of the truth, or shadow, or whatever
we shall name it.

18. So far then as from what is reachable for us, the One Who Is, and God, are
somehow rather names of the essence; and of these rather the One Who Is; not
only because, giving an oracle to Moses upon the mountain, and being asked the
appellation, whatever he might be, he addressed himself thus, "The One Who Is
has sent me," having commanded him to say to the people; but because we also
find this more proper. For the name of God, even if it has been etymologized
from running (theein), or burning (aithein), by those clever about these
things, because of the ever-moving and the consuming of wicked habits, for
from this he is also called a consuming fire, is nevertheless among things
said relative to something, and not free; just as also the expression Lord,
itself too being said to be a name of God: for "I," he says, "am the Lord your
God; this is my name." And, "Lord is his name." But we seek a nature, to which
being belongs by itself, and not bound to another; and Being is truly proper
to God, and whole, neither by what is before him, nor by what is after him,
for he was not, or will be, bounded or cut short.

19. And of the other appellations some are clearly of the authority, and some
of the economy, and of this a twofold one: the one above the body, the other
in a body; as the Almighty, and the King, either of glory, or of the ages, or
of the powers, or of the Beloved, or of those who reign; and the Lord, either
Sabaoth, which is of armies, or of the powers, or of those who lord. These are
clearly of the authority; and God, either of saving, or of vengeances, or of
peace, or of righteousness, or of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, and of all
spiritual Israel that sees God; and these of the economy. For since we are
administered by these three, both by fear of punishment, and by hope of
salvation, and further also of glory, and by practice of the virtues, from
which these come: the name of the vengeances administers the fear; that of the
salvations the hope; that of the virtues the practice; so that the one who
accomplishes any of these, as bearing God in himself, may the more hasten
toward the perfect, and the intimacy from virtues. These then are the names
common to the Godhead. And proper to the Unoriginate is Father; to the one
begotten without beginning, Son; to the one who has come forth or proceeds
unbegottenly, the Holy Spirit. But let us come to the calls of the Son, which
the discourse set out to say.

20. For he seems to me to be called Son, because he is the same as the Father
according to essence; and not that only, but also from there. And
Only-begotten, not because he is only from only and only, but because also in
only one manner, not as bodies. And Word, because he so stands toward the
Father, as word toward mind; not only because of the passionlessness of the
generation, but also the connectedness, and the declaring. And perhaps one
would say also as definition toward the defined, since this too is called
logos. For he who has conceived the Son, he says, for this is the "having
seen," has conceived the Father; and a concise and easy demonstration of the

Father's nature is the Son. For every offspring is a silent word of the one
who has begotten. And if one should also say because of his existing in the
things that are, he will not miss the account. For what is there that has not
been constituted by the Word? And Wisdom, as knowledge of things both divine
and human. For how is it possible that the one who has made should be ignorant
of the reasons of the things he has made? And Power, as preserving the things
that have come to be, and furnishing the power of their being held together.
And Truth, as one, not many by nature; for the true is one, and the false
many-cleft; and as a pure seal of the Father, and most unlying impress. And
Image, as consubstantial, and because this is from that, but the Father is not
from this. For this is the nature of an image, to be an imitation of the
archetype, and of that of which it is said; except that here there is also
more. For there it is of an unmoved of a moved; but here of a living and
living, and having the unvarying more than Seth of Adam, and the begotten of
every begetter. For such is the nature of the simple things, not to be like in
one respect, and unlike in another, but to be a whole type of the whole, and
the same rather than a resemblance. And Light, as brightness of souls being
purified both in word and in life. For if ignorance and sin are darkness,

and constitution and essentializing of every rational nature. For in him we

of us being inbreathed a breath from there, and Holy Spirit as many as are
capable, and so much, according as we open the mouth of the mind. And
Righteousness, because he is divider according to worth, and arbitrating
justly for those under law and those under grace, soul and body, so that the
one should rule, and the other be ruled, and the better have the hegemony
against the worse, so that the worse may not rise up against the better. And
Sanctification, as purity, that the pure may be contained by purity. And
Redemption, as freeing us who are held under sin, and giving himself as a
ransom in exchange for us, a purification of the inhabited world. And
Resurrection, as raising us up from here, and leading back to life those
deadened by sin.

21. These then are still common both to the one above us and to the one for
us. And those which are properly ours and of the assumption from here: Man,
not only that he may be contained by bodies through a body, otherwise he would
not have been contained because of the incomprehensibility of the nature; but
also that he may sanctify man through himself, becoming as leaven to the whole
lump, and uniting to himself the whole that was condemned he may loose it from
the condemnation, having become all things for all, whatever we are, except
sin, body, soul, mind, through as many as death; the common from these, man,
God seen, because of the understood. And Son of man, both because of Adam, and
because of the virgin, from whom he came to be; of the one, as forefather, of
the other, as mother, by law and not by law of generation. And Christ, because
of the Godhead; for this is the anointing of the humanity, not sanctifying by
energy according to the other christs, but by presence of the whole Anointer;
whose work it is that the Anointer is called man, and that the anointed is
made God. And Way, as bearing us through himself. And Door, as introducer. And
Shepherd, as lodging into a place of green, and nourishing upon water of rest,
and from here leading, and fighting in front against the beasts; turning the
wandering, bringing back the lost, binding up the crushed, guarding the
strong, and gathering toward the fold from there by words of shepherding
knowledge. And Sheep, as a victim; and Lamb, as perfect. And High Priest, as
the one who brings near. And Melchizedek, as without mother in what is above
us, and without father in what is according to us; and as without genealogy in
the above; "For who," he says, "will declare his generation?"; and as king of
Salem, and this is peace, and as king of righteousness, and as taking tithes
of patriarchs who are champions against the wicked powers. You have the
appellations of the Son. Walk through them, both as many as are lofty, in a
godly way, and as many as are bodily, compassionately; or rather wholly in a
godly way, that you may become God having gone up from below, through the

one who came down from above for us. Upon all, and before all, keep that, and
you will not slip in the loftier or the humbler names: Jesus Christ, yesterday
and today bodily, the same spiritually, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
