ON THE APOSTOLIC SAYING which says: "For there must also be heresies among
you, that the approved may become manifest."
=51.251=
αʹ. This spiritual theater of ours was sufficiently kindled the other day,
when I brought in before you by the word Jerusalem lamenting, and declaring
her own =51.252= calamities. For I saw your eyes then in travail, sending
forth fountains of tears; I saw, from what they suffered, each man's mind full
of wailing and =51.253= thrown into confusion. Therefore, perceiving it
quickly, I drew in the tragedy, I snatched the word from the midst, so as to
check beforehand that lament that was about to burst from each man's heart.
For a soul held by grief can neither speak nor hear anything sound. Why then
have I reminded you of those things now? Because the things that are about to
be said today also are akin to what was said then. For just as those things
checked our sloth in life, and corrected the neglect concerning what must be
done, so the things now to be said could make us more secure concerning the
precise observation of the doctrines; so that from all sides we may be
complete, coming unto a perfect man, unto the measure of stature, according to
the divine Apostle.
Then we treated the body for you, now we heal the head; then through the words
of Jeremiah, now through the sayings of Paul. What then are Paul's sayings,
which it is set before us today to work out? "For there must also be heresies
among you," he says, "that the approved may become manifest among you." The
question is no small one. For if Paul says this by way of counsel, and there
must be heresies, those who introduce the heresies are without blame. But this
is not so, it is not so; for the saying is not of one counseling, but of one
foretelling what is to come. For just as a physician, seeing the sick man
given to gluttony and drunkenness and other forbidden things, says that this
disorder must breed a fever, not legislating, nor counseling, but from the
present things, with consideration, foretelling the future; and a farmer
again, or a steersman, seeing gatherings of clouds and thunderbolts breaking
with lightnings, says that these clouds must breed rain and a violent storm,
nor is he exhorting, but proclaiming beforehand what will be; so also Paul has
set down the "must." Often then we too, seeing men colliding with one another
and washing themselves with harsh insults, say, These must come to blows and
be imprisoned, neither counseling ourselves, nor exhorting those men (for how
could we?), but from the present conjecturing the future. So then Paul too
does not say these things by way of counsel, that "There must also be heresies
among you," but proclaiming beforehand and prophesying what is going to be.
For that he does not counsel that there be heresies, he himself is the one who
says, "Even if an angel evangelize you besides what you received, let him be
anathema." He himself is the one who, when circumcision was being received out
of season, and was muddying the purity of the preaching, casts it out and
says, "If you be circumcised, Christ will profit you nothing."
How then, he says, did he also add a cause, saying, "That the approved may
become manifest"? The "that" in many places in the Scriptures is not of
causation, but of the outcome of the things. For example, Christ came, and
made the blind man see again; and that man worshipped him; but the Jews, when
this man had been healed, did everything so as to overshadow the miracle, and
they drove Christ away. Then therefore he says: "For



judgment I came into this world, that they who see not may see, and they who
see may become blind." Did he then come for this, that those men might become
blind? He did not come for this, but this came out, and he has set the outcome
in the form of causation. Again, the law was given, that =51.254= it might
check the rush of sins, that it might make those who received it more
reasonable. But the contrary came out through their sloth; for the sins
increased; and Paul says, "But the law entered in, that the trespass might
abound"; and yet it did not enter in for this, but that it might lessen the
trespass. But this came out through the ingratitude of those who received it.
So then here too the "that" is not of causation, but of outcome. For that
there is some other cause of the heresies, and heresies did not come to be for
this, that the approved might become manifest, but they took their pretexts
from elsewhere, hear Christ making this plain to us: "The kingdom of the
heavens," he says, "was likened to a man sowing good seed in his field; and
while men slept, there came an enemy man, and sowed the tares." Do you see
that heresies are for this reason, because the men slept, because they were
slothful, because they did not attend with precision to the things said? Lest
then someone say, Why did Christ permit it? Paul says that This permission
does you no harm; if you are approved, you will be more manifest still. For it
is not the same thing, when no one is tripping or deceiving, to stand in the
right faith, and, when countless waves are dashing against it, to remain
unshaken and unoverturned. Just as then the assaults of the winds, fanning the
trees from every side, make them stronger, if they are well rooted and with
precision; so also the souls that have been fixed in the foundation of the
right faith, however many heresies may assault them, they do not overturn, but
even make them stronger. What then, he says, of those who are weak, and are
overturned and easily tripped? Not even those suffer this from the assault of
the heresy, but from their own weakness. And I do not mean a natural weakness,
but that which is from choice, that which is also worthy of charges and lies
under chastisement and punishment, which we too are masters of correcting.
Therefore when we succeed we are praised, and when we do not succeed we are
punished.
βʹ. And that you may learn that nothing can harm those who are sober, I will
try to prove this from another place also. What could be more wicked than the
devil, what more polluted? Yet this wicked and mischievous one, having much
strength, having assaulted Job with all his devices, and having emptied his
whole quiver upon the house and the body of the just man, not only did not
overturn him, but even made him more illustrious. And this man then was not
even harmed by the devil; but Judas, because he was neglected and slothful,
gained nothing from the company of Christ, but remained a traitor after those
many exhortations and counsels; and the cause is that God does not compel nor
force one who is unwilling; just as he did not force that man either. Thus if
we too are sober, not even the devil can harm us; but if we are not sober, but
are slothful, we shall never gain even from those who benefit us =51.255=, but
we shall also undergo the last loss. So great an evil is sloth. The Jews, at
any rate, not only were not benefited when Christ came, but were even harmed;
yet not from Christ, but from their own sloth and ingratitude. And hear this
from Christ himself saying: "If I had not come," he says, "and spoken to them,
they would not have sin; but now they have no pretext concerning their sin."
Do you see that his presence both deprived them of pardon, and cut off their
defense?



So great an evil it is not to collect oneself, nor to manage one's own affairs
as one ought. This one may see happening also in the case of bodies; for the
sun itself is wont to darken the one who is sick in the eyes, but not even the
darkness can harm the healthy one. And I do not lengthen this argument idly;
but because many, leaving off accusing their own sloths, and correcting their
ingratitude and insensibility, do not do this, but go about seeking for
themselves cold defenses, and saying: If the devil had not been, we would not
have been destroyed; if there had been no law, we would not have sinned; if
there had been no heresies, we would not have been tripped. These are
pretenses and pretexts, O man; for nothing ever harms the sober man, just as
nothing benefits the one who sleeps and is slothful and betrays his own
salvation. This very thing then Paul too was hinting at when he said, "That
the approved may become manifest among you"; that is, do not be troubled, nor
be distressed; the heresies can harm you in nothing.
That then, even if the argument were about heresies, the saying would still
have no difficulty, is clear from these things. For it is prophecy, not
counsel; a prediction, not an exhortation; and the "that" is of outcome, not
of causation. But that the argument for him now is not even about doctrines,
but about poor and rich, about eating and not eating, about the prodigality
and gluttony of the well-off, about the abandonment of the poor that took
place at their hands, bear with me a little as I relate from above; for
otherwise what is said would not become clear to you. For when the apostles
began to sow the word of piety, three thousand came forward at once, and again
five thousand, and of all of them the heart and the soul was one. And the
cause of the concord, which also bound their love together, and gathered so
many souls into one, was the contempt of money. For "no one," it says, "said
that any of the things he possessed was his own, but they had all things
common." For when the root of evils was taken away, I mean love of money, all
the good things entered in, and thereafter they were bound tightly to one
another, there being nothing that divided them. For the "mine" and the
"yours," this cold word that has brought countless wars into the world, had
been banished from that holy Church, and they dwelt on earth as the angels
dwell in heaven, and neither did the poor envy those who were rich; for there
were no rich; nor did the rich overlook the poor; for there were no poor; but
all things were common. And not even one said that any of the things he
possessed was his own; for it was not, as it happens now, so then also. For
now, having their own things, they give to those in need; but then not even
this was so, but having stood off from the ownership of their own money, and
having set it in the midst and mixed =51.256= it together, those who formerly
were well-off were no longer even distinguishable; so that even if any pride
had come from disdain over money, this too was taken away altogether, all
being in one equality, and all the money having been mixed together. And not
from this only, but even from the very manner of the laying down one may see
their reverence. For as many as were owners of fields or houses, selling them,
brought the prices, and laid them at the feet of the apostles. He did not say
that they cast them into their hands, but, "They laid them at their feet,"
showing through this the shame and the reverence and the fear which they had
toward the apostles; for they thought they were not so much giving as
receiving. And this indeed is most of all to despise money; this is to nourish
Christ genuinely, when you do not do this with arrogance and pride, when you
furnish it as one benefiting yourself rather than the one who receives.
For if you are not so disposed, do not even give; if you do not think you are
receiving rather than giving, do not furnish it. This Paul also testified of
others later, saying thus: "And I make known to you, brethren, the grace of
God that has been given in the



churches of Macedonia, that their deep poverty abounded unto the riches of
their simplicity, that according to their power, I bear witness, and beyond
their power, of their own accord with much entreaty begging of us, they
completed the grace and the fellowship of the ministry unto the saints." Do
you see how he admires them more for this, that knowing it as a grace, and
begging, and entreating, they thus displayed their abundance in the matter of
money?
γʹ. For this reason we also admire Abraham, not only because he sacrificed a
calf, nor because he mixed flour, but because with much pleasure and humility
he received the strangers, running to meet them, serving them, calling them
lords, thinking he had found a treasure of countless goods, if ever he should
see a stranger passing by. For thus a double almsgiving comes to be, when we
both give, and when giving we furnish it with eagerness. "For God loves," he
says, "a cheerful giver." Since even if you should lay down countless talents
with arrogance and pride and vainglory, you have lost it all; just as that
Pharisee, who was tithing his possessions, because he also thought great
things and was puffed up, having lost everything, thus came down from the
temple. But it was not so in the case of the apostles; but rejoicing,
exulting, thinking they were gaining the greatest things, they thus brought
the money, and thought it a thing to be desired if the apostles should deign
to receive it. And just as some, being called to great offices, and about to
dwell in the more royal of the cities, having first turned all their substance
into money, thus migrate; so then also those men did at that time, having been
called to heaven, to the metropolis above and the kingdom there.
For since they knew that that is truly their fatherland, having turned their
substance into money, they sent it ahead there through the apostolic hands.
For it is of the last folly to leave any of our things remaining here, when we
are about to depart from here a little later; for whatever is left behind
becomes a loss =51.257=. Let all things then be sent ahead there, where we too
are thereafter going to spend our time continually. For these men also, having
reasoned these things, stripped off all their substance, and the achievement
became double; for they both corrected the poverty of those in need, and made
their own substance more and more secure, transferring their own treasures to
heaven. From this law then and custom there came to be a certain wondrous
practice then in the churches; for all the faithful, coming together after the
hearing of the teaching, after the prayers, after the communion of the
mysteries, when the assembly was dismissed, did not withdraw home at once, but
the rich and the more well-off, bringing foods and dishes from home, called
the poor, and they made common tables, common feastings, common banquets in
the church itself; so that both from the fellowship at the table, and from the
reverence from the place, and from every side their love was bound more
tightly, and much pleasure came to be for them, and much benefit. For the poor
enjoyed no ordinary consolation, and those who were rich, having reaped much
goodwill both from those who were fed and from God, for whose sake they did
these things, thus went home with much grace. And countless goods came from
this practice, and the sum of them all, the friendship was warmer at each
assembly, they being united to one another with such great kindness, both
those who were benefiting and those who were being benefited. This custom then
the Corinthians, as time went on, corrupted, and the more well-off, feasting
by themselves, overlooked the poor, and often did not wait for those who were
late, and who, being held by the necessities of life, as is the way of the
poor, were slow. And from this it happened that they, arriving later, withdrew
with shame, the table having been taken away, these having gone ahead



and those being late. Paul then, seeing many evils from this practice, some
already happening, some about to be (for there came to be contempt of the poor
among the rich and more pride, and among the poor despondency and hatred
toward those who were rich, and whatever else was likely to be born from these
evils), corrects this wicked and bitter custom. And see with how much
understanding and gentleness he entered upon the correction. For beginning he
says thus: "Now in this that I declare unto you I praise you not, that you
come together not for the better, but for the worse." And what is, "Not for
the better"? Your ancestors, he says, and your fathers both sold their own
substances and possessions and belongings, and had all things common, and much
love toward one another; but you, though you ought to emulate those men, not
only have done nothing of the kind, but even what alone you had, this too you
have lost, the banquets that took place at the assembly; therefore he says,
"That you come together not for the better, but for the worse." And those men
even yielded all their possessions to the poor; but you, sharing a table with
them, have deprived them even of this. "For first of all, when you come
together in church, I hear that there are schisms among you, and I partly
believe it."
δʹ. See how again he makes the correction with understanding. He said neither
"I disbelieve," nor "I believe"; but he left the saying in the middle, that "I
partly also believe"; I do not =51.258= believe completely, but neither do I
disbelieve completely; and whether this or that should happen, you are
entirely the masters. For if you correct it, I do not believe; but if you
remain in the same things, I believe. Therefore he both did not accuse, and he
accused. He did not accuse completely, that he might give them hope of
correction and a space for repentance; he did not leave them unaccused, lest
they remain in the same sloth. "I have not believed completely," he says; for
this is to say, "I partly believe." And he said this urging them to change and
to correct themselves and to lead him away from believing even in part any
such thing against them. "For there must also be heresies, that the approved
may become manifest among you." He has spoken then of the heresies. Here give
heed, that the "There must be heresies among you" is not about doctrines; but
about the discord at the tables. For having said, "For there must also be
heresies," he also added the manner of the heresies: "For when you come
together into one place, it is not to eat the Lord's supper." What is, "To eat
the Lord's supper"? It is not possible, he says, to eat the Master's supper;
meaning that supper which Christ delivered on the last night, when all the
disciples were with him. For at that supper both Master and slaves all
reclined together; but you, being fellow-slaves, have divided and split one
another. And he did not even drive away the traitor; for Judas too was with
them then; but you thrust aside the brother. Therefore he says, "It is not to
eat the Lord's supper," calling the Lord's supper that which is with concord,
that of all being called together in common. "For each one takes his own
supper beforehand to eat; and one is hungry, and another is drunken." And he
did not say, "One is hungry, and another eats," but by the name of drunkenness
he rather laid hold of them. Both there and here there is excess, he says. You
are bursting from gluttony, that man is wasting from famine; you partake even
of what is beyond need, but that man has not even enjoyed the necessities. The
double dread, the equality having been destroyed, this he calls heresies;
because they were disposed contentiously toward one another and were in
faction, and one was hungry, and another was drunken. And he said well, "When
you come together into one place."
For why do you come together? he says; what does the meeting want? why the
common gathering, when the table does not become common? The money we have
received is the Master's; let it then be set forth in common for our
fellow-slaves. "Have you not



houses to eat and to drink in? or do you despise the church of God," he says,
"and shame them that have not?" You think, he says, that you insult the
brother only, but the place also shares in the insult. For you despise a whole
church. For it is called church for this reason, that it receives all in
common. Why then do you bring the meanness of your house into the church? For
if you despise the brother, reverence the place; for the church too is
insulted. And he did not say, "You deprive them that have not," or, "You do
not pity them that have not," but what? "You shame them that have not." Most
shame-striking he has set forth their prodigality as a tragedy. For the poor
man, he says, does not care so much about the food as about the insult. See
how he also made a noble defense for this man, and laid hold of those men more
vehemently. "What shall I say to you? shall I praise you? in this I praise you
not." What is this? After the demonstration of the absurdity the =51.259=
accusation is lowered, and very reasonably, lest they become more shameless.
For before he had shown the matter to be absurd, he brought out a complete
sentence, saying: "Now in this that I declare unto you I praise you not"; but
when he had shown precisely that they were worthy of many charges, he uses a
lowered censure, leaving the more vehement part of the accusation to lie
stored in the construction and demonstration of what has been said. Then he
leads the argument out to the mystical table, wishing to frighten them more.
"For I," he says, "received from the Lord that which I also delivered unto
you." What sequence is this? You are discoursing about a common meal, and you
mention fearful mysteries? Yes, he says. For if those spiritual things, if the
fearful table is set forth common to all, both to rich and to poor, and the
rich does not enjoy this more abundantly, nor the poor less, but there is one
honor, and one approach; and until all have partaken and communicated of this
spiritual and holy table, the things set forth are not taken away, but all the
priests stand, waiting even for the poorest and most paltry of all; much more
must one do this in the case of the sensible table. For this reason I
mentioned that Lord's supper. "For I received from the Lord that which I also
delivered unto you, that the Lord Jesus in the night in which he was being
betrayed took bread, and having given thanks broke it, and said: This is my
body which is broken for many for the remission of sins. This do in
remembrance of me. Likewise also the cup after supper, saying: This cup is the
new covenant in my blood."
εʹ. Then having discoursed much about those who communicate unworthily in the
mysteries, and having laid hold of them vehemently and convicted them, and
having taught that those who take his blood and his body simply and as it
happens will undergo the same punishment as those who killed Christ, he again
led the argument out to the present subject, saying: "Wherefore, brethren,
when you come together to eat, wait for one another. But if any man hunger,
let him eat at home, that you come not together unto judgment." See how even
secretly he condemned their gluttony, and he did not say, "But if you hunger,"
but, "But if any man hunger," that each, being ashamed to appear himself
liable to the charges, may correct it beforehand. And he shut the argument
with fear of punishment, =51.260= saying: "That you come not together unto
judgment," that is, unto condemnation and unto reproach. For it is not food,
he says, nor a table, that which is with shame of the brother, that which is
with contempt of the church, that which takes place with gluttony or
prodigality. These things are not cheerfulness, but chastisement and
punishment. For you draw a great sentence upon yourselves, insulting the
brethren, despising the church, making the holy place a private house through
eating by yourselves. These things you too, having heard, beloved, stop the
mouths of those who simply use the apostolic saying and



teaching; correct those who use the Scriptures to the harm of themselves and
of others.
For you have learned concerning what he says, "For there must also be heresies
among you," concerning the discord that takes place at the tables, because
"One is hungry, and another is drunken." And along with the right faith let us
also display a manner of life that agrees with the doctrines, much kindness
toward the poor, making much provision for those in need; let us traffic a
spiritual traffic, let us seek nothing beyond need. This is wealth, this is
traffic, this is an unfailing treasure, to transfer all that we have to
heaven, and thereafter to be confident concerning the keeping of what is
stored. For the gain from almsgiving will be double for us, both that we no
longer fear concerning the money that has been laid down, lest robbers and
housebreakers and the wickedness of servants should spoil it, and that what
lies there has not simply been buried fruitless; but just as a root planted in
a rich field bears ripe fruit each year, so also silver planted in the hands
of the poor bears us spiritual fruit not only each year, but even each day,
boldness toward God, remission of sins, nearness of angels, a good conscience,
cheerfulness of spiritual exultation, a hope that is not put to shame, the
wondrous goods which God has prepared for those who love him, and for those
who with a warm and boiling soul seek the mercy of his appearing; which may it
be that all of us, having finished the present life in a manner well-pleasing,
may attain, the eternal joy of those who are being saved, by the grace and
compassions of the true God and our Savior Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory
and the might with the Father and his all-holy Spirit, unto the ages of ages.
Amen.
