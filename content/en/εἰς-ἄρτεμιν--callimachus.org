To Artemis edited by Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff

We hymn Artemis (for it is no light thing for singers to forget her), whose
care is bows and the shooting of hares and the abundant chorus and sport in
the mountains, beginning from when, still a child sitting on her father's
knees, she spoke these words to her parent: "Give me to keep my maidenhood
forever, papa, and many names, so that Phoebus may not contend with me. Give
me arrows and a bow. Let it be, father; I do not ask you for a quiver or a
great bow. The Cyclopes will at once craft arrows for me, and a well-bent bow.
But give me to bear the light, and to gird to the knee a tunic with a colored
border, so that I may slay wild beasts. Give me sixty Oceanid dancers, all
nine years old, all still unbelted girls. Give me twenty Amnisian nymphs as
attendants, who will tend both my hunting-boots and, whenever I no longer
shoot lynxes or deer, my swift hounds well. Give me all the mountains; and
assign me whichever city you wish. For it is rare when Artemis goes down to a
town. I will dwell in the mountains, and I will mix with the cities of men
only when women, worn by sharp birth-pangs, call me as helper, those whom the
Fates allotted me to aid from the first when I was born, because my mother
neither in bearing me nor in carrying me felt pain, but without labor she set
me down from her dear limbs." So the child spoke and wanted to touch her
father's beard, and she stretched out her hands many times in vain, until she
might reach it. And her father nodded, laughing, and stroking her said: "When
goddesses bear such children for me, I would care little for jealous Hera in
her anger. Take, child, all that you ask of your own will, and your father
will give still greater things besides. Three times ten cities I will grant
you, and not one tower only; three times ten cities, which will not know how
to exalt another god but you alone, and to be called Artemis's. And many
cities in common you shall measure out, inland and islands; and in all of them
there will be altars and groves of Artemis. And you shall be overseer of
streets and of harbors." So he spoke and confirmed the word with his head. And
the girl went to the white Cretan mountain, thick with forest; from there to
Ocean; and she chose many nymphs, all nine years old, all still unbelted
girls. And the river Cairetus rejoiced greatly, and Tethys rejoiced, because
they sent their daughters as companions to Leto's child.

Then she went after the Cyclopes. She found them on the island of Lipara
(Lipara is recent, but then its name was Meligounis), standing at the anvils
of Hephaestus around a mass of iron; for a great work was pressing: they were
making a horse-trough for Poseidon. And the nymphs were afraid when they saw
the dread monsters, like jagged crags, and under the brow, in all of them,
single eyes equal to a four-hide shield, glaring terribly, and when they heard
the clang of the anvil ringing loud and the great blast of the bellows
themselves and the heavy groan; for Aetna cried out, and Trinacria cried out,
seat of the Sicanians, and neighboring Italy cried out, and Corsica sent up a
great shout, whenever they lifted the hammers over their shoulders and,
striking in turn the bronze boiling from the furnace or the iron, toiled
mightily. Therefore the Oceanids could not endure to look them in the face
unflinchingly, nor to receive the noise in their ears. No wonder; even the
daughters of the blessed, when they are no longer little, never look on them
without a shudder. But when some girl is disobedient to her mother, the mother
calls the Cyclopes against her child, Arges or Steropes; and Hermes comes from
the inmost part of the house, smeared with burnt ash; at once he frightens the
girl, and she sinks into her mother's lap, putting her hands over her eyes.
But you, maiden, even earlier, when you were still three years old, when Leto
came carrying you in her arms, Hephaestus calling her so that he might give
the birth-gifts, and Brontes set you on his stout knees, you seized the shaggy
hair from his great chest and plucked it out by force; and the hairless part
still even now remains in the middle of his breast, as when mange, settled on
a man's temple, has eaten away the hair. Therefore very boldly you spoke this
to them then: "Cyclopes, come, craft for me too a Cydonian bow and arrows and
a hollow lid for the bolts; for I too am Leto's child, just as Apollo is. And
if with my bow I hunt a solitary beast or some monstrous animal, the Cyclopes
would eat it." You spoke; and they accomplished it; and at once you armed
yourself, goddess.

Straightway you went back after the puppies; and you came to the Arcadian fold
of Pan. He was cutting the flesh of a Maenalian lynx, so that his whelping
bitches might eat the food. And the bearded one gave you two half-white dogs,
three drop-eared, and one spotted, which dragged the lions themselves still
living to the fold, pulling them when they seized their throats; and he gave
seven Cynosurian hounds swifter than the winds, which are swiftest to chase
fawns and the hare that does not close its eye, and to point out the lair of
the deer and where the porcupine has its dens, and to lead on the track of the
gazelle. Going from there (and the dogs too were rushing with you), you found
at the foot of the Parrhasian mountain leaping deer, a great prize; they were
always pastured on the banks of the dark-pebbled torrent, larger than bulls,
and gold shone from their horns. Suddenly you were amazed and said to your
heart: "This would be a first catch worthy of Artemis." There were five in
all; and you took four quickly, running without a chase of hounds, so that
they might draw your swift chariot. But the one that fled across the river
Celadon, by Hera's prompting, so that later it might become a labor for
Heracles, the Ceryneian hill received. Artemis the Maiden, slayer of Tityos,
golden are your arms and your girdle, and you yoked a golden car, and you,
goddess, cast golden bits on the fawns. Where first did the horned vehicle
begin to carry you? On Thracian Haemus, from where the blast of Boreas comes,
bringing ill-blowing frost to those without cloaks. Where did you cut the
pine, and from what flame did you kindle it? On Mysian Olympus, and you put in
the breath of unquenchable light, which your father's thunderbolts drip. How
many times, goddess, did you try the silver bow? First at an elm, and second
you shot at an oak, third again at a beast. The fourth no longer at an oak,

but you shot it into a city of the unjust, who toward themselves and toward
strangers committed many sinful deeds, wretched men; on whom you will stamp
harsh anger. A plague grazes down their cattle, and frost their works, and old
men cut their hair over their sons, and the women either die stricken in
childbirth or, escaping, give birth to those of whom none stood upright on the
ankle. But those on whom you look smiling and gracious, for them the field
bears the ear well, and the offspring of four-footed beasts well, and wealth
increases well; nor do they go to the tomb except when they carry one who has
lived many years; nor does discord wound the race, which even among
well-founded houses has done harm; and the sisters-in-law and the husband's
sister share one table. Lady, of these may whoever is true be my friend, and
may I myself be so, queen, and may song always be my care; in it there will be
the marriage of Leto, and you much in it, and Apollo too, and all your labors,
and the hounds and the bows and the chariot-rims that easily carry you to be
gazed at, when you drive to the house of Zeus. There those who meet you at the
forecourt receive you: Hermes Acacesius your weapons, and Apollo whatever
beast you bring; at least formerly, before strong Alcides arrived. But now
Phoebus no longer has this labor, for so great a Tirynthian anvil always
stands before the gates waiting, if you come bringing any fat food; and all
the gods laugh at him unceasingly, and most of all his mother-in-law herself,
whenever from the car he carries a very great bull or a wild boar gasping, by
the hind foot. With this cunning speech, goddess, he very much advises you:
/Shoot at evil beasts, so that mortals may call you helper/
/as they call me. Leave the deer and the hares/
/to feed on the mountains; what would deer or hares/
/do to him? Boars ruin the works, boars the plants./
/And cattle are a great evil to men; shoot those too./
So he spoke, and quickly he was busy about the great beast. For not even when
his limbs were deified under the Phrygian oak did he cease from gluttony;
still he has that belly with which once he met Theiodamas at the plow. And the
Amnisian nymphs, when they have loosed the fawns from under the yoke, rub them
down, and beside them they bring much to graze, having reaped from Hera's
meadow the swift-growing trefoil, which even the horses of Zeus eat; and they
filled golden troughs with water, so that there might be a drink after their
heart. And you yourself go to your father's house; and they all alike call you
to a seat; but you sit beside Apollo.

And when the nymphs circle you in the dance near the springs of Egyptian
Inopus, or at Pitane (for Pitane too is yours), or in Limnae, or where,
goddess, you came from Scythia to dwell at Halai Araphenides and left the
rites of the Taurians, may my oxen then not for hire cut a four-acre fallow
under a stranger's plowman; for surely they would come back to the dung with
limbs and neck worn out, even if they were Stymphalian nine-year-olds, drawing
by the horn, which are by far the best at cutting a deep furrow; since never
did that god Helios pass by the fair dance, but he looks on, stopping his car,
and the lights grow long. Which of the islands, and which mountain, pleased
you most, which harbor, and which city? Which of the nymphs did you love above
the others, and which heroines did you take as companions? Speak, goddess, you
to us, and I will sing it to others. Of islands Doliche pleased you, of cities
Perge, of mountains Taygetus, and the harbors of Euripus. And above the others
you loved the Gortynian nymph, deer-slaying Britomartis of sure aim; for whose
sake once Minos, driven by love, ran down the mountains of Crete. And she, the
nymph, now hid under shaggy oaks, now in the meadows; and he went for nine
months over the rugged places and the cliffs and did not pause the chase,
until, being seized and already near, she leapt into the sea from the highest
crag and sprang into the nets of fishermen, which saved her; whence afterward
the Cydonians call the nymph Dictynna, and the mountain from which the nymph
leapt Dictean, and they set up altars and perform rites; and the garland on
that day is either pine or mastic, and hands are untouched by myrtle; for then
a myrtle branch caught in the girl's robes when she was fleeing; whence she
was greatly angry at the myrtle. Oupis, fair-faced lady, light-bearer, and the
Cretans call you by that name too from the nymph. And you made Cyrene your
companion, to whom once you yourself gave two hunting dogs, with which the
girl, daughter of Hypseus, won the prize beside the Iolcian tomb. And the
blonde wife of Cephalus son of Deion, lady, you made your fellow-huntress; and
they say you loved fair Anticleia as your own eyes. They first wore swift bows
and on their shoulders quivers holding arrows; and their right shoulders were
left free and the breast always showed bare. And you praised still more
swift-footed Atalanta, the boar-slayer, daughter of Iasius the Arcadian, and
you taught her both the driving of hounds and sure aim. The summoned hunters
of the Calydonian boar do not blame her; for the tokens of victory came into
Arcadia, and she still has the beast's tusks; nor do I think that Hylaeus and
foolish Rhoecus, even hating her, will find fault in Hades with the archer;
for their flanks will not join in the lie, those from which the Maenalian
ridge flowed with blood.

Lady of many halls, of many cities, hail, Chitone, resident at Miletus; for
Neleus made you his leader when he put to sea with ships from Cecropia.
Chesias Imbrasia, first-throned, and Agamemnon dedicated the steering-oar of
his own ship in your temple as a propitiation for the becalming, when you
bound the winds for him, when the Achaean ships sailed to harm the cities of
the Teucrians, angered over Helen of Rhamnus. Truly Proetus too seated two
temples for you, one of Coreia, because you gathered his daughters wandering
the Azenian mountains, and the one at Lousoi of Hemera, because you took the
wild spirit from the children. For you too the Amazons, lovers of war, once
set up an image on the shore at Ephesus under the trunk of an oak, and Hippo
accomplished a sanctuary for you; and they themselves, Oupis lady, danced the
war-dance around it, first the armed dance in shields, and then setting a wide
chorus in a circle; and the shrill thin pipes accompanied, so that they might
stamp together (for not yet had they bored through fawn-bones, Athena's work,
an evil for the deer); and the echo ran to Sardis and the Berecynthian
pasture. And they rattled with their feet in close beat, and the quivers
clattered. And afterward a wide foundation was built around that image, than
which Dawn will see nothing more divine nor richer; it could easily surpass
Pytho. Therefore also Lygdamis the violent, wandering, threatened to sack it;
and he led a host of horse-milking Cimmerians equal to the sand, who live
lying beside the cow's crossing of the Inachian girl. Ah wretched among kings,
how he sinned; for neither he himself was going to return back to Scythia, nor
any other of those whose wagons stood in the Caystrian meadow; for your bows
are always set before Ephesus. Lady of Munichia, watcher of the harbor, hail,
Pheraea. Let no one dishonor Artemis (for not even to Oeneus, when he
dishonored the altar, did fair contests come to the city), nor contend in
deer-shooting nor in sure aim (for not even the son of Atreus boasted at a
small price), nor woo the maiden (for neither Otus nor Orion wooed a good
marriage), nor flee the yearly chorus (for neither did Hippo without tears
refuse to circle around the altar). Hail, great queen, and meet the song
favorably.
