OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS ATHANASIUS, A DIALOGUE CONCERNING THE TRINITY,
IN WHICH ORTHODOX AND ANOMOEAN ARIAN CONVERSE. ANOMOEAN. Are you a Christian?
Orthodox. Very much so. Anom. What is Christianity? Orth. But the one thing
was necessary to say, and the other is not safe. Anom. Why? Orth. For to say
that I am a slave of Christ is necessary to say; but what Christianity is is
not safe, unless I know who the one asking is; lest I be found casting the
holy things to the dogs, or the pearls before the swine. For indeed, if you
ask me, Are you a believer or a cat =28.1117= echumen, I readily say what I
am; but what it is to be a believer, I do not say, lest the one ask ing also
be a Jew. Anom. But I, the one asking you, say to you that I am a Christian.
Orth. It was not enough for you that I said that I am a Christian; nor is it
enough for me that you say that you are a Christian; unless you tell me what
Chri stianity is. And even then you are not altogether a Christian; for
neither is knowing what Christianity is this, to be a Christian; since neither
is knowing what Judaism is this, to be a Jew; nor is knowing what Hellenism is
this, to be a Greek. Anom. Do you know God? Orth. Yes. Anom. As he himself
knows himself? Orth. No. Anom. Then you do not know him? Orth. I know him, as
it is possible for the nature of a human being to know. Anom. Otherwise then
do human beings know him, and otherwise he himself himself? Orth. Very much
so. Anom. How? Orthod. Because, unless something acquire the same knowledge as
God, it is impossible to know him as he himself knows himself. But to acquire
the same knowledge as God is impossible, not being of the same essence as he.
And we are not of the same essence as he. Therefore we do not have the same
knowledge as he. Anom. If then the Son should wish to reveal to someone, is he
not able? Orth. He reveals indeed, but he does not reveal to him thus, as he
himself knows himself; but as the one deemed worthy is able to know. Because
to Ezekiel from the loins and above he was known as electrum; and to Daniel as
ancient of days; and to Isaiah as covered by the sera phim. Anomoean. You
then, how do you know him, as elec trum, or as ancient of days? Orth. Having
learned from them I know that he is above all the things made known to them.
For if he were not above them, he would not have appeared differently; and
because of this the Only-begotten says: "No one has seen God at any time."
Anom. Is seeing one thing, and knowing another? Orth. Upon God it is not one
and another; for he himself took the vision upon the knowledge, saying: "So
long a time I am with you, and you have not known me? Philip, the one who has
seen me has seen my Father." Anom. If you know that he is above the things
that appear, you know what he is. Orth. You say a ridiculous thing; for is it
that, since I know that the soul is above the bodies that appear, I also know
what a soul is? Anom. I know him thus, as he himself knows himself. Orth. What
then is God? Anom. The unbegotten itself. Orth. To know that he is unbegotten,
this is to know God. Anom. Very much so. Orth. Then both Jews and Greeks know
this. You have then the same knowledge as the Jews and the Greeks, who do not
know him. For that the one who does not know the Son does not know the Father,
the Gospels have said. Anom. Does God then not know =28.1120= himself as
unbegotten? Orth. But this is not his essence, which we have grasped. Anom. Do
you say one Godhead, and three hypostases? Orth. Your former knowledge has
been dissolved. But I will answer you also thus: Hypostasis is one thing, and
Godhead another; not as one thing and another thing, but as hypostasis
signifying one something

signifying, and the Godhead another. For the hypostasis signifies "the being";
and the Godhead "the what-being." Anom. As, like what? Orth. As of Peter, and
Paul, and Timothy three hypostases, and one humanity. Anom. Then three gods?
Orth. May it not be said! Anom. If the hypostases are thus as of Peter, and
Paul, and Timothy, three gods; for three human beings, Peter, and Paul, and
Timothy. Orth. According to the divine Scriptures, no longer even they
themselves are three human beings. "For in Christ Jesus there is not male and
female, there is not Greek and Jew, there is not slave and free; but we all
are one in Christ." Anom. Then no longer are they three, Peter, and Paul, and
Timothy? Orth. They are three, but not three human beings. Anom. How? Orth.
Because they are three human beings, when they have a dissimilar heart, as
Greek, and Jew, and Christian; but when they say the same thing, and there are
not schisms in them, they are three hypostases, but one in the Lord, having
one soul and one heart; and they are three in number, but not by otherness of
nature or of heart. Anom. I call the three three. Orth. And I say three, when
there is schism in them; but if according to the holy Scriptures they become
fitted in the same mind, and in the same judgment, I say one new human being,
with the hypostases remaining. Anom. But these things have been said upon
human beings; but upon God otherwise. Orth. If upon those divided bodily, when
there is not schism of mind, the one and the one lie in the scripture, much
more upon the immaterial and the bodiless. Either then grant schisms there, so
that we may say three gods, the one willing this, and the other that; or, if
there is not schism, one God Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, as one human
being those fitted in Christ, as it has been written. Anomoean. Then there is
not a difference of Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit? Orth. In the nature, no;
in the will, no; in begetting and being begotten, and sending forth and
proceeding, yes. Anom. What is begetting and being begotten, and sending forth
and proceeding? Orth. That the Father begets, that is, has begotten; and the
Son is begotten, that is, has been begotten; and that the Father himself sends
forth the Spirit, that is, has sent forth. And the Spirit proceeds, that is,
has proceeded. Anom. He said, proceeds; for did he also, has proceeded? Orth.
And concerning the Son, "He begets me," =28.1121= he has said. For "Before all
hills he begets me," he said. As then upon the Son you understand "he begets
me" as He begot, so also upon the Spirit understand "proceeds" as has
proceeded; for it is a scriptural custom; as, "Who makes his angels spirits,"
that is, Who made; and, "Who founds the earth upon its security," that is,
having founded. Anom. Is this the difference? Orth. This. Anom. And do you say
the nature the same? Orth. The same. Anom. And how can it be the same nature,
if the one begets, and the other is begotten? Orth. But you nevertheless posit
that the one begets, and the other is begotten? Anom. Yes. Orth. If the one
begets, and the other is begotten; the one begetting begets from his own
nature, and the one being begotten is begotten from the nature of the one
begetting. Anom. May it not be for me to say that God begot from the nature!
Orth. Then he did not beget. Anom. He begot having created? Orth. First he
created him, and then he begot; or how? Tell me more clearly. For I do not
understand what it is, he begot having created. Anom. The offspring of God is
his creature, since he is simple and uncompounded; and he does not beget by
some things, and create by other things, but by those by which he begets, by
these he also creates. Orth. If he is simple and uncompounded, why do you say,
by those and by those? For where there is "by those and by those," there are
many. But if uncompounded, then not many. Anomoean. Then God is not simple?
Orth. But I even say beyond the simple. For indeed an angel is simple, and a
soul, and the light; for it is not composed from parts or members. Anom. If
then he is even beyond the simple, by those he begets and creates? Orth. Again
I say, do not say, by those he begets and creates; for the simple does not
have "by those and by those"; and much more that which is beyond simple does
not have "by those and by those." Anom. Then in the same way God begets, and

in the same way creates? Orth. No. Anom. Then he is compounded? Orth. May it
not be! Anom. If by other things he begets and by other things he creates, he
is compounded? Orth. I do not say, by other things he begets, and by other
things he creates, but otherwise he begets, and otherwise he creates. Anom.
How does he beget, and how does he create? Orth. By hypostasis he begets, and
by command he creates. For the command, having the power concurrent, presents
what the one commanding wishes; but begetting by hypostasis, he does not admit
a command, so that nothing may be in the middle of the begetting Father and
the begotten Son. Anom. Then not wishing he begot? Orth. The one begotten is a
living image of the one who begot, and the image of the hypostasis overpasses
the will. For first one must think the image of the hypostasis, and then the
wishing, or not wishing. And of the hypostasis the Son is offspring and
unvarying image; before the will think the image of the hypostasis. Anom. What
is it? Does the image overpass the will? Orth. Because you are able to think
the essence even without wishing this; but without an image you are not able.
For the will both begins and ceases; but =28.1124= the image of the essence
neither begins, nor ceases. And otherwise: Is he immortal wishing, or not
wishing? Anom. Wishing. Orth. Then before the will, not immortal. Anom. He
wishes everlastingly to be immortal? Orth. And everlastingly Father. For as
you are not able to say that not wishing he is immortal, nor that not wishing
Father; and as a will does not go before the immortality, so neither of the
Son; but he is everlastingly Son, having been begotten from a willing Father,
unvarying image of the hypostasis. Anomoean. Where has it been written that
the Son is image of the hypostasis? Orth. The impress of the hypostasis I
understand as nothing other than to be image of the hypostasis. Anom. For has
it not been written that the man is image and glory of God? Orth. But not
impress of the hypostasis. And it is one thing to be image of God, and another
impress of his hypostasis. For the work of the craftsman who worked has the
image of the art, but not of the hypostasis. Anom. I do not know what you say;
speak more clearly. Orth. The bench of the carpenter has the image of the art;
for if I see a bench, I know that it came to be by a carpenter; and if I see a
ship, I know that the work is by a shipwright, image and glory of the art of
the one who worked; not however also impress of the hypostasis. For the bench
or the ship does not have the impress of the one who worked; for the one is a
human being in hypostasis, and the bench or the ship is not. Thus also the
human being is image and glory of the God who set him. For indeed according to
holy Solomon: "Analogously their generator is contemplated and glorified from
that which has come to be;" not however is he impress of the hypostasis of the
one who made, but rather of the operation. But the Son is an enhypostatic
impress. For he says: "The one who has seen me has seen my Father;" and no
one, having seen a human being, has seen the Father. Anom. Where has it been
written that the Son is impress of the hypostasis? Orth. Beside the apostle
Paul in that to the Hebrews. Anom. Is it not read in church? Orth. From when
the Gospel of Christ was announced, the letter has been believed to be of
Paul; and with it there are 14 letters, all of Paul. Anom. Have you read that
in all of them his name is borne, but in this one not? Orth. Because he was
writing to Hebrews; and they had been instructed concerning him, that he
teaches apostasy; and so that they might not, having bristled at his name,
close the hearing; because of this he began from the fathers, saying: "In many
parts and in many manners of old God having spoken to our fathers in the
prophets, at the last of the days has spoken to us in a Son." And that the
letter is his is clear also from the end of the letter. For he mentions
Timothy also, saying: "Know our brother Timothy re leased =28.1125=, with
whom, if he come more quickly, I will see you." Anomoean. Even if we grant
that the Letter is of Paul, yet the impress of the hypostasis has the being in
the hypostasis, not in a life of its own? Orth. But it has been said not only
impress of hypostasis, but also Son, so that we may understand the
enhypostatic. For he has been called radiance,

because of being co-everlastingly from him; im press because of the likeness
of the hypostasis; Son because of the enhypostatic. Anom. And we say that all
things are from God. Orth. But the some are from his command creatively; and
the Son from the hypostasis begettingly; and the Spirit processionally. Anom.
And do you say the Spirit from the hypostasis? Orth. Since from where? Anom.
Where has it been written that the some are from the command, and the others
from the hypostasis? Orth. David says concerning those from the command:
"Praise the Lord from the heavens, praise him in the highest, praise him, all
his angels, praise him, all his powers, praise him, sun and moon, praise him,
all the stars and the light. Because he spoke, and they came to be; he
commanded, and they were created." And concerning the begetting of the Son it
has been written: "From the womb before the morning star I begot you;" and
concerning the Holy Spirit, "In the last days I will pour out from my Spirit
upon all flesh;" and in other places: "For a Spirit proceeds from beside me;"
and in other places: "By the Word of the Lord the heavens were made firm, and
by the Spirit of his mouth all their power." Anom. He said from womb and
mouth, not from hypostasis. Orth. Upon God when members or parts are said,
they are said human-passionately, but they are understood God-fittingly; and
if not, what bodily womb upon God are we to understand? I, hearing womb,
understand the begetting power of the nature, and mouth the teaching power;
for indeed also upon human beings the Scripture has at times used this custom,
as when it says: "From your fear, Lord, we have conceived in womb, and we have
brought forth a spirit of salvation upon the earth." For here too it does not
say our bodily womb, but the begetting power of the nature. Anomoean. Where
has it been written nature upon God? Orth. Beside the Apostle: "But then, not
knowing God, you were enslaved to those who are not gods by nature; but now,
knowing God, or rather being known by God," by nature, clearly. And in the
catholic Letters it has been written: "Through which the greatest and precious
promises have been given to us, that you may become partakers of divine
nature." And we become partakers of divine nature by the communion of the Holy
Spirit. Anom. Then =28.1128= is the Spirit also of divine nature? Orth. If we
by the communion of the Holy Spirit are of divine nature, is not the Spirit
the one furnishing us the communion? Anom. And so do you say the Spirit is of
divine nature? Orth. Not simply of divine nature, but of the essence. For the
Father himself said: "In the last days I will pour out from my Spirit upon all
flesh;" and the Son: "For you are not the ones speaking, but the Spirit of my
Father speaking in you." And Job called the Spirit divine, saying thus: "The
Lord lives, who has thus judged me, and the Almighty who has embittered my
soul; if indeed while the breath is still in me, and a divine spirit that
remains for me in nostrils, my mouth will not speak unjust things." You see
how he mentions the Holy Trinity: "The Lord lives," saying the Son; and, "The
Almighty who has embittered my soul," the Father; "And a divine spirit that
remains for me in nostrils," the Holy Spirit. For the Spirit is a fragrance of
Christ; and as from the fragrance through the nostrils the myrrh is known, so
also from the Spirit Christ. "For no one is able to say Lord Jesus except in
Holy Spirit." And the apostles were a fragrance of Christ, and are; since they
are also temples of the Spirit. For Paul says: "Do you not know that your
bodies are a temple of the Holy Spirit in you, which you have from God, and
you are not your own. Glorify then God in your body." Anomoean. If otherwise
God creates, and otherwise he begets, is he compounded? Orth. Compounded he is
not; for may it not be for me to say! but by hypostasis he begets, and by
command he creates. And if as he creates, so also he begets, his creature is
his offspring. Anom. Very much so. Orth. And he created the dog; dare to say
that the dog has been begotten from the womb, and some other of the creatures.
For I say,

that both the dog and all the irrational things together with all the rest of
the creation God created; say yourself also that he also begot them from the
womb, if indeed in the same way he creates and begets. Anom. Then otherwise he
creates, and otherwise he begets? Orth. Very much so. Anom. Is he not
uncompounded? Orth. I did not say he is not uncompounded, but he is even
beyond the uncompounded; and otherwise he begets and otherwise he creates.
Anom. How is it possible? Orth. The mind in us, which has come to be according
to the image of God, is it not uncompounded? and otherwise it begets a word,
and otherwise it creates the things it creates. Anom. How otherwise does it
beget and otherwise create? and yet the mind by those by which it creates,
also begets. Orth. A lie; for it begets a word from itself, but it creates
from matter a house, a ship, and as many such things. Anom. Then God also
creates from matter? Orth. The some from matter, and the others, not being, he
hypostasized. Anom. How? Orth. The matter and the =28.1129= heavens and the
angels he hypostasized from nothing. But the will, having the power
concurrent, hypostasized as many things as it wished; and the mortal animals
he made from the matter. Anom. This I accept. Orth. And do you not say the Son
simple and uncompounded? Anom. Yes. Orth. Then do you also say the creature of
the Son an offspring? Anom. No. Orth. If because of this you say the creature
of the Father an offspring, since he is simple, and the offspring a creature;
and the Son also is simple, and his creature is an offspring; and the Father
will be found father of the Son, and grandfather of the creation. Anom. But of
the Father the offspring is a creature, and of the Son the creature is a
creature. Orth. Since God is simple, his creature is his offspring, and the
offspring a creature; or how? Anom. Very. Orth. Then also of the Son the
creature is an offspring, he being uncompounded; or then not because of the
uncompoundedness of the Father is the offspring a creature, or the creature an
offspring, even if he is uncompounded. And the Son is offspring of the Father;
therefore not a creature. Anom. The Father is cause of the Son, and of the
creation the Son. Orth. And of the creation is not the Father also cause?
Anom. But through the Son. Orth. And do you posit at all that he is cause also
of the creation? Anom. Yes. For all things are from God. Orth. Then all the
offsprings also are his, dogs, ravens, vultures, and all the creation both
rational and irrational. Anom. I said that they were created through the Son;
and the Son alone has been begotten without a mediator, that is, has been
created. Orth. Since only from only, because of this do you call him both
offspring and creature; or how? Anom. Yes. Orth. And all the other things,
since they came to be through the Son, are creatures, but no longer also off
springs? Anom. Thus. Orth. The mediation then of the Son lessened the
operation of the Father. Anom. Why? Orth. Because, only begetting only, in the
same way he both begets and creates. But with the Son mediating, no longer is
his creature his offspring; and, as it appears, to the harm of the
principalities and the authorities, and of all the creation together, the Son
was begotten. Anom. Why? Orth. For all the creatures were going to be his
offsprings, if the Son had not been begotten. Anom. Then we were not created
through the Son? Orth. Very much so. But we were created through the Son, and
the Son has been begotten from God, and has not been created. Anom. He himself
says: "The Lord created me a beginning of his ways." Orth. And he himself
says: "They dug my hands and my feet, and they divided my garments for
themselves, and upon my clothing they cast a lot." Anom. What =28.1132=
likeness, "he created me," and, "they dug my hands and feet?" Orth. As what
was going to come to be upon the cross, he was saying as already having come
to be; so what was going to come to be upon the incarnation, he was saying as
already having come to be. At any rate he did not say: The Lord created me
before a beginning of a way; but since the way of the others was being
destroyed, and a way recent and living was going to come to be for us, because
of this he says: "The Lord created me a beginning of his ways for his works."
Anom. "Before the age he founded me," he says, "before making the earth."
Orth. Say yourself also: "You do not yet have forty years," and you were
founded before the age? so that you may hear: "Amen, amen I say to you, before
the ages came to be, I am." Anom. And was his incarnation before the ages came
to be? Orth. By the foreknowledge even the very

pas sion; so as for him to say: "They dug my hands and feet, and they gave
gall into my food, and for my thirst they gave me vinegar to drink." Anom. But
this was not said before the age, but from the times of David. Orth. And from
the times of the son of David, that is Solomon, the, "The Lord created me,"
has been said. Anom. But, "Before the age he founded me," has been said. Orth.
For "Before the age this mystery was hidden," as Paul says, "which now has
been manifested to the sons of human beings." And that he himself is the thing
founded before the ages, hear him saying also in other places: "For another
foundation no one is able to lay besides the one lying, which is Christ
Jesus," the one saying: "Before the age he founded me." And Christ Jesus has
been called God Word from then, from when the Word became flesh; and it was
said by the angel: "You will call his name Jesus." Therefore the, "The Lord
created me," and, "Before the age he founded me," has been said concerning his
incarnation, of the mystery truly hidden before the ages. Anom. And how is he
able to beget from his hypostasis, and not suffer? Orth. Do not examine the,
how is he able; but the, from the hypostasis, has been shown. Anom. How has it
been shown? Orth. That he is not a creature, has been shown. And that which is
not a creature is either unbegotten, or begotten, or proceeding as from a
cause. And he is not unbegotten, being a begotten Son; therefore then he is
from the hypostasis. Anom. And how is he able to be from the hypostasis? Orth.
If he is not from the hypostasis, he is either a creature, or unbegotten; and
he is not unbegotten, being Son; he is not a creature; for he did not command,
and he came to be; but from the womb he begot him; from the hypostasis then,
and not from things not being, as those that came to exist by command. Anom.
Then again you think God compounded, saying womb. Orth. May it not be to say
God compounded! but, as =28.1133= I said above, the womb, which the Scripture
said, of God, I understand as the begetting of the hypostasis. For indeed also
upon us, as has been shown beforehand, the Scripture has used this custom,
saying: "From your fear, Lord, we have conceived in womb, and we have been in
travail, and we have brought forth a Spirit of salvation." Saying in womb, not
the bodily, but in the hypostasis itself. Anom. You almost persuade me to
become a homoousiast. Orth. May God give you to become a Christian; for when
you confess the Son consubstantial with the Father, then you have become a
Christian. Anom. What is consubstantial? for I wish to learn this also. Is the
Son and Father the same one? Orth. May it not be said! Anom. What then is it?
for truly I wish to learn. Orth. Consubstantial is that which admits the same
account of the essence; as a human being differs in nothing from a human
being, insofar as he is a human being; an angel differs in nothing from an
angel, insofar as he is an angel; so also God differs in nothing from God,
insofar as he is God. Anom. Then the Father differs in nothing from the Son?
Orth. In being God and Father, no; in being Father and Son, yes; because the
one begot, and the other is begotten; and this is the consubstantial. Anom.
Then in no other thing does the Father differ from the Son? And yet it has
been written: "Why do you say I am good? No one is good except one, God."
Orth. Then the Son is not good? Anom. He himself said: "Why do you say I am
good? No one is good except one, God." Orth. You see that I said well, when
you confess the Son consubstantial with the Father, then you become a Chris
tian? Anom. I believe that I am a Christian. Orth. If you were a Christian,
you would not say that the Son is not good. Anom. I did not say that he is not
good, but that he himself said: "No one is good except one, God." Orth. And do
you say the Father differs in the goodness, from the Son? Anom. He himself
said: "Why do you say I am good? No one is good except one, God." Orth. He did
not say: Do not call me good, but, "Why do you say I am good?" Anom. And what
is the difference of, Do not call me good, and of, Why do you say I am good?
Orth. Much. Anom. What sort? Orth. The, Do not call me good, is a denial of
goodness; but the, "Why do you say I am good?" instead of, For what do you

call me good, not confessing me God? as, "And why do you call me, Lord, Lord,
and you do not do the things I say?" Since he approached him as a mere human
being, simply naming a teacher, because of this he says: "Why do you say I am
good," not believing that I am God? "For no one is good except one, God."
Anom. And we say that he is good, but not good thus, as the Father. Orth. And
yet it has not been said, No one is good, except one, the Father; but "No one
is good, except one, God." And how do you say the goodness of the Father
differs from the goodness of the Son? =28.1136= Do you think wickedness has
been mixed with the goodness of the Son, and because of this the goodness of
the Father differs? Anom. May it not be! I do not say wickedness has been
mixed with the goodness of the Son, but that the goodness of the Father
differs from that of the Son. Orth. But goodness differs in nothing from
goodness, insofar as it is goodness. Anom. Does the goodness of God not differ
from the goodness in us? Orth. For there is in us some other goodness besides
God himself. Anom. Then do the Jews and Greeks have God in themselves? Orth.
But neither does the one simply called a Christian have God in himself, but
that one has him who has beaten the body under and enslaved it, so as to be
able to say: "Since you seek a proof of Christ speaking in me." For such a one
hears from Christ: "The good human being from the good treasure of the heart
brings forth the good things." Anom. If there is a good human being who brings
forth from the heart the good things, how is no one good except one, God?
Orth. Because God is not good according to participation of goodness, but he
himself is goodness; and the human being is good by participation of goodness.
Anom. And are the angels not good by nature? Orth. By nature, no; by
participation, yes. For that which is good by nature, which is by nature, is
not turned. Anom. As what? Orth. A human being by nature is not turned; for he
does not become something else by nature; but by choice he is turned; for you
will see the same one at one time wicked, at another time good; as Paul, at
one time a persecutor, and at another time an evangelist; and the same one at
one time a disciple, at another time a betrayer, as Judas; and angels, at one
time wicked, and at another time good. Anom. For indeed are the angels at one
time wicked, and at one time good? Orth. The Scripture said: "For if he did
not spare angels who sinned." And if they sinned, it is clear that they fell
from the goodness. And in general all the worthy creation of God is called and
is good by participation of goodness; but God, being good by nature, himself
is goodness; wherefore also no one is good, except one, God. Anom. And is the
Son then good thus, as the Father? Orth. Very much so. Anom. And how can it be
said "No one is good, except one, God," if Son and Father are God? Orth. One
God Son and Father, not by the hypostasis, but by the same account of the
essence; as all human beings in Christ are one, not by the hypostasis, but by
the account of the essence and by the agreements. Anom. For is essence one
thing, and hypostasis another? Orth. One and another, not as a thing. Anom.
Then a composition, Orth. One and another, I said; not as a thing one and
another, but as the hypostasis signifying one something, and the essence
another something; as the grain of wheat is said, and is seed and fruit not as
a thing =28.1137= one and another; but seed signifies one something, and fruit
another something; because the seed is seed of the coming husbandry; and the
fruit is fruit of the past husbandry. Anom. What does the essence signify, and
what the hypostasis? Orth. The essence signifies the commonality; and if there
is anything proper of the essence, this is common of the hypostases under the
essence; and the hypostasis has a property, which is not common of the
hypostases of the same essence, together with also admitting number. Anom. For
does the essence not have a property? Orth. Neither does it have a property,
not being common of the hypostases of the essence; nor does it admit a plural
number, being one. And that which admits a plural number altogether has a
property; and that which has a property admits number. For the property itself
introduces the number. Anom. I do not understand what you say; speak

more clearly. Orth. Do you say the essence of the Father is immortal, and
incorruptible, and invisible, and craftsman, and God? Anom. Yes. Orth. And do
you say the Son likewise immortal, and incorruptible, and invisible, and
craftsman, and God? Anom. Yes. Orth. As then you say the essence of the Father
immortal, incorruptible, invisible, craftsman, so also do you say of the Son
the same essence? Anom. Yes. Orth. Do you not say the Son Father? Anom. No.
Orth. Then Father is not proper of the essence, but of the hypostasis. Anom.
How? Orth. Because the things proper of the essence are likewise common of the
Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit; but Father is not common;
therefore Father is not proper of the essence, but of the hypostasis. And if
the proper of the hypostasis is another, and the properties of the essence are
other, essence and hypostasis are not the same thing signified. Anom. Is the
unbegotten not proper of the Father? Orth. It is not. Anom. Then is there some
other of the things that are unbegotten? Orth. Yes. Anom. What? Orth. Begotten
is that which has a father, and unbegotten that which does not have a father?
Anom. Yes. Orth. Does the Holy Spirit have a Father? Anom. No. Orth. Then it
also is unbegotten; therefore the unbegotten is not proper of the Father.
Anom. But the Father is both unbegotten and ungenerated. Orth. Nor is the
ungenerated proper of the Father, but it is common of Father, and Son, and
Holy Spirit. Anom. How? Orth. Ungenerated is that which does not have a
generator; or how? Anom. Yes. Orth. And that which has a generator is
generated. And neither does the Son have a generator, nor the Spirit (for the
Son has been shown from the womb, and the Spirit from the mouth). Therefore
the ungenerated is not proper of the Father. Anom. What then other is proper
of the Father if =28.1140= neither the unbegotten is proper of him, nor the
ungenerated? Orth. Proper of the Father is, Father; proper of the Son is, Son;
of the Holy Spirit, to be Holy Spirit of God. Anom. For is the Father not holy
spirit? Orth. The Father also is holy spirit, but not Spirit of God. Then
proper of the hypostasis of the Father is, Father; for in no way is the Father
Son; and proper of the hypostasis of the Son is, Son; for in no way is the Son
Father; and proper of the hypostasis of the Holy Spirit is to be Holy Spirit
of God; and in no way is the Holy Spirit of God, Father or Son. And the
essence of Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, is common; for likewise immortal,
likewise incorruptible, likewise holy, likewise good. And because of this we
say one essence, three hypostases. Anom. Where has it been written that the
Spirit is called, of God? Orth. Paul the apostle says: "For no one of human
beings knows the things of the human being, except the spirit of the human
being in him; thus also the things of God no one knows except the Spirit of
God." Anom. The Spirit of God he says God himself. Orth. Read the Apostle, so
that you may hear him saying thus: "Things which an eye has not seen, and an
ear has not heard, and upon a heart of a human being have not gone up, which
God prepared for those who love him; but to us God also revealed them through
his Spirit; for the Spirit searches all things, even the depths of God. For
who of human beings knows the things of the human being, except the spirit of
the human being? thus also the things of God no one knew, except the Spirit of
God." Anom. And why should we not also say three essences? Orth. Because it is
necessary that property be given to the number; and the property is otherness;
and otherness of the essence makes heteroousion; and the heteroousion is not
likewise immortal, not likewise incorruptible, not likewise holy, not likewise
unturned. And likewise Father and Son and Holy Spirit, immortal, holy,
unturned. Therefore one essence of Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. And when
we say three hypostases of Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, we say that the
difference of the properties is true in the hypostasis, but not also in the
essence. Anom. And do you say at all a difference upon Father, and Son, and
Holy Spirit? Orth. This difference I say, that the Father is not Son, nor the
Son Father nor the Holy Spirit Father or Son; so that you may not suppose bare
names

hearing Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, but hypostases truly being and
existing; and when we say one essence, that whatever we understand the essence
of the Father to be, this the Son is, this also the Spirit of his Father;
concerning which the Son himself said: "For you are not the ones speaking, but
the Spirit of your Father speaking in you." And because of this =28.1141= it
has been said: "No one is good, except one, God;" the Father being good, and
the Son, and the Holy Spirit, not by participation of goodness, but by
identity of essence. Anom. For do you say the Spirit also good? Orth. The
prophet David says: "Your good Spirit will guide me in a straight land." Anom.
And human beings are called good. Orth. Yes, but by participation of goodness.
Anom. And I say the Spirit. Orth. Then is he able also to cast away the
goodness, as Judas, and as the devil? Anom. If he should wish, yes. Orth. May
it not be for me to be impious before the Lord, so as even to take this into
mind! Anom. Then do you say the Spirit good thus, as the Father and the Son?
Orth. Very much so; for indeed it is not good by participation of goodness,
but is auto-good, that is, goodness. At any rate Matthew the evangelist says
that the Lord said: "If then you, being wicked, know to give good gifts to
your children, how much more will your heavenly Father give good things to
those who ask him?" and Luke: "If then you, being wicked, know to give good
gifts to your children, how much more will the heavenly Father give Holy
Spirit to those who ask him?" You hear, if you have ears, that the evangelists
knowing the Spirit to be the same as the goodness, and the goodness as the
Spirit; the one called "the good things" the Spirit itself, and the other
called the Spirit itself "the good things." Anom. Then is the Spirit also God?
Orth. Opposite, no. Anom. What does opposite wish to be? Orth. That which is
arrayed against the will of the Father. And the Spirit is not arrayed against,
but is from the Godhead; and because of this we say one Godhead of Father, and
Son, and Holy Spirit. Anom. Then one hypostasis. Orth. No. Anom. If there is
one Godhead, there is also one hypostasis. Orth. If you knew the gift of God,
you would know that Godhead signifies the "being" common to the hypostases,
and hypostasis the "being," and essence "the identity." As humanity, the being
something common; and hypostasis the being; and essence the identity; for not
every hypostasis is Godhead, nor every hypostasis humanity. And if not every
hypostasis is humanity, nor every hypostasis Godhead, then hypostasis does not
signify the same as Godhead and humanity. Anom. Does the humanity not exist?
Orth. Yes, contemplated in individuals. Anom. Then it is also a hypostasis.
Orth. I said, contemplated in individuals. Anom. What is an individual, and
what is humanity? Orth. An individual is the hypostasis of each of us; and
humanity the commonality of the essence. Anom. And what is the difference of
commonality and hypostasis? Orth. That your hypostasis is not of all human
beings; and the humanity is common of all =28.1144= human beings. Anom. Is my
hypostasis not another thing than humanity? Orth. No. Anom. How then? Orth.
Because the humanity is common of all human beings, and the hypostasis the
property of each. Anom. Is the hypostasis of the Father not Godhead? Orth.
Yes. But the Godhead signifies the being something, and the hypostasis the
being. Anom. And is the hypostasis of the Son not Godhead? Orth. Yes. Anom.
Then two Godheads. Orth. No. Anom. How then? Orth. For it is not another and
another Godhead. Anom. It is another and another hypostasis. Orth. But I said
that Godhead and hypostasis do not signify the same; for the hypostasis the
being, and the Godhead the being something; for as there are not two
humanities of Peter and Paul, neither two Godheads of Father and Son. Anom.
Then not the hypostasis of the Father? Orth. The hypostasis of the Father is
also Godhead, and the hypostasis of the Son also Godhead; but not, since there
are two hypostases, also two Godheads. Anom. How not? Orth. Because also
immortality is hypostasis of the Father, and

immortality is hypostasis of the Son, and not two immortalities. Anom. And are
there not two immortalities? Orth. No. Anom. How? Orth. Because immortality is
not different; for immortality differs in nothing from immortality, insofar as
it is immortality. Anom. If truly there is another hypostasis of Father and
Son, how not another and another immortality? Orth. There is another and
another hypostasis of Father and Son, but not another and another immortality.
For indeed we also have another and another hypostasis, but not another and
another humanity. And the hypostasis of Peter is a human being, and the
hypostasis of Paul is a human being; and there are two hypostases, and not two
humanities. And we have another and another hypostasis, but not another and
another immortality, which we received through the one baptism. For if another
and another immortality, another and another baptism, and another and another
faith, and there is not "one Lord, one faith, one baptism." Anom. But our
hypostasis is another besides the immortality and the baptism; therefore also
the hypostasis of God is another besides the immortality. Orth. No. But the
hypostasis itself is also immortality; and not only immortality, but also
incorruptibility, and righteousness, and sanctification, and redemption, and
lordship, and power. And God is not these things according to composition, but
being said according to different conceptions; immortality because of the
unending; and incorruptibility, because of the insoluble; righteousness
because of the equal; sanctification because of keeping from sin; redemption
because of saving our life from corruption; lordship, because of ruling all
things; power, because of nothing being opposed to him. And all these things
are contemplated also around the hypostasis =28.1145= of the Son, likewise
with the Father, and not participatively; and because of this we say two
hypostases of Father and Son, but one Godhead, power, lordship, and
immortality, and incorruptibility, and as many such things. Anom. Then do you
also say one unbegottenness. Orth. I say the Son ungenerated, but not
unbegotten. Anom. For is ungenerated one thing, and unbegotten another? Orth.
Very much so. Anom. How? Orth. Because the begotten is that which is begotten
from the hypostasis, and because of this said from the womb; and the generated
that which is from the authority of the generator. "For all things as many as
he wished, he made." And how did he make? "He spoke, and they came to be; he
commanded, and they were created." Anom. I did not ask what begotten is, and
what generated; but whether the ungenerated is another, and the unbegotten
another. Orth. If the begotten is that from the hypostasis and from the womb,
that which is not from the womb, that is from the hypostasis, is unbegotten;
and if the generated is from the authority by command, the ungenerated is that
which exists not by command, but either being uncaused, or from the hypostasis
of the unbegotten. Anom. Then do you say two ungenerated? Orth. I say both
Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit ungenerated, but not three fatherless, nor
uncaused; but the Father, both fatherless, and uncaused, and ungenerated. For
indeed neither does the Father have a Father, nor a generator, nor a cause of
being as from a hypostasis. And the Son has a Father, but does not have a
generator; for he did not come to be, but was begotten. And the Holy Spirit
does not have a Father, for it was not begotten; it does not have a generator,
for it did not come to be; but it has as cause God, whose Spirit it is, from
beside whom it also proceeds. Anom. For do the other spirits not proceed from
beside him? Orth. And we were begotten from God; "For as many as received
him," it has been written, "he gave them authority to become children of God,
who were begotten not from bloods, nor from will of flesh, but from God." But
as we, having been begotten from God, were not begotten from the womb of the
hypostasis, so also the proceeding spirits from God are not from the mouth of
his hypostasis. And the Holy Spirit is Spirit from the mouth of the
hypostasis; "For by the Word of the Lord the heavens were made firm, and by
the Spirit of his mouth all their power." And the Father says: "Behold my
Child, my beloved, in whom

my soul was well pleased. I will set my Spirit upon him." Anom. If both the
Son is from the hypostasis, and the Spirit from the hypostasis, are they
brothers? Orth. May it not be! no one says this. For how is the Son
only-begotten, if there are two brothers? Anom. How then both the Son from the
hypostasis and the Spirit, and not two =28.1148= brothers? Orth. Because of
this the Son has been said not only from the hypostasis, but also from the
womb; and the Spirit has been said not only from the hypostasis, but also
Spirit of mouth; so that the difference may not come to be according to the
account from the hypostasis, but according to that from womb and mouth. Anom.
Then mouth is another, and womb another, and hypostasis another, Orth. Not
another and another, as a thing, as some three-named thing. Anom. But as what?
Orth. As the hypostasis signifying one something, and the womb another, and
the mouth another. Anom. What does the hypostasis signify, and what the mouth,
and what the womb? Orth. The hypostasis signifies the being; the womb the
begetting; the mouth the teaching. And how God begets the Son, I do not busy
myself; and how he sends forth the Spirit, likewise I do not busy myself; but
I believe that both the Son is begotten unspeakably and without passion, and
the Spirit proceeds unspeakably and without passion. Anom. Where there is
mouth, and womb, and hypostasis, what else is it than to say a composition?
Orth. The names do not make a composition; for they are not altogether said
upon members. Anom. Is the mouth not a mouth? and is the womb not a womb?
Orth. Not every mouth is the same mouth, nor every womb the same womb; for not
every mouth is a member, nor every womb is altogether a member. Anom. And how
can a mouth not be a mouth, and a womb not be a womb? Orth. The Scripture in
many places named a mouth of a sword; and no one is so mad as to say that the
sword has a member, which is called mouth; but it named the consuming itself
of the sword a mouth; and, from what womb crystal proceeds, having said, it
does not seek a bodily womb; and having said a face of fire, it does not say
the fire human-formed, and the one part of it to be a face, and the other
fire; and having said a face of earth, it does not say the earth human-formed.
Anom. For where has it been said that the earth has a face? Orth. "The one
fleeing will be as the one fleeing from the face of fire," it has been written
beside the prophet. And in Genesis Cain says: "If you cast me out today from
the face of the earth, I will also be hidden from your face." Anom. But when
these names are said upon animals, they signify members; but upon the
insensible things abusively. Orth. Does the soul not seem to you to be an
animal? Anom. Very much so. For indeed an angel also is an animal, and all the
spirits; for because of this the seraphim also are called animals and, "O God,
in the midst of two animals you will be known," it has been written. Orth. If
then God says: "Open your mouth, and I will fill it," what mouth does he say?
And if David in the 118th psalm: "I opened my mouth, I drew Spirit, because
=28.1149= I chose your commandments," what mouth does he say? And if Job says:
"And a true mouth will fill with laughter," what mouth does he say? And with
what mouth was Moses crying out hearing: "Why do you cry to me?" And who does
not know that God hears even those being silent, crying with unspeakable
groanings? And what womb does the prophet say: "From your fear, Lord, we have
conceived in womb, and we have been in travail, and we have brought forth a
spirit of salvation upon the earth?" As then the soul is said to have both a
womb, and a mouth, and eyes, and ears, and all the other members of the body,
and is not composed from members; so, when you hear upon God, the truly
bodiless, mouth or womb, do not think a composition, but womb the begetting,
mouth the teaching, eyes the contemplating, ears the hearing. And because of
this the Son has been said from the womb, since he has been begotten; and
because of this Spirit of mouth the Spirit, since teaching. And as you do not
say the Spirit begotten, lest you say two sons; so do not say it generated,
lest you say it one of the generated things, and be forced also to say slave
because of the prophet who said,

that "All things together are slaves of you." Anom. I said also at the
beginning of the discourse, you almost persuade me to become a homoousiast.
Orth. May it come to be for you to say, to become a Christian! For having
become a Christian, you will recognize that one must say consubstantial,
having been taught from the divine Scriptures. For as many things as are
proper of God, these are common of Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. Anom. How
has it been said beside the holy Apostle, that to God alone it is impossible
to lie? Orth. And this will be said upon Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. For
what is proper of the nature, this is common of the hypostases. Anom. And are
the seraphim able to lie? Orth. If they are not able to lie, no longer is God
alone unlying. Anom. He did not say that to God alone it is impossible to lie,
but that it is impossible for God to lie. Orth. Everything that is generated
is able to lie; and because of this the devil also, being in the midst of the
cherubim, was able to lie. And this is the turning of the rational powers, to
lie. And God, being unturned, is also unlying; and as he alone is unturned,
and alone immortal, and alone invisible, and alone incorruptible, so also
alone unlying; and this is common of Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. Anom.
How then has it been said concerning the Spirit, that it will not speak from
itself, but as many things as it hears, it will speak, if indeed it is of the
same nature? Orth. For this is what shows it also to be unlying, and being of
the same nature. Anom. How? Orth. Because everything generated, if it is
rational, when it wishes, also speaks from itself, since it also has a will of
its own, which it silences, so that it may say the will of God =28.1152=. And
the Spirit not thus, but as Spirit of God, it has the same will as God; and
this is the, "It will not speak from itself." For indeed also concerning
himself the Son said that "From myself I do nothing, but as the Father taught
me, these things I speak." Then did the Word of God, the wisdom of God, not
know what he would speak? May it not be for a Christian human being to reckon
this! but so that we may know the sameness of the nature, and the undivided of
the will, we are taught these things; and if not, Paul indeed says: "And
concerning the virgins I do not have a command of the Lord, but I give a
judgment as having been shown mercy;" and the Son receives a command, what he
should say, and what he will speak, and the Spirit does not speak from itself.
Anom. Then the Spirit does not have a will of its own. Orth. Opposite of God,
may it not be! for neither does it at one time wish the things of God, and at
another time its own; but if the Holy Spirit wishes anything, this is a will
of God; and if God wishes anything, this is a will of the Spirit. Anom. For do
the prophets not wish whatever God wishes? Orth. Not always; for there is a
time when they also spoke from themselves. For Moses first was saying: "I am
weak-voiced and slow-tongued, and Pharaoh will not hear me;" and the, "I beg
you, appoint another," what does it signify if not that there is another will
in himself, which he chastened, so that he might do that of God? And Aaron and
Miriam, being moved from themselves, murmured against Moses, when Miriam
became leprous, and the Lord was angered with wrath. Jeremiah, declining the
prophecy, was saying: "I am younger, and I am not able to prophesy." And Jonah
declined to such a point, as even to seem to flee from the face of the Lord.
And David, having done his own will, and numbering the people, is rebuked. For
I am silent concerning the things according to Uriah the Hittite. For indeed
all the rational creatures at one time speak their own things, at another time
the things of God; and the Spirit not thus; for not at one time the things of
God, and at another time its own; for there is not some other will in it,
since neither in the Son, but there is one will of Father, and Son, and Holy
Spirit. And because of this it has been said concerning the Spirit: "It will
not speak from itself;" and concerning the Son, that "From myself I do
nothing, but as the Father taught me, these things I speak;" not because he
does not know to speak something, but because the Father does not wish one
thing, and the Son wishes another, and the Spirit another. Anom. If even the
prophets did not always say the things of God, as the discourse showed, yet
the apostles did not even speak from themselves, but

always the things of God. Orth. The Scripture taught us that the apostles
spoke =28.1153= from themselves. Anom. How? Orth. If then you hear Paul saying
concerning the apostles, "But when I saw that they do not walk straight toward
the truth of the Gospel, I said to Cephas before all" (and you know the things
following, if you are a lover of letters), what else do you reckon, than that
they had spoken and done something from themselves, which was not
God-breathed? And concerning himself also he says: "For I am conscious of
nothing for myself; but I have not been justified in this." And the Spirit not
thus; and because of this it has been said that "It will not speak from
itself." Anom. But also all human beings at one time say their own things, and
at another time the things of God; but the angels always say the things of
God. Orth. Nor is this true. For if they always said the things of God, they
would not have sinned, so as to be said: "For if he did not spare angels who
sinned." Anom. But even if some of the angels sinned at some time, yet there
are angels without sin. Orth. We do not seek whether there are angels without
sin, but whether they are able to sin. For indeed we also have not died until
now, and we are mortal by being able to die, not by having already died; for
it is possible also for one of us not to die, as Elijah. And even if none of
us should die, yet the nature itself is mortal. So that also the myriads of
the angels did not lie, but they kept the words of God; but their nature was
shown turnable, and speaking from itself, by some of them having been turned
and speaking from themselves. And the Holy Spirit does not speak from itself.
For it is not of generated nature, so as also to be able to be turned. Anom.
For from where, that the Spirit is not able to be turned? Orth. That which
furnishes unturnedness to others must of necessity be unturned by nature.
Anom. For there are then also other unturned things. For if the Spirit
furnishes the unturnedness to others, those very ones, to whom the Spirit
furnishes the unturnedness, are unturned. Orth. But the some are unturned by
participation, and the others by nature, as the fire is by nature burning, and
the water becomes burning according to juxtaposition of the fire. Thus the
Spirit is by nature unturned, and those partaking of it unturned according to
participation. Anom. Do you not wish to say that the Spirit itself also is
Father? Orth. May it not be! Anom. If all the proper things of the Father are
also common of the Spirit and of the Son, the Spirit ought also to be Father
and Son. Orth. I did not say: The proper things of the Father are also common
of the Son and of the Spirit; but, The proper things of the essence of God are
common of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit; as the
incorruptible, the immortal, the unchangeable, the unlying, and as many such
things. Anom. Then God is another, and the Father another? Orth. May it not
be! but the, God, makes the nature known, and Father, the relation =28.1156=
toward the Son. As many things then as are proper of the nature, these are
common of the hypostases of the same essence. At any rate also of the
greatness of God there is not a limit, it has been said beside the prophet;
and this one would say also concerning the Son, and concerning the Spirit; for
neither of the greatness of the Son is there a limit, nor of the greatness of
the Spirit. "For where shall I go from your Spirit? and from your face where
shall I flee?" And of all the creatures there is both a limit and a quantity.
For I am able to think Gabriel, where Raphael is not; but Father and Son and
Holy Spirit I understand everywhere and in all and through all. Anom. And I
say that everywhere and in all and through all is Father and Son and Holy
Spirit. Orth. This then is consubstantial, that which admits the same account
of the essence. Anom. Then do you not say the Father greater than the Son?
Orth. As God of God, no; but as Father of a Son who became human. At any rate
he did not say: God is greater than I; but, "The Father is greater than I."
Anom. Then do you say the Son equal to the Father in the Godhead? Orth. Very
much so; for indeed the Apostle also says: "Let this be thought in you, which
also in Christ Jesus; who, existing in form of God, did not consider it
plunder to be

equal to God." Anom. What then he himself did not plunder, why do you give it
to him? Orth. Because he did not plunder, but has it by nature; and having
this, "he emptied himself, having taken form of a slave." For what praise was
it for the one not having the equal, and not plundering? For if this is
praise, the heaven also will be praised likewise, and Gabriel, and Raphael,
and each of the angels, and each of the archangels, because they did not
plunder the being equal to God. Anom. We say Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit,
as bishop, and presbyter, and deacon. Orth. But as one not being a bishop, but
being a human being, has the same nature as the bishop; likewise also the
deacon, being neither presbyter nor bishop, is nevertheless of the same
nature; so then do you yourself also say the Son is not Father, but of the
same nature as the Father; and the Spirit of God, not being Son nor Father,
but being of the same nature as the Father. Why then do you deceive the
hearers, not knowing the things you say, and you do not say three unlike
natures as angel, and human being, and horse? Anom. Thus I say; for if I do
not say this, I became a homoousiast =28.1157=. Orth. You knew then that the
one saying the Father as bishop, and the Son as presbyter, and the Spirit as
deacon, says consubstantial. And saying thus you thought yourself a Christian
until the present. And if the one saying this has not been judged by you to be
a Christian, you have then condemned yourself, and from your mouth you have
been judged not being a Christian. Anom. Then is the one saying consubstantial
not a Christian? Orth. The one saying consubstantial is a Christian; and the
one thinking that the one saying consubstantial is not a Christian, and
himself saying it, will be found condemning himself, as that wicked slave, the
one condemned from his own mouth. Anom. For how was I found saying it? Orth.
You said that, We say Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, as bishop, presbyter,
and deacon. Anom. I confess to have said, as bishop, presbyter, and deacon.
