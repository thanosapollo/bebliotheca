There are certain people clever at admonishing others while leaving themselves
aside. And if indeed they did it coming straight on, there would have been a
gain. For either one would have been persuaded, or, having persuaded, he would
have been rid of it. But now, putting forward others as servants of the
advice, they force one truly to fight with shadows. And the preambles too are
terribly circular from them: /as I have goodwill toward him,/ /that I am one
of those who love him/ and such things. And these things are worse than being
seasick. And so recently too someone, having made the same problem, ordered
one of my people to report to me that I had not rightly made a remark in
passing in the midst of the encomium and the speeches to the goddess; for it
was not likely, he said, that anyone should say something about himself and
his own affairs, especially among those who know accurately, and that everyone
certainly knows about our speeches how far they excel, for this he added, and
that if anything else has been accomplished for anyone, this too has been
accomplished for us; many such things he granted, that we should accept the
charge. Come then, let us answer this man too, since yesterday's host of ours
also has his due. From where then should we attempt, beginning, to re-educate
him, a man so solemn and driving so far in wisdom that he even attempts to
admonish others so easily? I, for my part, in eagerness, think I will make the
last first, for I will produce him as a witness against himself, that he
slanders. And let the man be present, whether he is present or not, for I do
not know who he happens to be, nor indeed him at all, but let him answer as if
present. And the question is of this sort, best of men: that

you grant the making of speeches, you do well; but tell us this much, whether
you grant making them, but do not allow one to know about these very things
which one makes; and what could be graceful in the one who makes, but does not
know what he makes? Is it then possible to know about the matters, but not
possible to think about what one knows? But this at least would be a riddle
and perhaps even beyond a riddle. Therefore about what one must think, about
these things the wise say one must also speak, unless you too here say
something else.

Consider then how you have granted the whole by giving the first one thing.
For if according to you yourself one of the fine things is the making of
speeches, but it is impossible to make anything well without knowing about it,
and indeed about what one knows one must also think, and about what one thinks
also speak, you have granted that about what one makes, about these one must
also speak. Yes by Zeus, but the things said were among those who know. What
then would you have done, or would you not have accused me if you caught me
lying, you who blamed as unfitting those things of which you say all men are
my witnesses? And as it seems, if I play Demosthenes, or Miltiades, or
Themistocles, or the one of the same name, then I am much better than you,
according to you yourself, at knowing the speeches fitting for each; but the
speeches which it is fitting for me to make about myself and my own, these you
are fairer at knowing? And how, if I yield to you to know better, would I
think rightly? And indeed their character I must guess at, but my own I
clearly, I think, understand. And now if it were needed to speak about Athena
herself, I was better than you at knowing, by your own words, but about the
speeches to Athena you are stronger than I? And you say the others know these
things, but you do not allow me myself? And you find fault with us, but I
shall not be allowed toward those such as you are, and that for the sake of
turning them? And you, being a hearer, have taken the figure of an orator, but
you think the orator should keep quiet about his own affairs, and speak for
others, but be silent for himself? What then if you had given us, having taken
out /wherever is the richest plain of lovely Calydon/

or that about which the Chalcidians and Eretrians once differed, or the middle
of Corinth and Sicyon, as the oracle said, when simply thus for the sake of
friendship itself being present and enjoying the finest things, such as ours
would be finest, unless this too offends you, and you did not even put down
for us in the part of a wage so much as, even if we talked some nonsense, to
pardon this? Do you not know that even if you put together the nomes in Egypt
and add the Babylonian land and give me this as a wage for these speeches, for
I will pain you even now, you will never come near the worth? But what is our
wage and fitting for us? Friendship, goodwill, understanding, memory, a
measured honor of the speeches, respect for the one who makes the speeches.
And since you have feared that these may seem near some arrogance or
boastfulness, come, let me console you.

Consider then, hearing how I am about these things, and if you catch me lying,
as Sophocles has it, say already that I think nothing, and strike out the
prophetic art. I think that the one who thinks more of himself than what he
has, or even who poses toward the many so as to astound them either with
speeches or also with deeds, is not sound, but that this is that braggart and
wizard, and whatever such you wish to call him; but whoever, knowing
accurately to what measure of power he has come, also thinks the worth toward
this, and seeks nothing more nor hunts it, this man exactly fulfills the limit
of freedom, just as also in money whoever I think puts around himself a bulk
more than his possessions and goes about swaggering through emptiness, and has
been eager in this to deceive those he meets, we call somewhere a braggart and
a fool and empty in his ways; but he again who thinks it right to appear
humbler than according to his power and who deliberately contracts himself
into the less willingly and is readier to deny what is than to confess it is
not much among free men, or even if you should call him paltry and stingy and
inferior to gain, you would rather hit what is fitting. Thus I think it is
also about speeches, and of all things so to speak in

nature each is judged toward its worth; and the simple man and not multiple is
this one, as I think, whoever knows both to think more of himself on his own
things, and knows to think less, and praises both his own and those of his
neighbors, if they come to him, and blames both his own and those of his
neighbors, if he finds something of that sort, nowhere preferring anything to
what seems to be true. Consider also from here. For perhaps you have not yet
met me; since you would have known how much I blame on my own. For the things
I strike out and recast, how do you think I am toward these? And I say that of
all thinking equally of their own there is nothing more unequal among humans.
But I think the divine writing too commands this outright and says Know
yourself, not simply thus, say you are nothing, but neither exceed nor fall
short. And the second writing confirms and seals that this is the mind of the
former; for Nothing in excess I think tends to this. The worth then both
Apollo, being both prophet and poet and according to both an interpreter of
the truth, exhorts one to honor, and you, if you think well and are not
hateful to the gods, will consider everywhere, and will consider how of all
existing things you will not show yourself dishonoring, but always taking it
on in everything, thus you will judge referring your judgment to that, of what
sort each of us is and of how much worth. For the same things are worth it to
some, and not to others; as when Thersites says /whom I having bound would
lead, or another of the Achaeans,/ it is laughter, I think. You having bound?
And if another of the Achaeans themselves does not bind you, will you not be
content? Will you not know you are talking nonsense against your back? But
when Achilles, I think, narrates /twelve cities of men I sacked with ships,
and on foot eleven I say throughout fertile Troy; from all of these I took
many and good treasures, and bringing them all I used to give to Agamemnon,/
there is no one of the Achaeans who will still be indignant. Why? Because the
words coincide with the deeds. Twenty-three, he says, cities I took. What
then? Did this same man take them all? We cannot contradict. But not some on
the sea, others on foot,

and some so many, others so many, but the hearers also know these things
together. Therefore all is true. But I also became most eager for the common,
he says. Until one shows that he was not most eager, he is not talking
nonsense. For these reasons the same man Odysseus whipped the one, and
supplicated the other. In order then that you may know that this is also
wholly an ancient and Hellenic custom, to think highly of one's own, and that
without this high-mindedness neither a deed among humans worthy of memory nor
a distinguished speech nor anything else would ever be accomplished, hear of
what sort and how many things have escaped you. I seem likely to make the
speeches brothers somehow to those of yesterday, for the answer too holds
almost to the same judgment. As many things as Homer appeared to you saying
himself on his own, bidding one to call the introducers, you remember; so I
let them go. And yet those verses almost say this outright, that Homer votes
himself to be the strongest of poets. Homer then is released for you. Consider
also Hesiod's high-mindedness, if you did not see it before. For he, beginning
the Theogony and hymning the Muses, immediately says /who once taught Hesiod a
beautiful song./ And so reckless and insolent was he that he did not even
remain so much as to complete the prelude for the goddesses, but saying that
the Muses hymn Zeus and

Leto and Iapetus and crooked-counselled Cronus, then he does not hold out, but
a little in the midst of the names of the gods he says /who once taught Hesiod
a beautiful song,/ as if, unless this too should seem to be a work of the
Muses, as to sing the gods so also to make Hesiod a poet, some portion of the
praise would be lost to them. And he says not only that he received the song
from them, but he adds also that it was beautiful; yes by Zeus, safely, you
would say, adding himself to the Muses.

Therefore we too, what we were led to say, we added to the Muses and to the
goddess. We said at any rate, if you remember, that there are even some of the
words we recalled outright which the goddess showed beforehand, unless your
part prevents. And by how much we

are more reasonable than Hesiod, learn, since you compel. He, making the hymn
to the goddesses in the midst, inserted this verse, an encomium so to speak of
himself; but we, having worked out purely the speeches to the goddess purely,
made a small unwritten remark in passing about ourselves. And Hesiod does not
simply thus, having said that he received the song from the Muses, get off,
nor upon the former addition about the song that it was beautiful, but so
boastful and ambitious was he that he says he also received a staff from them.
And this means something more for him than an idle hearer would understand.
Refer it to Homer, what he says about the staff, /but now the sons of the
Achaeans, the judges, carry it in their palms./ And I too, says Hesiod, am one
of these, the Muses' part. And in excess, dignifying his own, what does he
say? /to pluck a wondrous one;/ as not having taken a chance little branch,
but of many existing the staff most to be looked at for Hesiod. And he did not
even stop here of his insolence, but as if bursting you on purpose he adds
/and they breathed a voice into me./ And of the verse he made an end, but of
the encomium no more, but he added again a divine one, and what is still
finer, like the sophists excessive everywhere /that I might celebrate things
that will be and things that were before./ And this is what? He says the Muses
hymn things that will be and things that were before, and that this is their
nature. And I too then, he says, hymn these things which the Muses hymn. If
then it seems good to you to strike these out of Hesiod, we too will accept
the censure. But you will show first, I suppose, that you are worth the
verses.

Come then, let us go back again to the men about whom they discourse. Does it
not seem to you that Homer most clearly of humans has made Achilles speaking
about himself both other things and once for all thus? /but I sit by the ships
a useless burden of the earth,/ [whether you wish, he says, or not; and even
if he is still worse corrupted than now] /being such as no one of the
bronze-clad Achaeans./

Yes by Zeus, but Achilles plays the young man. But Odysseus, being a raw old
man, what does he say? And this speaking to that very man /O Achilles, son of
Peleus,/ if you think it right to be better in something else, I grant, /but I
would throw myself before you in thought,/ and he added that even much. For it
is not as the paltry schoolmasters mutilate the verse, it is not so, who
unloose "much" from the former, and lead it to the next, of which the
beginning of the verse leads. But these do not seem to me to understand much
about speeches. For it is an outright antithesis; for he says /you are better
than I and stronger not a little with the spear/ the former; then, /but I
would throw myself before you in thought much./ Hear then how they are
opposed. What there is "with the spear" is "in thought" here, and what was
"not a little," this instead of that is "much," as if he had also said thus
transferring, you are much better than I in the things of war, and I than you
these things not a little. And I will still lead you more firmly, if you wish.
For Homer about age does not much use such additions, as many as to recall in
the present, but for a judgment of excellence often, such as I mean, he says
somewhere speaking about Zeus and Poseidon /but Zeus was born earlier,/ only
so much, and he does not add much. And yet that it is much is clear even
without his adding it, both from his own words and from the others. For he
always sings to him, father of men and of gods. So how is he not by far the
eldest of all? Elsewhere then he says /and he was at once earlier and better,
the hero Protesilaus./ Only so much, that earlier, but how much he does not
say. But when he judges, he adds much, great, such things, /and of men again
by far the best was Telamonian Ajax, while Achilles was angry./ Then how? /for
he was much stronger./ This custom from home he also transfers onto Odysseus,
thinking it fitting for that man too to have been said thus, that I excel you
much in judgment. And Odysseus, I think, added it boldly. For he knew that
even

And what is still finer, for Nestor not even being some raw old man still, but
so exceedingly an old man and ripe that for him /two generations of speaking
men had perished, who before were reared and born together with him, and he
ruled among the third;/ so exceedingly will he seem to you as judge to play
the youth that there is no thing more talkative than him nor more arrogant.
For not even once having praised himself did he get off, but he is alike
everywhere; who first, reconciling the kings, says he associated with men not
according to those now, but by far the strongest of all, dignifying himself
through those; so that he did not even hide it, but said outright /and they
understood my counsels, and were persuaded by my word,/ being, he says, such.
And what is still greater /and I associated with them, having come from Pylos

/from far away from the Apian land,/ as being so exceedingly famous and
wonderful. And if you will say that /and I fought according to myself/ is

true and of my argument. For where he was inferior to those he said /and I

For I pass over of what sort also to Agamemnon the wise man of ours answers as
he goes through the ranks in haste. But the sweetest of all, for Achilles
arranging the contest over Patroclus and others winning other things, he
himself was not able to contend anything, but being this that is said no
longer anything for these things through old age which he formerly once won,
he speaks as if doing something thereby, and he says to the Achaeans not in
these very words, but somehow posing, that of you each, one has won the race
or will win, another perhaps wrestling, another something else; but I /in
boxing I defeated Clytomedes son of Enops, and Alcaeus of Pleuron in
wrestling, who stood up against me, and I outran Iphiclus with my feet though
he was good, and with the spear I surpassed Phyleus and Polydorus;/ so much
better am I than those now winning, even if I have yielded to old age.

And if again you wish, how great the thing of arrogance of the Phocian boxer,
/let him come nearer whoever will take the two-handled cup; but I say no one
will lead away the mule. For thus I will speak out, and indeed it will be
accomplished; I will break the flesh straight through, and crush the bones
together./ And let his kinsmen be present, he says, /who will carry him out,
overcome under my hands./ Did he then threaten, but not do it? Nothing else
then but he prophesied what he was going to do. Therefore this was not
boldness, I think. But also somehow following him, as indeed also Homer
defending him? that he said these things toward the others with no ill-will
nor with harsh arrogance, but as much as he was conscious of in himself, so
much he displayed, he says he struck him as he foretold, but when he saw the
blow was fatal, he himself already became one of Euryalus's kinsmen. For he

For they were not then first learning that Zeus is stronger than they. Other

the laws not to speak ill, not simply, but the false. As then in the speeches
toward another not speaking ill, but the false is terrible, so not even if
someone should speak well about himself would he justly have blame, as long as
speaking the false is absent. Since the one without falsehood will neither
speak ill of another unjustly nor flee to praise himself when needed. And I
think you have also heard Sappho toward some of the women seeming to be
fortunate, boasting and saying that the Muses truly made her both blessed and
enviable and that not even when she has died will there be forgetfulness. And
you hear also the Laconian saying toward himself and the chorus /the Muse has
cried out, the clear Siren./ And yet what such thing did I remark in passing
about myself and my own speeches? Did I compare them to the songs of the
Sirens, or is it more arrogant to bid one to compare a book against books than
to say that one's own is like the voice of the Muses? And add that too, that
the poet having asked the Muse herself at the beginning, that he might become
active under her, then as if he went out of himself and says
