* On the Ascension of the Lord
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of our father among the saints, John, Archbishop of Constantinople, the
Chrysostom, on the Ascension of our Lord Jesus Christ. A certain divine
festival, as it seems, is the present assembly, proclaiming the Master's
victory over death; and we have come to it, the crown of all the feasts. For
now the achievement of the whole economy has reached its end for us; and the
present season has come bearing the tokens of the feast of victory. For now
the Master has brought up into heaven "the firstfruits of our bodies." We
therefore celebrate a Master's return, in which he transferred our nature to
the heavenly things. For the God who dwelt in it raised his own temple, and he
who crossed the ethereal sea gathered to himself his own garment, filling all
things continually by the divine nature, yet by the economy according to the
flesh first going under the earth, and later, after the victory over death,
filling the heavenly dwelling with his own body. "Who shall speak the mighty
acts of the Lord, and make all his praises to be heard?" What word could
explain the greatness of so great an economy? Who shall proclaim with the
tongue the boundless wealth of the Master's ineffable generosity?
For him whom sin cast out from the life in paradise, divine grace has brought
up into heaven. The gatekeepers of death cried out, seeing the strange descent
of him who had come upon them contrary to expectation: "Who is this King of
glory?" The powers above the heavens also uttered this cry, struck with
amazement at the strange sight of him who had gone up with a body. "Lift up
your gates, you rulers," it says, "and be lifted up, you everlasting doors,
and the King of glory shall come in." For he who transferred with himself that
which is of one race with us has brought up human nature. Paradoxical is the
ascent of him who appeared in human fashion; for divine also is the descent of
him who was born of a virgin contrary to nature. What is seen is man, but what
is understood is God. He has made our nature a covering of the unseen Godhead:
"No one has ever seen God," it says. "The only-begotten Son, who is in the
bosom of the Father, he has declared him." He has come to us bearing the
unchanging image of the Father's nature; he has appeared who characterizes the
uncircumscribed Godhead of the paternal hypostasis. For he was a form like the
one who begot him: "He who was in the form of God," it says, "took the form of
a servant." The form of the servant does not falsify our nature. Therefore the
Godhead of the genuine offspring also confirms the likeness toward the Father.
This one, then, having wrought salvation for our nature through his own
becoming-man, has now fulfilled the aim of the economy; now he has received
the achievement of the benefaction toward us, and he who was covered with the
bodily cloud and who put on the human garment as a veil of his own Godhead has
taken up with the Godhead that which is kindred to our nature; now he who
appeared earthly has become heavenly again, not circumscribed in place
according to the boundlessness of the Godhead, but filling all things by the
divine nature, yet having appeared as man by economy. "Being found in fashion
as a man," it says, he covered the Godhead with a human wrapping.
This, then, he who caused the whole creation to subsist beyond nature has
brought up into the heavens. I cannot narrate the manner of his generation
before the ages; my tongue does not reach even to interpret the birth of the
coming-forth from the Virgin.
The things seen are human, but the things believed are divine. "Who is this
King of glory?" it says. These things the supercosmic powers cried out.
Strange is the one who is setting forth; for he who has gone up is contrary to
nature. For even the sight of the body was seen as light-like. For it received
the splendors of the divine rays. And he who assumed our nature has brought it
up into the heavens, that he might make heaven passable for those on earth. A
cloud he has made a chariot of his human wrapping; and riding upon it he
transferred his own garment toward heaven. As, then, the conception of the
birth from the Virgin is contrary to nature, so also the wonder of the ascent
into the heavens is paradoxical. For how is it possible for a human body to
fly over the heavenly vaults? But what nature did not admit, grace has
granted. "He went up," and the servant not into heaven, but "as into heaven."
For great is the interval between the servant's subsistence that came to be by
grace and the Master's God-befitting authority. For the wondrous Elijah, the
charioteer of the fiery chariot, did not fly over the heavenly vaults; but he
who transferred our nature to the heavenly things has made heaven passable for
men.
Now the reconciliations toward the heavenly King have reached their end, and
he who is joined to him in Godhead, yet who administered salvation for human
nature through his visitation among us, has reconciled to his own Begetter the
nature that was of old at war with the Father. Now he who mediated for each
nature has brought up that which is of one race with us. "For there is one
God," it says, "and one mediator of God and men." What is seen is man, but
what is believed is God. Who will be able to set forth in speech this wondrous
ascent of him who was taken up contrary to expectation? But what the tongue
does not reach, faith teaches. For he who was covered with the body has
brought up the firstfruits of our nature, as if offering to the Father a sheaf
from some flourishing field.
This saving ascension, then, we now celebrate; this Master's ascent we keep as
a festival, hymning the greatness of the Master's ineffable gift and
proclaiming the achievement of the economy concerning us, that we too, shining
through achievements and displaying the fruits of righteousness, may be eager
to run up toward that which is of one race with us, and may reach the royal
throne itself, where he who put it on has brought up what is kindred for our
sake, that we may also reign with the Master and enjoy the gifts from him,
having exchanged the earth for heaven and preferred the heavenly things to the
earthly, let us traffic in the heavenly wealth, that we may receive the
recompenses from the Master and enjoy the gifts of the Lord by the grace of
the only-begotten Son of God; to him be the glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
