The description of the present works needs a speech that contends with the
art; and if it should appear inferior to them, this too is a marvel of the
maker, that he has proved greater in doing than we who have merely spoken.
Hence it ends in praise of him, and to the Cause of good things it renders
the eulogy fitting unto Power, through whom the city has been granted to
enjoy the present blessings.

DESCRIPTION OF A CLOCK.
Let others look upon other things and, if they should wish, speak of them:
some of the pyramids of Egypt, such as the things sung of the Egyptians; and
someone sojourning in Babylon might see the temple of Bel Zeus and towers,
others upon others, one upon another, eight. And let him likewise take
Hephaestus and grant him houses of the gods to fashion, "which Hephaestus
made with knowing mind," and let him say that the same god is "the
Lame-footed," knowing that his art always needs toils and attendance; for
such is the not-moving of one who persists at his works. Having, then,
knowing wits and a body fixed in one place, now he makes the shield of
Achilles, a work extraordinary in Homer, and images that seem to live serve
him for the need and kindle the flame with automatic motion. And coming to
the island of the Phaeacians he made another marvelous work: there were dogs
for Alcinous, guardians of the court; and the dogs were not these ordinary
living ones, but gold, by Zeus, and silver, having motion through the art of
Hephaestus. These things, then, were myth to me and seemed a tale, and Homer
luxuriated in his art, speaking at his ease of things that neither were nor
ever came to be; but now, having seen the works and art of the present
Hephaestus ........ I permit these things even for those to be conceded as
true. I would wish, then, to speak these things, and I pray; for the sight
conquers speech, itself at a loss, being carried toward whatever it must, in
all directions; for it leaps up and down and wishes to look at everything,
then, shifting more quickly than it ought, it falls short of accuracy in
each of these; I know, having suffered this. For I did not persist in the
first things through desire for the others, and the second things, before I
had them as I ought, I withdrew to another sight. And there were windings of
the eyes, as for those who look upon the labyrinth by the Nile, as some
Ionic historian said. From where, then, should one begin? And what limit, my
friend, shall we set upon the speech? There is a house in the middle of the
city that bears measures, facing the royal stoa, and on the left a place
that is the summer haunt of countless people. A double pairing of columns
stands before it, divided toward the east and the west, and by a great
interval they make the house behind be seen, so that no one interferes with
the things seen. A folding of marbles holding together the middles of the
columns, with sharp iron stakes driven into them: this is a barrier against
anyone who is rash and contends to climb over. But a Gorgon too from on high
threatens grimly those who dare to approach with a more insolent mind,
exchanging her eyes as many times as there are parts of the day, just as
when Perseus, having cut off her head, killed her: if anyone caused harm, he
had to become stone at her appearing. Tragedy would have called her "a
blood-eyed and snaky maiden," if she had such piercing eyes. For when she
bears the token of having been struck, she is about to astound those who
look upon the present work. But let us go on to the rest, since I have also
touched the works from above. For doors stand in front, and those of the day
I think I somewhat conceal; and those of the night are higher, but I do not
yet write of these. Hence, if one should see in order those which we have
opened, we shall begin from above, and thus what first demands the hymn in
accuracy will be first for the speech; therefore let it wait for evening and
the lamp that will shine forth from the doors of the upper story. But it has
anticipated with some paradoxical motion, because it frightens the
spectators. But let us consider these middle things, how they stand. Certain
bronze eagles stand in a single row, having a number equal to the hours that
lie beneath. And all bear crowns, not crowned on the head nor showing a
victory of their own; but the tips of the claws of their feet, coming
together, hold the crowns, each awaiting the Heracles beneath them, when he
should come out from the doors that lie upon them, after the Sun standing
before these has passed them. For he walks, measuring the hour by motion.
And as ruler of these he has assumed a kingly form, holding up the celestial
sphere with his left hand, and by the raising of his right commanding the
doors to be released, as those who command the horses to go forth from the
starting-gates. An eagle, then, stands waiting, when the Heracles of Zeus
should come out from the doors that lie upon them, announcing the hour: the
first the first hour, and the rest according to the number. For there are
twelve hours, and all are Heracles, not idle, by Zeus, and doing nothing;
for to be idle at all was not dear to Heracles. And the labors of old are
even now a work for him, no longer of Eurystheus, but by the necessity of
art; and he is divided together with the labors, filling a double hexad.
The first contest, then, is a lion, and Nemea is the place for him. He slew
the hydra too, even if it defended itself with contentious heads. He blushed
to conquer a boar, but nevertheless he conquered. Golden horns had grown on
a hind, and they became Heracles'. And birds, moreover, troubled the heart
and were the work of the bow. Nor did he leave the Amazons unharmed: whether
seeking some girdle, I would not say; but he hated women, hearing them
called "a match for men." Dung troubled Augeas; and he was free of this too.
And Crete had a terrible bull, but not such as to conquer Heracles. And what
would you say of the mares of Diomedes? Were they not murderous up to this
point and equal to wild beasts? But Geryon too was rich in cattle, and he
had three heads; and I think that along with the cattle these too were taken
from him. Nor did he leave Hades untried of his excellence, taking up battle
against the dog there and showing the monster to the sun. There was need
also of golden apples for Eurystheus, lying at the extremity of the earth:
the apples were of the Hesperides, and a dragon was the guardian, not to be
borne in strength. But he lay struck down, and the apples were Eurystheus'.
These are the works and toils of Heracles; from these, hours and crowns and
bronze winged contrary to nature. When, then, he has thrust aside the bronze
doors and appeared with his labor, an eagle follows from above, spreading
its wings and with both feet bringing the crown equally upon the equal head;
and it lingers a little, as if enjoying the heroic head; then, having left
this to Heracles and having spread its two feet, it is lifted up and obtains
the place it had, laying its wings upon its sides and drawing them together
again upon itself, giving a good hunt, but it did not come together having
obtained it. As for the crowns, Heracles leans forward, so that he may be
seen by all as in the middle of a stadium, and then he turned to his own
place, having obtained what he longed for. And an eagle serves the crown,
since Zeus is the father of Heracles, and the eagle is the bird of Zeus.
Thus from heaven and from the father, as the wage of his sweats, it crowns
the victor in memory of the suffering; and it flies over and rules the
birds, just as this one was raised by fame; he needs no herald proclaiming
the crowns. And in another place occupying the middle space, great and
knowing how to astound and projected beyond the others, he does not yet draw
the down of the beard, and he stands naked, except insofar as the lion-skin
hangs from his shoulders behind, and with his left he holds up a
sounding-vessel. "This is called a lion," the craftsman said; and it hangs
from the middle and swings; and he is armed with a club, giving his right in
return also for that one's cry, and he raises it and gives a blow to the
bronze; and it, being aloft and struck with such force, both cries out
greatly and prolongs the sound. Upon one labor, then, one blow; upon the
second hour, a double. For at each he gathers also the number of the labors
that have gone before, until he has filled them; likewise six, lest, having
increased these in multitude, he wear out the hearing: the seventh he counts
as first and sounds once, then also two and so on, until, signaling the
others, the bronze should sound into a second hexad as many times as it
must. And it is heard more largely than even the sight is present. And for
those who have obtained their station far off, to know the measure of time
is easy for all. And Pan too hears; and a shaggy beard and a double horn on
the forehead show that this is he. And he fell into love of Echo, who gave
Pan over to laughter in her sports. And longing for Echo he perceives the
bronze and, struck with amazement, raises his right and turns his face
about, if somehow he might see the maiden, turning such turns as
unsuccessful love produces. And you would say that he also marvels at
Heracles, how great and of what sort he is. And with Pan present, satyrs too
ought not to be absent. And they laugh upon him and, having taken Pan in the
middle in comic fashion, looking at a face both amorous and wild and a
character mixed, both tame and harsh. But these are above the head of the
shrine, according to which the son of Alcmene is seen naked. And the son of
Tydeus, having obtained a station on the right, was then and is now a friend
of the trumpet; for he cries out upon Heracles when he has come to the last
labor, just as when he found the son of Peleus in Scyros. For he sang so
much even then, measuring equal days. And a certain servant, having heard,
brings preparation of a bath for his master, as is likely, the cooked dishes
already having been made ready. And another, carrying these from the market
at the beginning of day, hastens. And both seem to serve a difficult master;
for otherwise the running would not have been so great. And that shepherd
too, having obtained the opposite station, having given over to his left, to
speak poetically, the crook, both delights and smiles and raises his right
toward wonder. But on this side, around the labors, there is sound and
wonder and trumpet. And this wise one, having taken up the twelfth of the
contests from above, gives this to Heracles as a greater. This one is an
archer stationed under Diomedes, and the labor is golden apples again. This
one, then, has set an arrow upon the string, drawn toward the breast by the
right hand, while the left pushes the bow, the middle of both, the arrow
projecting so great a length outside the advanced point. And for accurate
sight he gathers his glance upon a slender mark, about to let fly the
arrow ...
