Having then so many proofs of his providence, those in the New, those in the

this. Therefore pry no further, knowing this clearly, that you have a Master
more affectionate than a father and more careful than a mother, more loving
than a bridegroom and a bride, who counts your salvation as his own rest and
rejoices over it more than you over deliverance from dangers and deaths (which
I showed through Jonah), and displaying every kind of love, =8.12= which a
father has for children or a mother for offspring, the vinedresser for plants,
the builder for his craft, the bridegroom for the bride, the young man for a
virgin, and wanting to set your evils as far from you as east is distant from
west, as heaven is higher than the earth (for we have shown this too), or
rather not only so much, but far more; just as we showed when we moved the
discourse about these things and exhorted you not to stop at the images, but
to go beyond them with your thoughts. For the providence of God is
uninterpretable and his care incomprehensible, his goodness unspeakable and
his loving-kindness unsearchable. =8.13= Knowing all these things then, both
through what he has declared, and through what he has done and through what he
will do, pry into nothing, and do not be a busybody, and do not say: Why this?
for what is this? For how is it not mad, and full of extreme mindlessness and
frenzy, never to pry into a physician when he cuts, burns, applies bitter
drugs, even if he is a servant, but for the master to lie in silence suffering
these things and to give thanks both for the burning and for the cutting and
for the drugs, and this with the future unclear (for many have even killed
many by doing these things), and to yield to him with much submission as he
does these things, and to do this same thing also in the case of a sailor and
a builder and those who pursue the other trades. =8.14= But I say it is
thought ridiculous for the layman and the inexperienced man to demand of the
craftsman the reasons of all that happens, yet to pry into that unspeakable
wisdom, the ineffable, the unutterable, the incomprehensible, and to seek why
this and that has happened, and this while knowing clearly that this wisdom is
unfailing, that his goodness is great, that his providence is unspeakable,
that all things done by him toward us meet a good end; only if our own affairs
are not cut across, that he wants no one to perish, but to save. How is it not
of surpassing madness, to pry from the very preludes and at once into him who
both wants



and is able to save all, and not even to wait for the end of the things that
are happening? 9.t Chapter 9. That one must neither pry, and that one must
wait for the ends of affairs 9.1 For most of all one must not be a busybody
either from the preludes or after these things; but if you are so curious and
meddlesome, wait for the end and see where these things arrive, and do not be
thrown into tumult, and do not be disturbed from the preludes. Since even
someone inexperienced, seeing the goldsmith at the beginning melting the gold
and mixing it with the ash and the chaff, if he does not wait for the end,
will think the gold has been lost; and if someone born and raised in the sea,
then suddenly moved inland and altogether unacquainted with the care of the
earth, 9.2 saw the grain that had been stored and kept behind doors and bars
and freed from moisture, suddenly brought out by the farmer, scattered,
thrown, and lying on the field for all who pass by, and not only not freed
from moisture, but even given over to mud and marshes and having no guard,
would he not think the grain was lost and condemn the farmer doing these
things? 9.3 But the condemnation is not of the nature of the thing, but of the
inexperience and mindlessness of the one who did not judge well, casting his
vote at once from the preludes. For if he waited for the summer and saw the
standing crops waving and the sickle sharpened and the grain that had been
scattered and left unguarded and had rotted and been dispersed and given over
to the mud, this grain rising and becoming many times more, 9.4 appearing more
beautiful and putting off its oldness and standing up with much vigor and
having as it were escorts and guards and raising a stalk to a height and
delighting the spectator and feeding him and providing much profit, then he
would be more amazed, that through such things the fruit was brought to such
plenty and brightness. 9.5 You too then, O man, most of all do not pry into
the common Master of us all; but if you are so contentious and bold as to rave
with this madness, at least wait for the end of the things that are happening.
For if the farmer waits a whole winter, not looking at what the grain suffers
in the season of frost, but at those things of which he is going to enjoy;
much more would you yourself be right, in the case of him who farms the whole
inhabited world and our souls, to wait for the end; and I mean an end not only
that in the present life (for often this will be here too) but also that in
the future. For the economy of both these lives looks toward one end, our
salvation and good standing. For even if it is divided in time, still it is
joined in purpose; and just as now it is winter, now spring, and each turn of
the year looks toward one thing, the ripeness of the fruits, so also in our
affairs. 9.6 When then you see the Church scattered, suffering the last
things, those who shine in it driven, scourged, its president carried off very
far away, do not look at these things only, but also at what will come out of
them, the wages, the recompenses, the prizes, the rewards; "For he who has
endured to the end, this one will be saved," he says. For in the Old, since
the word of the resurrection was not yet known, both were done in the present
life; but in the New this is not everywhere, but there are places where the
painful things are here, and the good things wait for our departure from here.
9.7 But still, even if the good things came out for them in this life and in
the present life, they would be especially admirable in this, those who have



not enjoyed these things, because neither knowing clearly the word about
resurrection and seeing the opposite of God's promises happening in the facts,
they were not scandalized, they were not thrown into tumult, nor were they
disturbed, but they yielded to his incomprehensible providence, being
scandalized by none of the things happening contrary, but knowing the resource
and ingenuity of his wisdom, they waited for the end, or rather even before
the end they bore gratefully whatever was dared against them and continued
glorifying the God who allowed these things to happen. Perhaps this word seems
unclear to you; therefore I will try to make it clearer. 10.t Chapter 10. That
the ancients also waited for the ends of affairs 10.1 To Abraham once become
old and already deadened from age for childbearing (for toward becoming a
father he was in no better state while living than those who have died), to
the just man then being old and having gone far beyond the bounds of natural
childbearing by a great surplus, and having as his partner Sarah, more barren
than a rock, God promises that he will make him father of so many offspring
that their multitude will be equal to the multitude of the stars. 10.2 And he,
though such hindrances were present, his age already brought down to extreme
old age and his wife maimed both from age and from nature (for old age was not
the only thing that hindered, but also the maiming of nature itself; for even
when she was young the workshop of nature was useless; for the woman was
barren). 10.3 Therefore Paul too, marking this very thing, spoke thus: "And
the deadness of Sarah's womb." He did not say simply the deadness of Sarah,
lest you suspect only the age, but the deadness of the womb itself, which had
come about not from years only but also from nature; but still, though such
hindrances were present, as I said, knowing what a promise of God is, how
ingenious and resourceful and hindered neither by laws of nature, nor by
difficulty of affairs, nor by anything else, but walking through the opposites
and bringing what was promised into deed, 10.4 he accepted what was said and
believed the promise and did not even allow the tumult from reasonings to be
stirred at all, and judged the power of the one who promised to be
trustworthy, as indeed it is, toward the fulfillment of the promise, not
examining how and in what manner these things will be and for what reason not
in youth, but in old age and late and slowly. 10.5 Therefore Paul also
proclaims him with a splendid voice, speaking thus: "Who against hope believed
in hope, that he might become father of many nations." What is "Against hope
in hope"? Against human hope, in the hope of God, which conquers all things,
which can do all things, which prevails over all. And he believed not only
that he would become a father, but of so many nations, the old man and
childless, who had a barren and aged wife, according to what was said: 10.6
"So shall your seed be. And not being weak in faith he did not consider his
own body already deadened, being about a hundred years old, and the deadness
of Sarah's womb; but toward the promise of God he did not waver in unbelief,
but was strengthened in faith, giving glory to God and being fully persuaded
that what he has promised he is able also to do." And what he means is of this
sort: Leaping up and springing at once from human weakness and running up to
the height of the one who promised and considering his unspeakable power, he
was clearly persuaded that what was said would altogether be. 10.7 And in this
especially he glorified God, by not prying, nor being a busybody, but by
yielding to the incomprehensible of his wisdom and power and not doubting at
all about what was said. Do you see that this especially is to glorify



God, always to yield to the incomprehensible of his providence and to his
unspeakable power and wisdom and not to pry, nor to be a busybody, nor to say:
Why this? for what is this? how will this be? 10.8 And this is not the only
marvel, but that after this promise he was also commanded to sacrifice that
son of his, the only-begotten and genuine, and not even then was he
scandalized. And yet there were many things able to scandalize one who was not
sober nor awake: and first the command itself, whether God accepts such
sacrifices and bids fathers to be child-killers and to end life by a violent
death and to give children over to an untimely death, and to become murderers
of those born to them, and wants his own altar to be stained with such blood
and a father's right hand to be armed against the only-begotten child and the
just man to be harsher than murderers; 10.9 and with this also the tyranny of
nature throwing him into tumult, disturbing him, not because he was a father
only, but because he was also affectionate and father of such a child,
genuine, only-begotten, beautiful to see, beautiful to understand. For he was
in the very flower of age and at the very peak of virtue, shining then with a
double fairness, both of soul and of body. =10.10= Nor was it a small thing
toward affection that he had been given wholly against hope. For you know how
lovable such children are, those granted against hope and expectation and not
by a law of nature, as that one was. And with all these the thing most able to
furnish scandal was the promise and the pledge; for what was commanded was
contrary to it. For what was promised was: "So shall your seed be as the stars
of heaven," but what was commanded was that the only-begotten son, from whom
he was going to fill the whole inhabited world, this one should be put out of
the way and given over to death and to a most harsh slaughter. =10.11= And yet
not even so was that just man scandalized, nor thrown into tumult, nor did he
suffer anything such as it was likely one of the mindless and those who crawl
on the ground would suffer. For he did not say to himself: What is this? Have
I been deceived? Have I been cheated? Is this a command of God? Away, I am not
persuaded; that I should become a child-killer is unjust, and that I should
stain my right hand with such blood. And how will the things of the promise
have an end? For if I destroy the root, from where the branches? from where
the fruits? if I bury the spring, from where the rivers? if I slaughter the
son, from where for me the multitude of offspring equal to the multitude of
the stars? =10.12= How then did he promise other things and now command
contrary things? None of these did he say, he did not think; but again taking
refuge in the unspeakable power of the one who promised, the ingenious, the
resourceful, that which shines through the opposites, that which is above the
laws of nature, that which is more powerful than all things, that which has
nothing opposing it, he accomplished this command also with much full
persuasion and slaughtered the son and stained the right hand and reddened the
sword and drove the knife through the neck; for even if not in the deed, still
in purpose he fulfilled all these things. =10.13= Therefore Moses too,
admiring him, spoke thus: "And it came to pass after these words, God tested
Abraham and said to him: Take your son, the beloved whom you have loved,
Isaac, and offer him up on one of the mountains which I will tell you." Are
these the words of the pledge, of the promise, those saying that he will be
father of a multitude of offspring and that his seed will be as the stars of
heaven? =10.14= See how after these words, hearing that he should slaughter
the son, he accepted to kill the one from whom so great a multitude was going
to be, and to sacrifice him and put him out of the way and bring him as a
victim to God. And Paul too, admiring him again, proclaimed him thus, saying:
"By faith Abraham has offered Isaac when he was tested." Then, showing how
great a thing he did and how much faith he displayed, he added: "And he
offered the only-begotten, he who had received the promises." =10.15= And what
he means is of this sort: It cannot be said that he had two genuine children
and expected, if this one were destroyed, to become father of that multitude
from the other, but he had him alone and from



this one alone the things of the promise hung; and yet he chose to sacrifice
this one, just as in the promise of his birth he was hindered toward faith
neither by his own deadness nor by the maiming of the woman's nature, so
neither here was he blocked by death. =10.16= Compare these things then with
what has now come to be and you will see your small-souledness and you will
see the cheapness of those who are scandalized and you will learn clearly that
the scandal comes to you from nowhere else than from not yielding to the
incomprehensible providence of God, but seeking everywhere the manner of the
economies and demanding reasons of the things that happen and prying into
each. =10.17= Which if Abraham had suffered, he was going to limp toward the
faith. But he did not pry, therefore he also shone and obtained all the things
promised; nor was he scandalized, neither by the old age, nor by the command
after these things; nor did he think the command a hindrance to the promise,
nor the sacrifice destructive of the promise, nor did he fall into despair of
the pledge, even though he had come to the very end of the affairs. For do not
tell me this, that God did not allow the command to come into deed, nor the
just man's right hand to be stained with blood; but consider this, that
Abraham knew none of these things, nor that he would receive the son alive,
and he did not know that he would return home thus, but he stretched his whole
purpose toward the slaughter. =10.18= For this reason he is also called a
second time from the heavens. For he did not say to him simply "Abraham," but
"Abraham, Abraham," by the doubling of the voice checking and reining back the
purpose strained toward the sacrifice; so wholly was he of the command. Did
you see how the whole had been fulfilled in his purpose and scandal nowhere?
And the cause is this, not prying into the things of God. =10.19= And what of
Joseph? tell me. Did he not suffer something of this sort also? For to him too
a great grace of promise had been given by God, and again the things that
happened were coming about contrary to the promises. For the promise was,
through the dreams, that the brothers would do obeisance to him, and a double
vision proclaimed this, both that through the stars and that through the
sheaves; but the things happening after these visions were contrary to the
sights. =10.20= For first a harsh war was kindled against him from this in the
father's house, and those who had loosed the same birth-pangs for him, cutting
through the laws of kinship and tearing the bonds of brotherly friendship
itself and shaking even the ordinances of nature, became enemies and foes and
wilder than wolves toward the brother from those dreams; and as wild and
untamed beasts taking a lamb in their midst, so they plotted against him each
day. =10.21= And the father of this war was irrational envy and unjust
jealousy; and boiling with anger they breathed murder each day, envy kindling
this furnace and raising the pyre. And since for the time they could work no
harsh thing against him, while he turned about indoors and spent time with the
parents, they slander his reputation and construct a shameful fame for him and
bring down a wicked blame, wanting by this to dissolve the father's affection
and to take him easy for a plot. =10.22= Then after these things, taking him
out of the father's eyes and finding him in a desert place bringing them food
and having come to visit them, they did not reverence the cause of the coming,
they were not ashamed of the brotherly table, but they sharpened the swords
and made ready for slaughter and all became brother-killers, having nothing
small or great to charge against the one about to be destroyed by them; but
envying, making war, slandering, over the things for which he ought to have
been crowned and proclaimed. =10.23= But he not even so turned away from their
company, but even in so much wickedness he displayed the brotherly
disposition; and they even so attempted to destroy him and they did destroy
him, as far as their part went, and they stained their right hands and the
brother-killing



they completed. =10.24= But the ingenious wisdom of God, resourceful even in
the impossibilities, even in the very pit and in the very bringing on of
death, snatched him from their defiled hands. For one of the brothers advised
that they stand off from that bloodshed; and God persuaded and prevented the
slaughter. Yet not even here did the terrible things stop, but they went
further again. For when they were prevented from destroying him, and their
anger still boiled and the things of wrath were at their peak and the surge of
the passion was great, they transferred the wrath to something else. =10.25=
For stripping him and binding him and throwing him into a pit, the raw and
inhuman and beastlike men, thus they enjoyed the table brought by him; and he
was in a pit fearing the last things, while they feasted and were drunk. And
not even here did they stop their madness, but seeing barbarian men, settled
far from their own country, going down to Egypt, they took and sold the
brother, constructing for him from this another death, somewhat longer and
harsher and full of much misery. =10.26= For being a youth and quite a youth
and raised with much freedom in a father's house and sharing in no slavery and
in none of the misery in slavery, consider what he suffered, suddenly becoming
a slave instead of a free man and a stranger instead of a citizen and enduring
the last captivity; not slavery only, but also being torn from father and
mother and all who belonged to him, naked, a stranger, homeless, cityless,
given over by a law of slavery into barbarian hands. =10.27= For what was not
able to throw him into tumult? the suddenness, the unexpected, the unrehearsed
against hope, the harshness of the disaster, that from brothers and beloved
brothers, having wronged them in nothing small or great, or rather even having
done them good, he should suffer these things? And yet he was disturbed by
none of these, but was led away through those merchants into Egypt, exchanging
slavery for slavery. =10.28= For there too he became a slave again and the
Hebrew, the well-born, free with a double freedom, both of body and of soul,
dwelt in a barbarian house; and not even from this was he disturbed, nor
scandalized at what had happened, remembering the visions that promised the
opposites, nor did he pry, why ever these things happen. =10.29= And the
brother-killers and wolves and beasts, though they had done these wrongs,
feast in the father's house; but he who was expected to be their king, a
captive, a slave, sold off into a foreign land, endures the last misery, not
only not having reigned over them, but even having become their slave and
having suffered, by a great diameter, the opposites of the promises. For then
he not only failed of a kingdom, but also fell from fatherland and freedom and
from the sight of his parents. =10.30= And not even here did the things of his
contests stop for him, but another deeper pit was dug, having death again and
slaughter, a death of reproach and a slaughter full of shame. For the
mistress, seeing him with unjust eyes and captured by the youth's beauty,
overpowered by his splendid appearance, herself again stitched deceits and
plots. =10.31= And spreading the nets of incontinence from every side, she
watched each day to catch the youth inside her own seine and to throw him into
the pit of adultery and to give him over to an undying death. And each day she
went out upon this hunt, goaded by the passion and by this incontinent desire.
And once finding him alone, she dragged him by force toward the unjust bed and
compelled him to break through another's marriage and attempted to ruin his
chastity. =10.32= But yet not even from this did that just man suffer anything
harsh; but the tyranny of desire and the tumults of young age and the plot of
an incontinent woman and the assaults of a mistress and the disturbances of
youth and all that was likely to come from that touch and from the



sight and the madness, he passed over with much ease, as some eagle stretching
a high wing, and stripping off even the garments themselves and leaving them
to the incontinent hands, he went out naked of garments, but having the
splendid wrapping of chastity, more conspicuous than the purple itself.
=10.33= From this a sword was sharpened against him again and death was
planned and the waves were raised to a greater height and the madness of the
woman was kindled more fiercely than a Babylonian furnace. For both the desire
was raised to a greater pitch thereafter, and anger, another most harsh
passion, was added with much wildness, and she looked murder and ran toward a
sword and desired a most lawless slaughter and was eager to destroy the
athlete of chastity and the contestant of endurance and patience. =10.34= And
rushing in to her own husband, reporting to him what had happened, not as the
truth of the matter held, but as she herself had constructed the drama of the
slander, she persuaded the judge of what she wished, accusing solitude and, as
one wronged, seeking vengeance, carrying about as proof of what she said the
garments of the innocent youth in those unclean hands. =10.35= And the
corrupted judge did not bring the accused into a court, did not grant him a
word, but condemned him who had not even seen a court, as caught, as convicted
and a completed adulterer, and threw him into a prison and gave him over to a
chain. And thereafter he who had bound on so many crowns of virtue was in the
prison with sorcerers, with grave-robbers, with man-killers, with those who
had dared the last things. =10.36= But yet none of these threw him into
tumult. And another who had offended the king was released, but he remained a
long time inside, paying the last punishment for the things for which he ought
to have been crowned and proclaimed. And not even so was he disturbed, nor
scandalized, nor did he say: "What is this? for what is this? I who was going
to reign over the brothers have not only fallen from this honor, but also from
fatherland and house and parents, and I was destroyed by those who were going
to do obeisance to me. =10.37= Then after the slaughter I was sold, I became a
slave of barbarians and I changed masters continually; and not even here did
the terrible things stop for me, but everywhere pits and reefs. For after the
plot of the brothers and the slaughter and the slavery, both the first and the
second, death was planned against me again and a slander harsher than the
former and a plot and an assault and a corrupted court and an accusation
having much shame and travailing with slaughter for us. =10.38= And without
sharing in a word, simply and as it chanced I was carried off to prison and I
am wrapped in chains with adulterers and man-killers and those who have dared
the last things. And the chief cupbearer was freed both from chain and from
prison; but I have not been able, even after him, to enjoy any immunity; and
for him the dream came into deed according to my interpretation; but I am in
incurable evils. =10.39= Did those visions foretell these things? did the
number of the stars? did the sheaves? Where are the things of the promises?
where the things of the pledges? Have I been deceived? have I been cheated?
for how will the brothers thereafter do obeisance to me, the slave, the
captive, the prisoner, the one thought to be an adulterer, the one in danger
of the last things, the one settled so far from them? All those things are
gone and have perished." =10.40= None of these did he say, he did not think,
but he waited for the end, knowing himself also the ingenuity of God and the
resourceful wisdom; and not only was he not scandalized, but he even prided
himself and adorned himself with the things that were happening. =10.41= And
what of David, tell me? Did he not, after being anointed as king and receiving
the scepters of the people of the Hebrews by the vote of God and setting up
that splendid trophy against the barbarian, suffer the harshest things of all,
being warred upon, plotted against by Saul, in danger concerning life itself,
sent out into perilous wars, continually toward the



wilderness driven, becoming a wanderer, a fugitive, cityless, homeless, a
migrant. =10.42= Why need one say the many things? At last he even fell from
the fatherland and from that whole country of his own, spending time among
barbarians the most hostile enemies and enduring a life harsher than slavery;
for he even lacked necessary food. And these things he suffered after the
coming of Samuel, after the anointing with oil, after the promise of the
kingdom, after the scepters, after the diadem, after the appointment of God
and the vote upon him. =10.43= And yet none of these scandalized him, nor did
this one say either: "What is this? I the king, who was going to enjoy so
great a rule, cannot even be a private man with safety; but I have become a
wanderer and a fugitive, cityless, homeless, a migrant, and I have been
carried off into a barbarian country and lacking necessary food and I see the
danger of the last things hanging over me each day. Where are the promises of
the kingdom? where the pledges of that rule?" None of these did he say, he did
not think; he was not scandalized by the things happening, but he too waited
for the end of the promises. =10.44= And one can speak of myriads of others
who, falling into harsh affairs, were not thrown into tumult, but holding to
the announcement of God, even if the things happening were contrary to the
promises, through this fairest patience reaped splendid crowns. You too then,
beloved, wait for the end; for it will altogether arrive, either here or in
the age to come; and yield everywhere to the incomprehensible of God's
providence and do not say: "But how will so many evils receive correction?"
and do not pry into the manner of God's wonder-working. 11.t Chapter 11. That
this did not happen at once, and that at the beginning the saints, seeing all
things contrary, were not scandalized 11.1 For neither did those just men then
seek this: How and in what manner will these things be; but seeing all things
according to human reasoning borne toward despair, not even so were they
thrown into tumult, nor were they disturbed; but they bore all things nobly,
having as a very great proof of the good things to come the power of the one
who promised, and from the contrariety of the things happening they did not
fall into despair. 11.2 For they knew clearly that being ingenious and wise,
he will be able even after the affairs have been given up to recover them more
than before and with much ease to set the end upon the things promised. You
too then, beloved, if the painful things receive a solution in the present
life, glorify God; if they end in the hard things, even so give thanks and do
not be scandalized, knowing clearly the infinite providence of God that cannot
be interpreted, and that all things will altogether receive the fitting end,
whether in the present life or in the future. 11.3 And if someone hearing the
future and being small-souled should be eager to see this happening here, we
will say that the true life and the firm and unmoved affairs then await us.
For the present things are a road, those a fatherland; the things here are
like spring flowers, those like unmoved rocks; there are crowns and
recompenses having no end, there rewards and prizes, there punishments and
unbearable penalties of those who have worked such evils. 11.4 What then will
you say to those who are still being scandalized? You do not speak of those
who shine more greatly, but you bring into the midst those who wear masks of
piety and are now being convicted. Do you not see the gold being refined? the
lead being convicted? the chaff being separated from the grain? the wolves

boasts in it? For on that fearful and dread day, when he comes displaying his
own
glory, when he comes in the glory of his Father, when the fearful tribunal is
present, when the whole nature of men stands by, when rivers of fire are
bubbling, when peoples of angels and of the powers above are poured down all
at once with him, when those myriads of prizes, when some shine as the sun,
others as stars, =17.12= when companies of martyrs, when choruses of apostles,
when ranks of prophets, when assemblies of noble men are all brought into the
midst, then indeed, then in that splendor, in that conspicuousness, he comes
bearing it sending out splendid rays. "For then," he says, "the sign of the
Son of man will appear in heaven, and the sun will be darkened and the moon
will not give its light, but the sign of the cross will appear." =17.13= O
splendor of the passion, o brightness of the cross; the sun is darkened and
stars fall like leaves, but the cross shines more manifest than all those,
occupying the whole heaven. Do you see how the Master adorns himself with
this? how he shows the thing to be his glory, when on that day he displays it
to the whole inhabited world with such brilliance? 18.t Chapter 18. That no
small gain has come to the church from the things that have happened 18.1 You
too then, when you see some being scandalized from the things that have
happened, first reckon this, that they have the scandals not from this, but
from their own weakness; and those who have not suffered this make it clear.
Then consider that many have also shone more greatly from this, glorifying God
and giving thanks to him with all zeal even for these things. Therefore do not
look at those who are being shaken, but also at those who stand firmly and
remain unmoved and become stronger by this; not at those who are thrown into
tumult, but also at those who sail with a fair wind and are far more than
those who have been swept away. And if those were more, one who does the will
of the Lord is better than myriads of lawless. 19.t Chapter 19. That many
martyrs have come to be in this matter, both living and dead 19.1 Consider how
many have also bound on a crown of martyrdom. For some were scourged, others
were thrown into prison, others were wrapped in chains as evildoers, others
fell from fatherland, others cast away property, others toward the



exile were moved, others were slaughtered, some both in the experience, others
in the purpose. 19.2 For even with spears being bared and swords sharpened and
threats happening each day and those in office breathing anger and stretching
out murders and myriads of kinds of punishments and penalties, they did not
yield, nor give in, but stood unmoved on the rock, choosing both to do and to
suffer all things, so as not to share in the lawlessness of those who had
dared such things, not men only, but also women. 19.3 For women too stripped
for this contest and in many places showed themselves more manly than men. And
not women only, but also youths and quite young boys. Are these then, tell me,
small things, for the Church to gain so great a people of martyrs? For all
these are martyrs. For not only those who were dragged into a court and
commanded to sacrifice and not persuaded, having suffered what they suffered,
would be martyrs; but also those who accepted to suffer something for any of
the things that seem good to God; and if someone should examine with
exactness, these rather than those. 19.4 For it is not equal to accept to
suffer something when such destruction and loss of soul is held out, and not
to perish, and to endure this same punishment for a lesser achievement. And
that not only those slaughtered, but also those prepared and become ready for
this, bound on a crown of martyrdom, both this very thing and what I said
before, that he who is slaughtered for lesser things is a completed martyr, I
will try to prove from the voice of Paul. 19.5 For beginning to number those
who shone among the ancestors, the blessed Paul, and making the beginning from
Abel, then going on to Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, added saying: "Therefore
we too, having so great a cloud of martyrs surrounding us." 19.6 And yet not
all these were slaughtered, or rather not even one, except two or three, Abel
and John; but all the others ended life by their own death. And John himself
was not slaughtered commanded to sacrifice and not accepting, nor brought to
an altar, nor dragged toward an idol, but for one word. For when he said to
Herod. "It is not lawful for you to have the wife of Philip your brother," he
both dwelt in the prison and endured that slaughter. 19.7 And if he who
avenged a marriage being made lawless, as far as it lay with him (for he did
not even set right what had been done badly, but only spoke, and was not able
to make it cease), if then he who only spoke and brought in nothing of his own
except this, since he was beheaded, is a martyr and first of martyrs; those
who have endured so many slaughters and have stripped not against Herod but
against those who rule the whole inhabited world, and have stood not by a
marriage being made lawless but by ancestral laws and ordinances of the Church
being abused, and have displayed boldness of speech both through words and
through deeds and die each day, both men and women and children, how would
they not be right to be numbered myriads of times into the chorus of martyrs.
19.8 Since Abraham too, not slaughtering the son in the experience,
slaughtered him in the intention and heard a voice from above saying: "You did
not spare your beloved son for my sake." Thus everywhere even the purpose,
when it is completed upon virtue, receives the whole crown. 19.9 And if that
one, not sparing a son, was thus proclaimed, these not sparing themselves,
consider how great a wage they will receive, standing not one, not two and
three days, but a whole life on this battle-line, being struck with insults,
outrages, abuses, slanders. For this is not small either. Therefore Paul also
admires it as great, saying: "This in part being made a spectacle by
reproaches and afflictions, and this in part becoming partners of those so
suffering." =19.10= What would one say of those who themselves also die and of
those who, contesting such things, anoint both men and women? And he admires
them well. For many also gave over property, so that



the prisoners and the exiles might have some consolation of so great a misery,
and they received the plunder of their goods with joy according to that
apostolic saying; others, some were cast out of fatherland, others even of
life itself. =19.11= Seeing then so great a wealth, so great a gain, so great
a commerce gathered for the Church, so many treasures being stored, those
formerly collapsed becoming more vehement than fire and those nailed to
theaters going out into the wildernesses and making the glens and the
mountains a church and, with no one leading the flock out, the sheep migrating
into the rank of shepherds and the soldiers into that of the general, for the
sake of boldness of speech and courage, and all performing the gatherings with
the fitting warmth, zeal, care, are you not struck and do you not wonder how
great an achievement has come from this? =19.12= For not only those living
rightly, but many also of those raving about the theaters and attending to
horse-races, kindled by a zeal more vehement than fire, put off all that
madness and almost leaped against the swords themselves, speaking boldly
toward rulers, despising tortures, laughing at threats, showing how much
virtue is able and how it is possible for the one greatly perishing, having
repented and migrated, to touch the vault of the heavens themselves. =19.13=
Seeing then so many prizes, so many crowns being plaited, so great a teaching
happening, from where, tell me, are you scandalized? From those who have
perished, he says. But, as I said and will not cease saying, let these reckon
the occasion of the destruction to themselves. For this the discourse,
contending through the whole, has shown us. Let me speak also of another
achievement. How many wearing a mask of piety, how many having a fabricated
meekness, how many thought to be some great ones, and not being so, were
suddenly convicted at the present time and the things of the deceit flowed
away for them and they appeared what they were, not what they pretended and
deceived? =19.14= And this is not small, but even very great toward the
benefit of those who are willing to attend, to distinguish those who have the
skins of sheep and not to mix the wolves so hidden with the true sheep. For
this time has become a furnace, convicting the coins that have bronze, melting
the lead, burning the straw, showing the precious materials more precious.
This Paul also meaning said: "For there must also be heresies, in order that
the approved may become manifest among you." 20.t Chapter 20. That even in the
apostles' time worse things happened 20.1 Let none of these then scandalize
you, not a priest now become base and leaping upon the flock more wildly than
any wolf, not one of those who rule displaying much rawness. For consider that
even in the apostles' time worse than these happened. 20.2 For he who then
held the scepter was the mystery of lawlessness (for thus Paul called him),
going through every kind of vice and hiding all by his wickedness, but this
harmed neither the Church nor those noble men at all, but even showed them
more splendid; and the priests of the Jews were so base and wicked that the
peoples were commanded to flee the zeal of their life; 20.3 "For on Moses'
seat," says the Savior, "the scribes and the Pharisees sat; all things then
whatever they tell you to do, do; but according to their works do not do"; and
yet what could be more wicked than priests whose zeal was going to destroy
those who imitated them? But yet even with those then ruling being such, those
who shone, those who were crowned, were not harmed at all, but were more
greatly glorified. One must not then be surprised at the



things happening. For everywhere trials are yoked to the sober from their own,
from strangers. 20.4 For this reason Paul too, seeing the snowstorms of
dangers being brought upon them and fearing lest some of the disciples be
thrown into tumult from this, writing said: "I sent you Timothy so that no one
be shaken in these afflictions; for you yourselves know that we are set for
this." And what he means is of this sort. 20.5 This is our life, this the
sequence of the apostolic way of life, to suffer myriads of evils. "For we are
set for this," he says. What is "We are set for this"? Just as wares have been
brought out for this, to be sold, so also the apostolic life is for being
insulted and for suffering badly, for never drawing breath, for having no
pause. 20.6 And as many as are sober, not only are not harmed at all from
this, but even gain more greatly. Therefore he also admires these, after
learning that they have stood nobly; and about others too he says these
things, that after his bonds and the chains they dared more abundantly to
speak the word without fear. 20.7 And what in the time of Moses, tell me? in
the midst of a barbarian country did God not allow magicians also to display
their things? does not Paul also mention this history? "And as Jannes and
Jambres," he says, "withstood Moses, so these too will withstand the truth."
This never failed, neither the scandals, nor those crowned through these.
Reckon then all these things and not these only, but also how great a gain has
come from the matter. 20.8 And consider this too, that there are also some
other unspeakable reasons of these things; for it is not possible for us to
know all things; and that the better things will arrive after these and the
wonder-working will be more; just as in the case of Joseph the beginning had
difficulty and the affairs went on for a long time, seeming to be contrary to
the promise; but later they became greater than what was expected. And in the
case of the cross too not at once, nor in the preludes and at the beginning,
did the whole achievement sprout, but the scandal went forward and few signs
happened for the sake of wonder and of the correction of those who dared these
things, and at once all things passed by. 20.9 For even if the veil of the
temple was then split and rocks were broken and the sun was darkened, still
these things happened in one day and in forgetfulness among the many. And
after these things at once the apostles were in flight, in persecutions, in
wars, in plots, hiding, being concealed, fearing, thus they preached the word;
and the people of the Jews in much power, leading, leading about, dragging,
tearing those who believed. For having the rulers with themselves, each day
they led and led about the apostles. =20.10= And why do I speak of the people
of the Jews and rulers? One tentmaker, occupied about hides, Paul (and what is
cheaper than a tentmaker?) used such madness as to drag men and women and give
them over into prison; and the crucified one endured seeing these things. But
see how after these things this persecutor too outshot all and the matter
shone beyond the sun and occupied the whole inhabited world. 21.t Chapter 21.
That both in the old and in the new there are many trials. 21.1 And if you
should say: "And for what reason both in the Old and in the New so many
dangers, so many trials, so many plots?" learn also the cause. What then is
the cause? The present life is a wrestling-school, a gymnasium and a contest,
a melting-pot, a dye-works of virtue. Just as then the tanners, taking the
hides, first tan them, stretch them, strike them and dashing them against
walls and stones and by myriads of other treatments having made them fit for
the reception of the



dye, thus they apply the precious color; 21.2 and the goldsmiths, throwing the
gold into the fire, give it over to the test of the furnace, so as to make it
most pure; and the trainers, in the wrestling-school, exercise those who
contest with many toils, falling upon them more vehemently than the opponents,
in order that, having achieved the whole in the exercise in their own bodies,
they may be ready at the contests and prepared toward the grips of the enemies
and easily undo them, so also God does in the present life. 21.3 Wanting to
refashion the soul as fit for virtue, he both tans and melts and gives it over
to a test of trials, so that both those collapsed and dissolved may be
tightened, and the approved may become more approved and unconquerable to the
plots of the demons and the snares of the devil, and all very fit for the
reception of the goods to come. 21.4 For a man, he says, untried, is
unapproved; and Paul: "Affliction works patience; and patience proof." Wanting
then to make them enduring and patient, he allows the coin to be rung in every
manner. 21.5 For this cause he also allowed Job to suffer what he suffered, so
that he might both appear more approved and stop the devil's mouth; for this
he also allowed the apostles, so that those too might become more courageous
and display their own power by this. For this too is no small cause. Therefore
he also said to Paul seeking relief and deliverance from the evils that held
him: "My grace is sufficient for you; for my power is perfected in weakness."
22.t Chapter 22. That the things of trials not only do not scandalize those
who judge fairly, but even benefit them, even if they happen to be Greeks 22.1
For even those who have not yet come to the word of Christianity gain more
greatly from this too, if they are sober. For when they see them being
wronged, insulted, dwelling in prison, being abused, plotted against, having
their heads cut off, being burned, being drowned and yielding to none of the
terrible things, consider how great a wonder they went away having of these
paradoxical athletes, both those then and those now, so that the things that
have happened not only do not work a scandal for the sober, but even become an
occasion of more teaching. 22.2 Therefore Paul also heard these things, that:
"My power is perfected in weakness." This one can find both in the Old and in
the New. For consider what it was likely for Nebuchadnezzar to suffer from
three children, slaves, captives, bound, being burned, being defeated with so
great an army present and not being able to prevail over three enslaved
bodies, subject, fallen from fatherland, freedom, honor, power, money, settled
far from their own. If then that burning had not been allowed, the prize would
not have become so splendid, the crown would not have been so bright. 22.3
Consider what it was likely for Herod to suffer, being convicted by a prisoner
and seeing him not even from the chain giving in as to boldness of speech, but
choosing rather to be slaughtered than to give up that fairest freedom of
speech. 22.4 Consider who, seeing these things and hearing, both of those then
living and of those after these things, even if he is greatly collapsed, but
has some small mind, how he goes away having gained the greatest things. For
do not tell me the offscourings of the mindless, the sluggards who are all
flesh and lighter than leaves. For these stumble not at these things only, but
at almost everything that has happened; as also the people of the Jews, both
eating manna and bread, likewise



was displeased, both being in Egypt and being delivered from Egypt and with
Moses present and having gone away. 22.5 But bring into the midst for me
those, the sober, the awake, and reckon how great a benefit they are likely to
harvest from this, seeing an unturned soul, an unenslaved mind, a tongue full
of boldness of speech, a wilderness-citizen prevailing over a king, being
bound and not giving in, being beheaded and not being silent; and do not even
stop at these, but examine also the things after these. 22.6 Herod cut off,
John was beheaded. Who then is among the beatitudes of all? who is to be
emulated? who is proclaimed? who is crowned? who is eulogized? who is praised?
who is wondered at? who until today convicts? 22.7 Does not in each Church the
one cry: "It is not lawful for you to have the wife of Philip your brother;"
and the other is posted even after death for the adultery and the lawlessness
and the boldness? And consider with what has been said, how great the power of
the prisoner and the weakness of the tyrant. 22.8 For the one was not able to
stop one tongue, but having destroyed it, he opened myriads of mouths instead
of that one and after that one; while the other even then at once frightened
him after the slaughter (for so the fear shook his conscience, that he then
thought him even risen from the dead working wonders), and now too and from
that time through all time he convicts him through the whole inhabited world
both through himself and through others. 22.9 For each one reading this Gospel
says: "It is not lawful for you to have the wife of Philip your brother"; and
apart from the Gospel in gatherings and companies, those at home, those in the
market, those everywhere, even if you go away into the country of the
Persians, even into that of the Indians, even into that of the Moors, even as
much earth as the sun looks upon and toward the very extremities, you will
hear this voice and you will see that just man still even now crying, sounding
and convicting the vice of the tyrant and never being silent, nor the
conviction withering by the multitude of time. =22.10= How then was that just
man harmed from the end? and how from the violent death? and how from the
chain? and how from the prison? And whom of those who have mind did he not set
upright, from what he said, from what he suffered, from what he still even now
preaches the same things which also then living? Do not then say: "Why was he
allowed to die?" For what happened was not death, but a crown, not an end, but
preludes of a greater life. Learn to philosophize and you will not only not be
harmed at all from such things, but you will even gain the greatest things.
=22.11= And what of the Egyptian woman? Did she not accuse? did she not
slander? did she not bind the just man? did she not throw him into prison? did
she not hang over him the danger of the last things? did she not destroy him,
as far as it lay with her? did she not put a wicked fame around him? How then
did it harm him either then, or now? For just as coals of fire covered by
chaff seem at the beginning to be shadowed, but suddenly eating through what
lies upon them, through those very chaffs they kindle a higher flame; so also
virtue, even if it seems to be abused, later indeed through the hindrances
themselves blooms more greatly and reaches the heaven itself. =22.12= For what
could become more blessed than that youth because of the slander, because of
the plot, not because of the throne in Egypt, nor because of the kingship
there? For everywhere the glories and the good standings and the crowns are
allotted together with the sufferings. Do not all =22.13= everywhere in the
inhabited world also sing this one? =22.14= And so great a multitude of time
has not withered his memory, but more splendid than royal images and more
lasting the images of his virtue and of his chastity are set up everywhere in
the inhabited world, in the country of the Romans, in the barbarians' country,
in each one's conscience, in each one's =22.15= tongue. =22.16= And we all see
him being held, being ruled, counseling the wretched and miserable harlot in
the things needed and contributing all things from himself toward her
salvation, putting her shamelessness to shame, quenching the



furnace, attempting to snatch her from a harsh winter and to moor her into the
calm; then since the surge prevailed and the ship became waterlogged, that one
being wrecked, fleeing the waves and running onto the solid rock of chastity
and leaving the garments in her hands and appearing more splendid in the
nakedness than those wrapped in purples and, as some champion and bright
trophy-bearer, setting up the trophy of chastity. =22.17= And not even in
these do we end the memory, but going further, we see him again being led away
to prison, bound, squalid, wasting there a long time. And for these things
especially we wonder at him again, we count him blessed, we are struck, we
praise. And if someone is chaste, considering him he becomes more chaste; and
if incontinent, more quickly by the narrative he migrates toward chastity and
is made better by the history. =22.18= Gathering then all these things, be
thrown into no tumult, but even gain from the things happening; and let the
patience of those who contest become a teacher of endurance to you, and seeing
the whole life of the noble and high men woven through such things, do not be
confused, nor disturbed, neither by private nor by common trials; for the
Church too from the beginning was thus nourished, thus grew. Be surprised then
at nothing; for nothing unlikely has happened. =22.19= But just as in worldly
things, not where there is chaff and grass, nor where there is sand, but where
there is gold and pearls, there pirates and wreckers and robbers and
wall-breakers continually trouble and plot, so also the devil, wherever he
sees wealth according to the soul being gathered and abundance of piety being
stretched, there he sets his devices and brings them on. But if those plotted
against are sober, not only are they not lessened at all from this, but they
even gather a greater wealth of virtue; which indeed has now also happened.
23.t Chapter 23. That the things happening are a very great sign of the good
standing of the Church, and that these things have helped many 23.1 And one
might make this a very great sign both of the wealth of the achievements and
of the courage, of the Church. For when that wicked demon saw her blooming,
standing well, brought up to a height in a brief turn of time, much zeal
happening in her and the increase of those standing well toward the better,
the migration of those living in sins into repentance, the whole inhabited
world being catechized from this city; he moved all his devices and kindled
civil wars. 23.2 And just as in the case of Job, now the casting away of the
money, now the childlessness, now the sickness of the body, now the tongue of
the wife, now the reproaches and the jeers and the insults of the friends he
moved against the just man and brought on every kind of devices; so also in
the case of the Church, through friends, through enemies, through those
belonging to the clergy, through those enrolled in the army, through those
honored with episcopacy, through many and all kinds of persons he moved the
things from himself. 23.3 But yet even having devised so many things, he not
only did not shake her, but even made her more splendid. For she did not so,
when not being troubled then, teach all as now she teaches the inhabited world
to endure, to be continent, to bear trials, to display patience, to despise
worldly things, to count wealth as nothing, to laugh at honor, to look past
death, to despise life, to overlook fatherland, household, friends, kinsmen,
to strip for slaughters of every kind, to tumble against swords, to think all
the splendid things of the present life, I mean honors and glories and



power and luxury, cheaper than spring flowers. 23.4 And these things not one
only teaches, nor two and three, but the whole people, and not through words
only, but also through deeds, through what they suffer, through what they
conquer, through what they prevail over those who plot, through what they bear
all things more firmly than adamant and stronger than rock, not moving
weapons, not kindling war, not shooting bow, not arrows, but each having put
on a wall of patience, of care, of meekness, of courage, by suffering badly
putting to shame from a great surplus those who do badly. 24.t Chapter 24.
That those who committed these outrages are already paying the penalty from
this as well 24.1 Now at least these with a bright face, with free eyes, using
unspeakable boldness of speech, throw themselves into the market, turn about
in houses, go of themselves to the gathering; but those who have worked these
things, at each of the devices which they bring on, covering themselves and
having a wicked conscience inside, trembling, fearing, thus they go about.
24.2 And just as beasts hard to kill, after a first and second
blow, falling more vehemently upon the points of the spears, push a harsher
blow against themselves, receiving the wounds into the very bowels; and the
waves breaking on the rocks, by the more vehement rush wipe themselves out and
dissolve; so also these, through the things they plot, dig the pits more for
themselves than for others. 24.3 For those plotted against have the inhabited
world as lovers, praisers, admirers, proclaimers, crowners, those who know,
those who do not know, those who learn their things from the affairs, those
from the report, myriads who share the pain, those who share the contest, all
who pray the good things together with them; but those who plot have so many
who hate them and far more, the accusers, the slanderers, those who convict,
the ones putting them to shame, those who curse myriads, those who desire to
see
them in punishment and penalty. 24.4 And these things here; but the things
there, what word will present? For if one who has scandalized one is condemned
to so great a punishment, that it is expedient that a millstone be hung on his
neck and he be drowned in the sea; consider how many penalties these will give
in that fearful court then, how many punishments they will undergo, as far as
it lay with them, having disturbed the whole inhabited world, having
overturned so many Churches, having warred upon so great a peace, having set
myriads of scandals everywhere? 24.5 But those who have suffered from them
what they suffered will stand with the martyrs, with the apostles, with the
noble and high men, shining from the achievements, from the sufferings, from
the crowns, from the prizes, from the much boldness of speech. 24.6 And they
will see them being punished, but to snatch them from the punishment, even if
they should wish myriads of times, they will not be able; but these will set a
supplication, and they will profit nothing. For if he who passed by one poor
man, Lazarus, endured so great a penalty and obtained no consolation, what
will these suffer, having persecuted and scandalized so many? 24.7 Reckoning
then all these things and gathering from the divine Scriptures as many as are
like these, constructing these narratives as a safe wall for yourselves, and
as drugs for those still weaker, stand firm and unmoved, waiting for the goods
laid up for you. 24.8 For altogether, altogether a recompense will be laid up
for you, not equal to the toils, but from a great surplus unspeakable. For
such is the loving-kind God; with much ambition those who choose to



do or to say some good thing he has been eager to conquer with the repayments
and the recompenses.
