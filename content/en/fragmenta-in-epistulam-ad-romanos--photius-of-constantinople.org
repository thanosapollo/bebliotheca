470 Rο+m1,1 He set Paul first, wishing not even to open his mouth without
remembering the Master's benefaction, as if to say: Paul, he says, who has
even the calling itself from benefaction, from grace, from the Master's
munificence; Paul, who bears all things from Christ, the calling, the grace,
the manner of life. Slave: for I have been wholly sold to him, having been
freed from the law and from sin. Slave: this is my dignity, from this I wish
to be known to all. Those who go about in the world wish to be made known from
worldly dignities, inscribing themselves consuls and generals, but for Paul it
is enough for greatness of dignity to be a slave of Christ. Rο+m1,2 Gospel of
God, because he was announcing God as good news: for Christ whom Paul was
announcing is God. Or rather he says of God the Father: for this one spoke
through his prophets concerning his true Son. Rο+m1,3–5 And of resurrection,
he says, by which our Lord Jesus Christ rose. He did not say concerning a son
of his, but of his Son, showing the one who is Son by nature and the one and
properly Son and only-begotten. Of him who was marked out, recognized, who
came into knowledge for human beings: for we are accustomed through certain
definitions to recognize the things we know securely and not in doubt. Then
how then was he marked out? How did he come into knowledge? From prophetic
oracles, from the powers which he performed, from the Holy Spirit which he was
giving to those who had believed in him, from his rising from the dead: for
the concurrence of these marks him off from all the others who have been
called sons according to grace, and shows him and teaches all that he is the
true and natural Son of God and God. Whence we can also, stringing these
things together in a direct line, both present the exceptional and
incommunicable character of his sonship and 471 furnish to the faithful a
clear knowledge concerning him, as if saying: Christ is the one proclaimed
beforehand through the prophets, who became according to flesh from the seed
of David, who by powers and by the supply of the Holy Spirit was benefiting
the race of human beings and through his own resurrection put death to death
and raised together those dead from of old; this is the Christ, this is the
Son of God, as one may say in a definition and a summary. Add if you wish
toward a clearer unfolding, through whom Paul also received grace of
apostleship, unto persuading the barbarian nations and preparing them to obey
the faith that is in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that is to believe in
him and to worship him as true God and Son of God. Rο+m1,5–7 Grace and
apostleship, i.e. grace unto apostleship. And such a figure of speech is among
many, and especially throughout the God-inspired Scripture, which also is
redundant. Unto obedience of faith. The faith came to be in his name: for the
nations did not see him, but when the name alone was being proclaimed those
were believing. Truly the apostleship of the disciples was full of grace,
because they persuaded the nations to obey and believe in Christ, and that
though they were both human beings and fishermen and unlettered. To all who
are in Rome, beloved. From the address itself he prefaces that they be
humble-minded: for having said to all and not divided them into rulers and
ruled, slaves and free, he advises them unnoticed to consider one another of
equal honor. Then, lest they say: what then? do you consider all of equal
honor, both good and worthless, both faithful and unfaithful? he added: called
saints: for all the saints are of equal honor to him, and there is no
distinction among them. Called. From where were you called? From what labors?
From what achievements? From where are you saints? But from the love of God
alone: for he himself, having loved us freely, and having poured out his blood
for us, called us unto sanctification and inheritance of his kingdom. And he
calls all the faithful saints. Rο+m1,11–12 The imparting a gift to you is not
mine alone, but of the


ministry on the one hand mine, and of your service on the other: for lest 472
having heard that they are still in need of a gift, and that they need to be
established, they still take it ill, he makes common both the spiritual gift
and their being established. And it is not, he says, that these things come to
be mine or of my choice and ministry alone, but it is common to you and to me;
and not even this simply, but through the faith in one another. How and in
what manner? If you yourselves have, he says, faith to receive the gift, and I
again faith to give it to you, then indeed the gift, being spiritual, is also
operated and given through the faith in one another: for if I have faith to
give and much struggle and zeal, but you do not have faith to receive, nor
desire and care about receiving, the spiritual gift does not come to you, even
if I wish it. Both things must therefore run together, both that from me and
that from you; but that from me is present (for I not only simply judge that
you ought to receive the gift but also long for it), what remains is that from
you. And this comes about through good works and fruits worthy of the gift:
for then you yourselves will have faith to receive and desire, when you
prepare yourselves worthy of the gift; and then still more I too will have
more faith and longing to give, so that from every side it is from you that
you receive. And when you receive the gift, the gain becomes common, and
clearly we are comforted and rejoice in one another. Through the faith in one
another: you on the one hand through receiving and accepting the faith, I on
the other through proclaiming and handing this over; or also you on the one
hand taking me as trustworthy to proclaim and teach, I again having judged you
trustworthy to be entrusted with the oracles of God and the more perfect
teaching; or I on the one hand seeing you receiving the proclamation eagerly
and from the ready, you again receiving my zeal and the care about it with
your own sight: for a teacher's zeal is great toward comfort of disciples, and
again disciples' readiness and eagerness toward a teacher's comfort. And this
also contributes to a greater full assurance of the faith for both, when the
one who proclaims sees the souls of human beings drawn paradoxically unto
obedience (for he rather wonders at the divinity and the majesty of the faith
and is rather strengthened unto proclaiming this), and when again the
disciples see the herald handing over the faith vigorously and with firm hope:
for they rather receive it and are rather established in it, and thus both are
comforted through the faith in one another. 473 Rο+m1,13–14 The in order that
I may have some fruit lies by hyperbaton: for the sequence would be: I often
intended to come to you, in order that I may have some fruit among you, but I
was hindered until now. But someone might say that if their faith was being
announced in the whole world, what fruit did he himself wish, having arrived,
to furnish? But we will say much indeed: for even if they had believed, they
still needed very many things. And this is shown both through the whole
epistle and a little before when he says unto your being established, and when
he exhorts not to judge one another, and when he exhorts to turn away from
dissensions and scandals, and a thousand things. Nothing then is unreasonable
both that their faith is announced and that they still need much of Paul's aid
and of the fruit-bearing through him. And see how he says: in order that I may
have some fruit also among you? Showing this on the one hand, that their fruit
was reckoned as Paul's own fruit, and he so rejoiced in the achievements of
the neighbors that he thought he himself also had the fruit. And showing this
again, that for the sake of their benefit he was eager to send the journey
toward them, but so that their fruit was truly also Paul's fruit: for it was
work and eagerness that the nations bear fruit. Well then he says: in order
that I may have some fruit, showing both things, both that they bear virtue as
fruit and that he [bears] these very ones: for in their fruit-bearing Paul's


fruit-bearing was being shown. But let these things be so. And it is also
possible, taking the passage not by hyperbaton, but having received it as it
has order, to hunt such a sense in it: I was hindered until now; why? he says.
Lest coming too quickly I still catch you fruitless, therefore I postponed the
presence, until I learn that among you also some fruit worthy of the faith is
sprouting and being brought to completion. What then if they even bear fruit?
What is the need of Paul's presence? Much indeed: for Paul was not waiting for
them to bear all the fruit, but some. And very reasonably: for just as it is
superfluous to exhort the one who is already perfectly bearing fruit unto
fruit-bearing, so it is laborious and produces hesitation [to exhort] that
which is not serviceable toward fruit-production and does not furnish good
hopes. And if there was even some fruit and this perfect, not even so was Paul
simply cut short from the presence: for he calls himself a debtor so that,
even if they were flourishing with works, he would not have stood back from
doing his own part. 474 And it is possible to see the thought also otherwise.
I was hindered until now; why? Because I was still gathering some fruit that
was lacking for me. And this sense one may see confirmed at the end of the
epistle, as the apostle recaps what was prefaced, more than the other
interpretations: for he says: therefore I was also being cut short from coming
to you, but now no longer having a place in these regions, I will come to you.
So that the fruit hoped for until now for gathering in the other regions was
hindering him from running to Rome, leaving the work unfinished among those.
Then the among you and what follows is as it were a solution of an objection,
as if: what? he says, do you not care to have fruit among us? Yes, he says,
this too among you I am eager for, this I seek, I am a debtor, I owe to all.
Rο+m1,16–18 But to the Jew first. For the Jew is required to believe first,
inasmuch as he has been instructed in the things concerning Christ from law
and the prophets more than the nations, and because, even if Christ sojourned
for the common salvation of the race of human beings, still among the Jews he
both was born and lived as a citizen and taught and worked the divine signs
among them; for all which the Jews are debtors to believe in Christ first
rather than the nations. For the righteousness of God, he says, is revealed in
the gospel, that is all virtue is made manifest through the gospel: for he is
often accustomed to call all virtue righteousness. And how is it revealed and
to whom? To those who approach the gospel from faith: for the gospel proclaims
virtue to all and exhorts all to hold to virtue, but it reveals the beauty of
virtue to those, and makes those lovers of it, who approach with faith. What
then? Does faith cease when virtue is accomplished? By no means, but it also
proceeds unto the more perfect and higher. He believed, therefore he practices
virtue; he accomplishes virtue, he advances unto more perfect faith: for the
first faith was a certain compunction of soul and eagerness, but the faith
grown together with the working of the virtues and perfected is unchangeable
confirmation and full assurance. And the one resembles a sowing of seed, the
other the working of the virtues and fruit-bearing, and the faith grown
together through them and perfected [resembles] enjoyment and blessedness.
Thus then the righteousness of God is revealed in the gospel from faith 475
unto faith. And he calls virtue God's righteousness, because virtue is
something divine, and because all good things are from God. From faith unto
faith. For one must believe that God appeared on earth with flesh, which is a
beginning; and again that the kingdom of the heavens will be for those who
have believed as an inheritance, which is the end of faith. And but the
righteous shall live from faith is a testimony of the things said and a proof:
for the saying is prophetic, spoken to Habakkuk, and he says something of this
sort: I spoke well, he says, that the earnest man proceeds from faith unto
faith through virtue, for indeed Habakkuk, he says, from of old cries this
through the same Spirit, that the earnest human being lives from faith, that
is he has his whole life stretched out in faith. Or more properly: since


true life for the righteous is to flourish and live again in the acquisition
of the virtues, the righteous, he says, that is the earnest man, will live
from faith the life according to the virtues: for here [he speaks] of the life
of the saying, the flowing and allotted one. The argument is not very proper,
and but the righteous shall live from faith would be constructive of
righteousness is revealed from faith. But let these things be so. And if one
should take righteousness of God not as that given in a human being from God,
but, nearer the wording, that which is in God and is operated by him, some
such apostolic sense seems to me to arise, that the righteousness of God which
he displays concerning us by a definition and law of loving-kindness is shown
in his gospel. How and in what manner? That from faith alone he saves us and
already makes alive those who have been put to death, when we have not
previously displayed works. And this too as he goes on Paul sets more clearly
in the things in which he says: and justifying the one from faith, and again:
having then been justified from faith, and again: if you confess with your
mouth the Lord Jesus, then also believe in your heart, you will be saved. This
then is of a righteousness of God that overcomes human loving-kindness, to
raise already and make alive from faith alone us who have been put to death by
sins, so that he well said beforehand that the gospel is power unto salvation
for the one who believes: for indeed the one who has believed is empowered
through the righteousness revealed in it to rise from the fall from sins and
to be made alive. And he well said: is revealed: for to those who do not
believe such righteousness of God has been hidden, but through those who
believe it is revealed and made manifest. But unto what, he says, is the one
who has believed made alive? Unto the most perfect faith and according to the
strength of an unchangeable habit: for the making alive is from faith unto
faith. And it is possible also otherwise to 476 understand: the righteousness
of God is revealed through the gospel, because taking from us only a beginning
and seed of faith, he himself increases and establishes and perfects us unto
this very thing. This is of a righteousness truly of God, that he, having
taken only a starting-point of choice, himself completes the whole: for lest
anyone say, when Paul says above that the gospel is power unto salvation for
the one who believes: and how is it possible to believe when one hears a
cross, hears death, hears sufferings? he adds the righteousness of God is
revealed in it. Let these not be, he says, a pretext: for if you only choose,
then you have rather to wonder at God's care and providence concerning you,
because having taken the faith from judgment, he himself supplies to you the
faith from full assurance and confirmation. But one of the unbelievers would
not recognize this, even if it were interpreted a myriad times, whereas the
faithful need only a reminder: for he knows this by experience, that himself
putting forward at the beginning the faith from choice, from this he runs up
and is raised unto the faith according to knowledge and perfection. Then lest
you think that he says something new and recent, he brings the prophet as
witness: the same thing, he says, the prophet Habakkuk also [said] of old to
me, that the life of the righteous is encompassed and bounded by faith,
beginning from the faith according to choice, and being perfected and
proceeding unto the faith according to divine cooperation and revelation.
Therefore the righteous always lives from faith. And shall live has been well
said, that is the righteous does not live from faith and then cease, but
through all he shall live, advancing in it and being established. And he said
shall live, not something else such as he advances from faith or is
established or something else, showing the intense disposition, that he will
not even choose to live nor to breathe without faith. If then the gospel is
also power unto salvation for the one who believes, and to believe is easier
(for it needs choice alone), and it is possible for the one who has believed
to be established according to the faith by God and to be perfected. And in
those perfected according to the faith a love and pleasure grows concerning
it, so that they choose not even to live without this. One must therefore
eagerly through all these things and with zeal approach the proclamation of
the gospel. Well then also Paul was saying: for I am not


ashamed of the gospel of Christ which procures so many good things for us. Of
those who hold the truth in unrighteousness, covering it, darkening it by
wickedness of judgment: for it was easier, if they wished, from 477 the things
seen to benefit both themselves and others unto knowledge of God. But they
rather through these very things covered together and held down the knowledge
of God so that it not shine forth, having made these very things gods, and
having wronged, as far as in them lay, both the truth of the knowledge of God
and themselves and indeed those very things which they seemed to honor. And it
is clear that he rebukes the wise of the Greeks and those like them.
Rο+m1,19–26 Because that which is known of God, which is what of him it is
possible to know, that he is maker, that he is provider, and the like. For his
invisible things, such as angels and the other incorporeal substances, are
grasped and clearly seen from the creation of the world. How? These too being
understood in the things made and numbered together. The syntax is therefore
by hyperbaton: either being understood in the things made, i.e. being in those
very things made and serving the will of God and fulfilling the things
commanded; or he says his invisible things, the very invisible of his
substance. And how is it possible, he says, to behold and understand the
divine, it being invisible? Yes it is, he says, from his creatures to behold
his divinity and power. Both his everlasting power and divinity. How from the
creation of the world is the divinity of God and everlasting power known?
Someone saw the world and in it beauties of stars, size and course of moon and
an inviolable order and sequence of the sun: the moon does not make day nor
the sun the night, the nights withdraw in good order for the days, and the
days yield to the nights the analogous share. The aether keeps its own place,
and the air stands under, and neither does that one come under the foreign
region, nor does the air stretch into the things above it. The earth stands,
not having the one who supports infinite weight and not even being able to
rest upon [something], the water has been poured out for it and poured around
with an addition of wonder: for it both makes an addition of the weight and
[is] easily dissolved and multiplies the cause toward being carried down, but
nevertheless it stands unmoved, proclaiming more brightly than any voice the
power that holds it. It bears myriads of fruits, varying in myriads of forms,
differing in myriads of powers, colors, sizes, being one, being earth. It
feeds infinite kinds of animals, tame, wild, live-bearing, egg-bearing, having
infinite other difference and dissimilarity according to generation, venomous,
antidotal, creeping, flying, walking, swimming. Why would one attempt to tell
the differences of which there will be no end? But well-ordered changes of
seasons, but yearly rains, but also the things that seem passions,
earthquakes, 478 thunderbolts, but if you inspect any of these, it sends the
one who attends on to the craftsman: what is that which holds together? what
is that which constrains? how is it steered? how is it preserved? It is
possible then to know clearly from the creation, that there is a craftsman,
that he is provider, that he is almighty, that he is good (for both the
crafting and the providence are of a good one), that he is everlasting: for
who will plot against the almighty? and if this one is not everlasting, he is
both held together and brought forth by another who is altogether everlasting;
and this [goes] to infinity, so that he is also everlasting. Thus then his
divinity and everlasting power is clearly seen in the things made, thus from
the creation of the world the knowledge of God comes about for human beings,
thus he made manifest that which is known of him in all: for that which is
known of God is these things and such things, but substance and nature are
altogether ungraspable to all. These things the Greeks also knew, but having
known they did not glorify him as God. But O of much vanity and folly: the
things which they imagined as concerning God in a manner befitting God, these
they attached very wretchedly and impiously to idols and demons and the works
of his hands, having exchanged the glory of God for a glory of idols. And
instead of revering God they were reverent toward idols, and [this is] an
intensification of hardness and mindlessness toward idols of perishable
things, and still the evil having no excess, toward idols of reptiles and
birds and four-footed things. See madness, or rather see a deed


of which it is not even possible to find a worthy name. But that from the
creation of the world the everlasting power and divinity of God is clearly
seen, and that having known God they did not glorify him as God, is clear. But
how were they made vain in their reasonings? For of what sort of reasonings is
it to worship stones and woods? Of what sort of reasonings to call these
craftsmen and gods and everlasting and guardians? Why then are you at a loss,
that they did not worship, that they did not make gods? Or this at least is
clear. But with what reasonings do you seek? But you have this too: for with
vain and darkened and foolish ones. Having known God, that is: what ever is
God? what ought to be supposed God? Having known this they failed willingly in
the things that follow. Otherwise: Because having known God, that is having
been able to know, if they had wished. And having known, he says, that as much
as they knew from the teaching of the things seen, their knowledge was also
completed. Or having known, he says, properly, because they also knew God
insofar as he is knowable to human beings; therefore he was also saying that
that which is known of God is manifest in them. What then is that which is
known of him? That he is provident, that he is creative, that he is good, that
he is master, 479 that he is one who has authority; these things they knew
must belong to God, and that one must revere him; and having known God in this
and having advanced well up to this, they turned aside for the rest and were
carried away from the truth: for having imagined these high and dignified
things concerning God, next they did not suppose and glorify as befits God,
nor as God, but they were brought down into altogether irrational and vain
thoughts, imagining and shaping God to be an idol of a perishable human being
and of irrational animals. And this passion and the last destruction happened
to them, because they were trusting reasonings with the whole of the grasp
concerning God. And they came round to this again from thinking that they are
wise and sufficient to hunt everything that is graspable for human beings. O
how great an evil is conceit. Therefore also having become completely foolish
they took the notions and names befitting God, such as the things said: the
creative, the good, the provident, the masterly, the one who has authority,
the revered and the like, and they attached them to the works of their hands,
having exchanged the glory of God in a likeness and so on. But see the
punishment, consider the recompense: they were abandoned by God and became
handed over unto passions of dishonor, having set these up for themselves as a
prelude of the unending punishment from there. The God handed them over, that
is the offense and drunken outrage toward God: for we are also accustomed to
say on the contrary that the silver destroyed him, although it is not the
silver that destroys but his prodigal and evil use of it. And the multitude of
flatterers corrupted him, although it is not those who corrupt but the
attending and being persuaded by them: for whether there are flatterers or not
is not in me, but whether to be persuaded by them or not is in my judgment.
And such usage is a myriad, both in converse with one another and in the
interpretation of writings, not of those according to us only, but also of
those standing outside our court. But they were made vain in their reasonings.
From the visible things and from the intelligible things, he says, which God
created, they were able to behold and recognize his power and divinity, but
they did not wish. Therefore they rendered themselves without excuse: for
which was easier, to make idols of circles and signs in heaven and spindles
and threads and fate and the oblique and the cutting and poles and antipodes
and antomoi and long nonsense, or to know that there is a craftsman and
guardian and helmsman of this visible and supernatural beauty? 480 Rο+m1,28
And here the God handed over, i.e. the insolence and daring toward God, their
own deed, the voluntary hardness; this handed them over unto the passions of
dishonor. Rο+m1,29–32 For this the Mosaic law commands clearly and sharply,
judging those who do such things worthy of death. Rο+m2,1–5 He speaks of an
excess of lawlessness: and they themselves were doing


the unlawful things and were running together with those who do them, and
whenever someone caught by someone was being led to judgment, they themselves
were sitting and were condemning that one whose equals they themselves were
doing and with whom they were running together unto the doing, and
nevertheless they were condemning him. Thus they knew that they sin, so that
much more they had already condemned themselves. Rο+m2,6–8 The he will render
is from a common [construction]: who will also render: for he will render the
good things to some, and to others wrath and anger and the like will be
rendered. And the figure of speech is double: for both from a common
[construction] and a zeugma. And consider that the good things he will render,
he says, but the punishment he will not render but it will be rendered; and he
did not even say this according to the wording, but left it to be thought
along. Thus he knows that to justify and to save is proper to God, but that he
punish and chastise [comes] from the hardness of those who sin and from not
repenting and turning. Rο+m2,15–16 The between one another of the reasonings
has been said by hyperbaton: for it is next after for not the hearers of the
law are righteous before God, but the doers of the law will be justified; for
it is an interpretation, how the ones are not justified, and the others are
justified. And he says that the court not needing witnesses, nor certain
proofs from outside, but from home from the reasonings themselves both the sin
is presented and the virtue becomes evident. Therefore even if human beings
481 acquit here, one must not take heart in these: for they will benefit those
not at all. And if again they condemn, one must not care: for they will harm
nothing there, but the consciousness and testimony of one's own reasonings
suffices both unto condemnation and unto acquittal. And why having said of the
reasonings accusing, did he not also say justifying? For thus both those being
punished would have been shown through the accusing, and those being saved
through the justifying. But now the sequence urges one to understand as upon
one and the same, both the accusing and the defending. We say then that one
must not take both upon the one and the same, but of the reasonings accusing
upon others, that is those who will be punished, and defending upon others,
that is those who will be saved. Why did he not say: justifying and
condemning? Because these do not justify nor are they punished, but they
accuse or defend; and the judge brings on the judgment of the punishment or of
the justification. Well then he says: of the reasonings accusing or also
defending. Again it is possible to say: what then? How do they defend when no
one accuses? For the accusing has been said, that one must take it upon those
who will be punished, and the defending upon those who will be justified has
been well said. How then do they defend when no one accuses? They accuse even
here (for there is not, there is not a human being to be seen from there
without sin), but the accusation, since the sins do not have weight and bulk,
is not vehement nor inerasable; but the reasonings bring forward a defense
toward it, the slipperiness of nature, the weakness, the loving-kindness of
God, the mercy. And that [accusation] ceases, and the defense wins. From the
defense then they are, even if the accusation went before, these worthy to be
indicated. But since among those who perish the sins are absurd and hard, and
the accusation vehement and inexorable, and nowhere the defense, he sets again
only upon them the accusing. And those things are pardonable which we sinned
according to reasonings, but unpardonable those which we dragged as far as
deeds; and again pardonable those which we have cleansed through repentance,
but unpardonable those to which we have not even brought the remedy through
this and the healing. And the sense of the saying can also be taken as the
wording has order, as: those who have received the law have both the law and
the conscience accusing upon the things in which they sin. But those who have
not received, he says, the law, yet doing from conscience the things of the
law, these put forward the conscience as witness of the good-working; for lest
anyone say: then they do the good by chance, if they do not 482 do it by law,
by no means, he says, but with conscience, having this as teacher and witness
of the good-working.


Therefore they are also worthy of greater acceptance, and they bring on a
greater refutation against those who sin with law. Then since not all who are
without law work the good, but there are some who also offend, he shows that
not even they will be unpunished: for even if they do not have a law that
accuses, he says, yet these too will undergo penalties of the reasonings
accusing between one another. And the or also defending is a confirmation
brought on from the contrary, as if he were saying that, just as when the
reasonings defend an acquittal follows without the law, so again when they
accuse the condemnation follows without the law. And when these things? At the
second coming. Rο+m2,18–21 He is formed with the form of the knowledge and of
the truth in the law who knows it and lives according to it: for he has set
the form of knowledge upon the knowing, and the form of truth upon the doing;
for that one shows the knowledge in the law true, who fulfills it through the
works, that one [shows it] faithful. But you, he says, have trusted yourself
to be an instructor and a guide and a teacher, not simply, but as having the
form of the knowledge in the law and of the truth in the law; but you live so
as not even ever having heard a legal teaching. You then who have trusted that
you have the form of the knowledge and of the truth, who have trusted that you
