=46.757= {1ORATION II.} 1 Yesterday the martyrs were calling the people to
themselves; now they are hosted in the lodging of the Church, self-invited.
And there is a certain convivial law, that these circulating feasts be given
in turn by the guests to one another. Therefore it is necessary that we also
repay the same contribution of the dinner to the martyrs. But since the
provision of our speech is poor, it is well that we entertain with the
remnants from those themselves, who yesterday were hosts, and today guests.
For even a small part from a rich table suffices to become a preparation of a
great feast, when the remnant is of such a sort. What then is this remnant?
You remember altogether at what point of the speech we were, when that
wished-for and sweet noise for us from the multitude of those gathered
confused the hearing of the things being said,


when that living sea of the church, flooding with the multitude of those
flowing in, was swelling toward the impulse of those always pressing,
imitating even in the sound the true sea, as if dashing against our very ears
the noise of the waves. In what things then we left the speech storm-tossed by
the noise, you remember altogether, you to whom it has been a care to have the
martyrs through memory. And the sequence of the speech was, as I think, this:
that those chosen for this contest were not some chance people, nor some mixed
and nameless crowd, having set out from low occupations, raised to this
dignity; but first through a good natural constitution of body, having
surpassed the rest in beauty and power and abundance of strength, they were
numbered in the military rolls; and after this, having been conspicuous by a
life according to virtue and a temperate citizenship, as some prize and reward
they received, having been perfected, the grace of martyrdom. And if it seems
good, that we may become more pleasant, let us take up all the things of the
martyrs in order, as if bringing the contest before the eyes of the present
theater. There was a certain ancient military company in the neighboring city,
sitting in front of the whole nation against the onsets of the barbarians; for
those, from a certain manifestation from God that was already there, the faith
was more eagerly pursued than the tactics. And perhaps it is not untimely to
recount in passing one achievement of the faith of those men. For when the war
against the barbarians had once arisen for them, and all the advantageous
places had been pre-occupied by the army of the adversaries, and the waters
had come into the power of the enemies, having come into extreme danger,
whether through inexperience of those commanding our side, or through some
better and more divine economy, that especially even through this the
difference of the Christians toward the foreigners might appear; since they
did not know what to do with the present things, and there was much
perplexity, no spring or outflow of waters appearing in the place, and there
was danger of yielding to the =46.760= adversaries, being besieged out by
thirst; then the noble ones, having left the help from weapons, determined to
call upon the unconquered and unconquerable alliance in the fearful things.
For having left in the camp those who had not yet received the faith, and
having withdrawn by themselves, they imitate the wonder-working that came to
pass in the time of Elijah the prophet, asking with a common and marshaled
voice that a release from the disaster come to them from things without
resource. And they were praying; and the prayer immediately became a work. For
while they were still persevering in the kneeling, a cloud, being taken from
somewhere by a violent wind, stood aloft above the camp of the enemies; then
having thundered extraordinary thunders, and having kindled flaming lightnings
against those lying below, it burst down water more abundant than rivers; so
that for the adversaries both the continuity of the thunderbolts and the
multitude of the rainstorm became a cause of complete destruction; but for
these who had arrayed themselves through the prayers, it became sufficient for
both, both for the victory over those arrayed against them, and for the
consolation of the thirst, the outflow of the torrents abundantly furnishing
them the drink. With these then our choir of the army was marshaled,
themselves also having been confirmed concerning the faith from those
narratives, and having been nourished in like practices, they were raised to
such greatness of nature, that by the excess of virtue they raised up envy
against themselves. For just as we learned in the things now read to us from
the history of Job, that the adversary of human life made Job's good repute an
injustice against himself, and for this reason demanded him for outrage,
because Job pained him, being true and just and blameless; in the same way the
one who grows upon the good things looked with an evil eye upon these great
contestants, and he did not bear seeing a grayness of character in the youth
of age. He saw the flower of bodies, being adorned in temperance; he saw


a certain armed choral station, dancing through the army to God, fair to look
upon, grim in the eye, exultant in thought, speed of feet, abundance of power,
symmetry of limbs, in all the advantages belonging to them the virtue of the
soul outshining the bodily good fortune. He walks about even through those
with envy, the one who walks about the inhabited world. He saw not one true
man, but a divine company of so many men, all true, just, god-fearing; he
demands those also toward his own will. And first he becomes a counselor to
the idol-mad ruler of the army, that he would not otherwise prevail in the
victory against the barbarians, unless he should first sacrifice those who
worship the name of Christ. And these having been shown as quickly as possible
through the good confession, and deserting toward the perfection through
suffering, the Enemy declined (as something humane) to bring on at once the
death through the sword, but having pressed them with bonds of iron, =46.761=
he made this the beginning of the punishment. But for them even the bond was
an adornment, and a polished and sweet spectacle to Christian eyes, a chosen
youth in so great a number, conspicuous in beauty, in stature beyond the
others, all at the same time being fitted together with one another by the
bond, as some crown or necklace having equal-sized pearls taken through in a
circle; such were the saints, both united through faith, and being tightened
toward one another through the bonds. For being even each by himself
beautiful, they became an addition of beauty to one another. As it happens in
the heavenly wonders, when through a clear and pure sky the grace of the stars
is varied through one another, each contributing its own radiance into the
common adornment of heaven. Such was the spectacle of the saints, truly, as
Ezekiel the prophet says somewhere, a vision of lamps being turned together.
The speech loves to linger in the beauty of the season. For it knows, as
Wisdom says, from the greatness and beauty of created things to reckon also
the beauty in secret, since even the purity of the soul shone through the
appearing, and the appearing man was a worthy dwelling of the unseen. How fair
then was the spectacle then to those who saw! fair I say to those who wish to
see the fair things, fair to angels, fair to the supermundane powers; but
bitter to the demons, and to as many as embraced the things of the demons, men
(if indeed men, those who have stood up so far in the majesty of nature)
soldiers of Christ, hoplites of the Holy Spirit, champions of the faith,
towers of the divine city; who mocked every punishment of outrages, every
rising of fears, every assault of threat, as some folly of children; as if
they had given over not the bodies to the outrages, but the shadows of the
bodies; who having wrestled down the flesh with flesh, and by contempt toward
death having thought little of all the tyrannical fears, were shown higher
than human measures. O you who well practiced upon the bodily trophies! O you
who well transferred the experience of warring into the array against the
devil; not arming the hands with a sword, nor putting forward the shield from
wood, nor fencing themselves with a bronze helmet and greaves, but having
taken up the panoply of God, which the general of the Church, the divine
Apostle, describes, shield, and breastplate, and helmet, and sword; thus they
went against the opposing power. And the heavenly grace was general of these;
and of the devil's array, the one who has the power of death. And the place of
the array for them was the court of the murderers; having stood in that they
contended, the ones skirmishing with threats, the others defending themselves
through endurance. The proposition from the adversaries was to forswear the
faith in the Lord, or to be punished with death.

Then a man did not bear one assault of the Evil one, brought to him through

separated from Christ; one good, to be with Christ alone; and all the other
things, laughter and shadow, and nonsense and dreamlike phantasms. For these
reasons the speech dares the unseen things, and says that all the supermundane
power rejoices at the achievement of the athletes. Still the speech is bold
against the undared things; still it dares to recount the supermundane, that
when these things were being achieved here in the engagement against the
adversaries the just president of the contest held forth the crowns upon the
victory, and the commander of the divine power prepared the prizes for the
victors, and the Holy Spirit entertained them with gifts of every kind. For
since the faith was confessed into the Trinity, for this reason the grace also
was measured back to them from the Trinity. And what was the grace? This very
thing, to be shown higher than the first contestants, I mean Adam and Eve.
Those brought down the human nature that was standing, through the sin; these
through endurance set it up again, lying in the fall of those who went before.
Those were driven from paradise upon the earth; these were housed from here
upon paradise; those armed death against themselves (for the weapon of death
is sin, he says); these through their own courage made useless death armed
with sin, by endurance of the =46.765= sufferings blunting the edge of the
sting; so as to say well, Where, death, is your sting? Where, Hades, is your
victory? What is more wretched than the fruit of a tree? What more cheap than
a tree? A fruit colored with some fairness and pleasure of taste made the
grace of paradise to be dishonored. But to these great contestants not even
the sun itself appeared sweet; but they were willingly alienated even from
this, that they might not fall away from the true light. What does the speech
say concerning Eve (for I am led on even beyond what is due to run down the
first-born)? She saw, it says, that it was pleasing to the eyes and fair for
tasting. Then they exchanged the grace in these things instead of paradise.
But for these the things seen were a pleasure according to taste: heaven, sun,
earth, men; fatherland, mothers, brothers, friends, kinsmen, companions of
age; of which what is sweeter to be seen? and what more precious to come into
taste? You know, children, the affection toward the parents. You know, the


fathers, the disposition toward the children. You know, you who look at the
sweetness in the sun; you are not ignorant, you who love brothers, of the
natural relation toward brotherhood; you understand, young man, the grace from
companions of age, how much they sweeten life for you. But for those all
things were hostile, all things alien. One good only, Christ. They denied all
things, that they might gain this one. The time of the bonds was not little,
and for the saints with the stretching out of the punishment the desire toward
the perfection came to its prime together. And as those who practice bodily
good condition, whenever they have acquired sufficient power in the trainer's,
thus go confidently to the contests; in the same way these also, having been
sufficiently trained toward piety through the bonds and the prison, were thus
led forth upon the crown of the contests. The sequence of the speech has come
upon the end, or rather upon the peak of the whole achievement. This was the
time; these the days of the contest; this the prelude of the Pascha, the
mystery of the holy forty. Forty are the days of propitiation for us, and
equal in number also the crowns of the saints. Do I perhaps seem to you to be
superfluous and a chatterer, recounting your wonders among you, and
entertaining the hearing with your own things? Yet that the speech may not run
past you incomplete, we too will run together with the saints toward the end
of the contests. There was frost on that day. And there is altogether no need
for you to learn of what sort the frost was, guessing from the present day, a
frost even slipping through the hairs themselves. You know the excess, both
the newcomers of the places and the natives, and you have no need to learn by
speech; but even another would tell the wonder-working of your winters; how
rivers flowing everlastingly stand still, the freeze opposing the stream and
turning the waves to stone; and the neighboring lake needs certain signs in
order to be recognized as being a lake, having been made =46.768= dry land
through the freeze, upon which it is customary for those who wish to ride
above its waves. I know that often even water from fire is devised by the
locals, whenever having broken off some chunk of the water they melt it with
fire as some bronze or iron, and make the stone water. Such was the season
according to the time of the contest, the evil having taken an intensification
contrary to nature from northern winds, as it is possible to hear from those
who recount the wonders. Since then, having proclaimed the name of the Lord
publicly and splendidly, and already having shown themselves as crowned
through such a proclamation, they were going upon the perfection through
death, this manner of the contest was devised for them: A command comes from
the tyrant, to punish the athletes with frost. O the weakness both of the
words and of the thoughts! How far the speech falls short of the worth! A
command of death, and frost, and punishment, and expectation of such a
chastisement; and the blessed youth in laughter and play and brightness took
the place of the punishment; it was a race of athletes upon the passion, a
race both holy and intense; and a rivalry to snatch first the crown of the
confession; equal was their zeal toward the victory; no one was seen later in
eagerness; but all with one mind having taken this place, as if then given
over to public baths, as themselves also about to brighten the bodies with the
bath, readily put off the wrapping of the tunics, all pronouncing the word of

one who brought us in; we brought nothing into the world, nor do we owe to
carry anything out; or rather having entered naked, we shall go away full of
the treasures from the good confession. Saying these things, and encouraging
themselves with such words, they gave the body to the freeze; and the nature
of the elements was overpowered by the freeze; but the nature of the martyrs,
how unenslaved it was; or rather the nature suffered its own, and the


painful things it received; but the greatness of nature of the athletes
contended even against nature itself. For the power was being dissolved little
by little, withering and being spent through the freeze; but the tension of
the soul became greater; the season of the body was being blackened, and the
beauty was going out of bloom, and the fair color of the flesh was withering

frost, and all limbs and sense-organs were being crushed by the bitterness of
the cold. For the flesh, becoming livid little by little, and swelling and

the sufferings of deadness in sensation. And thus death, going forward little
by little, was lengthened for them, being stretched out through three days.
For until so long as the =46.769= sensation lasted for them, they remained
upon the order in which they were stationed from the beginning, becoming
through all things victors against the adversary. But who would recount for me
the things upon these according to worth? What speech will sketch that divine
procession, when the holy bodies were processed through wagons upon the fire?
How instead of the one stolen by the devil the jailer was brought in against
him to the number by grace? Who will recount for me that mother, the root
worthy of the martyr, who, her own having been left behind by the executioner,
because he was still breathing, and for this reason not taken up with the
others upon the wagon, when she saw the humanity of the executioner upon the
champion, did not bear the insult; but she reviled that one, because he had
separated the athlete from the fellow-contestants; and she, standing beside
the martyr, already numbed and holding motionless from the freeze, and seeing
him constrained by a cold and weak gasping, living only so much as to perceive
the painful things, looking up at his mother with a dim and withered glance,
both nodding and comforting with a dead and strengthless hand, charging her to
bear it nobly? When the mother saw these things, did she suffer something
maternal? Were the inward parts moved together, or did she tear the tunic
around, or pour herself about the child, warming the withering with warm arms?
Far be it. Even to say something of such a sort is among the unfitting things.
Truly from the fruit we know the tree; a rotten tree cannot make fair fruits.
Since then the fruit of the martyrdom is fair, praise the mother who bore a
champion, the one being saved through the childbearing, as the Apostle says.
For since she presented such a fruit to God, she made a defense for the common
nature of women. You are not my child, she says; not a shoot of my labor;
having received God, you were begotten according to God. You received
authority to become a child of God. Run toward your Father, do not be left
behind by those of the same age, do not come second upon the crown, do not
make a mother's prayer incomplete; you will not pain a mother, having become a
crowned one and a victor and a trophy-bearer. Saying these things, and having
tensed herself beyond nature, or rather having been tensed by the Spirit, she
herself laid her own with the rest upon the wagon, going before in procession
of the athlete with a bright face. And what things upon these? The saints
contended with the air, they sanctified also the fire by their own addition,
having become matter for the flame. They brought the blessing also upon the
water. Through all things the divine words were fulfilled. The three children
take along cold and heat into the common hymnology; the cold, through the
freeze; the heat, through the burning; then they went through fire and water.

This then was what was sought: Whether even for the saints paradise is

the glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
