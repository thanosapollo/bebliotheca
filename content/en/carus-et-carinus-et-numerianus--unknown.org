* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The death of Probus has shown well enough that fate rules the state,
and that it is now raised to the highest point, now drawn back to the
lowest.

For when, through the years, it had been lifted by various movements
or struck down, varied now by some storm and now by good fortune, and
had suffered almost everything that mortality suffers in a single man,
it seemed that after so many kinds of evil it would now remain secure
in unbroken prosperity, after the vigorous prince Aurelian, with
Probus, by the will of senate and people, steering the laws and the
helm. But a huge collapse, after the manner of a shipwreck or a fire,
when the soldiers were fatally inflamed and so great a prince was
taken from their midst, brought the public hope back to such despair
that all feared Domitians, Vitelliuses, and Neros. For there is more
fear than hope from a prince's unknown ways, especially in that state
which, pierced with fresh wounds, had suffered and mourned Valerian's
captivity, Gallienus' luxury, and the empires of nearly thirty
pretenders claiming for themselves the slaughtered limbs of citizens.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

For if we wish to go back from the city's founding over the changes
the Roman state has suffered, we shall find none that has more
flourished in good or labored under evils.

And, to begin from Romulus, the true father and parent of the state,
what was that man's good fortune, who founded, established, and
strengthened the commonwealth and, alone of all founders, left a
finished city! What then shall I say of Numa, who fortified with
religion a city roaring with wars and heavy with triumphs? Our state
therefore flourished until the times of Tarquin the Proud, but having
suffered a storm from kingly ways it avenged itself not without heavy
ruin. It then grew until the time of the Gallic war, but, as if sunk
in a kind of shipwreck, with the city captured except the citadel, it
felt almost more evil than it was swollen with blessings.

It then restored itself to soundness, but was so weighed down by the
Punic wars and the terror of Pyrrhus that it felt the ills of
mortality from fear in its breast.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

It grew then, once Carthage was defeated, with commands sent across
the seas, but, afflicted by the discords of its fellows, its sense of
prosperity worn thin, it grew old, worn by civil wars, until Augustus.
Then Augustus restored it, if it can be called restored when liberty
was laid aside, yet in some fashion, even if sad at home, it
flourished among foreign nations. Having then suffered so many Neros,
it raised its head through Vespasian, and, not gladdened by all of
Titus' good fortune, wounded by Domitian's savagery, better than usual
through Nerva and Trajan until Marcus, it was torn by Commodus'
madness and cruelty. After these things it felt nothing good except
Severus' diligence until Alexander, Mamaea's son. It would be long to
string together all that follows; for it could not use Valerian as
prince and suffered Gallienus for fifteen years. Fortune, loving
variety and almost always hostile to justice, begrudged Claudius a
long reign. For so was Aurelian killed, so Tacitus consumed, so Probus
slain, that it may appear nothing is so pleasing to fortune as that
what stands in public acts be changed by a variety of events. But why
are we detained by such complaints and the chances of the times? Let
us come to Carus, a middling man, so to speak, to be placed among the
good princes rather than the bad, and far better if he had not left
Carinus as heir.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Carus' native city is so ambiguously reported by most that, given the
great variety, I cannot say which is the true one.

For Onesimus, who wrote Probus' life most carefully, contends that he
was born and educated at Rome but of Illyrian parents, but Fabius
Ceryllianus, who has most skillfully followed the times of Carus,
Carinus, and Numerian, asserts that he was born not at Rome but in
Illyricum, and of Punic, not Pannonian, parents. I remember reading in
some journal that Carus was a Milanese, but enrolled on the roll of
the council of the city of Aquileia. He himself, as cannot be denied,
as his letter shows, which as proconsul he wrote to his legate when he
was urging him to good duties, wishes to appear a Roman.

Letter of Carus:
/Marcus Aurelius Carus, proconsul of Cilicia, to Junius his legate.
Our ancestors, those Roman princes, used this custom in creating
legates, that they should show a sample of their own character through
those to whom they entrusted the state. I, indeed, if it were not so,
would not have done otherwise; nor have I done otherwise, if with your
help I am not deceived. See to it, then, that we do not differ from
our ancestors, that is, from Roman men./

You see from the whole letter that he wishes his ancestors to be
understood as Romans.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

His speech given to the senate also shows that claim of birth. For
when first he had been made emperor, he wrote thus to the senatorial
order. Among the rest:
/There is reason, then, to rejoice, conscript fathers, that one of
your order, of your stock even, has been made emperor; wherefore we
shall strive that foreigners may not seem better than your own./

In this place too it is clear enough that he wished to be understood
as a Roman, that is, sprung from Rome.

This man then, through civil and military grades, as the inscriptions
of his statues show, was made praetorian prefect by Probus and won
such love among the soldiers that, when so great a prince as Probus
was killed, he alone seemed most worthy of the empire.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

And, that I may not string together every least thing and those which
can be found in others: as soon as he received the empire, by the
consent of all the soldiers he undertook the Persian war which Probus
was preparing, having named his sons Caesars, and in such a way that
he assigned Carinus, with the choicest men, to defend the Gallic
provinces, but took with him Numerian, a youth both most choice and
also most eloquent, and he is said often to have said that he was
wretched, because he was sending Carinus as prince to the Gallic
provinces, and Numerian's age was not such that the Gallic command,
which most of all wants a steadfast prince, could be entrusted to
him. But these things elsewhere; for there exist also letters of
Carus in which he complains to his prefect of Carinus' character, so
that it may appear true what Onesimus says, that Carus had it in mind
to take the rank of Caesar from Carinus.

But these things, as we have said, are to be told elsewhere in
Carinus' own life. Now we shall return to the order.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

With huge preparation and all Probus' forces, after he had in great
part finished the Sarmatian war he was waging, Carus set out against
the Persians, and with no one meeting him he took Mesopotamia and came
as far as Ctesiphon, and, while the Persians were occupied with
domestic sedition, he earned the title Persicus. But when, greedy of
glory and urged on most of all by his prefect, who was seeking
destruction for both him and his sons because he desired to rule, he
had advanced too far, he was killed, as some say by disease, as more
say by lightning. It cannot be denied that at the time he died there
was such sudden thunder that many are said to have been killed by the
terror itself. When therefore he was ill and lying in his tent, a huge
storm arose, and he was killed by a monstrous flash, and by still more
monstrous thunder, as we have said. Julius Calpurnius, who dictated
for the records, gave such a letter to the prefect of the city about
Carus' death:

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

I have inserted this letter for this reason, that most say there is a
certain force of fate, that a Roman prince cannot pass Ctesiphon, and
that therefore Carus was consumed by lightning because he wished to
cross those bounds which have been fixed by fate. But let timidity
keep its own arts, to be trampled by valor; it is plainly permitted,
and will be permitted, as has been shown through the most sacred
Caesar Maximian, to defeat the Persians and advance beyond them, and I
think it will happen, if the promised favor of the gods is not
abandoned by our people.

Many things show that Carus was a good prince, and this also, that as
soon as he obtained the empire he crushed the Sarmatians so skillfully
by dividing the wars (they were so fierce at Probus' death that they
threatened to invade not only Illyricum but Thrace too and Italy) that
in very few days he gave the Pannonias security, with sixteen thousand
Sarmatians killed and twenty thousand of both sexes captured.

:PROPERTIES:

I believe this is enough about Carus; let us come to Numerian. His
history seems both more closely bound to his father and more
remarkable because of his father-in-law, and although Carinus was
older, and was also named Caesar before this man, yet it is necessary
that we speak first of Numerian, who followed his father's death,
afterwards of Carinus, whom Diocletian Augustus, a man the state
needed, killed after battles were fought.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

He was his father's companion in the Persian war. When Carus was
dead, after Numerian's eyes had begun to hurt, a kind of illness most
familiar to a man worn out by too much watching, as was natural, and
he was being carried in a litter, he was killed by the faction of
Aper his father-in-law, who was trying to seize the empire. But when
for very many days the soldiers were asking after the emperor's
health, and Aper was addressing them that he could not be seen for
this reason, that he was keeping his weak eyes from wind and sun, yet
the matter was betrayed by the stench of the corpse, all attacked
Aper, whose faction could not stay hidden, and dragged him before the
standards and headquarters. Then a huge assembly was held,

a tribunal was also made. And when it was asked who should be the
most just avenger of Numerian, who should be given to the state as a
good prince, all by divine consent named as Augustus Diocletian, to
whom many signs of empire were already said to have been made, then
commanding the household troops, a distinguished man, shrewd, loving
the state, loving his own, and tempered to all that the time had
required, always of deep counsel, yet sometimes shameless, but by
prudence and excessive stubbornness checking the movements of a
restless breast. When he had mounted the tribunal and been named
Augustus, and it was asked how Numerian had been killed, drawing his
sword and pointing to Aper the praetorian prefect he struck him,
adding in his words,
/This is the author of Numerian's murder./
So Aper, living a foul life and with ugly plans, met an end worthy of
his character. My grandfather reported that he was present at the
assembly when Aper was killed by Diocletian's hand; and he used to
say that Diocletian, when he had struck Aper, said:
/Boast, Aper, you fall by the right hand of great Aeneas.

Which I wonder at in a military man, although I plainly know that
very many soldiers take up sayings, whether in Greek or Latin, of the
comic writers or of such poets. The comic writers themselves,
finally, often bring on soldiers in such a way as to make them take
up old sayings. For even
/You are a hare yourself, and you look for game?/
is a saying of Livius Andronicus, and Plautus and Caecilius put many

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:

Diocletian always had in mind a desire for empire, and that with
Maximian knowing it, and my grandfather, to whom he himself had
reported this saying of the Druidess. Finally, as he was deep, he
laughed and was silent, yet in hunts, where there was opportunity, he
always killed boars with his own hand. Finally when Aurelian had
received the empire, when Probus, when Tacitus, when Carus himself,
Diocletian said,
/I always kill the boars, but another uses the meat./
That too is now known and common, that when he had killed Aper the
praetorian prefect, he is reported to have said,
/At last I have killed the fated Boar./
The same grandfather of mine used to say that Diocletian himself had
said he had no other reason for killing with his own hand except to
fulfill the Druidess' saying and to confirm his own empire, for he
would not have wished to become known as so cruel, especially in the
first days of empire, unless necessity had drawn him to this atrocity
of killing.

there remains for us Carinus, the most defiled of all men, an
adulterer, a frequent corrupter of youth (it shames me to say what
Onesimus put in writing), he too making ill use of the nature of his
own sex. This man, left by his father as Caesar, with the Gallic
provinces and Italy, Illyricum, the Spains and Britains and Africa
assigned to him, held the rank of Caesar, but on this condition that
he should do all that Augusti do. He stained himself with enormous
vices and huge foulness, banished all the best friends, chose or kept
every worst man, and made prefect of the city one of his clerks, and
nothing fouler could ever be thought or said. He killed the
praetorian prefect he had; in his place he made
Matronianus, an old go-between, one of his secretaries, whom he had
always had as accomplice and helper in debaucheries and lusts.
Against his father's will he came forward as consul, gave arrogant
letters to the senate. To the common people of the city of Rome, as
if to the Roman people, he promised the goods of the senate.

By marrying and putting away wives he took nine, most of them driven
out when pregnant. He filled the Palace with mimes, prostitutes,
pantomimes, singers, and pimps.

He had such a disgust for signing that he set to the signing some
impure man with whom he always joked at midday, whom he often rebuked

because he imitated his hand well. He had gems in his shoes, he used
no buckle unless jeweled, often even a belt doubled with gems.

Most, finally, used to call him the king of Illyricum. He never went
to meet prefects, never consuls. He granted most to wicked men and
always invited them to dinner.

He often served at his banquet a hundred pounds of birds, a hundred
of fish, a thousand of various meat, poured out a great deal of wine,
swam among apples and melons. He strewed both dining rooms and
bedchambers with Milanese roses, used baths so cold that they are
like the cells set underneath, the cold rooms always snow-cooled.
When in winter time he had come to a certain place in which there was
a lukewarm spring, as is usual by nature in winter, and had used it
in a pool, he is reported to have said to the bath-keepers,
/You have prepared me women's water./

And this is put forward as his most famous saying. His father used to
hear what he was doing, and cry,
/He is not mine./

He had finally resolved to put in his place Constantius, who
afterwards was made Caesar, but was then administering the
governorship of Dalmatia, because no man then seemed better, and
indeed, as Onesimus says, to kill him. It would be long if I should
wish to say more of his luxury; whoever wants to know it door to door,
let him also read Fulvius Asprianus, who tells all his deeds to
the point of weariness.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

When this man learned that his father had been consumed by lightning,
his brother killed by his father-in-law, Diocletian named Augustus,
he put forth greater vices and crimes, as if now free and, by the
deaths of his own, released from the reins of household piety, yet he
did not lack vigor of mind for claiming the empire for himself, for
he fought against Diocletian in many battles, but in the last fight
joined at Margus he was defeated and fell.

This was the end of three princes, Carus, Numerian, and Carinus.
After whom the gods gave Diocletian and Maximian as princes, joining
to such men Galerius and Constantius, of whom the one was born to
wipe out the disgrace received in Valerian's captivity, the other to
restore the Gallic provinces to Roman laws, four princes of the
world, surely, strong, wise, kind, and very generous, of one mind
toward the state, most respectful of the Roman senate, moderate,
friends of the people, most holy, grave, religious, and such princes
as we have always prayed for. Claudius Eusthenius, who was secretary
of letters to Diocletian, wrote their lives in separate books, which
I have said for this reason, that no one may require so great a
matter from me, especially since even the life of living princes is
not told without blame.

* Chapter 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

The empire of Carus and Carinus and Numerian had this as most
memorable, that they gave the Roman people games adorned with new
spectacles, which we have seen painted in the Palace around the
portico of the stables. For he exhibited a rope-walker who was
carried as if on the winds in buskins, and a wall-walker who ran
along the wall after eluding a bear, and bears acting a mime, and
likewise a hundred trumpeters sounding together at one blast, and a
hundred horn-players, a hundred flute-players, even a hundred pipers,
a thousand pantomimes and athletes, besides a scaffold whose flames
burned the stage, which Diocletian afterwards made more magnificent;
besides he summoned mimes from everywhere. He also exhibited a
Sarmatian game, and nothing is sweeter than that. He exhibited Cyclops
shows. Gold and silver were given to Greek artists and athletes and
actors and musicians, silk clothing was given too.

But all these things, I know not how much favor they have with the
people, are of no moment with good princes. Finally a saying of
Diocletian is reported, when a certain treasurer of his was praising

/Carus was well laughed at in his empire./

he used generosity most sparingly, saying the games ought to be more
chaste when a censor was watching.
