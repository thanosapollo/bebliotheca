* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Alcibiades, son of Clinias, an Athenian. In this man nature seems to have
tried what she can do. For all who have left an account of him agree that no
one was more remarkable than he, whether in vices or in virtues.

Born in a most distinguished city, of the highest lineage, by far the
handsomest of all his generation, and rich; fit for every task and full of
counsel (for he was a supreme commander both by sea and by land); eloquent, so
that he stood among the first in speaking, because the charm of his look and
of his speech was so great that no one could withstand him; when the time
demanded, hard-working and enduring; generous, splendid no less in his way of
life than at table; approachable, winning, serving the times with the utmost
cunning: yet the same man, as soon as he had relaxed and there was no cause
why he should bear the labor of the mind, was found luxurious, dissolute,
lustful, and intemperate, so that all marveled that in one man there was so
great an unlikeness and so diverse a nature.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
He was brought up in the house of Pericles (for he is said to have been his
stepson) and educated by Socrates. His father-in-law was Hipponicus, the
richest of all who spoke the Greek tongue, so that, if he himself had wished
to invent them, he could neither imagine more advantages nor obtain greater
ones than nature or fortune had bestowed. At the beginning of his youth he was
loved by many in the Greek fashion, among them by Socrates, of whom Plato
makes mention in the Symposium. For he has represented him recalling that he
spent the night with Socrates and rose from him in no other way than a son
ought to rise from a father. After he had grown more robust, he loved no fewer
men, and in that love, so far as it is permitted to speak of offensive things,
he did many things daintily and in jest: which we would relate, if we did not
have greater matters of more weight.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
In the Peloponnesian War, by his counsel and authority, the Athenians declared
war on the Syracusans. To conduct it he himself was chosen commander, and two
colleagues besides were given him, Nicias and Lamachus. While this was being
prepared, before the fleet sailed, it happened that in one night all the
Hermae that were in the town of Athens were thrown down except one, which
stood before the door of Andocides (and so that one was afterwards commonly
called the Mercury of Andocides). Since it was clear that this had not been
done without the great consent of many, and that it concerned not a private
but a public matter, great fear was struck into the multitude that some sudden
force might arise in the state which would crush the liberty of the people.
This seemed to fit Alcibiades most of all, because he was thought both more
powerful and greater than a private citizen: for he had bound many to himself
by generosity, and still more he had made his own by service in the courts.
Hence it came about that whenever he went out in public he turned all eyes
upon himself, and no one in the city was set as his equal. And so they had in
him not only the greatest hope but also fear, because he could both harm and
help to the utmost. He was also touched with infamy, because he was said to
celebrate the mysteries in his own house (which was an impiety by Athenian
custom), and this was thought to belong not to religion but to conspiracy.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
On this charge he was challenged by his enemies in the assembly. But the time
for setting out to war was pressing. Seeing this, and not ignorant of the
habit of his fellow-citizens, he demanded that if they wished anything to be
done concerning him, an inquiry should rather be held while he was present
than that he be accused in his absence on a charge born of envy. His enemies,
however, resolved that they must keep quiet for the present, because they
understood that he could not be harmed, and that they must wait for the time
when the fleet had sailed, so that they might attack him in his absence; and
so they did. For after they believed he had reached Sicily, they made him a
defendant in his absence for having violated the sacred rites. When a
messenger had been sent to him in Sicily by the magistrate that he should
return home to plead his cause, and he was in great hope of administering the
province well, he did not refuse to obey and boarded the trireme that had been
sent to fetch him. Carried by this to Thurii in Italy, turning over in his
mind many things about the unbridled license of his fellow-citizens and their
cruelty toward nobles, he judged it most useful to avoid the storm that hung
over him, slipped away secretly from his guards, and came first to Elis, then
to Thebes. But after he heard that he had been condemned to death and his
goods confiscated, and, what had never happened before, that the Eumolpid
priests had been forced by the people to curse him, and that, so the memory of
that curse might be more fully attested, a copy cut on a stone pillar had been
set up in public, he withdrew to Lacedaemon. There, as he himself was
accustomed to declare, he waged war not against his country but against his
enemies, who were the same as enemies to the state: for although they
understood that he could be of the greatest use to the republic, they had cast
him out of it and had obeyed their own anger more than the common advantage.
And so by his counsel the Lacedaemonians made friendship with the Persian
king, then fortified Decelea in Attica and, placing a standing garrison there,
kept Athens under siege. By his doing they also turned Ionia away from the
alliance of the Athenians. After this they began to be much superior in the
war.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
Yet by these things they were not so much made friends to Alcibiades as
alienated from him by fear. For when they recognized the outstanding prudence
of a most keen man in all matters, they feared that, led by love of his
country, he might at some time desert them and return to favor with his own
people. And so they began to seek a time for killing him. This could not be
hidden from Alcibiades any longer: for he was of such sagacity that he could
not be deceived, especially when he had set his mind to being on guard. And so
he betook himself to Tissaphernes, prefect of King Darius. When he had come
into closest friendship with him and saw that, after the Athenians' affairs
had gone badly in Sicily, their resources were waning and those of the
Lacedaemonians growing, he at first conferred through intermediaries with the
general Pisander, who had an army at Samos, and made mention of his own
return. For that man was of the same mind as Alcibiades, no friend to the
power of the people and a supporter of the oligarchs. Abandoned by him, he is
first received by the army through Thrasybulus, son of Lycus, and is made
general at Samos; afterwards, with Theramenes supporting, he is restored by a
decree of the people and, though absent, is set in equal command together with
Thrasybulus and Theramenes. Under their command so great a change of affairs
was made that the Lacedaemonians, who a little before had flourished as
victors, were terrified and sought peace. For they had been beaten in five
battles on land and three at sea, in which they had lost two hundred triremes,
which, having been captured, had come into the power of the enemy. Alcibiades
together with his colleagues had recovered Ionia, the Hellespont, and many
Greek cities besides that lie on the coast of Thrace, of which they had
stormed several, among them Byzantium, and they had attached no fewer by
counsel to friendship, because they had used clemency toward the captured.
Thus laden with booty, the army enriched, and the greatest deeds accomplished,
they came to Athens.

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
When the whole city had gone down to the Piraeus to meet them, so great was
the expectation of all for seeing Alcibiades that the crowd streamed to his
trireme, just as if he alone had arrived. For the people were persuaded that
both the earlier reverses and the present successes had befallen by his doing.
And so they attributed both the army lost in Sicily and the victories of the
Lacedaemonians to their own fault, because they had driven such a man from the
city. Nor did they seem to think this without cause. For after he had begun to
command the army, the enemy had been able to match them neither by land nor by
sea. When he had stepped from the ship, although Theramenes and Thrasybulus
had been in charge of the same affairs and had arrived together at the
Piraeus, yet all escorted that one man, and, what had never happened before
except to victors at Olympia, he was publicly presented with laurel crowns and
ribbons. He, weeping, received such goodwill of his fellow-citizens,
remembering the bitterness of former time. After he came into the city, a
meeting having been called, he spoke in such a way that no one was so harsh as
not to weep at his misfortune and show himself an enemy to those by whose
doing he had been driven from his country, just as if another people, not that
very people which was then weeping, had condemned him for sacrilege. His goods
were therefore restored to him at public cost, and those same Eumolpid priests
who had cursed him were forced to reverse the curse, and those pillars on
which the curse had been written were thrown into the sea.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:
This joy of Alcibiades was not overly lasting. For when all honors had been
decreed to him and the whole republic at home and in war had been handed over
to be managed at the judgment of one man, and he himself had asked that two
colleagues be given him, Thrasybulus and Adimantus, and this had not been
refused, having set out with the fleet to Asia, because he had conducted the
matter at Cyme less to his liking, he fell back into envy: for they thought
there was nothing he could not accomplish. From this it came about that they
attributed everything done less prosperously to his fault, saying that he had
acted either carelessly or maliciously, as then happened: for they charged
that, having been bribed by the king, he had been unwilling to take Cyme. And
so we think that an excessive opinion of his talent and virtue was especially
a harm to him: for he was feared no less than he was loved, lest, elated by
favorable fortune and great resources, he should desire a tyranny. By these
things it came about that they revoked his magistracy in his absence and put
another in his place. When he heard this, he was unwilling to return home and
betook himself to Pactye, and there he fortified three strongholds, Ornos,
Bizanthe, and Neontichos, and having gathered a force he was the first of a
Greek state to enter Thrace, thinking it more glorious to be enriched by the
booty of barbarians than of Greeks. From this he had grown both in fame and in
resources, and he had won himself a great friendship with certain kings of
Thrace.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:
Yet he could not withdraw from love of his country. For when at the river
Aegos Philocles, the Athenian general, had stationed his fleet and Lysander,
the Lacedaemonian general, was not far off, who was occupied in drawing out
the war as long as possible, because money was being supplied to them by the
king, whereas for the exhausted Athenians nothing was left besides arms and
ships, Alcibiades came to the Athenian army and there began to speak in the
presence of the crowd: if they wished, he pledged that he would force Lysander
either to fight or to seek peace; that the Lacedaemonians were unwilling to
contend with the fleet because they were stronger in land forces than in
ships; and that it was easy for him to induce Seuthes, king of the Thracians,
to drive him from the land: which done, he would of necessity either fight
with the fleet or settle the war. Although Philocles saw that this was truly
said, yet he was unwilling to do what was asked, because he felt that if
Alcibiades were received he would be of no account with the army, and if
anything favorable occurred he would have no share in it, whereas if anything
adverse happened he alone would be the defendant for that failure. Departing
from him Alcibiades said, "Since you resist the victory of your country, I
warn you of this, not to keep your naval camp next to the enemy: for there is
danger that the lack of discipline of your soldiers may give Lysander an
opportunity of crushing your army." Nor did that matter deceive him. For
Lysander, when he had learned through scouts that the Athenian crowd had gone
ashore to plunder and the ships had been left almost empty, did not let the
time for action slip, and by that onset he put an end to the whole war.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:
But Alcibiades, thinking those same places not safe enough for himself after
the Athenians had been defeated, hid himself deep in Thrace above the
Propontis, hoping that there his fortune could most easily be concealed. He
was mistaken. For the Thracians, after they perceived that he had come with a
great sum of money, laid an ambush and took away what he had brought, though
they could not capture him himself. Seeing that no place was safe for him in
Greece because of the power of the Lacedaemonians, he crossed into Asia to
Pharnabazus, whom indeed he so won by his courtesy that no one stood before
him in friendship. For he had given him Grynium, a stronghold in Phrygia, from
which he took fifty talents of revenue. Alcibiades was not content with this
fortune, nor could he endure that defeated Athens serve the Lacedaemonians.
And so he was carried by every thought toward freeing his country. But he saw
that this could not be done without the Persian king, and therefore he desired
that he be joined to himself as a friend, and he did not doubt that he would
easily succeed if only he had the power of meeting him. For he knew that
Cyrus, the king's brother, was secretly preparing war against him with the
help of the Lacedaemonians: if he disclosed this, he saw that he would enter
into great favor

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:
While he was attempting this and asking of Pharnabazus that he be sent to the
king, at the same time Critias and the other tyrants of the Athenians had sent
certain men to Lysander in Asia to inform him that unless he removed
Alcibiades, none of those things which he himself had established at Athens
would stand: therefore, if he wished his own achievements to remain, he should
pursue that man. The Laconian, moved by these things, decided that he must
deal more carefully with Pharnabazus; he therefore reports to him what had
been agreed between the king and the Lacedaemonians, unless he delivered
Alcibiades to him alive or dead. The satrap did not bear this and preferred to
violate clemency rather than that the king's resources be diminished. And so
he sent Susamithres and Bagaeus to kill Alcibiades, when he was in Phrygia and
was preparing a journey to the king. Those sent secretly give the neighborhood
in which Alcibiades then was the task of killing him. Since they did not dare
to attack him with the sword, at night they piled wood around the cottage in
which he was sleeping and set it on fire, so as to finish by fire the man they
despaired of overcoming by hand. But he, when he was roused by the sound of
the flame, although his sword had been taken from him, snatched the under-arm
weapon of his companion. For there was with him a certain guest-friend from
Arcadia who had never been willing to leave. He orders this man to follow him
and seizes what clothing there was at hand. Having thrown these into the fire
he passed through the force of the flame. When the barbarians saw that he had
escaped the fire, they killed him with missiles thrown from a distance and
brought his head back to Pharnabazus. But a woman who had been accustomed to
live with him covered the dead man with her woman's garment and burned him in
the fire of the building, which had been prepared to kill him while alive.
Thus Alcibiades, about forty years old, met his last day.

* Caput 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:
This man, defamed by most, three most weighty historians have raised with the
highest praises: Thucydides, who was of the same age, Theopompus, born
somewhat later, and Timaeus: and these last two, most given to speaking ill,
somehow agree in praising that one man. For they have declared of him those
things which we have written above, and this besides: that when he had been
born at Athens, a most splendid city, he surpassed all in the splendor and
dignity of his life; after he had been driven from there and come to Thebes,
he so devoted himself to their pursuits that no one could equal him in labor
and strength of body (for all the Boeotians serve the firmness of the body
more than the sharpness of talent); the same man among the Lacedaemonians,
whose customs set the highest virtue in endurance, so gave himself to hardness
that he surpassed all the Lacedaemonians in the frugality of his food and
dress; that he was among the Thracians, men given to wine and to the affairs
of Venus: these too he outdid in those things; that he came to the Persians,
among whom the highest praise was to hunt bravely and live luxuriously: that
he so imitated their custom that they themselves especially admired him in
these things. By these things he brought it about that, among whatever people
he was, he was set first and held most dear. But enough of this: let us begin
the rest.
