* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Lest it prove tedious to Your Clemency, great Constantine, to read each
emperor, or each emperor's child, in a book of his own, I have used restraint,
packing the two Maximini, father and son, into one volume. From here on I have
kept the order that Your Piety wanted Tatius Cyrillus, a man of the rank of
the Illustrious, who turned Greek into Latin, to keep as well.

I shall keep this not in one book only but in most that follow, except for the
great emperors, whose deeds, more numerous and more famous, want a longer
text.

Maximinus the elder shone under the emperor Alexander.

He began his soldiering under Severus. He came from a village

in Thrace next to the barbarians, born of a barbarian father and mother as
well, one of whom is said to have been born in Gothia, the other among the
Alani.

His father, they say, was named Micca, his mother Ababa. But Maximinus himself
gave out these names in his early years; later, once he came to empire, he
ordered them hidden, so that he would not seem an emperor born of barbarian
parents on both sides.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

In his first boyhood he was a shepherd, and already a leader of young men, and

one who would lie in wait for robbers and defend his own from raids. His first
service was in the cavalry, for he was striking in the size of his body,
famous among all the soldiers for courage, handsome in a manly way, fierce in
his manners, harsh, proud, scornful, yet often just.

This was the first cause of his becoming known under the emperor Severus:

on the birthday of Geta, the younger son, Severus was giving military games,
with silver prizes set out, that is, arm-rings, collars, and little belts.
This youth, half barbarian and scarcely yet master of the Latin tongue,
speaking almost Thracian, publicly asked the emperor to give him leave to
compete with those who already served in no mean rank.

Severus, astonished at the size of his body, first matched him with sutlers,
but with the strongest of them, so as not to break military discipline.

Then Maximinus beat sixteen sutlers in one sweat, taking sixteen rather small
prizes that were not military ones

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

and was ordered to serve. On the third day, as it happened, when Severus had
gone out to the parade-ground, he saw Maximinus leaping about in the crowd in
the barbarian way and at once ordered the tribune to check him and school him
in Roman discipline.

Then he, when he understood that the emperor was speaking about him, the
barbarian guessing that he was known to the prince and conspicuous among many,
came up to the feet of the emperor as he rode.

Then Severus, wishing to try how good he was at running, gave his horse its
head in many circuits, and when the old emperor had grown tired and the other
had not stopped running over a long space, he said to him:

/What do you want, little Thracian? Does it please you at all to wrestle after
the run?/

then

/As much as you like,/

he said,

/Emperor./

After this Severus got down from his horse and ordered the freshest and
strongest soldiers to be matched with him. Then, in his usual way, he beat
seven of the strongest in one sweat, and he alone of all was given by Severus,
after the silver prizes, a gold collar, and was ordered to stand always in the
court among the bodyguard.

From this, then, he became conspicuous, famous among the soldiers, loved by
the tribunes, looked up to by his comrades, able to get from the emperor
whatever he wanted, and helped by Severus even in posts of service, though he
was still very young. In the length and bulk of his body, in his looks, and in
the size and brightness of his eyes he stood out among all.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

It is agreed that he often drank a Capitoline amphora of wine in a day, and
ate forty pounds of meat, or, as Cordus says, even sixty.

What is well enough agreed is that he always abstained from vegetables, and
almost always from cold things, except when he had to drink. He would often
catch his sweat and put it in cups or a little vessel, so as to show two or
three pints of his own sweat.

For a long time under Antoninus Caracalla he led the ranks of the centuries
and often held the other military dignities. Under Macrinus, because he
bitterly hated the man who had killed his emperor's son, he left the service,
bought land in Thrace in the village where he had been born, and always traded
with the Goths. He was uniquely loved by the Getae as if he were a citizen of
theirs.

Any Alani who came to the river-bank showed him as a friend by gifts going
back and forth.

But when Macrinus had been killed with his son, and Maximinus learned that
Elagabalus was emperor as if he were Antoninus' son, being now of ripe age he
came to him and asked that he hold the same judgment of him that his
grandfather Severus had held. But he could do nothing with that filthy man.

For Elagabalus is said to have joked with him most foully, saying:

/You are said, Maximinus, to have worn out sixteen and twenty and thirty
soldiers at times; can you manage thirty times with a woman?/

Then he, when he saw the infamous prince beginning like that, left the
service. Yet he was held back by Elagabalus' friends, lest this too be added
to that man's fame, that he had driven from his army the strongest man of his
time, whom some called Hercules, others Achilles,

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

others Ajax. Under that most filthy man, then, he held only the honor of a
tribunate, but he never came to take his hand, never greeted him, racing this
way and that through the whole three years;

now he was busy with his fields, now with rest, now with feigned illnesses.

When Elagabalus had been killed, as soon as he learned that Alexander had been
named prince, he made for Rome.

Alexander received him with wonderful joy and wonderful thanks, so that he
spoke words like these in the senate:

/Maximinus, conscript fathers, a tribune to whom I have added the broad
stripe, has taken refuge with me, a man who could not serve under that filthy
beast, and who under my deified kinsman Severus was as great as you know him
by report to have been./

At once, then, he gave him the tribunate of the Fourth Legion, which he
himself had formed from recruits, and advanced him with these words:

/I have not entrusted veterans to you, my dearest and most beloved Maximinus,
because I feared you could not correct their faults, grown in them under
others.
You have recruits; make them learn their service after your habits, your
courage, your labor, so that you alone may make me many Maximini, men the
state would wish for./

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Having taken the legion, then, he at once began to train it. Every fifth day
he ordered the soldiers to run in arms, to fight sham battles among
themselves, and to inspect their swords, corselets, helmets, shields, tunics,
and all their arms every day. He even looked after their boots himself, so
that he offered himself as a father to the soldiers. But when some tribunes
rebuked him, saying,

/Why do you labor so hard, now that you are already in a place where you can
receive a command?/

he is said to have answered,

/As for me, the greater I become, the more I shall labor./

He himself wrestled with the soldiers, throwing five, six, and seven at a time
to the ground, though already well on in years.

At last, when everyone was jealous, and a prouder tribune, a man of great body
and known courage and therefore fiercer, had said to him,

/You do nothing great if, being a tribune, you beat your own soldiers,/

he said,

/Do you want us to meet?/

And when the other had nodded and was coming against him, he struck him on the
chest with his palm, threw him on his back, and at once said,

/Give me another, but a tribune./

Besides, as Cordus reports, he was of such size that he was said to have stood
eight feet six inches, and his thumb was so huge that he used his wife's
bracelet for a ring.

The rest is almost in the mouth of the crowd: that he would drag wagons with
his hands, move a loaded carriage alone, loosen a horse's teeth if he struck
it with his fist, break its legs if he struck it with his heel, crumble tufa
stones, split younger trees, and that some called him Milo of Croton, others
Hercules, others Antaeus.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

When these things had made the man conspicuous, Alexander, a judge of great
merits, put him, to his own ruin, over the whole army, with all the tribunes,
generals, and soldiers everywhere rejoicing. Then he drew the whole of that
army, which under Elagabalus had in large part gone slack, back to his own
military discipline, which, as we have said, proved most serious for
Alexander, a very good emperor, to be sure, but one whose age from the start
could be scorned.

For when he was in Gaul and had pitched camp not far from a certain city,
soldiers were suddenly sent in, as some say by Maximinus himself, as others
say by barbarian tribunes, and Alexander, fleeing to his mother, was killed,
Maximinus having already been named emperor. And some say one cause of
Alexander's being killed, others another. Some say Mamaea was the author of
it, wanting her son to leave the German war and make for the East, and that
for that reason the soldiers broke into mutiny. Others, that he was too severe
and had wanted to discharge the legions in Gaul as he had discharged them in
the East.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

But when Alexander had been killed, Maximinus was the first man from the body
of the soldiers, and not yet a senator, to be named Augustus by the army
without a decree of the senate, giving his son a share in the rule. Of that
son we shall soon tell the few things we know. Maximinus, however, was always
of such cunning that he governed the soldiers not only by courage but also
made them most devoted by rewards and profits.

He never took anyone's rations. He never allowed any soldier in the army to be
a smith or a craftsman of another trade, as most are, keeping the legions busy
only with frequent hunts. Yet among these virtues he was so cruel that some
called him Cyclops, others Busiris, others Sciron, not a few Phalaris, many
Typhon or Gyges.

The senate feared him so much that women even with their children made vows in
the temples, publicly and privately, that he might never see the city of Rome.
For they heard that some had been hung on crosses, others shut in the bodies
of animals newly killed, others thrown to wild beasts, others crushed with
clubs, and all
this without regard to rank, since he seemed to want to govern military
discipline, by whose example he wanted to correct civil affairs as well, which
does not suit a prince who wants to be loved. For he was persuaded that empire
is not held except by cruelty, and at the same time he feared that because of
the lowness of his barbarian birth he would be scorned by the nobility.

He remembered besides that at Rome he had been scorned even by the slaves of
nobles, so that he was not even seen by their stewards; and, as foolish
opinions go, he expected them to be the same toward him once he was already
emperor. So strong is the consciousness

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

of a low-born mind. For to hide his ignoble birth he killed all who knew
his stock, some of them even friends who had often given him many things out
of pity for his poverty. Nor was there a crueller animal on earth, putting
everything in his own strength as if he could not be killed. At last, when he
almost believed himself immortal because of the size of his body and his
courage, a mime in the theatre, they say, spoke Greek verses in his presence,
whose sense in Latin was this:

/And he who cannot be killed by one is killed by many.

the elephant is huge and is killed,

the lion is strong and is killed,

the tiger is strong and is killed;

beware of many, if you do not fear them one by one./

And these things were already said with the emperor himself present. But when
he asked his friends what the hostile jester had said, he was told that the
man was singing old verses written against harsh men; and he, being a Thracian
and a barbarian, believed it. He allowed no nobleman near him, ruling exactly
on the model of Spartacus or Athenio.

Besides, he killed all of Alexander's ministers in various ways and envied his
arrangements, and while he held that man's friends and ministers under
suspicion he grew crueller.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Being of such a character that he lived in the manner of wild beasts, he grew
gloomier and more monstrous by a plot prepared against him by a certain
Magnus, a man of consular rank, who had entered a plan with many soldiers and
centurions to stab him, since he wanted to transfer the empire to himself. The
kind of plot was this: when Maximinus wanted to throw a bridge and cross
against the Germans, it had been agreed that the plotters should cross with him,
the bridge should then be broken, he should be surrounded and killed on the
barbarian side, and Magnus should seize the empire. For he had begun to wage
all wars, and very bravely too, as soon as he was made emperor, skilled as he
was in military affairs, wanting to keep the reputation already held of him
and, above all, to surpass the glory of Alexander, whom he himself had killed.
For that reason, even as emperor, he kept the soldiers at exercise every day
and was himself in arms, always showing the army many things with his hand and
body.

And this plot, they say, Maximinus himself invented, so as to increase the
material for cruelty.

At last, without a trial, without an accusation, without an informer, without
a defender, he killed them all, took all their goods, and after more than four
thousand men had been killed he could not sate himself.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

There was also under him a plot, when the Osrhoene archers broke away from him
out of love and longing for Alexander, whom it was agreed among them Maximinus
had killed, nor could they be persuaded otherwise. At last they themselves
made Titus, one of their own, their leader and emperor, a man whom Maximinus
had already sent away as a private person. They even wrapped him in purple,
dressed him with royal pomp, and fenced him about as if they were his
soldiers, and against his will at that. But this man, sleeping at his own
house, was killed by one of his friends, Macedonius by name, who was pained
that the other had been set over him, who betrayed him to Maximinus and
brought his head to the emperor. But Maximinus first thanked him, then later
hated him as a traitor and killed him.

By these things he grew more monstrous day by day, in the manner of wild
beasts, which when wounded are more inflamed.

After this he crossed into Germany with the whole army and with the Moors and
Osrhoenes and Parthians and all whom Alexander was leading with him to war,
and he dragged the eastern auxiliaries with him especially for this, that none
are stronger against Germans than light archers.

Alexander had a wonderful apparatus of war,

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

to which Maximinus is said to have added much. Entering Germany across the
Rhine, then, through thirty or forty miles of barbarian soil he burned
villages, drove off flocks, took plunder, killed very many barbarians, brought
the soldier back rich, took countless captives, and, had not all the Germans
fled to the marshes and woods, he would have brought all Germany under Roman
sway. He himself besides did much with his own hand, when he had even entered
a marsh and would have been surrounded by the Germans, had not his own men
freed him as he stuck fast with his horse. For he had this barbarian rashness,
that he thought an emperor must always give his own hand as well. At last he
fought a kind of naval battle in the marsh and killed very many there.

When Germany had been beaten, then, he sent a letter to the senate and people at
Rome, written at his dictation, whose sense was this:

/We cannot, conscript fathers, speak as much as we have done. Through forty or
fifty miles of the Germans we have burned villages, driven off flocks,
carried away captives, killed men in arms, fought in a marsh. We would have
reached the woods, had not the depth of the marshes forbidden us to cross./

Aelius Cordus says this speech was altogether his own. It is believable; for
what is there in it that a barbarian soldier could not do? He wrote to the
people with the same sense but with greater reverence, because he hated the
senate, by which he believed he was much scorned. He ordered pictures painted
besides, showing the war as it had been waged, and set up before the Curia, so
that a painting might speak his deeds. After his death the senate ordered
these pictures taken down and burned.

There were also under him many other wars and battles, from which he always
came back first as victor and with huge spoils and captives. There is a speech
of his sent to the senate, of which this is a sample:

/In a short time, conscript fathers, I have waged as many wars as none/

of the ancients, I have brought as much plunder onto Roman soil as could not
be hoped for, I have led so many captives that the Roman lands alone scarcely
suffice.

The rest of the speech is not needed for this matter.

When Germany had been pacified, he came to Sirmium, preparing to make war on the
Sarmatians and in his heart longing to bring the northern parts as far as the
Ocean under Roman sway, which he would have done, had he lived, as Herodian
says, a Greek writer who, as far as we see, favored him greatly out of hatred
of Alexander.

But when the Romans could not bear his cruelty, because he called up
informers, sent in accusers, invented charges, killed the innocent, condemned
all who had come to trial, made the richest men the poorest and sought money
from nowhere except another man's harm, then without offence killed many men
of consular rank and many generals, hauled others about in dry wagons, held
others in custody, in short omitted nothing that seemed to work toward
cruelty, they prepared a revolt against him. And not only the Romans, but,
because he raged against the soldiers as well, the armies that were in Africa,
in a sudden and huge mutiny, made the old Gordian, a most weighty man who was
proconsul, emperor. The order of that plot was this.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

There was a procurator of the fisc in Libya who in his zeal for Maximinus had
stripped everyone. He was killed by the country people, then also by some
soldiers, they overcoming those who were defending the accountant in honor of
Maximinus.

But when the authors of his killing saw that they must help themselves with
sharper remedies, they forced Gordian the proconsul, a man, as we have said,
worthy of reverence, older in years, flourishing in every kind of virtue, sent
to Africa by Alexander on a decree of the senate, though he cried out against
it and threw himself on the ground, covered with purple, to rule, pressing him
with swords and every kind of weapon. At first, indeed, Gordian had taken the
purple unwillingly; later, when he saw that it was not safe for his son or his
family, he took the empire willingly and was named Augustus by all the
Africans with his son at the town of Thysdrus. From there he came quickly to
Carthage with royal pomp and guards and laurelled fasces, and from there sent
a letter to the senate at Rome, which, after Vitalianus, the commander of the
praetorian soldiers, had been killed, was received with thanks out of hatred
of Maximinus.

The old Gordian and the young Gordian were also named

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

Augusti by the senate. Then all the informers were killed, all the accusers,
all the friends of Maximinus. Sabinus, the prefect of the city, was killed,
struck down among the people.

When these things had been done, the senate, fearing Maximinus still more,
openly and freely named Maximinus and his son public enemies. Then it sent
letters to all the provinces, that they should help the common safety and
liberty; and these were heeded by all. At last everywhere Maximinus' friends
and administrators and generals, tribunes and soldiers were killed. A few
cities kept faith with the public enemy; these, having betrayed those who had
been sent to them, quickly reported them to Maximinus through informers.

This was a sample of the senate's letter:

/The senate and Roman people, having begun to be freed from a most grim beast
through the Gordian princes, greets the proconsuls, governors, legates,
generals, tribunes, magistrates, and the several cities and municipalities
and towns and villages and forts, with the safety which it has now first
begun to recover. With the gods favoring we have obtained the proconsular
Gordian, a most holy and weighty senator, as prince; we have named him
Augustus, and not him only, but also, as a support for the state, his son
Gordian, a noble young man. It is now yours to consent, so that the safety of
the state may be secured, crimes warded off, and that beast and his friends,
wherever they are, hunted down. Maximinus with his son has also been judged
an enemy by us./

Temple of Castor on the sixth day before the Kalends of July, Junius Silanus
the consul read the letter received from Africa of Gordian the emperor, father

/Unwillingly, conscript fathers, have the young men to whom Africa was

bear the necessity. It is yours to judge what you wish, for I shall waver

When the letter had been read the senate at once cried out:

/Gordian Augustus, may the gods keep you. May you rule happily; you have freed

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

When Maximinus received this decree of the senate, a man fierce by nature, he
so flared up that you would have thought him not a man but a beast. He threw
himself against the walls, sometimes threw himself on the ground, shouted
without order, snatched a sword as if he could kill the senate, tore his royal
clothing, beat the court attendants, and, had the youth not withdrawn, as some
authors say, he would have torn out his young son's eyes. The cause of his
anger against his son was this, that he had ordered him to go to Rome when he
was first made emperor, and the son, out of too much love of his father, had
neglected it. He thought that if the son had been at Rome the senate would
have dared nothing.

His friends, then, received him blazing with anger into his chamber. But when
he could not hold his fury, in order to get forgetfulness of his thought, he
is said on the first day to have buried himself in wine until he did not know
what had been done. On the next day, his friends having been admitted, who
could not bear to see him but were silent and silently praised what the senate
had done, he held a council as to what needed to be done. From the council he
went out to an assembly, in which assembly he said much against the Africans,
much against Gordian, more against the senate, and urged the soldiers to
avenge their common injuries.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

The assembly, at last, was wholly that of a soldier, of which this is a
sample:

/Comrades, we bring you a thing you know. The Africans have broken faith, for
when did they ever keep it? Gordian, a feeble old man and next to death, has
taken the empire. Those most holy conscript fathers, who killed both Romulus
and Caesar, have judged me an enemy, though I fought for them and conquered
for them, and not only me but you also and all who think with me, and they
have called the Gordians, father and son, Augusti. Therefore if you are men,
if you have strength, let us go against the senate and the Africans, whose
goods you will all have./

He then gave a donative, and a huge one at that, and began to set out toward
Rome with the army.

* Chapter 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

But Gordian in Africa first began to be harassed by a certain Capelianus, to
whom, as he governed the Moors, Gordian had given a successor. When he had
sent his young son against him, and the son had been killed in a most fierce
fight, he himself ended his life with a noose, knowing that there was much
strength in Maximinus and in the Africans no force, much treachery rather.
Then Capelianus, the victor, in Maximinus' name killed and outlawed all of the
dead Gordian's party in Africa and spared no one, so that he seemed to do
these things from Maximinus' own mind. He then subjected cities, plundered
shrines, divided offerings among the soldiers, cut down the common people and
the leading men of the cities. He himself besides was winning the soldiers'
minds, playing for the empire if Maximinus perished.

* Chapter 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

When these things were announced at Rome, the senate, fearing Maximinus'
cruelty, both natural and now necessary, after the two Gordians were dead,
created as emperors Maximus, from the prefecture of the city, a man who had
held very many offices with distinction, humble in birth but famous in
virtues, and Balbinus, softer in his ways.

When these had been named Augusti by the people, the little grandson of
Gordian was also named Caesar by the soldiers and the same people. With three
emperors, then, the state was propped against Maximinus. Of these, however,
Maximus was stricter in life, weightier in prudence, more constant in courage.

At last both the senate and Balbinus entrusted the war against Maximinus to
him. When Maximus, then, had set out to war against Maximinus, Balbinus was
pressed at Rome by intestine wars and domestic seditions, especially after

[omitted]

Gallicanus and Maecenas were killed by the people. And that people was torn by
the praetorians, when Balbinus could not well resist the seditions. At last a
great part of the city was burned.

And Maximinus the emperor had been refreshed by hearing of Gordian's death and
of Capelianus' victory over his son, but when he received another decree of
the senate, by which Maximus and Balbinus and Gordian were named emperors, he
understood that the senate's hatreds were perpetual and that he was truly held
an enemy by the judgment of all.

* Chapter 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

He entered Italy, then, more fiercely. When he learned there that Maximus had
been sent against him, raging more violently he came to Emona in square
formation. But this was the plan of all the provincials, that they should take
up everything that could furnish food and withdraw within the cities, so that
Maximinus with his army might be pressed by hunger. At last, when he first
pitched camp in the plain and found no provisions, his own army, inflamed
against him because it was laboring with hunger in Italy, where it believed it
could refresh itself after the Alps, first began to murmur, then even to say
some things freely. When he wanted to punish this, the army flared up greatly
but put off its silent hatred for a time, which it later showed at the proper
time. Many, indeed, say that Emona itself was found empty and deserted by
Maximinus, who foolishly rejoiced that the whole city, as it seemed, had
yielded to him.

After this he came to Aquileia, which shut its gates against him, armed men
having been posted about the walls, nor did the defence fail, Menophilus and
Crispinus, men of consular rank, being the authors of it.

* Chapter 22

When Maximinus, then, was besieging Aquileia in vain, he sent envoys into that
city, to whom the people had almost consented, had not Menophilus with his
colleague resisted, saying that the god Belenus too had answered through the
soothsayers that Maximinus was to be beaten. Whence afterward Maximinus'
soldiers are said to have boasted that Apollo must have fought against them,
and that the victory had been not Maximus' or the senate's but the gods'.

Some say they invented this because they blushed to have been beaten, armed as
they were, by men almost unarmed. So, after making a bridge of casks,
Maximinus crossed the river and began to besiege Aquileia from close at hand.
There was then a huge assault and crisis, when the citizens defended
themselves from the soldiers with sulphur and flames and other defences of
that kind; of whom some were stripped of their arms, others had their clothes
set on fire, others had their eyes put out, and the engines too were thrown
down. Amid this Maximinus, with his young son whom he had named Caesar, went
about the walls, as far as he could be safe enough from the throw of a
missile, now asking his own men with words, now the townsmen. But he
accomplished nothing, for many insults were heaped on him because of his
cruelty, and on his son, who was most beautiful.

* Chapter 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23
:END:

Therefore Maximinus, hoping the war was being dragged out by the cowardice of
his own men, killed his generals, at a time when he least ought to have done
so, by which he made the soldiers still angrier at him. To this was added that
he was failing in provisions, because the senate had given letters to all the
provinces and to the keepers of the ports, that nothing of provisions should
come into Maximinus' power. It had sent besides through all the cities men of
praetorian and quaestorian rank, to keep watch everywhere and defend
everything against Maximinus. It was brought about at last that he himself,
while besieging, suffered the straits of the besieged. Amid this it was
announced that the world had agreed in hatred of Maximinus. Therefore the
soldiers, whose families were on the Alban Mount, growing afraid, in the
middle of the day, as it happened, when there was a rest from the fighting,
killed both Maximinus and his son as they lay in the tent, and showed their
heads, fixed on poles, to the Aquileians. In the neighboring town, then,
Maximinus' statues and images were at once taken down, and his prefect of the
guard was killed with his more famous friends. Their heads were also sent to
Rome.

* Chapter 24
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h24
:END:

This was the end of the Maximini, worthy of the father's cruelty, unworthy of
the son's goodness. When they were dead there was huge joy among the
provincials, and the heaviest grief among the barbarians.

But once the public enemies were killed, the soldiers were received by the
townsmen at their asking, and first on this condition, that they should
worship before the images of Maximus and Balbinus and Gordian, since all said
the earlier Gordians had been taken among the gods.

After this a huge store of provisions from Aquileia was quickly carried into
the camp, which was laboring with hunger, and after the soldiers were
refreshed, they came to an assembly on another day, and all swore in the words
of Maximus and Balbinus, naming the earlier Gordians divine.

It can scarcely be said how great the joy was when Maximinus' head was carried
through Italy to Rome, all running to meet it for the public gladness. And
Maximus, whom many think Pupienus, was preparing war at Ravenna through German
auxiliaries. When he learned that the army had agreed with him and his
colleagues, and that the Maximini had been killed, he at once dismissed the
German auxiliaries which he had prepared against the enemy and sent a
laurelled letter to Rome, which made huge joy in the city, so that all gave
thanks through altars and temples and shrines and holy places. Balbinus,
however, a man more timid by nature and one who trembled even when he heard
Maximinus' name, made a hecatomb and ordered that prayer be made through all
the cities with a like sacrifice.

Then Maximus came to Rome, entered the senate, was given thanks, held an
assembly, and from there they withdrew as victors into the Palace with
Balbinus and Gordian.

* Chapter 25
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h25
:END:

It is of interest to know what kind of senate decree there was, or what kind
of day it was in the city, when it was announced that Maximinus had been
killed. First of all, the man who had been sent from Aquileia to Rome, by
changing horses, ran with such force that he came to Rome on the fourth day,
having left Maximus at Ravenna. And it happened to be a day of games, when
suddenly, while Balbinus and Gordian were seated, a messenger entered the
theatre, and before anything was indicated the whole people cried out,

/Maximinus is dead./

Thus both the messenger was anticipated and the emperors who were present
showed the public joy by a nod and by consent. When the spectacle was then
dissolved, all at once rushed to their religious rites, and from there
the princes ran to the senate, the people to an assembly.

MAXIMINUS THE YOUNGER

* Chapter 27
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h27
:END:

Of his stock we have spoken above. He himself was of such beauty that he was
loved far and wide by the bolder women, and some even wished to conceive by
him. He seemed able to be of such height that he would have reached his
father's stature, had he not perished in his twenty-first year, in the very
flower of youth, or, as some say, in his eighteenth. He was trained in both
Greek and Latin letters for his first schooling.

For he used as Greek teacher of letters Fabillus, many of whose Greek epigrams
are still extant, especially on images of the boy himself, who made Greek
verses from those Latin ones of Virgil when he was describing the boy himself:

As when the morning star, bathed in Ocean's wave,
lifts his sacred face to heaven and looses the darkness,
such was the young man, famous under his father's name.

He used Philemon as Latin grammarian, Modestinus as jurist, Titianus as
orator, the son of the elder Titianus, who wrote most beautiful books of the
provinces and was called the ape of his time because he had imitated
everything. He also had as Greek rhetorician Eugamius, famous in his time.

Junia Fadilla, great-granddaughter of Antoninus, had been betrothed to him;
afterward Toxotius, a senator of the same family, who died after the
praetorship, and whose poems too are extant, received her. The royal pledges
remained with her, which (as Junius Cordus says, who hunts these things down)
are said to have been of this kind: a single-strand necklace of nine pearls, a
hair-net with eleven emeralds, a bracelet with a little row of four hyacinths,
besides gilded clothes and all the royal things and the other marks of
betrothal.

* Chapter 28
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h28
:END:

The young man Maximinus himself was of the most insolent pride, so that even
when his father, a most cruel man, rose for many men of rank, he remained
seated. He was of a merrier life, most sparing of wine, greedy of food,
especially of game, so that he ate nothing but boar, ducks, cranes, and
everything taken in the hunt. The friends of Maximus and Balbinus and Gordian,
and especially the senators, spoke ill of him because of his too great beauty,
they who did not want that looks, as if fallen from heaven, to be uncorrupted.

At last, in that time when, going about the walls around Aquileia with his
father, he was asking the surrender of the city, nothing was thrown at him but
filth, which was far from his life. He was so careful of his clothes that no
woman in the world was more gleaming. He yielded monstrously to his father's
friends, but so as to give and lavish. For at his greetings he was most proud,
and he stretched out his hand and suffered his knees to be kissed, sometimes
even his feet, which the elder Maximinus never suffered, who used to say:

/May the gods forbid that any freeborn man plant a kiss on my feet./

And since we return to the elder Maximinus, a pleasant thing must not be
passed over. For since Maximinus was, as we have said, eight feet and almost a
half, some placed his shoe, that is, his royal boot, in a grove that is
between Aquileia and Arcia, because it was agreed to have been a foot larger
than the measure of a man's footprint, whence also it has been drawn into
common speech, when people spoke of tall and awkward men,

/Maximinus' boot./

I have put this in so that no one who reads Cordus may believe I have passed
over something that belonged to the matter. But let me return to the son.

* Chapter 29
:PROPERTIES:

Concerning this young man Alexander Aurelius writes to his mother Mamaea,
wanting to give him his sister Theoclia, in these words:

/Mother, if the elder Maximinus, our general and a very good one at that, did
not have something barbarian in him, I would already have given your Theoclia
to the younger Maximinus. But I fear that my sister, trained in Greek
refinements, could not bear a barbarian father-in-law, although the young man
himself seems handsome and scholarly and trained toward Greek refinements.
This is what I think, but I still ask your advice, whether you want
Maximinus, Maximinus' son, as a son-in-law, or Messalla from a noble family,
a most powerful orator and most learned as well, and, unless I am wrong, one
who will be brave in war if he is applied to it./

This is Alexander on Maximinus. Of him we have nothing further to say.

Yet, lest anything seem to have been omitted, I have also put in a letter of
the father Maximinus, already made emperor, who says he allowed his son too to
be named emperor for this reason, that he might see, either in a painting or
in truth, what the younger Maximinus was like in purple. The letter was of
this kind:

/I have allowed my Maximinus to be named emperor both because of the affection
a father owes a son, and also so that the Roman people and that ancient
senate might swear they had never had a more beautiful emperor./

The same young man also used a gold cuirass on the model of the Ptolemies; he
used a silver one too; he used a doubled shield overlaid with gold and a
gilded spear. He also had silver swords made, and gold ones as well, and in
short whatever could help his beauty. He also had helmets set with gems, and
cheek-pieces too.

These are the things that it was decent to know and say of the boy. Whoever
wants to know the rest, about the affairs of Venus and of love with which
Cordus sprinkles him, let him read the same man; for we shall make an end of
the book here, hurrying on to other things, as we are bidden, as if by public
law.

The omens of empire, indeed, were these: a snake coiled around his head as he
slept; a vine planted by him brought huge purple grapes within a year and grew
to a wonderful size. His shield burned under the sun; a little lance was so
split by lightning that the whole of it, even through the iron, was cleft and
made two parts; from which the soothsayers said there would be two emperors,
not long-lived, from one house, of the same names. His father's cuirass was
seen by very many stained not, as usual, with rust, but all over with a purple
color. For the son these were the omens: when he was given to a grammarian, a
certain kinswoman of his gave him all the Homeric books, purple, written in
gold letters. The little boy himself, when he had been asked to dinner by
Alexander in honor of his father, because he lacked a dinner robe, received
one of Alexander's own. When he was an infant, a carriage of Antoninus
Caracalla suddenly coming through the public way, which was empty, he climbed
in and sat, and he was only with difficulty and trouble driven out by the
coachmen. Nor were there lacking those who told Caracalla the infant must be
guarded against. Then he said,

/It is a long way off that this one should succeed me./

For at that time he was among the obscure and too small.

The omens of death were these: as Maximinus with his son was coming against
Maximus and Balbinus, a certain woman with hair loosed met them in mourning
dress and cried out

/Maximini, Maximini, Maximini,/

and said nothing more and died. For she seemed to have wanted to say

/Help./

Dogs around his tent at the second halt, more than twelve, howled and laid
down their breath as if weeping, and at first light they were found dead. Five
hundred wolves came at once into that city into which Maximinus had withdrawn;
many say Emona, others Archimea, at any rate one which, deserted by its
citizens, lay open to Maximinus as he came. It is long to pursue everything;
whoever wants to know it, I would have him, as I have often said, read Cordus,
who wrote all these things even to the point of a little fable.

No tombs of theirs stand. For their corpses were thrown into running water,
and their heads were burned in the Campus Martius while the people insulted
them.

* Chapter 32
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h32
:END:

Aelius Sabinus writes, which was not to be omitted, that there was such beauty
of face in the son that even his head, already dead, already black, already
dirty, already wasted, with the gore running, still seemed the shadow of a
beautiful face. At last, though there was huge joy that Maximinus' head was
seen, there was almost equal grief that the son's was carried with it.
Dexippus adds that the hatred of Maximinus was so great that, after the
Gordians had been killed, the senate created twenty men to set against
Maximinus.
Among these were Balbinus and Maximus, whom they made princes against him. The
same writer adds that, in Maximinus' sight, when he had already been deserted
by the soldiers, both his prefect of the guard and his son were killed. Nor
are historians lacking who say that Maximinus himself, when he had been
deserted and had seen his son killed before his eyes, killed himself with his
own hand, so that nothing womanish might happen to him.

* Chapter 33
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

This too must not be passed over, that the Aquileians were of such loyalty
against Maximinus for the senate that they made ropes from women's hair when
they lacked bowstrings for shooting arrows. Which is said to have been done once
at Rome as well, whence in honor of the matrons the senate dedicated a temple
to Venus Calva. Indeed, a thing that must not be silent in any place, although
both Dexippus and Arrianus and many other Greeks have written that Maximus and
Balbinus were made emperors against Maximinus, that Maximus was sent with an
army and prepared war at Ravenna, and that he did not see Aquileia except as
victor: Latin writers have said that not Maximus but Pupienus fought Maximinus
at Aquileia and that the same man won. Whence that error was born I cannot
know, unless perhaps Pupienus is the same man as Maximus, which I have
therefore set down with testimony, lest anyone believe I did not know this,
which in truth would create great astonishment and wonder.
