* A. Pal. vi. 336
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:
These dew-wet roses, and this close thyme with them,
are laid for the Muses who keep Helicon.
Pythian Paean, the dark laurel is yours;
the rock at Delphi first dressed you in that honor.
This pale goat, horned, will stain the altar
while he strips the last spray from the terebinth.

* A. Pal. vi. 177
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
Fair Daphnis, pale of skin, who on the pipe
sings country hymns, set these things up for Pan:
his pan-pipes, throwing-stick, and pointed spear,
the fawnskin, and the pouch that once held apples.

* A. Pal. ix. 338
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
Daphnis, you lie on a bed of leaves, a spent body
taking its rest. Fresh snares stand on the hills.
Pan is after you, and Priapus too, with saffron
ivy bound about his beautiful head; they come
as one into the cave. Run. Run. Shake off
the heavy sleep that has you.

* A. Pal. ix. 437
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
Goatherd, once you have rounded that lane and those oaks
you will find a figwood image, newly cut,
three-legged, still in its bark, with no ears,
yet hung well enough to do the work of Cypris.
A holy close runs round it, and a stream
that never fails pours from the rocks, and everywhere
laurel, myrtle, and sweet cypress thrive.
A vine has spilled its grape-bearing tendrils
all about the place. In spring the blackbirds
throw clear, many-noted songs into the air,
and tawny nightingales answer, warbling
honey from their throats. Sit there, then,
and pray graceful Priapus to lift from me
this longing for Daphnis. I will kill a fine kid
at once. If he will not, then when I have my wish
I will make three offerings: a heifer, a shaggy
he-goat, and the fold-lamb I keep. May the god
hear me kindly.

* A. Pal. ix. 433
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
By the Nymphs, play me something sweet
on the twin flutes. I will take the harp
and start a strain; Daphnis the cowherd
will weave his piping in. Close by,
behind the cave of the shaggy oak,
we shall rob goat-loving Pan of sleep.

* A. Pal. ix. 432
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
Poor Thyrsis, what is won if you melt
both eyes away with tears?
The kid is gone, that pretty young one, gone
to Hades. A rough wolf took her in his claws.
The dogs are barking. What is won, when of her
neither bone nor ash is left?

* A. Pal. vii. 659
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:
You left a son still in his infancy,
and you yourself, Eurymedon, died in your prime
and have this tomb. You sit among the godlike;
the city will honor the boy, remembering
what a father he had.

* A. Pal. vi. 337
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:
Paean's son came even as far as Miletus
to keep company with Nicias, a healer of the sick,
who seeks him every day with sacrifice.
He had this image cut from fragrant cedar,
and promised Eetion, for that fine hand,
the highest wage. Eetion put all his craft
into the work.

* A. Pal. vii. 660
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:
Stranger, Orthon of Syracuse lays this charge
on you: never go out drunk on a winter night.
That was my fate too. Instead of my great
homeland I lie here, wearing foreign earth.

* A. Pal. vi. 338
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:
To all nine of you, goddesses, Xenocles
set up this marble image as a thank-offering.
He is a musician; no one will say otherwise.
Praise has come to him for that skill,
and he does not forget the Muses.

* A. Pal. vii. 661
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:
This is Eusthenes' monument. The sophist
read faces, and could take a thought from the eye.
His friends buried him well, a stranger
in a strange land, and he, who made hymns,
was wonderfully dear to them. All that a dead
sophist should have, he has. Weak as he was,
he still found people to care for him.

* A. Pal. vi. 339
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:
Demomeles the chorus-master, who dedicated
the tripod, Dionysus, and you, sweetest
of the blessed gods, was moderate in all things.
With a chorus of men he took the prize,
keeping his eye on what was fair and fitting.

* A. Pal. vi. 340
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:
This Cypris is not the Common one. Appease her
as Heavenly, the offering of chaste Chrysogone
in Amphicles' house, where she shared children
and a life with him. Year by year it went better
for them, beginning from you, lady. Mortals
who tend the deathless have more themselves.

* A. Pal. ix. 435
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:
This table treats townsman and stranger alike.
Lay money down, take it up, as the counters
come to the account. Let someone else make
excuses. Caicus counts foreign coin
even at night, for anyone who wants it.

* A. Pal. vii. 658
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:
I shall know, traveler, whether you give the good
any more, or whether the coward has from you
the same share. "Peace to this tomb," you will say,
"for it lies light over Eurymedon's sacred head."

* A. Pal. vii. 662
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:
This girl went to Hades before her time,
in her seventh year, ahead of many her age,
poor child, missing the brother of twenty months,
an infant who had tasted a loveless death.
Alas, Peristeris, what piteous things you suffered.
How ready the daimon has made the worst
for humankind.

* A. Pal. ix. 599
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:
Look hard at this statue, stranger, and say
when you come home: I saw Anacreon's image
in Teos, if any of the old song-makers
was a man apart. Add that he delighted
in the young, and you will have told
the whole man truly.

* A. Pal. ix. 600
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:
The tongue is Dorian, and the man is Epicharmus,
who first found out the art of comedy.
Bacchus, they set him here in bronze,
not flesh, the citizens of Syracuse,
as they would honor a man of their own city.
He left a store of sayings; those who keep them
have their wage. He told all men much
that serves a life. Great thanks are due to him.

* A. Pal. xiii. 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:
The song-maker Hipponax lies here.
If you are a knave, do not come near the tomb.
If you are honest, and come of decent people,
sit down in courage, and nap if you like.

* A. Pal. vii. 663
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:
Little Medeios made this tomb by the road
for the Thracian woman, and wrote on it "Cleita."
She will have thanks for the care with which
she reared the boy. And after all? They still
call her a woman who was of use.

* A. Pal. vii. 664
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:
Stop and look at Archilochus, the old poet
of iambs, whose vast fame has traveled
through night and on toward dawn.
The Muses loved him, and Delian Apollo too,
so tuneful he was, so sure a hand
at making verses and singing to the lyre.

* A. Pal. ix. 598
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:
This man first among the older song-makers
wrote for you of Zeus's son, the lion-fighter,
the ready-handed one: Peisander of Camirus,
and told how many labors he brought through.
The people, that you may know it plainly,
set the poet himself here in bronze,
many months and years after.

From the Berenice. Athenaeus vii. 284.
If any man whose living is the sea,
and whose nets are his ploughs, asks hunting luck
and wealth, and at nightfall offers this goddess
the holy fish they call white (the holiest of all),
he would set his nets and draw them full
out of the water.

* Megara
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23
:END:
Mother of mine, why do you wound your heart
like this, grieving past all measure, and why
has the old flush left your face? What have I done
that you are so distressed? Is it because
your glorious son suffers pains without bound
under a worthless man, a lion under a fawn?
Ah me, why have the deathless gods
so slighted me? Why did my parents bear me
to such a lot? Unlucky I, who came
to a blameless husband's bed, and honored him
equal to my two eyes, and still revere him,
and still feel awe for him in my heart;
yet of living men none was more luckless,
nor tasted in his own thoughts so many cares.
Hard man: with the bow Apollo himself gave him,
or with some Ker's dread shafts, or an Erinys',
he killed his own children and took the dear life
from them, raging through the house, and the house
was full of slaughter. I saw them, wretched I,
with my own eyes, struck down by their father.
That did not come even as a dream to another.
They called hard on their own mother, and I
could not help them: an unconquered evil
stood too near. As a bird wails over chicks
still nestlings, while a dread snake in the thick
bushes eats them, and she flies above them
crying shrill, the lady mother, and cannot
save her young, because to come any closer
is a great terror of that pitiless beast:
so I, cursed in my children, wailing
for my dear offspring, went through the house
on maddened feet. Would that I too had died
with them, and lain with poisoned arrow
through the liver, or gone down under your
gentle shafts, Artemis, great queen over women.
Then our parents would have wept us in their hands,
set us with many gifts on one pyre,
gathered all our bones into one gold urn,
and buried us where we were first born.
But now they dwell in Thebes that rears horses,
ploughing the deep clod of the Aonian plain,
and I in Tiryns, Hera's rocky city,
wretched, wound my heart with many pains
always alike, and of tears I have not
even a little pause. My husband I see
for a little time with my eyes in our house;
for him the work of many toils stands ready,
which he works, wandering over land and sea,
with a mind of stone or iron strong
in his breast. But you melt like water,
weeping the nights and as many days as Zeus
sends. No other of my kin could stand by
and cheer me; the house wall does not shut them in.
They all live beyond the piney Isthmus.
I have no one toward whom I might look
and, as a woman wholly luckless, cool my heart,
except Pyrrha my sister, and she herself
grieves more for her own husband Iphicles,
your son. I think you have borne the most
wretched children of all, both to a god
and to a mortal man.
So she spoke, and the fuller tears from her cheeks
poured over the lids into her lovely bosom,
remembering her children, and her parents after.
Alcmene likewise wet her white cheeks with tears,
and groaning heavily, even from the heart,
spoke to her dear daughter-in-law in close words:
Unhappy girl, what has fallen into that
shrewd mind of yours? Why stir us both
by speaking cares that cannot be forgotten?
These have not been wept now for the first time.
Is it not enough, what holds us, coming
newest every day? Truly one would be
fond of lament who should count.........
.........upon our griefs.
Take heart. We have not met such a fate
from the god. And I see you yourself, dear child,
toiling with pains that do not wear out.
I know what it is to be distressed, even when
there is a surfeit of good cheer. I lament you
terribly, and pity you, because you share
our grievous daimon, which hangs heavy
over our head. Let Kore know it, and Demeter
in her fair robe, who greatly harm the man
that willingly swears a false oath and makes
himself an enemy: I cherish you no worse
than if you had come from under my womb
and were my late-born maiden in the house.
Nor do I think this wholly escapes you.
So never say, my dear one, that I do not care
for you, not even if I weep denser
than fair-haired Niobe. There is no blame
when a mother wails over a child who suffers.
I labored ten months before I saw him,
holding him under my liver, and it brought me
near to Aidoneus who bars the gate;
so hard in birth I bore evil pangs for him.
Now my son is gone on a foreign land,
finishing a new labor, and I do not know,
ill-fated, whether I shall receive him
returning here, or not. Besides that, a dread
dream startled me through sweet sleep, and I fear,
having seen a hostile vision terribly,
lest it do something hateful to my children.
My son appeared to me with a well-made mattock
in both hands, Heracles in his strength,
and with it he was digging a great trench,
as if he had taken the work for hire,
at the edge of some flourishing field,
naked, without cloak or well-girded tunic.
When he came to the end of the whole task,
toiling at the strong fence of a vineyard
that bears wine, he was about to lean the spade
on the jutting bank and put on the clothes
he had worn before. Suddenly over the deep
trench unquenchable fire blazed, and about him
unspeakable flame coiled. He kept drawing back
on swift feet, eager to escape Hephaestus'
ruinous force, and always before his flesh,
as a wicker shield, he plied the mattock,
and peered this way and that, lest the hostile
fire burn him. Great-hearted Iphicles,
eager to help him, as it seemed to me,
slipped and fell on the ground before he came,
and could not stand up straight again,
but lay unmoving, like a feeble old man
whom joyless age has forced, against his will,
to fall, and he lies fast on the earth
until some passer-by, in awe of the trembling
gray chin, pulls him up by the hand.
So on the earth lay Iphicles the shield-bearer.
I kept weeping, seeing my children helpless,
until sweet sleep fled from my eyes,
and at once shining dawn came. Such dreams,
dear one, startled my mind all night.
May all this turn upon Eurystheus
away from our house. May my heart prove
a true prophet for him, and may the daimon
fulfill nothing else besides.
