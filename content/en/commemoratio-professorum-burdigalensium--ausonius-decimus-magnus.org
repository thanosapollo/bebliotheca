* Section Preface
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I will commemorate you as well, men no kinship bound to me. Fame did, and the
piety of a city I love, and a zeal for books, and the busy care of teaching.
Death made you famous. Perhaps one day someone who wants to show the same
piety will speak for my shade as I have spoken for yours.

* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2

** TIBERIUS VICTOR MINERVIUS, ORATOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

You come first, Minervius, the pillar of Bordeaux, a second Quintilian in the
rhetor's gown. Under you as master Constantinople was once illustrious, then
Rome, and then your own city: not that she rivals the majesty of those two,
but she is stronger in this one title, that she is home. Calagurris may claim
Fabius as her son as long as she likes, so long as the chair at Bordeaux is
not the lesser. He sent a thousand young men into the forum, and added twice a
thousand to the senate's roll and to the purple gowns.

Me too: but the bordered toga is a long story, so I will keep quiet, and sing
of you for your own sake, not for the honor I received. If the contest is in
books of panegyric, you belong with the Panathenaics. If one would rather
unroll the mock lawsuits of the schools, Quintilian holds a divided palm.
Speech poured from you in a torrent, yet it rolled gold, not muddy filth as
well. And that gift of Demosthenes which he named first three times so lived
in you that he himself would yield.

Shall I add the natural gifts of a divine endowment, how retentive a mind you
had?
What you heard or read once you held as if it were fixed; the ear and the book
kept the same faith. We once watched you, through a long match at the board,
count every throw that had fallen: how many paces, as the spin rushed on, turn
after turn, were cut away through the hollow boxwood; and then, going back
faithfully point by point, you told which had been given and which had been
called back after long delays.

No envy stained your mind. Then a tongue witty with much salt, and pleasant
jokes that never turned to a quarrel. A shining table, which no censor's rule
would blame, and which Piso Frugi would not refuse to call his own: sometimes
rich on birthdays and at a feast, yet not so as to thin a modest store.

Though you lacked an heir, having lived sixty years, we wept for you as for a
father and as for a young man. And now, if anything remains after the last
fates, you still live, remembering the time that has perished; or if nothing
is left and the long rest has no feeling, you lived for yourself: your fame is
our gain.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3

** LATINUS ALCIMUS ALETHIUS, RHETOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h1
:END:

Later generations will not charge me with undutiful silence, Alcimus, or say I
served your memory poorly, or that I forgot you. Our own day sets you alone
against the men of old. You were the glory of the courts and of the Camenae,
the one pattern in letters that learned Greece kept at Athens, and that Rome
keeps through Latium.

Shall I speak of your character, and of the rule you held to the end of your
life? Famous in praise, busy with letters, you ran from every kind of
ambition. No one was graver, or kinder, or freer with the poor. You would
plead when the court needed you, and teach with a will when the school did.
Those you raised to sacred fame will live in every later memory; and you will
give Julian more fame than the scepters he held for a little while.

Your books will do more for Sallustius than the consulship ever added. You
gave your sons a pattern of character, of grace, and of eloquence. Forgive me
if my dutiful pen has hurt you. Love is the offender: I cannot produce what
you deserve, and I still keep the office, too busy in the wrong way.

Lie quiet, and pay for a perishable body with glory.

** LUCIOLUS, RHETOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

Sad dirge, tell of Luciolus the rhetor: schoolfellow, then master, then
colleague. He was eloquent and learned, whether he built his work by the law
of meters or loosed it in the measures of prose. Lachesis tore him from his
father by a bitter death, and left two children, one of each sex. His obscure
heir in no way matched his deserts, though your fame is a help now. Gentle
friend, good brother, most faithful husband, dutiful son, a father: we are
sorry that this was your lot.

You were courteous to guests. You never shouted at clients, and never spoke
harsh words to servants. As you kept a quiet character, so keep quiet shades,
and take this gift from Ausonius, friend: farewell.

** ATTIUS PATERA THE FATHER, RHETOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1
:END:

Though in years you outstripped those named before, Patera, noble in speech,
you will not lack the honor of a sad dirge, teacher of powerful rhetors. You
flourished in the time just before mine, and I as a young man saw you an old
man. You were born of Druid stock among the Baiocassi, if fame does not cheat
belief. You draw a race consecrated from the temple of Belenus, and from there
you have your names: yours, Patera, for so the mystic ministers of Apollo name
their servants. Your brother and father were given a name from Phoebus, and
your son a name from Delphi.

No one in that time had so much learning, or so many runs and turns of
speech: retentive, eloquent, with a clear fluency, gifted in song and in
culture, modest in jokes that carried no bile, sparing of wine and food,
cheerful, chaste, handsome, even in old age like the old age of an eagle or a
horse.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6

** ATTIUS TIRO DELPHIDIUS, RHETOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

Eloquent, learned, quick of tongue and wit, pleasant in your jokes,
Delphidius: lie woven into your father's tearful dirge, as you followed him
and rivaled his praise. Almost from the cradle you were a noble poet of the
god. You went before the rest with an Olympic wreath, and as a boy you
celebrated Jove.

Soon you ran like a strait in flood and bound epic into meter, so that no book
of poems ever wove its speech by so even a law. Your eloquence, praised in
many ways, shone at home and abroad. You courted the prefect of the praetorian
cohort, or the judges of the provinces, and stood by clients whose fame and
safety had been wounded.

You would have been happy if you had stayed among quiet letters and tended the
work of the Camenae; if an impulse for revenge had not armed hatreds that
great lawsuits stirred; and if you had not raised yourself in the restless
palace of a tyrant's day. You always called a remote hope to yourself and
scorned what lay in your path. You liked your own good better than the good
fate offered, and you longed for a great deal, wandering through every grade
of office, having deserved more than you held.

So when a heavy storm of charges followed, you were handed over to your
father's distresses. Soon you were a rhetor, but you did not persist in
teaching, and you disappointed the care of fathers. By God's gift you tasted
fewer evils, because you were snatched in mid-life, and because a straying
daughter's error and a wife's punishment never wounded you.

** TO ALETHIUS MINERVIUS THE SON, RHETOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1
:END:

O flower of young men, glad hope of a father, and a gift your city could not
count on, rhetor Alethius: you were a teacher in your first years. At an age
when a grown man might still have been learning without shame, you were
already, in the bordered toga, matched with your father, and after Patera a
teacher too.

He, famous in reputation and in merit, enlarged the walls of proud Rome.
Greater than either, you were the chosen of Bordeaux, your homeland. You ruled
the bright standards of the cohort, and you had every good thing fate can
give, not without the bite of heavy envy. Fortune came too soon: she gave you
all things and snatched them away. Your rhetoric never came to the harvest of
grown years. You made a noble marriage through the wife's family, without the
father's peace, and you had the riches of both houses without an heir.

Like grass at the solstice, shown and snatched at once, you left in youth the
prayers of your people, dressed in goods that would not last. How truly Horace
spoke, with a voice that tells the fates: "Nothing is blessed on every side."

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8

** LEONTIUS THE GRAMMARIAN, SURNAMED LASCIVUS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h1
:END:

You who keep glad and cheerful manners, who love feast-days, jokes, vows, and
play, remember with Leontius's name the yearly dirge of one who has died. He
let himself be called Lascivus. He never refused a name unworthy of the
honesty of his life, because friendly ears liked it. In letters he gained only
so much title as a slender chair needs, enough that, once set in the list, he
might be taken for one of the grammarians.

You were always the companion of my youth, though you were heaped with more
years, and even now you grow warm again in my marrow, gentle Leontius! It is a
joy as well, in sad care, to keep the tearful song of a remembering complaint:
a gift thankless to you, and owed in our verse.

** TO THE GREEK GRAMMARIANS OF BORDEAUX
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

After these, shall I first remember Romulus, or Corinthius, or Spercheus and,
with him, the son of Menestheus, among the grammarians of the Attic Muses? All
had a busy zeal for teaching, a slender fruit, and thin speech. But they
taught in our time, and so they must be commemorated.

The third of these was not my master. The rest taught me in my first years, so
that I might not be raw in words, or speak without polish. I believe a slower
sense in my mind stood in the way, and a harmful wandering of boyhood never
applied itself to Greek studies.

May light turf cover you. May the roofs of the tomb defend the buried ashes.
May the inscription of my voice pay the last honor.

** TO IUCUNDUS, GRAMMARIAN OF BORDEAUX, BROTHER OF LEONTIUS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h1

And you, whom they say rashly seized a chair, and whom they think did not
deserve the grammarian's name, I will still call with my voice: simple, good,
friend, companion, Iucundus, dearer for this very zeal. You were not equal to
the name, but you loved it, and you belong among the men who earned it.

**
TO THE LATIN GRAMMARIANS OF BORDEAUX, PHILOLOGISTS
MACRINUS, SUCURO, CONCORDIUS, PHOEBICIUS

Ammonius, Anastasius, Grammarian of the Pictavi

Now I will set them forth as the pious honor of a tearful duty brings each man
to mind: men of humble stock, place, and merit, who still brought the study of
grammar to the raw minds of Bordeaux. Let Macrinus be among them. My boyhood
was first entrusted to him. And Sucuro, of freedman stock, sober and useful to
the minds of boys. And you, Concordius, who fled your homeland and traded a
barren chair for one in another city. Nor will I pass over the old man named
Phoebicius. He was temple-keeper of Belenus and took no help from that office;
yet, as it pleased, born of the Druid stock of the Armorican nation, he
obtained a chair at Bordeaux by his son's work.

Let the series stand. I will sing of Ammonius as well. It is a pious thing to
commemorate a grammarian of my native city. He gave first elements to raw
boys. He was scanty in learning and unquiet in manners, and so, as he
deserved, he had a thin fame.

Strike a tearful strain for Anastasius too, Muse, and remember, dirge, a
slight grammarian. Ambition took this man born at Bordeaux and gave him to
Poitou. There he was poor, and kept a thin living and dress, and in old age he
lost the slender little glory of both homeland and chair. Yet our honor has
commemorated him, lest the tomb cover name and bones alike.

** TO HERCULANUS, SISTER'S SON, GRAMMARIAN OF BORDEAUX
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h1
:END:

Herculanus, you set out from our bosom and school, and you offered your uncle
more hope than any fruit he could enjoy. You shared the school, and you were
almost the successor of my chair, had not the turning of slippery youth thrown
you headlong, off the straight Pythagorean path.

Be quiet, and cherish a seat among restful shades. I have already long
remembered you among kindred names.

** TO THALASSUS, LATIN GRAMMARIAN OF BORDEAUX
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h1
:END:

Your office and name, Thalassus of early years, I heard as a little child, and
I scarcely even remember. What you looked like, what merit you had, of what
stock of parents: a later time celebrates nothing of you.

Fame reported you only as a young grammarian, and even then so thin that now
none of it remains. But whoever you were, because you lived as a teacher in
our time, take this gift of ours: farewell.

** TO CITARIUS, SICILIAN OF SYRACUSE, GREEK GRAMMARIAN OF BORDEAUX
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14-h1
:END:

And you, dear Citarius, will be remembered by me. You deserve to be celebrated
among good grammarians. The glory of Aristarchus and Zenodotus among the
Greeks would be yours, if ancient honor still followed. The Muse of Cean
Simonides yields to the poems you first composed in your youth.

Born in a Sicilian city, you came a stranger to ours, and you quickly brought
it forth cultivated in studies. You soon married a noble and wealthy woman,
and by the envy of fate you died without a child. We honor you dead with a
remembering rite, as we cherished you living with the gift of friendship.

** TO CENSORIUS ATTICUS AGRICIUS, RHETOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15-h1
:END:

Agricius, you were a match for the first in the merit of eloquence, yet you
were placed later. You were born and died in a recent time, so we had put you
off, not left you out. In whatever number, a sad dirge will remember you. The
honor of the tomb is one, whether it comes late or is given beforehand.

Your race stood as high as your glory in speaking, a glory known in the seat
of the Athenaeum. Once conferred on Nazarius and famous Patera, that chair
trained many young men excellently. Now, with wife and children still living,
and a son-in-law, you cherish the shades of your ancestors and their
monuments.

** TO NEPOTIANUS, GRAMMARIAN AND LIKEWISE RHETOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16-h1
:END:

Witty, courteous, an old man with a youthful spirit: your mind was soaked with
no bile and with much honey, and mixed nothing bitter through all your life.
You were the healing of our heart, Nepotianus, a sharer of serious things as
of jokes.

Silent man, you would outdo Amyclae by keeping quiet. Ulysses would not have
left you telling stories, he who left the singing melodious maidens. Upright
and chaste, sparing, frugal, abstemious, eloquent, yielding to no rhetor in
style, and a disputant to match Cleanthes the Stoic: you knew Scaurus and
Probus in your inmost heart, and you were more retentive than Cineas of
Epirus. Companion and messmate, a guest continually.

It was too little that you were a guest: you stirred my mind. No one gave
counsel from a mind so pure, or hid given counsel in a deeper store. You were
famous in the honor of a governorship you held. You lived ninety years, and
leaving two children you met death, with much grief both of your people and of
mine.

** AEMILIUS MAGNUS ARBORIUS, RHETOR OF TOULOUSE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17-h1
:END:

Already wept among kindred shades, uncle, you will now be remembered among the
rhetors. Piety had that other work. Let this one keep the revered names of
men, for the honor of the homeland. Let us celebrate with double honor a
parent twice deserving, Arborius, whose father was Arborius and whose
grandfather was Argicius.

Your father's line was Aeduan. Your mother Maura came of the Tarbelli. Both
were of a race of nobles. A noble and dowered wife, a house and school, and
cultivated friendships of princes fell to you as a young man, while rich
Toulouse held Constantine's brothers, set apart under the show of exile. From
there your fame struck the citadel of Byzantium and the city of the Thracian
Propontis, Constantinople. There, rich in wealth and honored by a learned
Caesar, you died, Magnus, with your fathers still living.

But the piety of the prince Augustus restored you to your native seat and the
monuments of your people. From this the yearly day, with a thankless rite,
renews the cause for tears and the mournful duty.

** EXUPERIUS, RHETOR OF TOULOUSE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18-h1
:END:

Exuperius, I must remember you: eloquent without art, grave in gait and in
huge words, handsome of face and, to sum it, graceful in movement and bearing.
No one had a more beautiful plenty of speech. If you took it only by ear, the
blast of it would please. Shake it down, and you would know it gave forth
nothing of solid sense.

First the gown of Palladian Toulouse revered you, then drove you out with
equal lightness. Narbo received you from there. There, as rhetor, near the
turning-point of youth, at a great fee for teaching, you formed the sons of
Dalmatius, fatal names of kings, then still boys. They soon obtained the
Caesarean name and gave you the honor of a governor and the Spanish tribunal.
You left very rich, and you ended quiet manners and peaceful years in a
Cadurcan seat. But the rights of the homeland and the origin of your parents
call you, so that at Bordeaux you may give back a rhetor's name.

** TO MARCELLUS, SON OF MARCELLUS, GRAMMARIAN OF NARBONNE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19-h1
:END:

Nor will I be silent of you, Marcellus, son of Marcellus, whom a harsh mother
drove from city and home. But powerful fortune quickly restored all and
increased it. First Narbo gave you a lost homeland. Here the noble host
Clarentius, moved by your outstanding nature, gave you his daughter in
marriage. Soon a school, many a hearer, and youth in the bordered toga gave
you the grammarian's name and riches. But fortune never favors an unbroken
course, especially when she has found a man of crooked nature. I must not load
the fates on myself. I undertook to remember men. It is enough to have said
that all things perished together: yet not the name. I do not cheat you of
that. You are received among grammarians of very slender merit.

** SEDATUS, RHETOR OF TOULOUSE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20-h1
:END:

It would be a failure of piety if I left you silent, Sedatus, though you
obtained the office of teaching abroad. We share a homeland. By the lot of
powerful fate you obtained Toulouse as the seat of your school. There you had
a marriage and children and a rich old age, and fame such as is equal to a
great rhetor. Yet the homeland brings you back from a foreign region and
claims you as citizen once death has come.

And though you spent wandering labor on a divided city, origin takes its own
judgment about you. Your offspring now, following the parent's custom, makes
Narbonne and Rome noble by studies. But why do we complain? After a long time
fame, willing or not, will bring them too back to Bordeaux.

** STAPHYLIUS, RHETOR, CITIZEN OF AUCH
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21-h1
:END:

Thus far I have kept the law of commemorating citizens, whether they taught at
home or abroad. But it is right to join to the citizens one outsider: you,
Staphylius, born of the stock of the Nine Peoples. You were to me what a
father was, and what an uncle was, one man both: the one as Ausonius, the
other as Arborius. In grammar you were a match for Scaurus and Probus, a most
ready rhetor, skilled in the history of Livy and Herodotus. Every system of
learning was known to you, as much as Varro stores in six hundred volumes. A
golden mind, a persuasive voice, then quiet speech: you were neither a delayer
nor a hurrier. A handsome old age, a shining bearing, anger and grief far off;
and the turning-post of a quiet life was fitting.

** CRISPUS AND URBICUS, LATIN AND GREEK GRAMMARIANS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22-h1
:END:

You too, Crispus, will come commemorated into a future time by the gift of a
sad dirge. You taught the first years, and those still raw in speech, the
first signs of new elements. You were once believed to burn with neat wine, so
that you might bring forth rivals to the passages of Virgil and Flaccus.

And to you, ranked after Latin compositions, Urbicus, famous in Greek, I will
close the song thus with ἐλελείσω. Joined to your Crispus, you used to speak
prose and verse with the same rush, so that soon you would bring back in
speech the old heroes once commemorated in Homer's song: the sweet son of
Plisthenes in few words, the snowy sayings as of the Dulichian torrent, and
King Nestor singing words sweet in utterance with the nectar of a honeyed
voice.

Both easy of speech, both learned in all poems, skilled in the fictions of
myth and in history, both of freedman stock, yet men whom it would have been
fitting to call sons of freeborn fathers.

** TO VICTORIUS, UNDER-TEACHER OR PROSCHOLUS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23-h1
:END:

Studious Victorius, retentive, swift, always in unknown books, reading nothing
except what is hidden, you took more care to unroll papers eaten by moths and
Opican than you took in ready studies. What law the pontiffs held, what
treaties, what pedigree the sacrificing men of Cures had once before Numa;
what Castor published of all doubtful kings; what Rhodope published from her
husband's books; what pontifical law, what ordinances of the ancient Quirites,
what resolutions of the fathers, what Draco or Solon ordained, what laws
Zaleucus gave the Locrians, what Minos under Jove, what Themis before Jove:
these were known to you more than our Tullius and Maro, and whatever is stored
in Latin history. Perhaps long reading would have given you those also, had
Lachesis not hastened the last journey. You were tinted with the slender honor
of our chair, slight as you were, having barely tasted the grammarian's name.
Afterward you died on the far shores of Cumae, to which you had crossed from
the Sicilian coast. But now rejoice, remembered in the line of nobles, if
pious care reaches your shades.

** TO DYNAMIUS OF BORDEAUX, WHO TAUGHT IN SPAIN AND DIED THERE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h24-h1
:END:

I will not cheat you either, Dynamius, of a sad complaint: townsman of my
native city and advocate, whom wounded fame drove out on a charge of adultery,
whom little Ilerda fostered in her hiding-places, whom a Spanish wife enriched
while he lay hidden. For there you were a rhetor under a changed name, a
rhetor disguised under the surname of Flavinus, lest guilt should be able to
betray its fugitive. A late wish had given you back to the homeland, but soon
the Ilerdan house drew you, lingering, back again. Whatever your flight and
the fame of your life may have been, old friendship joins you to me. Take my
duty now, late, once death has come, Dynamius, if the shades have any feeling.
Though you lie dead on different shores, pious care commemorates you in sad
elegies.

* Section 24

** TO ACILIUS GLABRIO, JUNIOR GRAMMARIAN OF BORDEAUX
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h25-h1
:END:

Fallen with equal shortness of learning and of life, Glabrio, I will
commemorate you in sad elegies. Your name is drawn from the pedigree of noble
ancestors, Glabrio of the Acilii, Dardanian offspring. Once you were a match
for me as a boy, soon a pupil, and then, when I had been made rhetor, a
grammarian.

In the forum you were a protection to defendants, and in the fields a tiller,
worthy to enjoy long the goods you had gained. Easy, cheerful, kind,
abstemious: as good always at giving counsel as silent once it was given, as
much every grace to your people as soon a grief. Snatched by a bitter death
you leave all things, Glabrio: wife and children, father and mother left
behind, alas, lost under how many names! Long wept by us, never enough; take a
bitter farewell, Glabrio, commemorated forever.

** CORONIS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h26-h1
:END:

Know that the names you read, drawn from the first line of the book, were
teachers of my native city, in the study of the grammarian or the rhetor or in
both. It is enough for me to have remembered them once death has come. Praise
is a bait of the living. It will be enough to call names only by voice for
their tombs. Therefore, you who read the sad pastime of this page, do not seek
eloquence, but duty. By that duty pious care makes parental offerings to
famous and learned men, while it remembers the glories of an outstanding
homeland.

** THE POET
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h27-h1
:END:

Farewell, shades of famous rhetors. Farewell, honest teachers, if history or
verse or the forum made any of you noble, or if the teaching of medicine or of
Plato gave any of you to lasting glory. And if any care of the living pleases
the dead, and the honor of those who survive is a help, receive the sad
tending of my song, woven with a tearful complaint. Let unmoved ash keep the
seat of the tomb. Let the memory of names live, until that world which is
common to all returns by the gift of God the Judge.
