* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Marcus Antoninus, a man who lived as a philosopher through his whole life and
who in holiness of life surpassed every emperor,

had as father Annius Verus, who died in the praetorship; as grandfather Annius
Verus, twice consul and prefect of the city, enrolled among the patricians by
Vespasian and Titus as censors;

as uncle Annius Libo, a consul; as aunt Galeria Faustina Augusta; as mother
Domitia Lucilla, daughter of Calvisius Tullus, twice consul; as paternal
great-grandfather Annius Verus, of praetorian rank, from the municipality of
Succuba in Spain, made a senator; as maternal great-grandfather Catilius
Severus, twice consul and prefect of the city; as paternal grandmother Rupilia
Faustina, daughter of Rupilius Bonus, a man of consular rank.

He was born at Rome on the sixth day before the Kalends of May, on the Caelian
Hill, in the gardens, in the second consulship of his grandfather and the
consulship of Augur.

His family, running back to its origin, is shown to draw its blood from Numa,
as Marius Maximus teaches, and likewise from the Sallentine king Malemnius,
son of Dasummus, who founded Lupiae. He was brought up in the place where he
was born and in the house of his grandfather Verus, next to the dwelling of
Lateranus. He had also a younger sister, Annia Cornificia, and as wife Annia
Faustina, his cousin. At the beginning of his life Marcus Antoninus had the
name of Catilius Severus, his mother's grandfather; after his father's death,
however, he was called by Hadrian Annius Verissimus; after the manly toga,
Annius Verus. When his father died he was adopted and brought up by his
paternal grandfather.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

From first infancy he was grave. Once he had passed the years that are kept
under nurses' care, he was given over to great teachers and came to the
doctrines of philosophy.

For his first letters he used Euphorion as schoolmaster and Geminus the comic
actor, and Andron as musician and likewise as geometer. To all of these, as
authors of their arts, he paid the highest honour. He used besides as
grammarians, in Greek, Alexander of Cotiaeum, and in Latin Trosius Aper and
Pollio and Eutychius Proculus of Sicca.

As orators he used, in Greek, Aninius Macer, Caninius Celer, and Herodes
Atticus; in Latin, Cornelius Fronto. But he paid much honour among these to
Fronto, for whom he even asked a statue in the senate. Proculus, indeed, he
advanced as far as a proconsulship, taking the burdens on himself.

He gave himself fiercely to philosophy, and that while still a boy. For when
he had entered his twelfth year he took the philosopher's dress, and after
that the hardness, studying in a cloak and lying on the ground; only with
difficulty, at his mother's urging, would he lie on a couch spread with hides.

He also used the teacher of Commodus, whose kinship had been destined for him,
Apollonius of Chalcedon, a Stoic philosopher.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

So great was his zeal for philosophy that, even after he had already been
taken into the imperial house, he still came to Apollonius's house for the
sake of learning. He heard also Sextus of Chaeronea, Plutarch's nephew, Junius
Rusticus, Claudius Maximus, and Cinna Catulus, all Stoics. As a student of the
Peripatetic school he heard Claudius Severus, and above all Junius Rusticus,
whom he both revered and followed, a man strong at home and in the field, most
skilled in Stoic discipline; with whom he shared all his public and private
counsels; to whom he always gave a kiss even before the praetorian prefects;
whom he even designated consul a second time; for whom after death he asked
the senate for statues. He paid his teachers so much honour that he kept
golden images of them in his household shrine and always honoured their tombs
with visits, victims, and flowers. He studied law as well, hearing Lucius
Volusius Maecianus. He spent so much work and labour on his studies that he
harmed his
body, and in this alone his boyhood was blamed. He also frequented the public
schools of the declaimers, and among his fellow students he especially loved,
of senatorial rank, Seius Fuscianus and Aufidius Victorinus, and of equestrian
rank Baebius Longus and Calenus, toward whom he was most generous, so much so
that those he could not set over the state because of their quality of life he
kept enriched.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

He was brought up in Hadrian's bosom, who called him, as we said above,
Verissimus, and who conferred on him equestrian rank at six years, and in his
eighth year enrolled him in the college of the Salii. In the Salian priesthood
he received an omen of empire: when all were throwing their crowns onto the
god's couch according to custom, the others stuck in other places, but his, as
if by his hand, was fitted to the head of Mars. In that priesthood he was
leader of the dance, seer, and master, and he inaugurated and dismissed many
with no one prompting him, because he himself had learned all the songs.

He took the manly toga in the fifteenth year of his age, and at once, by
Hadrian's will, the daughter of Lucius Ceionius Commodus was betrothed to him.
Not long after he was prefect of the Latin Festival. In that honour he showed
himself most brilliantly, acting for the magistrates and at the banquets of
the emperor Hadrian. After this he granted his whole paternal estate to his
sister, when his mother called him to a division, and answered that he was
content with his grandfather's goods, adding that his mother too, if she
wished, might confer her own estate on his sister, so that the sister should
not be inferior to her husband. He was of such compliance, moreover, that he
was sometimes forced either to go to the hunts, or to go down to the theatre,
or to be present at the shows.

He also gave some work to painting under the master Diognetus. He loved
boxing, wrestling, and running, and fowling, and he played ball extremely
well, and hunted. But the study of philosophy drew him away from all these
pursuits and made him serious and grave, yet without wholly wiping out in him
the courtesy which he showed especially to his own, then to his friends, and
even to those less known, being thrifty without stubbornness, modest without
sloth, grave without gloom.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Things standing so, when after the death of Lucius Caesar Hadrian was seeking
a successor to the empire, and Marcus was not thought fit, being eighteen
years old, Hadrian chose for adoption Antoninus Pius, husband of Marcus's
aunt, on this condition, that Pius should adopt Marcus, yet so that Marcus too
should adopt Lucius Commodus. Indeed, on the day he was adopted, Verus saw in
dreams that he had ivory shoulders, and when he asked whether they were fit to
bear a burden, he found them stronger than usual. When he learned, however,
that he had been adopted by Hadrian, he was more frightened than glad, and
being ordered to move into Hadrian's private house he left his mother's
gardens unwillingly. And when those of his household asked why he was sad at
passing into a royal adoption, he set out what evils empire holds in itself.

Then first he began to be called Aurelius instead of Annius, because by the
right of adoption he had passed into the Aurelian family, that is,
Antoninus's. He was therefore adopted in the eighteenth year of his age, in
the second consulship of Antoninus, now his father, and, Hadrian proposing it,
exception being made for his age, he was designated quaestor. Adopted into the
palace house he showed all his own kin as much reverence as he had shown when
a private man, and he was no less thrifty and careful of his own property than
in a private house, willing to act, speak, and think by his father's rule.

When Hadrian had died at Baiae and Pius had set out to bring back his remains,
Marcus, left at Rome, fulfilled the last rites for his grandfather and, though
quaestor, gave a gladiatorial show as if a private man. Immediately after
Hadrian's death Pius, through his wife, sounded Marcus, and, the betrothal
which he had with the daughter of Lucius Ceionius Commodus being broken,

he had wanted her betrothed, she being still of unequal age; after taking
counsel he said that he was willing. When this had been done, Pius designated
Marcus, still quaestor, consul with himself, gave him the name Caesar, made
him, already consul-designate, one of the six commanders of the Roman knights'
squadrons, sat by him when he and his colleagues were giving the games of that
office, ordered him to move into the House of Tiberius, adorned him, though he
resisted, with courtly rank, and received him into the priestly colleges at
the senate's bidding. He also designated him consul a second time, when he
himself entered his fourth together with him.

Through the same time, though occupied with such honours and taking part in
his father's acts so as to be formed for ruling the state, he attended his
studies most eagerly.

After this he took Faustina as wife, and when a daughter had been born he was
given tribunician power and proconsular command outside the city, with the
added right of making five proposals. And he had such weight with Pius that
Pius never readily promoted anyone without him. Marcus was in the highest
obedience to his father, though there were not wanting those who whispered
some things against him, and above the rest Valerius Homullus, who, when he
had seen Lucilla, Marcus's mother, worshipping a statue of Apollo in a garden,
whispered,

/She is asking now that you close your day and her son rule./

Which had no weight at all with Pius; such was Marcus's honesty and

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

such his modesty in sharing imperial power. He had such care for his
reputation that even as a boy he always warned his procurators not to do
anything too arrogant, and sometimes, returning inheritances offered him to
the next of kin, he refused them. In short, for twenty-three years he lived in
his father's house so that Pius's love grew daily, and except for two nights
in all those years, at different times, he never stayed away from him. For
this reason Antoninus Pius, when he saw the end of his life near, summoned
friends and prefects, commended him to all as successor to the empire and
confirmed it, at once gave the tribune the watchword Equanimity, and ordered
the golden Fortune that used to stand in his bedchamber to pass to Marcus's
bedchamber. He handed part of his mother's goods to Ummidius Quadratus, his
sister's son, because she was already dead.

After the death of the deified Pius, forced by the senate to take public rule,
he designated his brother as partner in the empire, named him Lucius Aurelius
Verus Commodus, and called him Caesar and Augustus. From then they began to
rule the state together, and then first the Roman empire began to have two
emperors titled Augustus, when he shared with another the empire left to him.
He soon took the name Antoninus himself, and, as if he were father of Lucius
Commodus, he called him Verus, adding the name Antoninus, and betrothed his
daughter Lucilla to his brother. For this union they ordered boys and girls of
new names to be enrolled for the grain dole.

When they had done what had to be done in the senate, they went together to
the praetorian camp and promised twenty thousand sesterces apiece to the
soldiers for the sharing of empire, and to the rest in proportion. They laid
their father's body in Hadrian's tomb with a magnificent funeral, then, a
public halt of business following, the order of the funeral was also carried
out publicly. Both praised their father from the rostra, created a priest for
him from their kinsmen and an Aurelian priestly college from their closest
friends.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Having obtained empire they both behaved so civilly that no one missed Pius's
mildness, though Manilius, a mime-writer of their time, mocked them with
impunity.

They gave their father a funeral show.

Marcus gave himself wholly to philosophy as well, seeking the citizens' love.
But the first flood of the Tiber, the heaviest under them, broke in on that
happiness and security of the emperor. It damaged many buildings of the city,
killed a great many animals, and brought a very heavy famine. Marcus and Verus
tempered all these evils by their care and presence. There was also at that
time the Parthian war, which Vologaesus, prepared under Pius, declared in the
time of Marcus and Verus, after Attidius Cornelianus, then governing Syria,
had been put to flight.

A British war also threatened, and the Chatti had broken into Germany and
Raetia. Against the Britons Calpurnius Agricola was sent, against the Chatti
Aufidius Victorinus. For the Parthian war, with the senate's consent, brother
Verus was sent; Marcus himself stayed at Rome, because city affairs demanded
an emperor's presence. And Marcus escorted Verus as far as Capua, honoured him
with accompanying friends from the senate, adding the chiefs of all the
offices. But when Marcus had returned to Rome and learned that Verus was ill
at Canusium, he hurried to see him, vows having been taken in the senate,
which, after he returned to Rome, hearing that Verus had crossed, he at once
discharged.

Verus, after he came to Syria, lived in pleasures at Antioch and Daphne and
trained himself in gladiatorial arms and hunts, though, waging the Parthian
war through his legates, he had been hailed emperor; while Marcus at every
hour bent over the acts of the state and bore his brother's pleasures
patiently, though very unwillingly and against his will. In short, everything
needed for the war Marcus, stationed at Rome, both arranged and ordered.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Things were done prosperously in Armenia by Statius Priscus, Artaxata being
taken, and the name Armeniacus was conferred on both emperors, which Marcus
first refused from modesty, then later accepted. When the war was finished
both were called Parthicus. But this name too Marcus refused when offered, and
later accepted. The name Father of the Country, offered in his brother's
absence, he put off until the same man's presence. In the middle of the war he
escorted both Civica, Verus's uncle, and his own daughter, who was to be
married, entrusted to his sister and enriched, as far as Brundisium, sent her
to him, and at once returned to Rome, recalled by the talk of those who said
Marcus wanted to claim for himself the glory of the finished war and was
therefore setting out for Syria. He wrote to the proconsul that no one should
meet his daughter as she travelled.

Meanwhile he so fortified suits for freedom that he first ordered every
citizen to declare children born free within the thirtieth day, a name having
been given, before the prefects of the treasury of Saturn. Through the
provinces he established the use of public record-offices, where the same
should be done about origins as at Rome before the prefects of the treasury,
so that if anyone born in a province should plead a case of freedom, he might
bring attestations from there. He strengthened this whole law on assertions of
freedom, and passed others on bankers and auctions.

He made the senate judge in many inquiries, and especially those that
concerned himself. He ordered that inquiry into the status of the dead be made
within five years.

so that their authority might grow with the practice of law.

He enrolled many of his friends in the senate with aedilician or praetorian
rank. To many senators who were truly poor and without crime he granted
tribunician and aedilician rank, and he enrolled no one in the order whom he
himself did not know well. This too he granted senators: whenever judgment had
to be given on anyone's life, he would handle it in secret and only then bring
it into public, and he would not let Roman knights sit on such cases. Always,
when he could, he attended the senate, even if nothing was to be referred, if
he was at Rome; if he wished to refer something, he came even from Campania.
He often attended elections even into the night, and never left the
senate-house until the consul had said

/We detain you no longer, conscript fathers./

He appointed a judge for appeals made from the consul.

He applied singular care to the administration of justice. He added court days
to the calendar, so as to set two hundred and thirty days a year for doing
business and deciding suits.

He first made a praetor for guardianship, since before tutors were asked of
the consuls, so that tutors might be treated more carefully. As for guardians
of adults, whereas before they were given only under the Plaetorian law,
either for prodigality or for madness, he ruled that all adults should receive
them without causes being shown.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He was careful of public expense and checked the calumnies of informers,
setting a mark of infamy on false accusers.

He scorned informations by which the treasury would be increased. He devised
many prudent measures for public maintenance. He gave many cities curators
from the senate, so as to spread senatorial rank more widely. In time of
famine he gave Italian cities grain from the city and looked to the whole
grain supply. He restrained gladiatorial shows in every way. He also
restrained theatrical gifts, ordering that stage-players receive five gold
pieces each, yet so that no giver of games should go beyond ten gold pieces.

He tended the streets of the city and the roads most carefully. He provided
seriously for the grain supply.

By appointing judges of the law for Italy he provided for it, on the model by
which Hadrian had ordered men of consular rank to administer the law.

He modestly provided for the Spaniards, exhausted by an Italian enrollment,
even against Trajan's precepts. He also added laws on the twentieth of
inheritances, on the trusts of freedmen, on maternal goods, and likewise on
sons' successions for the maternal share, and that foreign-born senators
should hold a fourth part in Italy. He further gave the curators of regions
and roads power either to punish or to send to the city prefect for punishment
those who had exacted anything from anyone beyond the taxes. He restored old
law more than he made new. He kept prefects with him, by whose authority and
risk he always declared the law. He used Scaevola especially, a man learned in
the law.

:END:

With the people he dealt no otherwise than is done under a free state. In all
things he was most moderate in turning men from evil, inviting them to good,
rewarding them with abundance, freeing them by indulgence; he made good men of
bad, the best of good, bearing even some men's jibes moderately. For when he
warned a certain Vetrasinus of detestable repute, seeking office, to clear
himself of the people's opinions, and the man answered that he saw many who
had fought with him in the arena now praetors, he bore it patiently. And lest
he easily punish anyone, he did not order a praetor who had done some things
very badly to abdicate the praetorship, but assigned the jurisdiction to his
colleague. He never favoured the treasury when judging cases of profit.
Indeed, though he was firm, he was also modest.

After his brother returned victorious from Syria, the name Father of the
Country was decreed to both, since Marcus, in Verus's absence, had behaved
most moderately toward all senators and men. A civic crown was also offered to
both. Lucius asked that Marcus triumph with him. Lucius asked besides that
Marcus's sons be called Caesars. But Marcus was of such moderation that,
though he had triumphed together, yet after Lucius's death he called himself
only Germanicus, which he had won in his own war. In the triumph they carried
Marcus's children of both sexes with them, yet so that they carried the girls
too, still unmarried. They also watched the games decreed for the triumph in
triumphal dress.

Among the rest of his piety this moderation too is to be praised: after a boy
had fallen he ordered mattresses put under the rope-dancers, whence even today
a net is stretched.

long held in suspense by the skill of those who were there, so that when the
eastern war was already finished the Marcomannic could be carried on. And when

German war as emperors. So great was the terror of the Marcomannic war that
Antoninus summoned priests from everywhere, performed foreign rites, purified
Rome with every kind of lustration, and was delayed from setting out to war.
He also celebrated banquets of the gods in the Roman rite for seven days. So

Then the Antonini sanctioned very harsh laws on burying and tombs, when they
provided that no one should build a tomb onto a country house, which is still
observed.

The pestilence consumed many thousands and many of the nobles, for the most
distinguished of whom Antoninus set up statues. Such was his clemency that he
ordered common funerals to be carried out at public cost, and to a vain fellow
who, seeking with certain accomplices an occasion to plunder the city,
harangued from a wild fig-tree on the Campus Martius, saying fire would fall
from heaven and the end of the world would come if he himself, falling from
the tree, were turned into a stork, and who, when he had fallen at the
appointed time and let a stork out of his bosom, was brought to him and
confessed, he granted pardon.

Yet both emperors set out in military cloaks, the Victuali and Marcomanni
throwing everything into confusion, and other nations too, who had fled,
driven by the barbarians farther up, making war unless they were received.

That setting-out was of no little use, once they had come as far as Aquileia.
For most of the kings both withdrew with their peoples and killed the authors
of the tumult.

The Quadi, having lost their king, said they would not confirm the one who had
been created until it had pleased our emperors. Lucius nevertheless set out
unwillingly, though most were sending to the emperors' legates asking pardon
for the revolt. Lucius, because the praetorian prefect Furius Victorinus had
been lost and part of the army had perished, thought they should return.
Marcus, however, judging that the barbarians were feigning both flight and the
rest of what shows security in war, so as not to be crushed by the mass of so
great a preparation, held that they should press on. At last, having crossed
the Alps, they advanced farther and settled everything that belonged to the

It was decided, Lucius urging it, that letters should be sent ahead to the

* Chapter 15

:END:

It was Marcus's custom at the circus games to read, listen, and sign, from
which indeed he is said to have been often baited with popular jokes.

The freedmen Geminas and Agaclytus had much power under Marcus and Verus.

Such was Marcus's holiness that he both hid and defended Verus's vices, though
they displeased him most violently, and when he was dead he named him divine,
relieved and advanced his aunts and sisters with honours and salaries decreed,
and honoured him with very many rites. He dedicated to him a priest and an
Antoninian priestly college and all the honours that are held for the deified.

There is no emperor whom some heavy rumour does not touch, so far that even
Marcus came into talk, that he had taken Verus off either by poison, cutting a
sow's womb with a knife smeared on one side with poison, offering the poisoned
part to his brother to eat and keeping the harmless part for himself, or at
least through the physician Posidippus, who is said to have let his blood at
an untimely moment. After Verus's death Cassius revolted from Marcus.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Marcus was of such kindness to his own that, as he conferred all the ornaments
of honours on all his kin, so on his son, and a wicked and impure one at that,
he quickly gave the name Caesar, and soon a priesthood, and at once the name
of emperor and a share in a triumph and the consulship. At that time the
emperor ran on foot in the Circus beside the triumphal car in which his son
sat.

After Verus's death Marcus Antoninus held the state alone, much better and
more fruitful in virtues, since he was no longer hindered by Verus's errors,
either of a hot simplicity and truth, from which the other suffered by an
inborn fault, or of those things that had especially displeased Marcus
Antoninus from the first time of his age, either the principles of a crooked
mind or its manners. For he himself was of such calm that he never changed his
face in grief or joy, given to Stoic philosophy, which he had both received
through the best masters and gathered himself from every side. For Hadrian too
had prepared this same man as successor, had not boyish age stood in the way.
Which indeed appears from this, that he chose this same man as son-in-law for
Pius, so that the Roman empire might come to him, as to a worthy man, whenever
it should.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

Therefore after this he treated the provinces with great moderation and
kindness. He did well against the Germans. The Marcomannic war in particular,
as great as never was in any memory, he finished both with courage and with
fortune, and that at a time when a heavy pestilence had killed many thousands
both of the people and of the soldiers. He therefore freed the Pannonias from
bondage, the Marcomanni, Sarmatians, Vandals, and the Quadi too being crushed,
and triumphed at Rome with Commodus, whom he had already made Caesar, his son,
as we have said.

When he had exhausted his whole treasury for this war and could not bring
himself to order anything extraordinary of the provincials, he held an auction
of imperial ornaments in the forum of the deified Trajan and sold gold cups
and crystal and murrine, even royal vessels and his wife's silk and
gold-embroidered dress, even gems, many of which he had found in Hadrian's
more sacred store. This sale was held for two months, and so much gold was
brought in that, after he had pursued the rest of the Marcomannic war as he
wished, he later gave the buyers leave, if anyone wished to return what he had
bought and recover the gold, to know that it was allowed; nor was he hard on
anyone who either did not return what he had bought or did.

Then he allowed the more distinguished men to give banquets with the same
state as himself, or with like attendants. In a public show he was so
open-handed that he exhibited a hundred lions at one release together and had
them killed with arrows.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

When therefore he had reigned in the love of all, and was both called and
loved now as brother, now as father, now as son, as each man's age allowed, in
the eighteenth year of his empire and the sixty-first of his life he closed
his last day. So great was the love of him on the day of the royal funeral
that no one thought he should be lamented, all being sure that, lent by the
gods, he had gone back to the gods. At last, before the funeral was laid away,
as most say, which had never been done before and never after, senate and
people, not in divided places but in one seat, named him a gracious god.

This man, so great and of such a kind, joined to the gods in life and death,
left his son Commodus; who, if he had been fortunate, would not have left a
son. And it was too little that every age, every sex, every condition and rank
gave him divine honours, unless also he was judged sacrilegious who did not
have his image in his house, who by fortune either could have it or ought. To
this day statues of Marcus Antoninus stand in many houses among the household
gods. Nor were there wanting men who, taking it that he had foretold many
things in dreams, agreed on future things that proved true. Hence a temple was
also founded for him, Antoninian priests appointed, and a college and priests
of that cult and all that antiquity decreed for a consecrated shrine.

Some say, and it seems likely, that Commodus Antoninus, his successor and son,
was not born of him but of adultery, and they weave such a little tale in
common talk: that Faustina once, Pius's daughter, Marcus's wife, when she had
seen gladiators pass, burned with love of one of them, and when she was
suffering a long illness, confessed the love to her husband. When Marcus had
referred this to the Chaldeans, it was their counsel that the gladiator should
be killed and Faustina should bathe herself in his blood and so lie with her
husband. When this was done, the love was indeed loosed, but Commodus was born
a gladiator, not an emperor, who as emperor exhibited gladiators in nearly a
thousand fights publicly, the people looking on, as will be shown in his life.

This is held likely from this, that the son of so holy an emperor was of such
manners as no trainer, no stage-player, no man of the arena, none at last
compacted from the wash of all dishonours and crimes.

Many however say Commodus was born wholly of adultery, since it is well enough
agreed that Faustina at Caieta chose both sailors and gladiators as lovers.
When this was told to Antoninus Marcus, that he should divorce her if
he did not kill her, he is said to have said

/If we send the wife away, let us return the dowry too./

And what was the dowry held to be?

The empire, which he had received from his father-in-law, adopted at Hadrian's
wish.

So much indeed does the life, holiness, calm, and piety of a good emperor
avail that no near person's envy stains his fame. At last it did not hurt
Antoninus, since he always kept his own manners and was not changed by
anyone's whispering, that his son was a gladiator, his wife infamous; and he
is held a god even now, as it has always seemed and seems to you yourselves,
most sacred emperor Diocletian, who honour him among your divinities not as
the rest but specially, and often say you wish to be such in life and clemency
as Marcus was, even if in philosophy Plato himself could not be, should he
return to life.

And these things, indeed, briefly and in a heap.

But these are Marcus Antoninus's deeds after his brother. First his body was
brought to Rome and laid in the tombs of his ancestors. Divine honours were
then decreed. Then, when he was thanking the senate for having consecrated his
brother, he showed secretly that all the war plans by which the Parthians were
overcome had been his, and added besides certain things by which he showed
that now at last he would act as if from the beginning in the state, that man
being removed who had seemed too slack. The senate took it no otherwise than
Marcus had said, so that he seemed to be giving thanks that Verus had left
life. Then he conferred very much right and honour and money on all the
sisters and kinsmen and freedmen. For he was most careful of his own fame,
seeking to the truth what each man said of him, correcting what seemed well
blamed.

Setting out to the German war, not having run through the time of mourning, he
gave his daughter to Claudius Pompeianus, son of an aged Roman knight, of
Antiochene stock and not noble enough (whom he later made consul twice),
though his daughter was Augusta and daughter of an Augusta. But Faustina and
she who was being given had these nuptials unwilling.

When the Moors were wasting almost all the Spains, things were well done
through his legates. And when the Bucolic soldiers had done many grave things
through Egypt, they were beaten back by Avidius Cassius, who later seized a
tyranny.

In the very days of setting out, staying in retreat at Praeneste, he lost his
son, named Verus Caesar, seven years old, a tumour under the ear having been
cut out. He mourned him no more than five days, and even when consulted he
gave himself back to public acts in the middle of them. And because the games
of Jupiter Best and Greatest were on, he did not wish them interrupted by
public mourning, and ordered that only statues be decreed for the dead son,
and a golden image to be carried through the circus in the procession, and
that his name be inserted in the Salian hymn.

The pestilence still pressing, he restored the cult of the gods most carefully
and prepared slaves for military service, as had been done in the Punic war,
whom he called Volunteers after the example of those so named then. He also
armed gladiators, whom he called the Compliant. He made soldiers even of the
bandits of Dalmatia and Dardania. He armed the Diogmitae too. He also bought
German auxiliaries against the Germans. With all care besides he prepared
legions for the German and Marcomannic war.

And, so as not to be a burden to the provincials, he held an auction of palace
things, as we have said, in the forum of the deified Trajan, in which besides
clothes and cups and gold vessels he even sold statues with paintings of great
artists. He destroyed the Marcomanni in the very crossing of the Danube, and
restored the plunder

to the provincials. All the nations from the Illyrian frontier as far as Gaul
had conspired: Marcomanni, Varistae, Hermunduri and Quadi, Suebi, Sarmatians,
Lacringes and Buri, these and others with the Victuali, Osi, Bessi, Cobotes,

the fiercest nations, the soldiers imitating him, the legates and praetorian
prefects also leading the army, and he received the Marcomanni in surrender, a
great many being brought over into Italy.

Always, indeed, he conferred with the best men not only on war but also on
civil things, before he did anything. In short this was always his chief
sentence:

/It is fairer that I follow the counsel of so many such friends than that so
many such friends follow the will of me alone./

Indeed, because from his training in philosophy Marcus seemed hard for the
labours of soldiering and for all of life, he was bitterly carped at; but to
those who spoke ill he answered either in speech or in letters.

And many nobles perished in the German or Marcomannic war, or rather the war
of very many nations, for all of whom he set up statues in the Forum of
Trajan. Therefore his friends often urged him to leave the wars and come to
Rome, but he scorned it and stood fast, and did not withdraw until he had
finished all the wars. He made provinces consular from proconsular, or
proconsular from consular, or praetorian, according to the need of war. He
checked affairs disturbed among the Sequani by censure and authority.

Affairs were also settled in Spain, which had been disturbed through
Lusitania. Having summoned his son Commodus to the frontier, he gave him the
toga of manhood, for which he distributed a largess to the people and
designated him consul before the time.

If anyone was ever proscribed by the city prefect, he did not take it gladly.
He himself was most sparing in gifts of public money, which is put down rather
to praise than to blame; yet he both gave money to good men and brought help
to towns that were failing, and remitted tribute or taxes where necessity
forced it.

When absent he ordered with force that the Roman people's pleasures be cared
for through the richest givers of games. For this was the people's talk, when
he had taken the gladiators to war, that by taking away their pleasures he
wanted to drive the people to philosophy.

For he had ordered, so that trade not be hindered, that pantomimes be shown
later by nine days. There was talk of pantomimes loved by his wife, as we said
above, but he cleared all this by his letters.

The same Marcus forbade riding in cities on horses or in carriages. He put
away mixed baths. He set in order the loosening manners of matrons and of
noble youths. He removed the rites of Serapis from the vulgarity of the
Pelusia.

There was indeed a rumour that under the guise of philosophers certain men
were troubling the state and private persons, which he cleared.

It was Antoninus's way to punish all crimes with a lighter penalty than the
laws usually inflict, though sometimes he remained inexorable against those
clearly guilty of grave crimes. He himself heard capital cases of honourable
men, and with the highest equity, so that he rebuked a praetor who had heard
the accused too quickly and ordered him to hear them again, saying it
concerned their dignity that they be heard by him who judged for the people.
He kept equity even toward captured enemies. He settled countless men from the
nations on Roman soil. By his prayers he wrung a thunderbolt from heaven
against the enemy's engine, and obtained rain for his own when they were
suffering from thirst.

He wished to make Marcomannia a province, wished also Sarmatia, and would have
done it, had not Avidius Cassius rebelled under him in the East and called
himself emperor, as some say, Faustina wishing it, who despaired of her
husband's health. Others say Cassius, having feigned Antoninus's death, called
himself emperor, when he had named Marcus divine. And Antoninus indeed was not
much moved by Cassius's revolt, nor did he rage against his affections.

But he was judged an enemy by the senate and his goods were proscribed through

There were also tumults at Rome, as if Cassius were coming while Antoninus was

The army also killed Maecianus, Cassius's ally, to whom Alexandria had been

and at the same time asked that no senator be killed in the time of his rule,

He pardoned the cities that had agreed with Cassius, pardoned the Antiochenes
too, who had said many things against Marcus for Cassius. From whom he had
taken spectacles and public assemblies and every kind of meeting, against whom
he sent a very heavy edict. Yet Marcus's speech, put in Marius Maximus, which
he used among his friends, shows them seditious. At last he would not see
Antioch when he went to Syria, for he would not see Cyrrhus either, from which
Cassius was. Later however he did see Antioch. He was at Alexandria, dealing
mildly with them.

He did much with kings and confirmed peace, all the kings and the envoys of
the Persians meeting him.

He was dearest to all the eastern provinces. Among many he also left traces of
philosophy. Among the Egyptians he behaved as a citizen and a philosopher in
all the stadia, temples, places.

And though the Alexandrians had said many things in Cassius's favour, he
nevertheless pardoned all and left his daughter among them. He lost his
Faustina at the roots of Mount Taurus in the village of Halala, dead of a
sudden illness. He asked the senate to decree Faustina honours and
a temple, she having been praised, though she had suffered heavily from a
rumour of unchastity. Which Antoninus either did not know or pretended not to.
He founded a new order of Faustinian girls in honour of his dead wife, and
congratulated
himself that the senate had named Faustina divine.

He had had her with him even in summer camps, so as to call her Mother of the
Camp. He also made a colony of the village in which Faustina died and built
her a temple. But this temple was later dedicated to Elagabalus.

He suffered Cassius himself, in his clemency, to be killed; he did not order
him killed. Heliodorus, Cassius's son, was deported, and others of the
children received free exile with part of the goods.

Cassius's sons however received even more than half of the paternal estate and
were helped with gold and silver, the women even with ornaments; so that
Alexandria, Cassius's daughter, and Druncianus his son-in-law had free power
of wandering, being commended to his aunt's husband. At last he grieved that
Cassius had been destroyed, saying he had wished to pass through the empire
without senatorial blood.

Eastern affairs ordered, he was at Athens and went to the rites of Ceres, to
prove himself innocent, and entered the sanctuary alone. Returning to Italy he
suffered a very heavy storm by ship. Coming into Italy through Brundisium he
himself took the toga and ordered the soldiers to be in togas, and never under
him were the soldiers in military cloaks. When he came to Rome he triumphed.
And from there he set out to Lavinium. Then he joined Commodus to himself as
colleague in tribunician power, gave a largess to the people and marvellous
spectacles; then he corrected many civil things, set a limit to the cost of
the gladiatorial show.

Plato's sentence was always in his mouth, that cities flourish if either
philosophers rule or those who rule philosophize. He joined his son to the
daughter of Bruttius Praesens, the nuptials being celebrated after the example
of private men, for which he also gave a largess to the people.

Then, turning to finish the war, he died in the conduct of that war, his son's
manners already slipping from his own rule. For three years afterward he waged
war with the Marcomanni, Hermunduri, Sarmatians, and the Quadi too, and if he
had lived one year more he would have made provinces of them. Two days before
he expired, his friends being admitted, he is said to have shown the same
opinion of his son that Philip showed of Alexander, when he thought ill of
him, adding that he took it too hard, leaving a son surviving.

For already Commodus was showing himself base and bloody.

His death was of this kind: when he had begun to be ill, he called his son and
first asked of him that he not scorn the remains of the war, lest he seem to
betray the state. And when the son answered that he first desired health, he
allowed him to do as he wished, asking nevertheless that he wait a few days
and not set out at the same time.

Then he abstained from food and drink, wishing to die, and increased the
disease. On the sixth day, his friends being called, laughing at human things
and scorning death, he said to his friends,

/Why do you weep for me and not think rather of the pestilence and of the
common death?/

And when they wished to withdraw, groaning he said,

/If you now dismiss me, I bid you farewell, going before you./

And when he was asked to whom he commended his son, he answered

/To you, if he shall have been worthy, and to the immortal gods./

The army, learning of his bad health, grieved most violently, because they
loved him uniquely. On the seventh day he grew heavy and admitted only his
son, whom he at once dismissed, lest the disease pass to him. The son
dismissed, he covered his head as if wishing to sleep, but in the night he
breathed out his spirit. He is said to have wished his son to die, when he saw
he would be such as he proved after his death, lest, as he himself said, he be
like Nero, Caligula, and Domitian.

It was given him as a charge that he advanced his wife's adulterers, Tertullus
and Tutilius and Orfitus and Moderatus, to various honours, though he had
caught Tertullus even breakfasting with his wife. About whom a mime on the

his wife's adulterer, and the slave said three times

/Tullus,/

and the fool still asked, the other answered

/I have already told you three times, he is called Tullus./

And about this indeed the people said much, others too, blaming Antoninus's
patience.

Before the time of his death, indeed, before he returned to the Marcomannic
war, he swore on the Capitol that no senator had been killed with his
knowledge, and said he would have saved even the rebels if he had known. For
he feared and prayed against nothing more than a rumour of avarice, of which
he clears himself in many letters. They also gave it as a fault that he had
been feigned and not so simple as he seemed, or as either Pius or Verus had
been. They also gave it as a charge that he had confirmed court arrogance by
removing his friends from common society and from banquets.

He decreed consecration for his parents, and honoured even his parents'
friends, when dead, with statues.

He did not quickly believe those who canvassed, but always sought long what
was true.

Fabia strove, Faustina being dead, to come into marriage with him. But he took
to himself as concubine the daughter of his wife's steward, lest he put a
stepmother over so many children.
