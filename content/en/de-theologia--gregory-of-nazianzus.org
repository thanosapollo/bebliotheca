* On Theology

Second theological oration on theology 1 Since we have purified the theologian
by the discourse, having gone through what sort of person he must be, and with
whom one must philosophize, and when, and how much: that as far as possible he
be pure, so that light may be grasped by light; and with the more diligent, so
that the word may not be barren falling into barren soil; and when we have
calm within from the outer whirling, so as not, like those who rave, to be cut
apart by the spirit; and as much as we have contained, or are contained: since
these things are so, and we have plowed ourselves divine plowings, so as not
to sow upon thorns, and we have leveled the face of the earth, having been
formed by the Scripture and having formed it: come, let us already approach
the discourses of theology, setting at the head of the discourse the Father,
and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, concerning whom the discourse is, so that
the one may be well pleased, the other may cooperate, and the Spirit may
inspire; or rather that one illumination may come to be from the one Godhead,
divided unitarily and joined dividedly, which is also a paradox. 2 Now as I go
up eagerly upon the mountain, or to speak more truly, both eager and in agony,
the one because of the hope, the other because of the weakness, that I may
come inside the cloud and converse with God (for this God commands), if anyone
is Aaron, let him go up with me and stand near, even if he must remain outside
the cloud, accepting this. If anyone is Nadab, or Abihu, or of the eldership,
let him go up, but let him stand far off, according to the worth of the
purification. If anyone is of the many and unworthy of such a height and
contemplation, if he is altogether unholy, let him not even approach, for it
is not safe; if he is purified at least for a time, let him remain below, and
hear only the voice and the trumpet, the bare words of piety; and let him look
at the mountain smoking and struck with lightning, a threat and a wonder
together for those unable to go up. If anyone is a wicked and untamed beast
and altogether unreceptive of words of contemplation and theology, let him not
lurk in the woods maliciously and with evil habit, so that he may snatch some
doctrine or saying, leaping suddenly, and tear the healthy words with his
insults, but let him still stand far off, and withdraw from the mountain, or
he will be stoned, and crushed, and perish badly as a bad one; for true and
solid words are stones for the beastlike. Whether he be a leopard, let him die
together with his spots. Whether a lion seizing and roaring and seeking what
food he shall make of our souls or words; whether a swine trampling the fair
and translucent pearls of the truth; whether an Arabian and alien wolf, or
even sharper than these in sophisms; whether a fox, some deceitful and
faithless soul, and now one thing now another, conforming itself to times and
needs, which dead and stinking bodies nourish, or small vineyards, the great
ones having escaped; or some other of the flesh-eaters, and rejected by the
law, and not clean for eating and enjoyment. For the Word, having withdrawn
from these, wishes thus to be inscribed on solid and stone tablets, and these
on both sides, because of both the apparent of the law and the hidden; the one
for the many who remain below, the other for the few who reach above. 3 What
is this that I have suffered, O friends and initiates and fellow-lovers of the
truth? I was running as if I would grasp God, and thus I went up upon the
mountain, and I parted the cloud, having come inside away from matter and the
material things, and having gathered myself into myself as far as possible.
But when I looked, I scarcely saw the hinder parts of God; and this, by the
rock


being covered, by the Word of God incarnate for us; and having peered a
little, not the first and unmixed nature, known to itself, I mean to the
Trinity, and as much as remains inside the first veil and is covered by the
cherubim, but as much as is last and reaches to us. And this is, as far as I
know, the majesty in the creatures and in the things put forth and
administered by him, or, as the divine David names it, magnificence. For these
are the hinder parts of God, as many indications of him as come after that
one, just as the shadows and images of the sun upon waters show the sun to
weak eyes, since it is not possible to look at it itself, it overcoming sense
by the unmixedness of the light. Thus then you will theologize, even if you
are Moses and a god to Pharaoh, even if you reach as far as the third heaven
according to Paul, and hear unspeakable words; even if you become above that,
having been deemed worthy of some angelic or archangelic station and rank. For
even if something is wholly heavenly, even if superheavenly, and much higher
than us in nature, and nearer to God, it is farther from God and from perfect
grasp than it rises above our composite and low and downward-weighing mixture.
4 We must begin then thus again. To conceive God is hard; to express him
impossible, as one of the theologians among the Greeks philosophized, not
without art as it seems to me, so that he may seem to have grasped him by
saying it is hard, and may escape refutation by the inexpressible. But to
express him is impossible, as my argument goes, and to conceive him still more
impossible. For what has been conceived speech might perhaps indicate, if not
moderately, yet dimly at least, to one whose ears are not altogether corrupted
and sluggish in understanding. But to encompass so great a thing with the
understanding is altogether impossible and impracticable, not only for the
lazy and those who stoop below, but even for the very lofty and God-loving,
and likewise for every generated nature, and for those whom this gloom and
this thick little flesh obstruct toward the comprehension of the true. And I
do not know whether it is not so even for the natures above and intellectual,
which through being near God, and being lit by the whole light, might perhaps
also be made clearer, if not altogether, yet more perfectly and more
distinctly than we, and some more or less than others, according to the
proportion of rank. 5 Let this then lie here; but as for us, not only the
peace of God surpasses every mind and grasp, nor as many things as are stored
up in promises for the righteous, things neither visible to eyes, nor audible
to ears, nor contemplable by understanding, even a little, nor the accurate
comprehension of creation; for be persuaded that of this too you have only the
shadows, when you hear: I shall see the heavens, the works of your fingers,
moon and stars, and the fixed reason in them; as not seeing now, but going to
see at some time; but much before these the nature above these, and from which
these are, is ungraspable and incomprehensible; I mean, not that it is, but
what it is. For our preaching is not empty, nor our faith vain, nor is this
what we dogmatize; lest again you take our fairness as a beginning of atheism
and slander, and lift yourself up as if we confessed ignorance. For it differs
very much, being persuaded that something is, from knowing what in the world
this is. 6 For that God is, and the productive and sustaining cause of all
things, both sight is a teacher, and the natural law; the one striking upon
the things seen, both those well fixed and those that travel, and unmovedly,
so to speak, moved and carried; the other through the things seen and ordered
syllogizing their leader. For how would this all even have come to subsist, or
cohere, if God had not both given being to all things and held them together?
For neither does anyone seeing a kithara most beautifully wrought and its
harmony and good order, or the kitharody itself


hearing, think of anything other than the craftsman of the kithara and the
kithara-player, and will run up to him in thought, even if he happens to be
unknown to the eyes. Thus also for us the productive is clear, and that which
moves and keeps the things made, even if it is not encompassed by thought; and
very mindless is the one who does not go forward as far as these things
willingly and follow the natural proofs. But not even this is God, whatever we
have imagined, or formed, or speech has sketched. And if anyone ever came into
a conception of this even to some extent, what is the proof? Who has arrived
thus at the last of wisdom? Who has ever been deemed worthy of so great a
gift? Who has thus opened the mouth of the understanding and drawn in spirit,
so as to grasp God by the Spirit that searches all things and knows even the
depths of God, and no longer need what is ahead, already having the last
object of desire, toward which every way of life of the lofty and every
thought hastens? 7 For what in the world will you suppose the divine to be, if
you trust all the rational approaches? Or toward what will the argument lead
you when tested, O you most philosophical and most theological and boasting in
the measureless? Is it a body? And how the infinite, and unbounded, and
unshaped, and intangible, and invisible? Or are these also bodies? What
license; for this is not the nature of bodies. Or a body, but not these? What
thickness; so that the divine may have nothing more than we. For how is it
worshipful, if circumscribed? Or how will it escape being composed of elements
and being resolved into them again, or even being dissolved altogether? For
composition is a beginning of strife; strife of separation; and that of
dissolution; and dissolution is altogether alien to God and to the first
nature. Therefore not separation, lest there be dissolution; nor strife, lest
there be separation; nor composition, lest there be strife; for this reason
not a body, lest there be composition. The argument going up from the last
things to the first thus stops. 8 And how will it be saved that God goes
through all things and fills all things, according to: Do I not fill heaven
and earth? says the Lord, and: The Spirit of the Lord has filled the inhabited
world, if the one would circumscribe, and the other be circumscribed? For
either he will pass through a void of the all, and all things will be gone for
us, so that God may be insulted, both having become a body, and not having as
many things as he has made; or he will be a body in bodies, which is
impossible; or he will be plaited and set alongside, as whatever of liquids
mix, and the one will cut, and by the other he will be cut, which is both more
absurd than the Epicurean atoms and more old-wifely; and thus the argument
about the body will fall apart for us, and will have no body, nor any
solidity. And if we shall say it is immaterial, if it is the fifth, as it
seemed to some, and carried in the circular motion, let there be some
immaterial and fifth body, and if they wish, even bodiless, according to their
autonomous motion and fiction of the argument; for I will not differ about
this now. But in what respect will he be among the things moved and carried,
not to speak of the insult, if the maker will be moved with the same things as
the things made, and the carrier with the things carried, if indeed they will
grant even this? And what is it that moves this again? And what is it that
moves the all? And that, what? And what again that? And this to infinity. And
how is he not altogether in a place, if indeed he is carried? And if they
shall say something else besides the fifth, if it is angelic, from where is it
that angels are bodies, and what are these? And how much would God be above an
angel, whose minister is an angel? And if above these, again a swarm of bodies
without reason has been introduced, and a depth of nonsense, able to stand
nowhere. 9 Thus then God is not a body for us. For neither has anyone of the
God-inspired yet said this or accepted it, nor is it the argument of our
court. It remains then to suppose him bodiless. But if bodiless, not even this
yet is representative and comprehensive of the essence, just as neither the
unbegotten, and the beginningless, and the unchangeable, and the
incorruptible, and as many things as are said to be about God or around God.
For what does it belong to him being, according to nature and hypostasis, not
to have a beginning, nor to go out of himself, nor to be bounded? But it
remains to comprise the whole being


to philosophize further and to examine further for the one who truly has the
mind of God and is more perfect in contemplation. For as it is not enough to
say body, or to have been generated, toward presenting and making clear also
the that, about which these are, but one must also say the subject of these,
if the thing conceived is going to be presented perfectly and sufficiently;
for this embodied and generated and corrupted thing is either a human or an ox
or a horse; so neither will he who busies himself about the nature of the
being stop there as far as saying what he is not, but he must, besides saying
what he is not, also say what he is, inasmuch as it is also easier to grasp
one thing than to deny all things one by one, so that from both the denial of
the things he is not, and the positing of that which he is, the thing
conceived may be comprised. But he who says what he is not, while keeping
silent what he is, does something similar, as if when asked how many twice
five is he should say that it is not two, nor three, nor four, nor five, nor
twenty, nor thirty, nor any, to speak concisely, of the numbers within the
decad or the decimal numbers; but should not say that it is ten, nor fix the
mind of the questioner upon what is sought. For it is much easier and shorter
to make clear from what it is as many things as it is not, than from taking
away what it is not to indicate what it is. Or this at least is clear to
everyone. 10 And since the divine is bodiless for us, let us examine a little
further: whether this is nowhere, or is somewhere? For if nowhere, one of the
overly examining might ask, how it would even be. For if the non-being is
nowhere, that which is nowhere is perhaps not even being. And if it is
somewhere, altogether since it is either in the all or above the all. But if
in the all, either in some part, or everywhere. And if in some part, it will
be circumscribed by the lesser, the some; and if everywhere, by a greater and
much other, I mean the contained of the containing, if the all is going to be
encompassed by the all, and there is to be no place free of circumscription.
These things, if in the all. And where before the all came to be? For this too
is not small toward perplexity. And if above the all, was there then nothing
marking it off from the all? And where is that which is above this? And how
was the overtopping and the overtopped conceived, there being no boundary
cutting these and marking them off? Or must there altogether be the middle,
and that by which the all is bounded and that which is above the all? And what
else is this than place, which we fled? And I do not yet say that the divine
is altogether circumscribable, even if graspable by thought; for grasp too is
one form of circumscription. 11 For what reason then have I gone through these
things, and perhaps more busily than according to the hearing of the many, and
according to the now prevailing type of discourses, which having dishonored
the noble and simple has introduced the crooked and riddle-like; so that from
the fruits the tree is known, I mean that the one working such doctrines is
darkness from the gloom of the things said? Not so that I myself may seem to
say paradoxes, and appear excessive in wisdom, plaiting bonds and loosing
things held; this indeed the great wonder of Daniel; but that I might make
clear that which the argument set out from the beginning to say. And what was
this? That the divine is not graspable by human thought, nor is the whole of
it, as much as it is, to be imagined; and this neither through envy, for envy
is far from the divine nature, at least the passionless and only good and
sovereign, and especially of its own creatures concerning the most honorable;
for what is there for the Word before the rational? since even to have come to
subsist itself is of the extreme goodness; nor for honor of himself and glory
of the full, so that by the unattainable he may have the honorable and the
venerable. For this is altogether sophistic and alien, not only of God, but
not even of a moderately decent human, who is conscious of something right in
himself, to procure the first place from preventing others. 12 But if also for
other causes, those nearer to God would know, and the overseers and
contemplators of his unsearchable judgments, if indeed there are some so great
in virtue, and walking in the tracks of the abyss, as the saying goes. As much
then as we have grasped, measuring the hard to contemplate with small
measures, perhaps, lest by the


ease of acquisition the loss of what was acquired also become easy; for what
is acquired with toil is wont to be held more, and what is acquired easily to
be spat out most quickly, as able to be taken again; and thus the not being
ready to hand of the benefaction is established as a benefaction, for those
who have a mind at least. And perhaps, so that we may not suffer the same as
the fallen Lucifer, from containing the whole light to rear the neck against
the Lord almighty, and to fall from the lifting up a fall most pitiable of
all. And perhaps, so that there may be some further prize from there of love
of toil and of a bright life for those purified here and patient toward the
desired. For this reason this bodily gloom stands in the middle of us and God,
as the cloud of old of the Egyptians and the Hebrews. And this is perhaps what
he set as darkness his hiding-place, our thickness, through which few and a
little peer. Let those then philosophize this to whom it is a care, and let
them go up as far as possible of the examination. But that at least is known
to us the prisoners of the earth, as the divine Jeremiah says, and those
wrapped in this thick little flesh, that just as it is impossible to overstep
one's own shadow, even for the one very much in haste, for it always
anticipates as much as it is caught, or for the sight to approach the visible
things without the light and air in the middle, or for the swimming nature to
slip outside the waters, so it is impracticable for those in bodies to come to
be altogether with the things thought without the bodily things. For always
something of ours will fall in between, even if the mind, having separated
itself as much as possible from the things seen, and having come to be by
itself, attempts to strike upon the kindred and invisible things. And you will
know thus. 13 Are not spirit and fire and light, and love and wisdom and
righteousness, and mind and word, and such things, the appellations of the
first nature? What then? Will you conceive spirit without motion and pouring?
Or fire outside of matter, and of the upward motion, and of its own color and
shape? Or light not mixed with air and let go from what as it were generates
and illuminates? And what mind? Not the one in another, whose motions are the
thoughts, whether at rest or put forth? And what word besides the one keeping
quiet in us, or being poured? For I shrink from saying, being dissolved. And
if also wisdom, what besides the habit, and that in theorems, whether divine
or also human? And righteousness and love, not praiseworthy dispositions, and
the one antagonist of injustice, the other of hatred, being stretched and
slackened, coming to be and departing, and altogether making us and altering
us, as colors do bodies? Or must we, having stood off from these, see the
divine itself by itself from these, as far as possible, gathering some partial
imagination from the likenesses? What then is the device from these and not
these? Or how all these things, and each perfectly, the one in nature
incomposite and incomparable? Thus our mind grows weary of going out of the
bodily things, and of conversing with the bodiless things naked, while it
looks with its own weakness at the things above power. Since every rational
nature desires God and the first cause; but is unable to grasp, for the
reasons I said. And growing weary with longing, and as it were convulsing, and
not bearing the loss, it makes a second voyage, either to look toward the
things seen, and to make some of these God, knowing badly, for what of the
visible things is higher and more Godlike than the one seeing, and how much,
so that the one may be worshipping, the other worshipped? or through the
beauty of the things seen and the good order to recognize God, and to use as a
guide the sight of the things above sight, but not to be deprived of God
through the magnificence of the things seen. 14 From this some the sun, some
the moon, some a multitude of stars, some heaven itself together with these,
to which they have also given to lead the all according to the quality or
quantity of the motion; some the elements, earth, water, air, fire, because of
the needful, without which it is not even possible for human life to cohere;
and some whatever each of the visible things they happened upon


they worshipped, setting up as gods the fairest of what they saw. And there
are those who also images and molded things, first of their own, the more
passionate and more bodily, and honoring those who have departed with
memorials; then also of strangers, those after them and far from them, by
ignorance of the first nature, and by following of the handed-down honor, as
lawful and necessary, since custom confirmed by time was thought law. And I
think some also serving power, and having praised strength, and having
wondered at beauty, made the one honored a god in time, taking also some myth
as an ally of the deception. 15 And the more passionate of them even thought
the passions gods, or honored them with gods, wrath, and bloodshed, and
wantonness, and drunkenness, and I do not know what else of the things like
these, having found this neither fair nor just as an apology of their own
sins. And some they left below, some they hid under the earth, this alone
wisely, and some they led up into heaven. O the ridiculous allotment. Then
having attached to each of the molded things some name of gods or daemons,
according to the license and autonomy of the error, and having set up statues,
of which also the costliness is a bait, with bloods and fat-smoke, and some
even with acts very shameful, madnesses and human-killings, they thought to
honor this. For such it was fitting that the honors also of such gods should
be. And already even with beasts, and four-footed things, and reptiles, and
the most shameful and most ridiculous of these, they insulted themselves, and
bringing they added to these the glory of God; so that it is not easy to judge
whether one must despise more the worshippers or the worshipped. And perhaps
even much more the servers, because being of a rational nature, and having
received the grace of God, they set forward the worse as better. And this is
the sophism of the evil one, having used the fair for the bad, such as are
most of his malices. For having taken their longing wandering according to the
search for God, that he might pull the power around to himself, and steal the
desire, as one leading a blind man by the hand who longs for some road, he has
dashed some down one place and some another, and scattered them into one pit
of death and destruction. 16 These then thus; but the Word having received us
longing for God, and not enduring the unled and unsteered, then striking upon
the things seen and meeting those from the beginning, neither stopped as far
as these, for it was not of reason to give the leadership to those of equal
honor according to sense, and through these leads toward that which is above
these, and through which being is present to these. For what is it that
ordered the heavenly and the earthly, both as many as through air and as many
as upon water, or rather the things before these, heaven, and earth, and air,
and the nature of water? Who mixed these and divided them? What is the
communion of these toward one another, and congenital unity, and common
breathing? For I praise the one who has said it, even if he is alien. What is
it that has moved these and leads the unceasing and unhindered motion? Is it
not the craftsman of these, and having put a reason in all, according to which
the all is carried and conducted? And who is the craftsman of these? Is it not
the one who made these and brought them into being? For such a power is not to
be given to the spontaneous. For let coming to be belong to the spontaneous.
Of whom is the ordering? And this too, if it seems good, let us grant. Of whom
is the keeping and guarding according to the reasons by which it first came to
subsist? Of some other, or of the spontaneous? Of another clearly besides the
spontaneous. And this, what else in the world except God? Thus the word from
God, and congenital to all, and first law in us, and joined to all, has led us
up to God from the things seen. And now let us speak beginning again. 17 God,
what in the world he is in nature and essence, neither has any of humans ever
found, nor will he find. But if he will find it sometime, let this be sought
and philosophized among those who wish. And he will find, as my argument goes,
whenever the


godlike and divine thing, I mean our mind and reason, mixes with what is its
own, and the image goes up to the archetype, of which it now has the desire.
And this seems to me to be the thing much philosophized, that we shall someday
know even as we have been known. But as for now being, a certain short outflow
is all that reaches to us, and as it were a small radiance of a great light.
So that even if someone knew God, or has been witnessed to have known, he knew
so much, as to appear more lightlike than another not equally illumined. And
the exceeding was thought perfect, not by the truth, but being measured by the
power of the neighbor. 18 For this reason Enoch hoped to call upon the Lord.
Hope was the thing accomplished, and this not of knowledge, but of calling
upon. And Enoch was translated, but it is not yet clear whether having
comprised the nature of God or going to comprise it. And of Noah the fair
thing is the well-pleasing, of the one entrusted even to save a whole world
from waters, or seeds of a world, in a small wood fleeing the flood. And
Abraham was justified from faith, the great patriarch, and sacrifices a
strange sacrifice and antitype of the great. But he did not see God as God,
but fed him as a human, and was praised, having reverenced as much as he
grasped. And Jacob imagined some high ladder, and an ascent of angels, and
anoints a pillar mystically, perhaps so as to indicate the stone anointed for
us, and gives to a place the appellation Form of God in honor of the one seen,
and wrestles with God as with a human, whatever in the world the wrestling of
God toward a human is, or perhaps a comparison of human virtue toward God, and
he bears symbols of the wrestling upon the body, showing the defeat of
generated nature, and as a prize of piety he receives the change of the
appellation, having been renamed from Jacob Israel, this indeed the great and
honorable name; but that neither he himself nor anyone above him until today
of the twelve tribes, of whom he was father, has boasted, that he contained
the whole nature or sight of God. 19 And for Elijah neither a violent wind,
nor fire, nor earthquake, as you hear of the history, but some slight breeze
sketched the presence of God, and these not nature; for which Elijah? whom
even a chariot of fire leads up toward heaven, making clear the above-human of
the righteous. And Manoah the judge earlier, and Peter the disciple later, how
have you not wondered at them? the one not even bearing the sight of the God
imagined, and for this reason saying, We have perished, O woman, we have seen
God; as if not even a divine imagination is containable for humans, much less
nature; and the other not even admitting the appearing Christ to the boat, and
for this reason sending him away. And yet Peter was hotter than the others
toward recognition of Christ, and for this reason blessed, and entrusted with
the greatest things. And what would you say about Isaiah, and Ezekiel the
overseer of the greatest things, and the remaining prophets? of whom the one
saw the Lord Sabaoth sitting upon a throne of glory, and this one circled and
praised and hidden by the six-winged seraphim, and himself being purified by
the coal, and being fitted for the prophecy; and the other also describes the
chariot of God the cherubim, and the throne above them, and the firmament
above it, and the one imagined in the firmament, and some voices, and
impulses, and acts, and whether these were some daytime imagination,
contemplable only by the saints, or a true sight of night, or an impression of
the ruling part associating with the things about to be as present, or some
other unspeakable form of prophecy, I cannot say; but the God of the prophets
knows, and those being worked upon in such things. Yet neither these about
whom the discourse is, nor any other of those according to them, stood in the
substance and essence of the Lord, according to what is written, nor saw or
declared the nature of God.


20 But to Paul, if the things were utterable which the third heaven furnished,
and the progress as far as that or ascent or taking up, perhaps we would have
known something more about God, if indeed this was the mystery of the rapture.
But since they were unspeakable, let them be honored by us too with silence.
And let us hear this much of Paul himself saying, that we know in part, and we
prophesy in part. These and such things the one not a private person in
knowledge confesses, the one threatening a proof of the Christ speaking in
him, the great champion and teacher of the truth. Wherefore also he sets all
the knowledge below as nothing above the mirrors and the riddles, as standing
in small likenesses of the truth. And if I do not seem to some overly
excessive and busy examining such things, perhaps they were no other things
than these, which cannot now be borne, which the Word himself hinted, as going
to be borne someday and made clear; and which not even the world below would
be able to contain, John the forerunner of the Word, the great voice of the
truth, determined. 21 Every truth then and every argument is hard to
conjecture and hard to contemplate; and as with a small tool we craft great
things, hunting the knowledge of the beings with human wisdom, and striking
upon the intelligibles with the senses, or not without senses, by which we are
carried about and wander, and we do not have it, encountering the things naked
with the mind naked, to approach the truth somewhat more, and for the mind to
be formed by the grasps. But the argument about God, by how much more perfect,
by so much harder to reach, and having more objections and more laborious
solutions. For every thing that stands in the way, even if it is very brief,
has checked the course of the argument and hindered it, and cut the motion
forward; as those who with the reins suddenly pull aside horses being carried,
and overturn them by the unexpectedness of the shake. Thus Solomon, who was
