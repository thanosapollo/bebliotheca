* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Many deeds having been accomplished by your ancestors, in which not they alone
then were able, but you also now are able to take pride, and many trophies
having been raised both for all Hellas in common and privately for the city
itself, in which she contended alone against the other Greeks and against the
barbarian, there is no work so great nor such valor, with which it is not
possible for the other cities also to compete. For some of these they
accomplished with you, and some privately. And that I may not, after recalling
them, then by comparing be thought either to prefer one city to another in the
things they dispute, or, looking to advantage, like the orators, to praise
more scantily those that fall short, this alone I wish to say on your behalf,
for which we have nothing equal to discover among the other Greeks, handed
down to us from ancient fame. You took the hegemony from the Lacedaemonians
not by force but by reputation for justice, and the laws among you reared
Aristides the Just. And yet these proofs, brilliant as they are, you confirmed
nevertheless, I think, by still more brilliant deeds. For to seem just might
perhaps happen even falsely to someone, and perhaps it is not strange that
among many worthless men one should prove worthy. For is not some Deioces also
hymned among the Medes, and Abaris among the Hyperboreans, and Anacharsis
among the Scythians? About whom this was wonderful, that though they were born
among the most unjust nations they nevertheless honored justice, two of them
truly, and the one pretending for the sake of need. But a whole people and
city lovers of just deeds and words, outside of yours, is not easy to find. I
wish to remind you of one of the deeds among you, though they are many. For
when Themistocles after the Median wars was intending to introduce a proposal
to burn secretly the dockyards of the Greeks, then not daring to speak to the
people, but agreeing to trust the secret to one man, whichever the people
should elect by vote, the people put forward Aristides; and he, having heard
the proposal, concealed what was said, but brought out to the people that
nothing was more advantageous nor more unjust than the plan; and the city
voted it down at once and declined it, very magnanimously, by Zeus, and in the
manner that was fitting for men reared under the witness of the most prudent
goddess.

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Therefore if these things were among you of old, and from that time even to
you still there is preserved of the ancestors' virtue as it were some small
spark, it is reasonable that you should not look to the magnitude of the
things done, nor whether someone as if flying through air walked through the
land with impossible speed and untiring strength, but consider by whom these
things have been accomplished with justice, and then if he appear acting with
justice, all of you perhaps both privately and publicly will praise him, but
if he has neglected justice he would reasonably be dishonored among you. For
nothing is so much a brother to wisdom as justice. Those therefore who
dishonor this you would justly drive out also as impious toward the goddess
among you. I wish then to report to you the things concerning myself, though
you are not ignorant of them, nevertheless, so that if anything has escaped
notice (and it is likely that some things even of those that most of all ought
to be known to all) may become known to you and through you to the other
Greeks. Let no one then suppose me to be talking nonsense or babbling, if I
should attempt to make some speeches about things that have happened before
the eyes of all, not only long ago but also a little earlier; for I wish no
one to be ignorant of anything of mine, though it is likely that different
things escape different people; and I will begin first from my own ancestors.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And that our father's line has sprung from the same source as Constantius's
father's line is clear. For our two fathers were brothers on the father's
side. And being so near kinsmen, what this most humane emperor did to us:
having killed without trial six cousins of mine and of his own, and my father,
his uncle, and besides another common paternal uncle and my eldest brother,
and having wished to kill me and another brother of mine, but finally imposing
exile, from which he released me, but from the other, a little before the
slaughter, he stripped the name of Caesar: why must I now, as if from a
tragedy, measure out the unspeakable things? For he repented, they say, and
was terribly stung, and he thinks he is unfortunate in childlessness from
this, and he supposes that he fares unsuccessfully against the Persian enemies
from these things. These things the people about the court then used to
chatter, and also my late brother Gallus, hearing this name now for the first
time; for having killed him contrary to the laws he did not even allow him to
share in the ancestral tombs nor thought him worthy of holy memory.

As I said then, they used to say so much and indeed even persuaded us that he
had done some things being deceived, and others yielding to force and the
disturbances of an undisciplined and turbulent army. So much they sang to us,
shut up in a certain farm among those in Cappadocia, allowing no one to
approach, having recalled him from exile at Tralles, and taking me, still
quite a boy, away from the schools. How could I speak here of the six years
which we spent living on another's estate, like those kept in the forts among
the Persians, with no stranger coming to us nor any of our old acquaintances
permitted to visit us, living shut off from every serious study and from all
free intercourse, being fed among the splendid households and exercising with
our own slaves as if they were companions? For none of our age-mates came or
was permitted.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

From there I was with difficulty released fortunately, thanks to the gods, but
my brother was confined in the court unfortunately, if ever anyone was. For if
anything savage and harsh appeared about his character, this grew from the
mountain nurture. Therefore I think the one who forcibly gave us this nurture
justly has this blame also, from which the gods through philosophy showed me
clean and free of the disease, but to him no one granted it. For immediately
when he had come from the fields into the palace, as soon as he had put the
purple garment on him, he began at once to envy and did not stop until he had
destroyed him, not even content with stripping off the purple garment. And yet
he was at least worthy of life, if he did not appear fit to be emperor. But he
had to be deprived of this also. I grant it, provided he first gave an
account, as criminals do. For the law does not forbid the one who has bound
robbers to kill them, yet say that those stripped of the honors they had, and
become private men from magistrates, must be destroyed without trial. For what
if he had been able to show the authors of the faults? For certain letters had
been given to him, Heracles, how many accusations they had against him, at
which that man, being angry, more unrestrainedly and least of all like an
emperor let go his wrath, yet he had done nothing not even worthy of not
living. How so? Is this not the common law for all men, Greeks and barbarians
alike, to defend oneself against those who begin injustice? But perhaps he
defended himself more bitterly. Yet not entirely outside of what is
reasonable; for it is likely that one may do something even to an enemy under
anger, as has been said before. But to gratify one eunuch, the chamberlain,
and besides the steward of the cooks, he handed over his cousin, the Caesar,
who had become his sister's husband, the father of his niece, whose sister he
himself had formerly married, toward whom he had so many claims of the kindred
gods, to be killed by his bitterest enemies; and me he released with
difficulty after dragging me this way and that for seven whole months and
keeping me under guard, so that if some one of the gods, wishing me to be
saved, had not then furnished me the noble and good Eusebia well-disposed, I
would not even have escaped his hands then. And yet by the gods my brother had
done nothing even appearing to me in a dream; for I was not with him nor did I
visit nor walk to him, and I wrote seldom and about few things. So having
escaped from there I was going gladly to my mother's hearth; for I had nothing
paternal nor had I acquired from so much as my father was likely to have
possessed, not the smallest clod, not a slave, not a house; for the fair
Constantius inherited in my place the whole paternal estate, and to me, as I
said, he did not give even a grunt of it; but even to my brother he gave
little of the paternal things, having taken from him all the maternal.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

As many things then as he did toward me before sharing with me the most august
name, but in deed throwing me into the bitterest and harshest slavery, even if
not all, at least most of them you have nevertheless heard. When I was going
at last to the hearth, being saved gladly and with difficulty, a certain
sycophant appeared about Sirmium, who stitched up troubles for those there as
intending a revolution; you know of course by report Africanus and Marinus;
nor did Felix escape you, nor as many things as were done concerning the men.
But when this matter was reported to him, and Dynamius suddenly, another
sycophant, from the Celts announced that Silvanus was all but about to appear
as his enemy, being altogether afraid and frightened he sends for me at once,
and having ordered me to withdraw a little into Hellas he called me from there
again to himself, having never seen me before except once in Cappadocia and
once in Italy, Eusebia having contended that I might take courage for my own
safety. And yet I inhabited the same city with him for six months, and indeed
he even promised to see me again. But the eunuch hateful to the gods, his
faithful chamberlain, became my benefactor even unwillingly without my
knowing; for he did not allow me to meet him often, perhaps not even when he
wished, but he was the chief cause; for he was afraid that if some familiarity
arose between us I might then be loved and, appearing faithful, be entrusted
with something.

When I arrived then for the first time from Hellas, immediately through the
eunuchs about the service the late Eusebia was exceedingly kind to me. And a
little later when this man came (for indeed the things concerning Silvanus had
been accomplished), entrance into the court is given at last, and the
so-called Thessalian persuasion-by-force is thrown around. For when I firmly
refused the association in the palace, they gathered as in a barber's shop and
shear the beard, and they put a cloak on me and dress me up, as they then
supposed, a very ridiculous soldier; for none of the adornment of those scum
suited me; and I walked not looking around and swaggering as they did but
looking at the ground, as I had been accustomed by the pedagogue who reared
me. Then therefore I furnished them laughter, and a little later suspicion,
then so great an envy blazed up.

But here I must not omit those things, how I consented, how I accepted
becoming under the same roof with those whom I knew had ruined my whole
family, and suspected would not long after plot against me also. How many
fountains of tears I let go and what laments, stretching my hands toward the
acropolis among you when I was summoned, and entreating Athena to save the
suppliant and not to give him up, many of those among you who have seen are
witnesses for me, and the goddess herself before the others, that I even asked
death of her at Athens before that journey. That the goddess did not betray
the suppliant nor give him up, she showed by deeds; for everywhere with me she
also stationed the guards from every side, taking angels from Helios and
Selene.

And something of this sort also happened. Having come to Mediolanum I lived in
a certain suburb. There Eusebia sent to me often, showing kindness and bidding
me write and take courage about whatever I might need. Having written to her a
letter, or rather a supplication having such oaths: /So may you use children
as heirs; so may God give you this and that; send me home as quickly as
possible,/ I suspected that it was not safe to send letters into the palace to
an emperor's wife. I entreated the gods by night to show me whether I must
send the writing to the empress; and they threatened, if I should send it, a
most shameful death. That I write these things truly, I call all the gods as
witnesses. The letters therefore I held back from sending for this reason. And
from that night a reasoning entered me, which perhaps it is also worth your
hearing. /Now,/ I said, /I am intending to array myself against the gods, and
I have thought it better to counsel for myself than those who know all things.
And yet human prudence, looking only to the present, would gladly and with
difficulty hit even the briefly unerring. Therefore no one counsels either
about the things to the thirtieth year or about things already happened; for
the one is superfluous, the other impossible; but about the things in hand and
those of which there are already some beginnings and seeds. But the prudence
among the gods, looking to the longest, or rather to everything, both
indicates rightly and does the better; for they themselves are causes of the
things that will be just as of the things that are. Therefore it is likely
that they know about the present things./ For a while then the second judgment
seemed to me according to this wiser than the former. But looking to justice I
said at once: /Then you are angry if any of your possessions should deprive
you of its own use or even run away when called, whether it happen to be a
horse or a sheep or a little ox, but wishing to be a man, not even of the herd
nor of the rabble but of the decent and moderate, you deprive the gods of
yourself and do not permit them to use you for whatever they wish? See that
besides acting too unwisely you also act neglectfully of the just things
toward the gods. And where is courage, and what is it? Ridiculous. At any rate
you are ready both to fawn and to flatter from fear of death, when it is
possible to throw all things down and permit the gods to act as they wish,
having divided with them the care of yourself, just as Socrates also thought
right, and to do the things in your power as may be possible, but to make the
whole depend on them, and to possess nothing and seize nothing, but to receive
simply the things given by them./

This judgment I thought not only safe but fitting for a moderate man, since
the things of the gods also indicated this way; for to throw oneself, from
caution about future plots, into a shameful and obvious danger seemed to me
terribly turbulent; I yielded and obeyed. And the name of Caesar and the
little cloak were quickly thrown around me; but the slavery upon this and the
fear hanging every day over the soul itself, Heracles, how great and of what
kind: bolts of doors, doorkeepers, the hands of the servants being searched,
lest someone bring me a little letter from friends, a foreign attendance; with
difficulty I was able to bring into the court four of my own servants, two
quite small boys and two larger, to attend me more familiarly, of whom one
alone both shared the secrets toward the gods and as far as possible
cooperated secretly; and he had been entrusted with the keeping of my books,
being the only one of my many companions and faithful friends, one physician,
who also, because he was a friend undetected, went abroad with me. And I so
feared these things and was so timid toward them that even when many of my
friends wished to come in to me I prevented them, very unwillingly, desiring
to see them but hesitating to become a cause of misfortunes to them and to
myself. But these things are from outside; the following are in the affairs
themselves.

Having given me three hundred and sixty soldiers he sent me into the
overturned nation of the Celts, already in the middle of winter, not so much
as commander of the armies there as to obey the generals there. For it had
been written to them and expressly commanded to watch not the enemies so much
as me, so that I might not attempt some revolution. These things having
happened in the way I said, about the summer solstice he permits me to go to
the camps to carry around his costume and image; for indeed this also had been
said and written, that he gives the Gauls not an emperor but the one who would
bring his own image to them.

And when the first year of command had not gone badly, as you have heard, and
a serious thing had been accomplished, having returned again to winter
quarters I came into the utmost danger. For I was not permitted to assemble an
army; for another was master of this; and I myself being shut off with a few,
then being asked for aid by the neighboring cities, having given them most of
what I had, I myself was left alone. Those things then were done thus at that
time. And when the commander of the armies also, having come under suspicion
with him, was taken away and released from the command, seeming not very
suitable, I myself was thought a least serious and clever general, since I had
shown myself gentle and moderate. For I did not think I ought to fight against
the yoke or to counter-command, unless perhaps I saw something of the
extremely dangerous things either being overlooked that ought to happen or
happening that ought not to happen at all. And when some had treated me
unbecomingly once and a second time, I thought I ought to honor myself with
silence, and for the rest I carried around the cloak and the image; for of
these I then supposed I had been shown master.

From which Constantius, thinking the Celtic affairs would advance a little,
but not come to so great a change, gives me the command of the armies at the
beginning of spring. And I campaign when the grain is at its height, very many
Germans dwelling fearlessly about the sacked cities among the Celts. The
number of the cities is about forty-five, the walls plundered, apart from the
towers and the lesser forts. And of all the land on this side of the Rhine
which the barbarians occupied, the size is as much as the river, beginning
from its own sources, encompasses as far as the Ocean; and those dwelling
nearest us were three hundred stades from the bank of the Rhine, and still
triple this was the breadth left desert by the plunder, where the Celts were
not even permitted to pasture their cattle, and some cities empty of
inhabitants, beside which the barbarians were not yet settled.

Finding Galatia in this state I recovered the city of Agrippina on the Rhine,
captured some ten months before, and the fort Argentoratum nearby toward the
foothills of the Vosges itself, and I fought not without glory. Perhaps such a
battle has come even to you. There, the gods having given me the king of the
enemies as a prisoner, I did not begrudge Constantius the success. And yet if
I was not permitted to triumph, I was master to slaughter the enemy, and
indeed to lead him through all Celtica and display him to the cities and as it
were revel in the misfortunes of Chnodomarius. I thought I ought to do none of
these things, but sent him at once to Constantius himself, then returning from
the Quadi and Sauromatae. It happened therefore that I having contended, and
he having merely marched and met friendly with the nations dwelling by the
Ister, not we but he triumphed.

Then after this a second year and a third, and all the barbarians were driven
from Galatia, and most of the cities were recovered, and very many ships were
brought up from Britain. I brought up a fleet of six hundred ships, of which I
built the four hundred in not even ten whole months and brought them all into
the Rhine, no small work because of the barbarians lying near and dwelling
nearby. Florentius at any rate thought this so impossible that he promised to
pay the barbarians two thousand pounds of silver as a fee for the passage, and
Constantius, having learned about this (for he communicated with him about the
gift), wrote to me ordering me to do the same, if it should not appear
altogether shameful to me. And how was it not shameful, when it appeared such
to Constantius, who was exceedingly accustomed to court the barbarians?
Nothing however was given to them; but having campaigned against them, the
gods defending me and standing by, I received a portion of the nation of the
Salii, and I drove out the Chamavi, capturing many cattle and women with
little children. And I so frightened all and made them cower before my
approach that I immediately took hostages and furnished the grain-transport a
safe conveyance.

It is long to enumerate all and to write the particulars of as many things as
I did in four years; but the heads: I crossed the Rhine a third time while
still Caesar; I demanded back from the barbarians beyond the Rhine twenty
thousand captives; from two contests and one siege, having taken a thousand, I
captured them alive, not the useless age but men in their prime; I sent to
Constantius four numbers of the strongest infantry, three others of the
lesser, two most honored squadrons of cavalry; I recovered cities, now indeed,
the gods willing, all, but then I had recovered a little fewer than forty. I
call Zeus to witness and all the gods, both city-protecting and kindred,
concerning my purpose toward him and my loyalty, that I have become such
toward him as I would have chosen a son to become toward me. I have honored
him then as no one of the Caesars has honored any of the emperors before. At
any rate he accuses me of nothing to this day concerning those things, even
though I spoke freely to him, but he invents ridiculous causes of anger. /You
detained Lupicinus,/ he says, /and three other men;/ whom even if I had killed
when they had plotted against me openly, he ought to have let go the anger
over those who suffered for the sake of concord. But having done them nothing
ungracious I detained them as turbulent by nature and war-makers, spending
very much on them from the public funds, but taking away nothing of what
belonged to them. See how Constantius legislates to proceed against these. For
does the one who is harsh over those who belong in no way not reproach me and
laugh at my folly, that I courted to this point the murderer of father,
brothers, cousins, of our whole common hearth and kindred, so to speak, the
executioner? And consider how even having become emperor I still dealt with
him in a courting way from what I wrote.

And of what sort I was toward him even before these things you will know from
this. Perceiving that of the things going wrong I myself would inherit the
ill-repute and the danger, but most would be worked out by others, first I
entreated, if it should seem good to him to do these things and it had been
altogether decided to address me as Caesar, to give me good and serious men as
my subordinates; but he first gave the most wicked. And when the one most
wicked obeyed even very gladly, but none of the others thought it fit, he
gives me unwillingly a man also very good, Sallustius, who because of his
virtue immediately became suspect to him. Not content with such a thing, but
looking to the difference of character and perceiving that he trusted the one
too much and paid no attention at all to the other, taking hold of his right
hand and his knees: /Of these,/ I said, /none is intimate with me nor has been
before; but knowing them from report, at your command, I consider them my
companions and friends, honoring them equally with old acquaintances. Yet it
is not just either that my affairs be entrusted to these or that theirs share
danger with us. What then do I entreat? Give us written things like laws, from
what we must abstain and as many things as you permit us to do. For it is
clear that you will praise the one who obeys and punish the one who disobeys,
even though I think most of all that no one will disobey./

As many things then as Pentadius immediately attempted to innovate, there is
no need to say; but I opposed innovating and he becomes hostile to me from
there. Then having taken another and prepared a second and a third, Paul,
Gaudentius, having hired the famous sycophants against me, he arranges that
Sallustius withdraw as a friend to me, and that Lucillianus be given as
successor at once. And a little later Florentius also was an enemy to me
because of the greedinesses which I opposed. These men persuade Constantius to
take me away from all the armies, perhaps also being somewhat stung by
jealousy of the successes, and he writes letters full of much dishonor toward
me, and threatening the Celts with devastation; for I almost need to say he
ordered the whole most warlike soldiery without distinction to be led away
from Galatia, assigning this work to Lupicinus and Gintonius, and he wrote to
me that I should oppose them in nothing.

Here however how could I tell you the works of the gods? I was intending (and
they themselves are witnesses) having thrown off all the imperial luxury and
preparation to keep quiet, and to do nothing at all. And I was waiting for
Florentius to arrive and Lupicinus; for the one was about Vienna, and the
other in the Britains. In this there was much tumult among all the private men
and the soldiers, and someone writes an anonymous writing into the city
neighboring me to these Petulantes and Celts (for so the units are named); in
which many things had been written against that man, and many lamentations
over the betrayal of the Gauls; and indeed the writer of the writing also
bewailed my dishonor. This being brought moved all those who most minded
Constantius's affairs to attack me most vigorously, so that I might already
send out the soldiers before similar things were thrown even among the other
numbers. For there was not even anyone else present of those seeming
well-disposed to me, but Nebridius, Pentadius, Decentius, the one sent by
Constantius himself for this very thing. And when I said they must still wait
for Lupicinus and Florentius, no one listened, but all said the opposite, that
it must be done, if I did not wish to add this as a proof and evidence to the
previous suspicions. Then they added that /Now if they are sent out it is your
work, but if these men arrive Constantius will credit this not to you but to
them, and you will come into blame./ They persuaded me then to write to him,
or rather they forced me; for that man is persuaded to whom it is possible
also not to be persuaded, but those to whom it is possible to force need
nothing of persuading; therefore those who have been forced are not among the
persuaded but among the compelled. We considered here what road they must
walk, there being two. I thought they ought to turn the other way, but they
again compel them to go that one, lest this very thing happening should
furnish the soldiers as it were some starting-point of sedition and become a
cause of some tumult, and then having once begun to mutiny they should throw
all things into confusion at once.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

The fear of the men seemed not altogether unreasonable.

The units came, I met them according to what was customary for them, I urged
them to keep to the road; they remained one day, until which I knew nothing of
what they had planned; let Zeus, Helios, Ares, Athena and all the gods know
that not even near did any such suspicion come to me until late afternoon
itself; but already in the evening about sunset it was reported to me, and at
once the palace was surrounded, and all were shouting, while I was still
considering what must be done and not yet strongly believing; for I happened,
my wife still being alive, to have gone up privately to rest toward the nearby
upper room. Then from there (for the wall was open) I worshipped Zeus. And
when the shout became still greater and all were in tumult in the palace, we
asked the god to give a sign. And he showed it to us and ordered us to be
persuaded and not to oppose the eagerness of the army. Yet even when these
signs also had happened to me, I did not yield readily, but I held out as long
as I was able, and I accepted neither the address nor the crown. But when
being one I was not able to master many, and the gods wishing this to happen
both spurred them on and charmed my judgment, at about the third hour, I do
not know which soldier giving me a torque, I put it on and went into the
palace, groaning from the heart itself within, as the gods know. And yet I
ought surely, trusting the god who showed the sign, to take courage; but I was
terribly ashamed and sank down, if I should seem not to have obeyed
Constantius faithfully to the end.

There being then much dejection about the palace, Constantius's friends
immediately intending to seize this opportunity stitch a plot against me at
once and distributed money to the soldiers, expecting one of two things,
either to set them at variance with one another or even to attack me
altogether openly. Someone of those assigned to my wife's procession,
perceiving it being done secretly, first reported it to me, and when he saw me
paying no attention, going out of his mind like those possessed by a god he
began to shout publicly in the marketplace: /Soldiers and strangers and
citizens, do not betray the emperor./ Then wrath falls upon the soldiers, and
all ran to the palace with their arms. And finding me alive and rejoicing like
those who have seen friends from the unexpected, they embraced and clasped me
from one side and another and carried me on their shoulders, and the affair
was somehow worth seeing, for it resembled a divine possession. And when they
had surrounded me from every side, they demanded all Constantius's friends for
punishment. How great a contest I contested wishing to save them, all the gods
know.

But how then did I deal with Constantius after this? Not even today in the
letters to him have I used the title given me by the gods, but I have written
myself Caesar, and I have persuaded the soldiers to swear to me that they
would desire nothing, if he should permit us to inhabit the Gauls without
fear, having consented to what has been done. All the units with me sent
letters to him, entreating concerning concord between us. And he instead of
these things set the barbarians upon us, and proclaimed me an enemy among
them, and paid wages so that the nation of the Gauls might be plundered, and
writing he exhorted those in Italy to watch those from the Gauls, and about

Besides these he still writes to me now as Caesar, and he has not even
undertaken ever to make a treaty with me, but he sent a certain Epictetus, a
bishop of the Gauls, as one who would give me pledges concerning my own
safety, and he chatters this through all his letters, that he will not take
away my life, but about the honor he mentions nothing. And I think his oaths
must be written, as the proverb has it, in ash, so trustworthy are they; but I
hold to the honor not only for the noble and fitting but also for the sake of
the friends' safety; and I do not yet mention the bitterness being exercised
everywhere on earth.

These things persuaded me, these appeared to me just. And first I committed
them to the gods who see and hear all things. Then having sacrificed about the
departure, and the omens having been good, on that very day on which I was
about to speak to the soldiers about the journey this way, both for my own
safety and much more for the welfare of the common things and the freedom of
all men and of the Celtic nation itself, which he has already twice given over
to the enemies, not even sparing the ancestral tombs, he who greatly courts
foreigners, I thought I ought both to take on the most powerful nations and
resources of most just money from silver mines and gold mines, and if he
should still even now be content with concord toward us, to remain within what
is now held, but if he should intend to make war and relax nothing from the
former judgment, to suffer or do whatever is dear to the gods, since it is
more shameful to appear weaker than he by cowardice of soul and ignorance of
mind than by multitude of force. For if now he should prevail by multitude, it
is not his work but of the many hands; but if he caught me waiting in the
Gauls and loving life and avoiding the danger, having cut me off from every
side, in a circle by the barbarians and in front by his own armies, both to
suffer the utmost I think was at hand and still the shame of the affairs, no
less than any penalty at least for the temperate.

Having thought these things, men of Athens, I both went through them with my
fellow-soldiers and I write to the common citizens of all the Greeks. And may
the gods who are masters of all give us their alliance to the end, as they
promised, and grant that Athens both be well treated by us as far as power
allows and have for ever such emperors as will most of all and especially
revere and love her.
