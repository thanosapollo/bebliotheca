* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I would never have put into writing the life of Heliogabalus Antoninus, who
was also called Varius, lest anyone know that such a man had been a prince of
the Romans, had not this same office already been held by a Caligula, a Nero,
and a Vitellius.

But since the same earth bears both poisons and grain and other things that
heal, both serpents and tame beasts, a careful reader will make his own
compensation when he has read of Augustus, Trajan, Vespasian, Hadrian, Pius,
Titus, and Marcus set against these monstrous tyrants. At the same time he
will understand the judgment of the Romans: those men ruled long and died a
natural death, while these were killed, dragged, and even called tyrants,
whose very names one would rather not speak.

So when Macrinus had been slain, and with him his son Diadumenus, who had
received an equal share of the empire and the name Antoninus besides, the
empire was conferred on Varius Heliogabalus, because he was said to be the son
of Bassianus. In fact he was the priest of Heliogabalus, that is of Jupiter or
of the Sun, and he had taken the name Antoninus either as proof of his birth
or because he had learned that the name was so dear to the nations that even
Bassianus the parricide was loved for its sake. He was first called Varius,
later Heliogabalus from the priesthood of the god Heliogabalus, for whom he
built a temple at Rome on the site where the shrine of Orcus had stood, and
whom he brought with him from Syria. Last of all, when he received the empire,
he was called Antoninus, and he was the last of the Antonines in the Roman
empire.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

He was so wholly given over to his mother Symiamira that he did nothing in the
state without her will, though she herself lived like a whore and practiced
every shame in the palace. She had lain in adultery with Antoninus Caracalla,
so that Varius, or Heliogabalus, was commonly thought to have been conceived
from that union.

Some even say that his schoolfellows gave him the name Varius because he
seemed conceived of mixed seed, as one would expect of a whore's child. After
his father Antoninus, as he was called, had been killed by Macrinus's faction,
he is said to have fled to the temple of the god Heliogabalus as to a
sanctuary, lest Macrinus kill him, for Macrinus, with a son both wasteful and
cruel, exercised the empire with the utmost savagery. But enough of the name,
though he defiled that sacred name of the Antonines which you, most sacred
Constantine, so revere that you have had Marcus and Pius formed in gold among
the Constantii and the Claudii, as if they too were your ancestors, taking to
yourself the virtues of the old that suit your own character and are dear and
welcome to you.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But to return to Antoninus Varius: once he had the empire he sent messengers
to Rome, and when every rank had been stirred, and the whole people as well,
at the name Antoninus, which seemed restored not in title only, as it had been
in Diadumenus, but in blood, since he had written himself the son of Antoninus
Bassianus, a huge longing for him arose. There was besides the rumor that is
commonly granted to new princes after tyrants, a rumor that does not last
unless it is backed by the highest virtues, and that many middling princes
have lost.

When at last Heliogabalus's letter was read in the senate, blessings were at
once spoken for Antoninus and curses against Macrinus and his son. Antoninus
was named prince with all willing and eager to believe, as men's wishes will
hurry toward credulity when they long for what they hope to be true.

But as soon as he entered the city, setting aside the affairs of the province,
he consecrated Heliogabalus on the Palatine Hill beside the imperial house and
built him a temple, striving to move into it the image of the Mother, the fire
of Vesta, the Palladium, the sacred shields, and all that the Romans held
holy, and working to this end, that no god should be worshipped at Rome except
Heliogabalus. He said besides that the religions of the Jews and the
Samaritans and the Christian devotion should be transferred there, so that the
priesthood of Heliogabalus might hold the secret of every cult.

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Then, when he first held a sitting of the senate, he ordered his mother to be
summoned into the senate. When she had come she was called to the consuls'
benches and took part in the drafting, that is, as a witness to the making of
a senate decree. He was the only emperor under whom a woman entered the senate
as if of the highest rank, in the place of a man.

He also made on the Quirinal Hill a women's senate, that is, a senate of
women, where before there had been a meeting of matrons, but only on solemn
days, and if ever some matron was granted the ornaments of a consular
marriage, which the old emperors conferred on their kinswomen, and most of all
on those whose husbands had not been ennobled, lest they remain without rank.
But under Symiamira ridiculous decrees of the senate were passed on the laws
of matrons: in what dress each should walk abroad, who should give way to
whom, who should come to whose kiss, who should ride in a carriage, who on a
horse, who on a pack animal, who on an ass, who in a mule cart, who in an ox
cart, who in a chair, and whether the chair should be of hide or of bone or
plated with ivory or with silver, and who might have gold or gems on their
shoes.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

So when he had wintered at Nicomedia and was doing everything in a filthy way,
being used by men and using them in turn, the soldiers at once repented of
what they had done in conspiring against Macrinus to make this man prince, and
they turned their minds toward Alexander, cousin of this same Heliogabalus,
whom the senate had named Caesar after Macrinus was killed. For who could bear
a prince who took lust in every hollow of his body, when no one would bear
even a beast of that kind? At Rome, finally, he did nothing else but keep
agents who hunted out well-endowed men and brought them to the palace so that
he might enjoy them. He also played at home the story of Paris, taking the
part of Venus himself, so that his clothes would suddenly drop to his feet,
and naked he would kneel, one hand to his breast and the other to his private
parts, his hinder parts thrust out and offered to the man who used him. He
shaped his face besides in the look with which Venus is painted, his whole
body smoothed, thinking this the chief fruit of life, if he seemed worthy and
fit for the lust of many.

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

He sold honors and ranks and powers both by himself and through all his slaves
and the servants of his lusts. He chose men for the senate without regard to
age, property, or birth, for money alone, having sold even military commands
and tribunates and governorships and generalships, and procuratorships and
palace offices as well. The charioteers Protogenes and Cordius he had first as
partners in the chariot race, later as partners in every part of life and
action. Many whose bodies pleased him he brought from the stage and the Circus
and the arena into the palace. Hierocles he loved so much that he kissed his
groin, a thing even indecent to say, claiming that he was celebrating the
rites of Flora.

He violated the chastity of a Vestal Virgin. He profaned the rites of the
Roman people by taking away the inner shrines. He wished to put out the
everlasting fire. He wished to put out not only the Roman religions but those
throughout the world, his one aim being that the god Heliogabalus should be
worshipped everywhere. He broke into the store of Vesta, which only the
virgins and only the priests may enter, himself stained with every contagion
of morals, and with those who had stained themselves. He tried to carry off
the sacred inner shrine, and when he had seized an earthen jar as if it were
the true one, which the chief Vestal had shown him as a fake, and had found
nothing in it, he dashed it down and broke it. Yet he took nothing from the
religion, because several like it are said to have been made, so that no one
could ever carry off the true one. That being so, he still carried off the
image which he believed to be the Palladium, and after coating it with gold he
set it in the temple of his god.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

He also took up the rites of the Mother of the gods and received the bull
sacrifice, in order to snatch away her image and the other sacred things that
are kept hidden deep. He tossed his head among the castrated fanatics and
bound his private parts and did all that the eunuch priests are used to do,
and he moved the stolen holy thing into the inner shrine of his own god. He
also staged Salambo with all the wailing and tossing of the Syrian cult,
making for himself an omen of the doom that hung over him. He used to say that
all the gods were servants of his god, calling some his chamberlains, others
his slaves, others attendants for various tasks. He wished to bring from their
own temple the stones that are said to be divine, and the image of Diana of
Laodicea from her sanctuary, where Orestes had set it.

They say that Orestes did not set one image of Diana in one place, but many in
many places. After he had purified himself at the Three Rivers by the Hebrus
according to an oracle, he also founded the city of Oresta, which must often
be stained with the blood of men. Hadrian ordered the city of Oresta to be
claimed for his own name at the time when he had begun to suffer from madness,
according to an oracle, when he had been told to steal into the house or the
name of some madman. For from that they say his insanity was softened, the
insanity through which he had ordered many senators killed, who were saved,
and for whom Antoninus earned the name of Pius, because he later brought into
the senate those whom all believed to have been put to death by the prince's
order.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

He also slaughtered human victims, having chosen for this, throughout all
Italy, noble and handsome boys whose fathers and mothers were both living, I
suppose so that the grief of each parent might be greater. Every kind of
magician was at hand for him and worked daily, while he urged them on and gave
thanks to the gods that he had found their friends, as he inspected the
children's entrails and tortured the victims according to the rite of his own
people.

When he entered on the consulship he threw to the people to be scrambled for
not coins of silver or gold, nor sweets, nor small animals, but fat oxen and
camels and asses and slaves, repeating that this was an imperial thing.

He hunted down the memory of Macrinus with cruelty, but much more that of
Diadumenus, because he had been called Antoninus, naming him a false
Antoninus, and at the same time because from a most wasteful man he was said
to have come out very brave, excellent, grave, and severe. He even forced some
writers to argue unspeakable things, or rather things unbearable to tell,
about that man's luxury, things that should not even be told in a biography.

He made a public bath in the palace buildings, and at the same time opened the
Bath of Plautinus to the people, so that from it he might collect well-endowed
men. Care was taken that from the whole inner city and from the sailors those
they called donkey-pronged should be sought out. So they named those who
seemed the more manly.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

When he wished to bring war on the Marcomanni, which Antoninus had finished
most gloriously, some told him that through Chaldeans and magicians Marcus
Antoninus had brought it about that the Marcomanni should always be devoted
and friendly to the Roman people, and that this had been done by spells and a
consecration. When he asked what that consecration was and where it was, it
was kept from him. For it was agreed that he sought the consecration in order
to destroy it, in the hope of stirring up the war, and most of all because he
had heard there had been an answer that the Marcomannic war was to be ended by
an Antoninus, whereas this man was called Varius and Heliogabalus and a public
joke, and he defiled the name of Antoninus, which he had seized. This was put
about most of all by those who were angry that well-endowed men of larger
purse were set against them for the exercise of his lusts. From this they even
began to think of his death. So much for affairs at home.

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

But the soldiers could not bear that plague under the covering name of
emperor, and they first threw talk among themselves, then in circles, all
turning toward Alexander, who was already Caesar, named by the senate at the
time when Macrinus was killed, cousin of this Antoninus, for one grandmother,
Varia, belonged to both, from whom Heliogabalus was called Varius.

Under him Zoticus had so much power that all the chiefs of the offices treated
him as if he were the master's husband. This same Zoticus, abusing that kind
of intimacy, would sell all Heliogabalus's words and deeds in empty smoke,
piling up huge riches as far as he could, threatening some, promising others,
deceiving all, and as he came out from the emperor he would go to each man
saying,

/As for you, I said this./

/As for you, I heard this./

/As for you, this will happen./

Such men there are who, if they are admitted to too close a familiarity with
princes, sell the reputation not only of bad princes but of good ones as well,
and who feed on infamous rumor mongering from the stupidity or innocence of
emperors who do not see it. He married and lay with him, even having a
brideswoman, and he would cry

/Have at it, Cook!/

and that at the very time when Zoticus was ill. He would then ask philosophers
and the gravest men whether they too in youth had suffered what he himself
suffered, and that most shamelessly. For he never spared filthy words, showing
indecency with his fingers as well, nor was there any shame in an assembly or
with the people listening.

* Caput 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He made freedmen governors, legates, consuls, generals, and he stained every
rank with the low birth of ruined men. When he had invited noble friends to
the vintage and sat down at the baskets, he began to ask each of the gravest
whether he was ready for Venus, and when the old men blushed he would cry

/He blushed; all is well./

taking silence and a blush for consent. He added besides what he himself would
do, without any covering of shame. After he saw the old men blush and keep
quiet, either because age or because dignity refused such things, he turned to
the young men and began to ask everything of them. When he heard from them
what suited their age, he began to rejoice, saying that it was a truly free
vintage that he was celebrating in that way. Many say that he first invented
this, that at the vintage feast many jokes against masters should be spoken
even with the masters listening, jokes which he himself had composed, and in
Greek most of all. Most of these Marius Maximus reports in the life of this
same Heliogabalus. There were shameless friends, some of them old men and
philosophers in look, who arranged their hair in a net, who said they suffered
certain shameless things, who boasted that they had husbands. Some say they
pretended this, so that by copying his vices they might become dearer to him.

* Caput 12
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

To the prefecture of the guard he raised a dancer who had done stage work at
Rome. He made the charioteer Cordius prefect of the watch, and the barber
Claudius prefect of the grain supply. To the remaining honors he promoted men
recommended to him by the monstrous size of their private parts. He ordered a
muleteer to have charge of the five percent tax on inheritances; he ordered a
runner too, and a cook, and a locksmith. When he entered either the Camp or
the Senate house he brought with him his grandmother, Varia by name, of whom I
spoke above, so that by her authority he might become more respectable,
because he could not by himself. Nor before him, as I have already said, did a
woman enter the senate so as to be asked to the drafting and to give an
opinion. At banquets he placed worn catamites next to himself above all, and
took special joy in handling and touching them, nor did anyone more often hand
him the cup when he had drunk.

Among these evils of a most shameless life he ordered Alexander, whom he had
adopted, to be removed from him, saying that he regretted the adoption, and he
sent word to the senate that the name of Caesar should be taken from him. But
when this was brought out in the senate there was a huge silence, for
Alexander was an excellent young man, as was later proved by the character of
his rule, since he displeased his father because he was not shameless. He was,
besides, his cousin, as some say. He was loved by the soldiers as well, and
acceptable to the senate and to the equestrian order. Yet the madness did not
fail to go as far as the outcome of that worst wish, for he sent assassins
against him, and in this way: he himself withdrew to the Gardens of Old Hope,
as if forming vows against some new young man, leaving in the Palace his
mother and grandmother and his cousin, and he ordered that the excellent young
man, necessary to the state, should be butchered. He also sent a letter to the
soldiers in which he ordered that the name of Caesar be taken from Alexander.
He sent men to smear mud on the inscriptions of his statues in the Camp, as is
commonly done with tyrants. He sent also to his guardians, commanding them,
with hope of rewards and honors, that they should kill him in whatever

But as soon as the inscriptions of the statues had been smeared with mud, all

where Varius was, in order to defend Alexander and at last drive from the
state that unclean man of a murderous mind. When they had come to the Palace

later led them to the Camp. Symiamira, the mother of Heliogabalus, had
followed them on foot, anxious for her son. From there they went to the
gardens, where Varius was found preparing a chariot race, yet waiting most
intently for the moment when it should be announced to him that his cousin was
dead. Suddenly terrified by the noise of the soldiers he hid himself in a
corner and covered himself with the bedchamber curtain that hung at the
entrance of the room. He sent the prefects, one to quiet the soldiers in the
Camp, the other to soothe those who had already come into the gardens.
Antiochianus, then, one of the prefects, by reminding them of their oath
persuaded the soldiers who had come into the gardens not to kill him, because
not many had come, and most had stayed with the standard, which the tribune

had held back. So much in the gardens. In the Camp, however, the soldiers said
to the prefect as he begged them that they would spare Heliogabalus if he
would send away from himself the unclean men and the charioteers and the
actors and return to a decent life, and most of all if those were removed who,
to everyone's grief, had the most power with him and sold all his decisions,
whether in truth or in empty smoke. At last Hierocles, Cordius, and Mirissimus
were removed from him, and two shameless intimates who made him, from a fool,
a greater fool. The soldiers further charged the prefects not to let him live
in that way any longer, and that Alexander should be guarded, and that no
violence should be done him, and at the same time that the Caesar should not
see any friend of the Augustus, lest there be any copying of the shame. But
Heliogabalus with huge entreaty kept demanding Hierocles back, that most
shameless man, and day by day he pushed on plots against the Caesar. At last
on the first of January, when they had been designated consuls together, he
refused to go out with his cousin. At the end, when his grandmother and mother
told him that the soldiers were hanging over him for his destruction unless
they saw concord between the cousins, he put on the bordered toga at the sixth
hour of the day and went out to the senate, his grandmother having been
summoned to the senate and led to a chair. Then he refused to go to the
Capitol to take the vows and perform the solemn rites, and everything was done
by the city praetor, as if the consuls were not there.

Nor did he put off the killing of his cousin, but fearing that the senate
would turn to someone else if he killed the cousin, he suddenly ordered the
senate to leave the city, and all who lacked carriages or slaves were ordered
to set out at once, some being carried by porters, others on chance animals
hired for a fee.

Sabinus, a man of consular rank, to whom Ulpian wrote books, because he had
remained in the city, he ordered to be killed, after calling a centurion, in
rather soft words. But the centurion, rather deaf in one ear, believed he was
being ordered to drive him from the city, and so he did. Thus the centurion's
defect was Sabinus's salvation. He also removed Ulpian the jurist as a good
man, and Silvinus the rhetorician, whom he had made the Caesar's teacher.
Silvinus was in fact killed; Ulpian was spared.

But the soldiers, and most of all the Guard, either knowing what evils were
being prepared against Heliogabalus, or seeing that ill will was turning on
themselves, formed a plot to free the state. First they killed the accomplices
[omitted] by a kind of death in which they slew some by tearing out their
vitals and stabbed others from below, so that death would match the life.

After this they made a rush on him and killed him in a latrine to which he had
fled. He was then dragged through the public way. An insult was added to the
corpse, that the soldiers should throw it into a sewer. But when the sewer by
chance would not take it, it was thrown from the Aemilian Bridge into the
Tiber, a weight fastened so that it should not float, lest it should ever be
buried. His corpse was also dragged through the spaces of the Circus before it
was hurled into the Tiber.

His name, that is the name Antoninus, was erased by order of the senate, and
that of Varius Heliogabalus remained, since he had kept the other by
affectation, wishing to seem the son of Antoninus. After death he was called
the Tiber man, and the Dragged, and the Unclean, and many things, whenever
those deeds that seemed to have been done under him had to be pointed out. He
alone of all the princes was both dragged and thrown into a sewer and hurled
into the Tiber. That befell him from the common hatred of all, against which
emperors ought especially to guard, since those who do not earn the love of
senate, people, and soldiers do not even earn tombs.

Of his public works, besides the temple of the god Heliogabalus, whom some
call the Sun and others Jupiter, and the restoration of the Amphitheatre after
the fire, and the bath in Sulpician Street, which Antoninus the son of Severus
had begun, none remain. The bath, indeed, Antoninus Caracalla had dedicated,
both by bathing and by admitting the people, but the porticoes had been
lacking, which were later built by this false Antoninus and finished by
Alexander.

He was the last of the Antonines (though many later think the Gordians
Antonines by surname, who were called Antonii, not Antonini), so hateful in
life, in habits, and in wickedness that the senate even erased his name. I
myself would not have called him Antoninus except for the sake of recognition,
which often forces one to speak even those names that have been abolished.
With him was also killed his mother Symiamira, a most infamous woman and
worthy of her son. It was provided before all else, after Antoninus
Heliogabalus, that a woman should never enter the senate, and that the head of
the man through whom that was done should be devoted and cursed to the dead.

Many obscene things about his life have been put into writing, which, because
they are not worthy of memory, I have thought I should report only those that
belonged to luxury, some of which he is said to have done as a private man,
some already as emperor, since he himself used to say that among private men
he imitated Apicius, and among emperors

Otho and Vitellius. For he was the first of all private men to cover couches
with golden coverlets, because at that time it was allowed by the authority of
Marcus Antoninus, who had sold all the imperial furnishings in public. Then he
gave summer banquets in colors, so that one day he would present green,
another glassy, then blue in turn, always varying through all the summer days.
He was the first then to have silver self-boilers, the first also cooking
pots. Then vessels of a hundred pounds of silver, carved, and some stained
with the most lustful figures. Mastic wine and pennyroyal wine and all these
things which luxury now keeps he first invented. For rose wine, received from
others, he made more fragrant by the grinding of pine cones as well. In short,
these kinds of cups are not read of before Heliogabalus. Nor had he any life
except seeking out pleasures. He first made minced dishes of fish, first of
oysters and smooth oysters and other sea shells of that kind, and of lobsters
and prawns and squills. He also strewed dining rooms with rose, and couches
and porticoes, and so walked about, and that with every kind of flower,
lilies, violets, hyacinths, and narcissus. He swam only in pools dyed with a
noble unguent or with saffron. Nor did he lie easily on couches unless they
had hare's fur or the underwing feathers of partridges, often changing the
mattresses.

He sometimes so despised the senate that he called them slaves in togas, the
Roman people the tiller of a single farm, holding the equestrian order in no
place at all. He often called the city prefect after dinner to drink, bringing
in the prefects of the guard as well, so that if they refused, the masters of
the offices would compel them. He wished also to make city prefects through
the several regions of the city, so that there should be fourteen in the city,
and he would have done it if he had lived, meaning to promote all the most
shameful men and men of the lowest trade.

He had beds made of solid silver, both for dining rooms and for bedchambers.
In imitation of Apicius he more often ate camels' heels and combs taken from
living cocks, tongues of peacocks and nightingales, because whoever ate them
was said to be safe from pestilence. He also served to the palace people huge
platters stuffed with the guts of mullets and with flamingo brains and
partridge eggs and thrush brains and the heads of parrots and pheasants and
peacocks. He ordered such great barbels of mullets to be served that he
presented them in place of cress, wild celery, beans, and fenugreek, in full
bean dishes and platters. That is especially to be wondered at.

He is said to have given naval circus games in canals full of wine, to have
poured cloaks of vine flower perfume, and to have driven four-horse teams of
elephants on the Vatican, the tombs that stood in the way having been torn
down. He also yoked camels four at a time to chariots at a private spectacle
in the Circus. He is said to have collected serpents through the priests of
the Marsic people and suddenly, before dawn, when the people are used to
gathering for the crowded games, to have poured them out, and many were hurt
by bite and by flight. He used a tunic all of gold, he used a purple one, he
used a Persian one of gems, saying that he was weighed down by the burden of
pleasure. He had gems in his shoes as well, and engraved ones at that, which
moved all to laughter, as if the carvings of noble artists could be seen on
gems that stuck to his feet. He wished also to use a jeweled diadem, so that
he might become more beautiful and more fit for a woman's face. He used it at
home as well. He is said also to have promised a phoenix to his guests, or in
place of it a thousand pounds of gold, so that he let them go in the palace.
He presented swimming pools of sea water, most of all in inland places, and he
handed the same over to individual friends as they swam, and again filled them
with fish. In summer he made a mountain of snow in the garden of the house,
the snows having been brought in. At the sea he never ate fish; in places
farthest from the sea he always served all sea things. Inland he fed country
people on the milt of morays and pike.

He always ate fish cooked as if in sea water, with their own color, in a blue
sauce. He presented momentary pools of rose wine and roses, and he bathed with
all his people, furnishing hot rooms of nard. He also furnished balsam in the

for friends, clients, and slaves. He never dined for less than a hundred
thousand sesterces, that is, thirty pounds of silver; sometimes, however, he
dined for three million sesterces, all that he spent having been reckoned. He
even outdid the dinners of Vitellius and of Apicius. He drew fish from his
ponds by oxen. Passing through the market he wept over the public beggary. He
bound hangers-on to a water wheel and with the turning sent them under the
water and rolled them back to the top, and he called them river Ixions. He
also paved open spaces in the Palace with Laconian and porphyry stones, which
he called Antonine. Those stones lasted down to our memory, but lately they
have been dug up and cut away. He had resolved also to give one huge column,
to be climbed from inside, so that he might set the god Heliogabalus on the
top, but he did not find so great a stone, though he thought of bringing it
from the Thebaid.

He often shut up drunken friends and suddenly at night let in lions and
leopards and bears, disarmed, so that when they woke in the same bedroom they
found lions, bears, leopards at daylight or, what is heavier, at night, from
which many died of fright. For many cheaper friends he spread bladders in
place of couches and blew them out while they were dining, so that they were
often found suddenly dining under the tables. He first invented spreading the
crescent couch on the ground, not on dining couches, so that from the feet the
bladders might be loosened by boys to let out the air.

In mimic adulteries he ordered those things that are usually done in pretense
to be done in truth. He often bought whores from all the pimps and freed them.
When in private talk the conversation had arisen, how many ruptured men there
could be in the city of Rome, he ordered all of them to be noted and presented
at his baths, and he bathed with them, some even of honorable rank. He watched
gladiators fighting before a banquet, and boxers often. He spread a dining
room for himself at the top of the hunting theatre and, while he lunched,
presented criminals and beast hunts for himself. To hangers-on at the second
table he often presented a wax dinner, often a wooden one, often an ivory one,
sometimes earthen, sometimes even of marble or stone, so that all they were
shown to look at, of different matter, was what he himself was dining on,
while they only drank through the several courses and washed their hands as if
they had eaten.

He is said to have been the first of the Romans to use a garment wholly of
silk, though half silk was already in use. He never touched washed linen,
saying that those who used washed linens were beggars. He was often seen in
public after dinner in a Dalmatian tunic, calling himself Fabius Gurges and
Scipio, because he was in that dress in which Fabius and Cornelius had been
brought out in public by their parents as young men, to correct their manners.

He gathered all the whores from the Circus, from the theatre, from the
Stadium, and from all places and baths into public buildings and held an
assembly among them as if it were a military one, calling them fellow
soldiers, and he argued about kinds of postures and pleasures. Afterward he
brought into such an assembly pimps, worn catamites collected from everywhere,
and the most luxurious little boys and young men, and whereas he had gone out
to the whores in women's dress with a breast thrust out, he went to the
catamites in the dress of boys who are prostituted. After the assembly he
announced to them, as if to soldiers, three gold pieces as a donative, and he
asked them to beg of the gods that they themselves might have others to
recommend.

He joked with the slaves so as to order them to bring him a thousand pounds of
cobwebs, a prize having been set, and he is said to have collected ten
thousand pounds of cobwebs, saying that from this too one should understand
how great Rome was. He sent to hangers-on, in place of a yearly allowance of
stores, jars with frogs and scorpions and with snakes and monsters of that
kind. He shut up an endless number of flies in jars of that sort, calling them
tamed bees.

He invented certain kinds of lusts, so as to outdo the group vices of the old
wicked, and he knew all the apparatus of Tiberius and Caligula and Nero.

It had been foretold to him by Syrian priests that he would die by violence.
He had therefore prepared cords twisted with purple and silk and scarlet, with
which, if it were necessary, he might end his life by a noose. He had prepared
golden swords as well, with which he might kill himself if some force pressed.
He had prepared poisons also in thunderstones and jacinths and emeralds, with
which he might make away with himself if something heavier threatened. He had
also made a very high tower, with gilded and jeweled boards laid before him,
from which he might throw himself down, saying that even his death ought to be
costly and after the look of luxury, so that it should be said no one had
perished thus. But none of that availed, for, as we have said, he was both
killed by common soldiers and dragged through the streets and led most
filthily through the sewers and sent down into the Tiber.

This was the end of the name of the Antonines in the state, all knowing that
this Antoninus had been as false in life as in name.

It may perhaps seem strange to someone, venerable Constantine, that this
plague which I have related was in the place of princes, and that for nearly
three years. So there was then no one in the state who would draw that man
from the helm of Roman majesty, though for Nero, Vitellius, Caligula, and the
rest of that kind a tyrant killer was never wanting. But first of all I myself
ask pardon, because I have handed down in writing these things which I found
among various authors, though I have kept silent about many wicked things, and
things that cannot even be spoken without the greatest shame. Those things,
however, which I have spoken I have covered, as far as I could, by putting
forward a veil of words. Then I believed that what Your Clemency is used to
say should be regarded

/It is fortune to be emperor./

For there have been less good kings, and the worst. One must act, however, as
Your Piety is used to say, so that those may be worthy of empire whom the
force of fate has brought to the necessity of ruling. And since this man was
the last of the Antonines, and afterward this name was not frequent in the
state in the place of princes, this too must be added, lest some error arise
when I begin to tell of the two Gordians, father and son, who wished
themselves to be said of the house of the Antonines: in them the name was not
first a surname but a first name. Then, as I find in most books, they were
called Antonii, not Antonini.
