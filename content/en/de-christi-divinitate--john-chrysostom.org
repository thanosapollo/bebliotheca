And what is this? "There was," he says, "a feast of the Jews, and Jesus went

zeal to rid them of their infirmity. When therefore their assembly was full,
and the theater complete, then coming into the midst he displayed the things
that make for the salvation of their soul. There lay then a multitude of the
sick waiting for the stirring of the water, and the first who went down was
healed after the =48.804= stirring of the water, but the second no longer; but
the medicine was spent, the healing of the grace was exhausted, and thereafter
the waters remained empty, having wiped up the whole infirmity of the first
who went down. And very reasonably; for the grace was a servant's. But not so
was it when the Master came; nor was it only the first who went down into the
pool of the waters of baptism who was healed, but the first, and the second,
and the third, and the fourth, and the tenth, and the twentieth; and if you
should name ten thousand, and twice as many, and thrice, and boundless in
number, and if you should throw the whole world into the pool of the waters,
the grace is not even a little diminished, but remains the same, cleansing all
those. So great is the interval between a servant's power and the Master's
authority. That one healed one, this one the whole world; that one through the
year one, this one each day, if you wish to throw in ten thousand, renders
them all healthy; that one going down and troubling the water, this one not
so, but it is enough only to call his bare name upon the waters, and to store
up in them every ground of healing. And that one healed the maiming of bodies,
but this one sets right the wickedness of the soul. Do you see how through all
things the interval appears great and boundless?

2. There lay then a multitude of the sick waiting for the stirring of the
water; for the place was a spiritual hospital. Just as then in a hospital one
may see many with the eye cut out, maimed in the leg, ailing in another
member, then all sitting together in common, and waiting for the physician: so
too in that spot one might see the multitude of those who had come together.
In these porches there was a certain man having thirty and eight years in his
infirmity. Jesus seeing this man lying, and knowing that he already had a long
time, says to him, "Do you wish to become healthy?" The sick man answered him
and said, "Yes, Lord; but I have no man, that, when the water is troubled, he
may throw me into the pool; and while I am coming, another goes down before
me." Why did Jesus, passing by all the others, come to this man? That he might
display both the power and the loving-kindness: the power, because the disease
had by now become incurable, and the affairs of his infirmity had come round
to helplessness; and the loving-kindness, because the one most worthy of mercy
and of benefaction, this man the guardian and lover of men saw before the
others. Let us not then simply pass by the passage, nor the number of the
thirty and eight years which he had in his infirmity. Let all hear, as many as
wrestle with continual poverty, as many as live together with infirmity also,
as many as endure circumstances of things of this life, as many as have
undergone a winter and a surge of unexpected evils. This paralytic lies as a
common harbor of human misfortunes. For no one is so foolish, no one so
wretched and miserable, as, looking toward this man, not to bear all =48.805=
that is brought upon him nobly and with all eagerness. For if it had been
twenty years, if ten, if five only, would they not have been enough to
dissolve the tension of his soul? But he remains thirty and eight years, and
does not withdraw, and displays much patience. Perhaps the length of the time
seems marvelous to you, but if you hear his very words, then especially


you will know all his philosophy and his patience. Jesus stood over him, and
says to him, "Do you wish to become healthy?" And who would have been ignorant
of this, that he wished to become healthy? Why then does he ask? Not as being
ignorant; for he who knew the secrets of the mind knew far more the things
plain and clear to all. Why did he ask? Just as then he says to the centurion,
"I will come and heal him," not being ignorant of what he was about to say,
but knowing beforehand and understanding it very exactly, and wishing to give
him a beginning and a pretext, so that he might uncover to all the reverence
in shadow, and say, "Lord, I am not worthy that you should enter under my
roof": so also in the case of this paralytic, knowing what he was about to
say, he asks if he would wish to be healed, not because he was ignorant of
this, but that he might furnish him some pretext and starting-point to tell
the tragedy of his own misfortune, and become a teacher of patience. For if he
had healed the man in silence, we would have suffered the last loss, not
having learned the endurance of his soul. But Christ does not set right the
present things only, but also deems the future things worthy of much care.
Being then a teacher of endurance and patience he uncovered him to all who
inhabit the world, by the question throwing him into the necessity of an
answer: "Do you wish to become healthy?" What then did that man? He was not
displeased, he was not angry, he did not say to the one who asked, "You see me
paralyzed, you have known the long time of my infirmity, and you ask if I wish
to become healthy? Have you come to laugh at my misfortunes, and to make a
comedy of another's evils?" For you know how small-souled the sick are, even
if they should lie on a bed for a year only; but where the disease was
stretched out thirty and eight years, how is it not likely that all philosophy
was spent, consumed in the long time? But nevertheless he neither said nor
thought anything of that sort, but with much gentleness he makes the answer,
and says, "Yes, Lord; but that I have no man, that, when the water is
troubled, he may throw me into the pool." See how many things came together at
once and besieged the man: disease, and poverty, and desolation of those who
would stand by him. "And while I am coming, another goes down before me." This
is more pitiable than all, and enough to bend even a stone. For I seem to
myself to see the man crawling each year, and coming to the very mouth of the
pool, and hanging at the very end of the good hope

each year; and what is greater, =48.806= that he endured these things not for
two and three and ten years, but for eight and thirty. And he displayed all
the zeal, but fell away from the fruit; and the race took place, but the prize
of this race was another's, through those many years; and what is harder, that
he also saw others being delivered. For you know this well, that we take a
more exact perception of our own evils, when we see others who have fallen
into the same terrible things and have been delivered. For this reason the
poor man also, when he sees another being rich, then more perceives his own
poverty; and the one who is sick is more pained, when he sees many of the
sufferers putting off the infirmity, but himself having no good hope. For in
the prosperities of others we behold our own misfortunes more clearly; which
indeed was happening then also to this man. But nevertheless wrestling with
disease and poverty and desolation for so long a time, and seeing others being
delivered, and himself always having attempted, but never having had strength,
and not even after these things hoping again that he would be delivered from
the terrible thing, not even so did he withdraw, but each year he ran. But we,
if we pray once to God about anything whatever, and do not receive, at once
are distressed, and fall into the last listlessness; so that we withdraw from
the asking, we slacken the zeal.


Is it possible either to praise the paralytic worthily, or to blame our sloth?
For of what defense would we be worthy, and of what pardon, when, that man
having endured thirty and eight years, we so quickly fall back?

3. What then does Christ? When he showed that he is worthy of healing, and
justly came to him before the others, he also says to him, "Rise, take up your
bed, and walk." Do you see how he was harmed in nothing from the thirty and
eight years, since he bore what happened with patience? For both his soul
became more philosophical in this long time, being tried in the misfortune as
in a furnace, and he received the healing with more glory. For not an angel,
but the Master of the angels himself was healing him. And why did he also
command him to take the bed? For one reason first and foremost, that he might
thereafter rid the Jews of the observation of the law. For when the sun is
shining, one ought no longer to sit by the lamp; the truth having been shown,
one ought no longer to hold to the type. For this reason, if ever he dissolved
the sabbath, he worked a very great sign in it, that the excess of the wonder,
striking those who saw, might little by little drag out and take away the
observation of idleness. And second, that he might stop their shameless
mouths. For since they were playing the knave about the judgment of the
wonders, and were attempting to insult the glory of the things that were
coming to pass, as a kind of trophy and undisputed proof of the health, he
commanded the carrying of the bed to be displayed, lest they say about this
man also what they said about the blind man. And what did they say about that
one? "This is he, this is not he, it is he himself." Lest then they say such
things about this man also, the bed becomes an accuser of their shamelessness,
being carried on high. There is =48.807= also a third thing to say, not less
than what has been said. For that you may learn that not human art, but divine
power worked the whole, he commanded him to carry the bed, furnishing the
greatest and clear proof of the true and genuine health, lest any of those
blasphemous men say that the paralytic, playing along and doing a favor to
Christ, pretended a mere walking. For this reason he also commands him to
carry a load upon his shoulders. For if his limbs had not been well set firm,
and the joints tightened, he would not have been able to bear so great a bulk
upon his shoulders. And besides all these things that also was shown, that
when Christ commands, all things come to be at once, both riddance of disease
and return of health. For physicians, even if they rid of diseases, yet have
not strength to bring the ailing man back to health at once, but they need
another long time, that of recovery, so as to scrape off little by little the
remains of the infirmity from the body and cast them out. But Christ not so,
but in one turn of a moment he both rid him of the infirmity and brought back
the health, and no time came in between, but at the same time that holy voice
leapt out from the holy tongue, and the disease of the body ran away, and the
word became a deed, and all the infirmity was healed from every side. And just
as some maidservant in revolt, whenever she sees her own master, shrinks, and
returns to the fitting good order: so also the nature of the body, like some
maidservant then in revolt, and having worked the paralysis, when she saw her
own Master come, returned to her own good order, and took up the becoming
adornment. And the voice worked all these things; for the words were not bare,
but words of God, of which he says that "Mighty are the works of his words."
For if he made man when he was not, much more did he set right again one who
had become rotten and dissolved. Here I would gladly ask those who busy
themselves about the essence of God, how did those members come together? how
were the bones tightened?


how was the dissolved tension of the belly strengthened? how were the loosened
sinews stretched again, and how did the power that had been thrown down rise
and stand upright? But they would not be able to say the how. Therefore marvel
at the thing that has come to pass only, do not be over-curious about the
manner.
When therefore he did what was commanded, and took the bed, the Jews seeing
him say, "It is sabbath, and it is not lawful for you to take up the bed on
the sabbath." They ought to have worshipped the one who worked it, they ought
to have marveled at what had come to pass, but they discuss about sabbath,
truly those who strain out the gnat and swallow the camel. What then did that
man? "He who made me healthy, that one said to me, Take up your bed, and
walk." Do you see the good sense of a man? he confesses the physician, and
says that the lawgiver of the command is trustworthy; and just as the blind
man reasoned with them, =48.808= so also this man. And how did that one
reason? They were saying to him, that "This man is not from God, because he
does not keep the sabbath." What then did that one? "We know," he says, "that
God does not hear sinners; but this man opened my eyes." And what he says is
something like this: If he transgressed the law, he sinned; and if he sinned,
he would not have had so great strength; for where there is sin, there is no
display of power; but he did have strength, therefore he did not sin by
transgressing the law. So also this man reasons. For the saying, "He who made
me healthy," hinted this, that if this is the one who displayed power, he
would not be justly liable to charges of lawbreaking. What then do these men?
"Where is the man who said to you, Take up your bed, and walk?" See the
ingratitude and the insensibility, see a soul full of arrogance. For the eyes
of those who envy see nothing healthy, but only from where it is possible to
find a handle. So also these men, though the one who was healed confessed both
things, both that he healed, and that he commanded him to take up the bed, hid
the one and spoke the other; they hid the wonder, and put forward the
dissolving of the sabbath. For they did not say, "Where is the one who made
you healthy?" but having kept silent about that, they said, "Where is the one
who said to you, Take up your bed, and go?" But this man did not know; for
Jesus had withdrawn, there being a crowd in the place. This is a very great
defense of the man, this a proof of Christ's care; that when you hear that
when he came he did not receive him in the same way as the centurion, nor say,
"Speak a word, and my servant will be healed," you may not accuse him of
unbelief, since indeed he did not know him; for he did not even know who he
was. For how would he have known him, never having seen him before? For this
reason he was saying, "I have no man, that he may throw me into the pool"; as,
if he had known him, he would not have mentioned a pool, nor the going down
there, but would have asked to be healed in the way in which he also was
healed. But he thought him to be one of the many, and a mere man, and for this
reason he mentioned the former healing. And a proof of Christ's care is again
leaving the one who was healed, and not making himself plain to that man. For
lest the Jews suspect that this witness was a substitute, and that with Christ
present and persuading him he was saying this, ignorance and the not being
present took away this suspicion; for the evangelist said that he did not know
who he was.

4. For this reason only he also sends the one who was healed by himself, that
as they wish, taking him apart, they may torture what has come to pass, and
having taken sufficient proof of the matter, they may cease from the untimely
madness. For this reason he himself utters nothing, but furnishes them the
proof through the deeds, sending out everywhere a voice most clear, and
brighter than any trumpet. For thus the testimony was thereafter also without
suspicion, "He who made me healthy, that one said to me, Take up your bed,


and go." The paralytic becomes an evangelist, a teacher of the unbelieving, a
physician and a herald unto their shame and condemnation; a physician, not
through voice, but through works, not through words, but through the deeds
themselves. For he was carrying a clear and undisputed =48.809= proof, and
what he said, he showed through the body. After these things Jesus finds him,
and says to him, "See, you have become healthy; sin no longer, lest something
worse happen to you." Did you see a physician's wisdom? did you see care? he
did not rid him of the present disease only, but also makes him safe toward
the future; and very seasonably. For when he was on the bed, he said nothing
of that sort, he did not remind him of sins; for the soul of those who are
ailing is somehow hard to please and miserable; but when he had driven out the
infirmity, when he had brought him back to health, when he had furnished the
proof of his power and of his care in deed, then he makes the counsel and the
exhortation in season, appearing trustworthy thereafter through the works
themselves. Why then did that man go away and make him evident to the Jews?
Wishing to take them as partners of the true teaching. But they for this
reason hated him, he says, and persecuted him. Attend to me here; for here is
the whole contest. For this reason they persecuted him, because he was doing
these things on the sabbath. Let us see then how he defends himself; for the
manner of the defense shows us whether he is of those who are subject, or of
those who are free, whether of those who serve, or of those who command. What
had come to pass seemed to be a very great lawbreaking; for indeed someone
once gathering sticks on the sabbath was stoned for this, because he was
carrying burdens on the sabbath. This great sin was charged against Christ,
that he dissolved the sabbath. Let us see then whether first he asks pardon as
a slave and one subject, or whether as having authority and displaying
sovereignty, as Master, and sitting upon the law, and himself having given the
commandments. How then does he defend himself? he says, "My Father works until
now, and I work." Did you see authority? And yet if he were inferior and less
than the Father, what was said is not a defense, but a greater charge and a
harsher accusation. For when someone does what it is lawful for the greater
one alone to do, then being caught is charged and says, that "Because the
greater one did it, for this reason I also did it," not only does he not
release himself from the charges by this manner of defense, but he also
renders himself liable to a greater blame and accusation. For it is of pride
and arrogance to attempt things greater than one's dignity. And Christ then,
if he were inferior, what was said would not have been a defense, but a
greater charge; but since he was equal to him, for this reason it is not a
charge. And if you wish, I will make what I say plain upon an example. To the
king alone it is lawful to wear purple, and to have a diadem upon the head, to
no other. If then some one of the many should appear wearing this dress, then
also being dragged into a court should say, that "Because the king wears this
dress, for this reason I also wear it," not only does he not dismiss himself
from the accusation, but he also renders himself liable to a greater
chastisement and punishment by the manner of the defense. Again, to let the
most shameful =48.810= go from chastisement and punishment, such as murderers,
robbers, tomb-robbers, and those who have dared other such things, belongs to
kingly munificence alone. If then some judge, having let go the one condemned
without the king's mind, is charged, and he too says, that "Because the king
also lets go, I also let go," not only is he not delivered by this manner, but
he also kindles a greater wrath against himself; and very reasonably. For it
is not just that inferiors, mounting up in their drunken outrages upon the
authority of the greater ones, should furnish themselves the defense from
there, since this is a greater insult


to those who entrusted the rule to them. For this reason, if someone is
inferior, he will never defend himself in this manner; but if a king and being
of the same dignity, he will say this with boldness. For as the preeminence of
the rule is one, so also the authority would reasonably become one. So that if
someone should appear defending himself thus, there is every necessity that he
be of the same dignity as that one whose authority he puts forward for
himself. Therefore since Christ also thus justified himself toward the Jews,
he showed us indisputably that he is of the same dignity as the Father. And
let us transfer, if it seems good, the example onto the words of Christ, and
the work which he worked. Let then the dissolving of the sabbath with
authority be what the purple and the diadem and the letting go of those
liable are. As then those things are lawful for the king alone, and for none
other of those subject, but if someone should appear doing them, and doing
them justly, there is necessity that that one also be king: so also here,
since Christ appears doing these things with authority, then being charged he
puts forward the Father saying, "My Father works until now," there is every
necessity that this one also be equal to that one who does with authority. For
he would not, if he were not equal to him, have used this manner of
justification. And that you may learn more clearly what is said, the disciples
once dissolved the sabbath in plucking the ears and eating on the sabbath; he
himself also dissolved it now; the Jews charged those men also, they charged
this one also. Let us see how he defends those men, and how he defends
himself; that from the difference you may learn his preeminence and his
defense. How then does he defend those men? "Have you not read what David did,
when he hungered?" For when he defends the slaves, he takes refuge in their
fellow-slave David; but when for himself, he raises the word to the Father:
"My Father works, and I work." And what working does he mean? perhaps someone
might say: "For in six days God rested from all his works." The daily
providence. For he did not only bring the creation into being, but also, once
it has been brought into being, he holds it together; and if you should name
angels, and archangels, and the powers above, and simply all things visible
and invisible, they enjoy that one's providence; and if they should become
empty of that energy, =48.811= they go and flow apart and perish. Wishing then
to show that he is of those who provide, and not of those provided for, of
those who work, not of those who are worked upon, he said, "My Father works,
and I work"; wishing to display the equal standing toward the Father.

5. Remember these things, and keep them with all exactness, and weave together
the philosophy from the way of life with the rightness of the doctrines; which
I also exhorted the other day, and I exhort now, and I will not cease
exhorting; and nothing so much makes a way of life and philosophy as the
spending of time here. For just as the land that lies fallow, having no one
who waters it, is full of thorns and thistles, but that which enjoys farming
hands blooms and is leafy and abounds much with fruit: so also the soul, the
one that enjoys the watering of the divine oracles, blooms and is leafy and
abounds much with the fruit of the Spirit; but the one that stands in drought
and neglect and scarcity of such watering is made desert and grows wild with
wood and brings forth many thorns, the nature of sin. And where there are
thorns, there are dragons and serpents and scorpions and all the power of the
devil. And if you distrust the word, come, let us compare those who were left
behind and ourselves, and you will see then the great interval; or rather let
us examine ourselves, who we are when we enjoy divine teaching, and who we
happen to be when we are deprived of this benefit for longer. Let us not then
betray so great a gain. For the spending of time here is a ground of all the
good things; from here withdrawing both a husband will appear


more honorable to a wife, and a wife more longed-for to a husband. For it is
not beauty of body that makes a woman beloved, =48.812= but virtue of soul,
not paints and underlinings, nor gold and costly garments, but temperance and
gentleness and fear fixed toward God. And the noetic beauty is not to be
practiced to exactness anywhere else, but in this. marvelous and divine place,
the apostles, the prophets wiping off, adorning, scraping away the old age of
sin, bringing on the prime of youth, casting out every stain, every wrinkle,
every spot from our soul. Let us then, both men and women, be eager to settle
this beauty in ourselves. For that of the body both disease withered, and
multitude of time corrupted, and old age quenched, and death coming on took
away entirely; but that of the soul neither time, nor disease, nor old age,
nor death, nor any other of such things is able to outrage, but it remains
continually blossoming. And that of the body often calls those who see into
incontinence, but the beauty of the soul draws God himself to his own love;
just as the Prophet also says, speaking to the Church: "Hear, daughter, and
see, and incline your ear, and forget your people, and the house of your
father, and the king will desire your beauty." That therefore, beloved, we may
become friends to God, let us practice this beauty each day, wiping off every
stain by the reading of the Scriptures, by the prayers, by the almsgivings, by
the concord toward one another; that the king, having fallen in love with our
comeliness according to the soul, may deem us worthy of the kingdom of the
heavens; which may it be that we all obtain, by the grace and loving-kindness
of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the Father glory together with the Holy
Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
