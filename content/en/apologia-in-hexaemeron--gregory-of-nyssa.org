OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA,
AN APOLOGETIC TO PETER HIS BROTHER, CONCERNING THE
HEXAEMERON.
PROOEMIUM.

You do these things, O man of God, bidding us dare the undareable and to set
our hand to such matters of which not only the attaining is impossible, but
not even the attempting, according at least to my account, is without blame.
For of the things philosophized by the great Moses according to divine
inspiration in the cosmogony, those that seem, according to the ready sense of
the writings, to stand contrary, you have ordered us by some sequential
thought to bring into a connected series, and to show the holy Scripture
agreeing with itself; and this after that inspired contemplation of our father
upon the matter at hand, which all who have known it admire no less than the
things philosophized by Moses himself; doing this well and reasonably, I
think. For as the ear of grain stands toward the seed, being from it and yet
not being it, or rather being it in power, but altered in size and beauty and
variety and shape: one might say that the thoughts worked out 64 by the great
Basil through a more laborious contemplation hold the same relation toward the
voice of the great Moses. For what that one said in few and easily
circumscribed words, these our teacher, increasing through lofty philosophy,
made not an ear of grain but a tree, according to the mustard seed likened to
the kingdom, which trees itself in the heart of the one who cultivates, so
that it becomes from every side ample in thoughts, and spread out with
doctrines instead of branches, and stretching up in height toward the aim of
piety, so that even the high and sky-faring souls, which the Gospel names
birds of heaven, can nest in the greatness of such branches. For a kind of
nest of the soul is the assent concerning what is sought, resting upon itself
the restless busyness of the mind, as some much-wandering flight. How then is
it possible, against such and so great a tree of discourses, to plant over
against it the small slip of our thought? Or this neither you command, nor
would I ever accept, that our labor be set opposite to the industry of our
father and teacher? But as farmers work wonders, devising a difference of
fruits in one plant; and the manner of the husbandry is this: stripping a
small leaf from another tree together with the bark lying at the base, they
fit it to another greater plant, at one part, by a cut of the bark, so that
that inserted thing, fattened by the natural moisture of the greater, may grow
up into a branch. So I too, grafting my thought, as some small shoot, upon the
moisture of the great tree, the wisdom of our teacher, will try to become a
branch of that, being implanted as far as is possible for me in the thoughts,
and watered by the supply of the starting-point given us from there.

For I think that some have not well recognized the aim of the labors of his
upon the Hexaemeron, for which reason they accuse that a clear knowledge has
not been handed down to them concerning the sun, how after three days the
luminary is constructed, not even together with the other stars, as if it were
impossible for the daily measure to be marked off by dawn and evening, unless
the sun altogether, setting at evening,


rising at dawn, should make it. Likewise they do not accept the creation of
two heavens, saying that even if the Apostle has mentioned a third heaven, the
difficulty remains no less in this part; because, one heaven having come to be
in the beginning, and after these the firmament, and another heaven not having
been written in addition by Moses as a second creation, it is unprovable to
think a third heaven besides these two, since neither after the firmament has
another come to be, nor does the account of the beginning allow us to think
something older beforehand. For if in the beginning heaven came to be, it is
clear that from then the creation began; for it would not have been named
beginning by the word, if it had another beginning lying above itself; for
what is second in order neither is a beginning nor is called one. And yet Paul
has made mention of a third 65 heaven, which the creation does not have.
Therefore here too the mention of the second is among the things sought. Those
then who bring forward these and such things do not seem to me to have
considered the aim of the teaching of our father, who, discoursing to so great
a people in a populous church, of necessity made the word suitable to those
receiving it. For among so many hearers, there were many also who were capable
of the higher discourses; but many times more were those who did not reach the
finer examination of the thoughts, untrained men and artisans occupied with
the seated crafts, and the people among the women unexercised in such lessons,
and the youth of the children, and those more advanced in time, all these
needed such discourses, which with some easily grasped leading of the soul,
through the appearing creation and the beauties in it, guide toward the
knowledge of him who has made all things.

So that if one judges what is said toward the aim of the great teacher,
nothing is lacking in what has been said. For he did not set up a more
contentious account, wrestling eagerly against the objections of the
questions, but he was wholly of the simpler interpretation of the words, so as
to set the account forward as suitable to the simplicity of the hearers,
together with his interpretation somehow also ascending with the hearers of
greater things, exhibiting the varied lessons of the outside philosophy. So as
to be understood by the many, and admired by those who excel. But if you, as
upon the Sinaitic mountain leaving the much people below, and being raised in
thought above the others, are eager to enter together with the great Moses
into the gloom of the contemplation of the unspeakable things, in which that
one, having come to be, both saw the unseen and heard words of things
unutterable, and you seek to know the necessary order of creation, how, heaven
and earth having come to be, the light awaits the divine command, that there
may be light, while the darkness was even without a command. And if the light
needed nothing toward lighting up the underlying air, and marking off the time
by night and day, what was the need of the construction of the sun? And if at
the beginnings the earth came to be together with the heaven, how is what has
come to be unconstructed? For to construct and to make seem to differ in
nothing according to the sense. If then to make is the same as to construct,
how is what has been made unconstructed? And the puzzles about the moist
substance, as if it were not possible for the fluid to sit upon the heavenly
vault in the spherical shape. For how would the moisture be seated upon the
convex, the fluid by every necessity flowing over from the always-peaked part
of the sphere toward the sloping parts of the shape? And how, the underlying
being unstable, will it have rest in itself, always slipping from its own
base? And how will it not be scattered, the swiftest revolution of the pole
altogether beating off what sits upon it? And yet even that the moist nature
is consumed appears unconvincing to those who contradict. Because the
collections of the waters are always 68 seen in equal measure, in springs and


rivers and lakes, except if some of the springs, having a surface supply of
water, and bubbling up from the overflow of rains or snows, in the manner of a
torrent both cease and increase together with the inflow from above. But in
those from which the stream is poured forth ever-flowing, no lessening or
increase having come about, it is necessarily confessed that nothing of the
moist substance is spent. For it is not possible that what is being spent
remain continuously in equal measure; nor would fire, if it were in truth
consuming of water, remain upon its own measure, unincreased and unfed. For it
is not possible that the nature of fire not increase together with the
material being spent. If then you, busying yourself with these and such
things, you who stretch out toward all the high things, and the things lying
in the gloom of the vision of Moses, both to see them yourself by your
prudence, look not toward another, but toward the grace in you, and by the
spirit of revelation made clear to you through the prayers, to search out the
divine depths. But since according to the apostolic law we must yield to one
another through love; and it is proper to praiseworthy servitude to bring the
commands to work, I will try, in brief as far as is possible, to uncover the
conception about these things, using your prayer as ally toward the account.
And let this be witnessed for me before the attempt, that we set nothing as a
contrary dogma to the holy Basil concerning the things philosophized by him
according to the cosmogony, not even if the account should come from some
sequence toward another interpretation; but let those things be held, and let
them bear the second prizes after the inspired Covenant alone; and let ours be
set before the readers as attempted scholastically in some exercise, no harm
coming to anyone through these, if something besides the common conception
should be found in what is said. For we do not make the account a dogma, so as
to give a starting-point to those who slander; but we confess that we only
exercise our own thought upon the thoughts at hand, not to deposit an
interpretive teaching for those after. Let no one then demand of my account
that it wrestle against the objections proposed to us from the holy Scripture,
and from the things interpreted near at hand by our teacher, as many as seem
not to agree with the common conceptions. For this is not set before me, to
invent some advocacy for the contradictions that appear from the ready sense;
but let it be granted me to examine with authority, toward my own aim, the
sense of the sayings; if somehow it should become possible for us, by the
alliance of God, the wording remaining upon its own force, to think some
connected and sequential contemplation in the creation of the things that have
come to be. "In the beginning," he says, "God made the heaven and the earth,"
and the things next in order, as many as the account of the cosmogony
contains; these then are the things that have come to be according to the
Hexaemeron. And it is necessary, I think, before the examination of the
writings, that this be confessed 69 by the account, that upon the divine
nature power is concurrent with will, and the will becomes the measure of the
power of God; and the will is wisdom. And it is proper to wisdom to be
ignorant of nothing, how each thing would come to be. And with knowledge power
also is connatural; so that at the same time he knew what needed to come to
be, the strength that works out the beings ran together, bringing what was
thought into activity, and lagging behind after the knowledge in nothing, but
jointly and without interval the work is shown together with the counsel.

For the counsel is power at the same time, both deliberating beforehand how
the beings would come to be, and furnishing the starting-points toward the
existence of the things thought. So that all the things of God about the
creation are to be thought together: the will, the wisdom, the power, the
substance of the beings. This being so, one would no longer be straitened,
searching about matter, seeking the how and the whence; such as it is possible
to hear people saying, If God is immaterial, whence the matter, how the
quantitative from the unquantitative, and the visible from the unseen, and
from the sizeless and unbounded, that which


is bounded altogether by some bulk and magnitude? And all the other things as
many as are seen about matter, how or whence did he who has nothing such in
his own nature bring them forth? For there is one solution of each of the
things brought forward to us about matter, neither to suppose the wisdom of
God powerless, nor the power unwise; but that these are with one another, and
both are shown one, so that at once and at the same the one is seen together
with the other. For his wise will was made manifest by the power of the things
being worked, and his working power was perfected in the wise will. If then
wisdom and power are in the same and at the same, he neither is ignorant how
matter would be found toward the construction of the beings, nor is he unable
to bring what was thought forward into activity. And being able all things, he
laid down together all the things through which matter is constituted, by the
wise and powerful will, toward the working-out of the beings: the light, the
heavy, the compact, the rare, the soft, the resistant, the moist, the dry, the
cold, the hot, the color, the shape, the circumscription, the interval; all of
which by themselves are concepts and bare thoughts. For none of these by
itself is matter, but having run together toward one another, they become
matter. If then by the transcending of wisdom and of power he both knows all
things and is able all things, perhaps we somehow approach the lofty voice of
Moses, who says that in a sum (for this Aquila has rendered instead of
beginning) heaven and earth have been made by God. For since the prophet has
made the book of Genesis introductory toward knowledge of God, and the aim for
Moses is to guide those enslaved to sense through the appearing things toward
the things lying above sensory grasp; and our knowledge through sight is
bounded for us by heaven and earth; for this reason the account named the
extremes of the things known to us through sense as encompassing the beings,
so that by saying that the encompassing things have come to be from God, he
might take in together everything contained within the extremes 72, and
instead of saying that God made all the beings at once, he said that in a sum,
or in the beginning, God has made the heaven and the earth. And the
signification of the two voices is one, of beginning and of sum. For through
each equally the at-once is made clear; for in the sum he sets around that all
things have come to be collectively, while through the beginning the
instantaneous and unextended is made clear. For beginning is alien to every
interval-thought. As the point is beginning of the line, and the atom of bulk,
so also the instant of temporal interval. The at-once laying-down of the
beings by the unspeakable power of God was therefore named by Moses beginning,
or rather sum, in which the all is said to have been constituted. Having said
the extremes of the beings, and having indicated the middles together with the
extremes according to what is left silent. And I say extremes because of human
sense, which neither sinks into the things under earth, nor passes through the
heaven. Therefore the beginning of the cosmogony proposes this to be thought,
that of all the beings God laid down the starting-points and the causes and
the powers collectively in an instant, and in the first impulse of the will
the substance of each of the beings ran together, heaven, aether, stars, fire,
air, sea, earth, animal, plants; which all were seen by the divine eye, being
shown by the account of the power, by him who, as the prophecy says, "knows
all things before their generation." And to the power and wisdom laid down
together toward the perfection of each of the parts of the world, some
necessary series according to a certain order followed, so that fire both
preceded and shone forth before the others of the things contemplated in the
all, and thus after that, what necessarily follows the preceding, and upon
this a third, as the technical nature compelled; and a fourth and a fifth, and
the rest of the sequential following, not appearing thus by some automatic
coincidence, according to some unordered and chance bearing. But as the
necessary order of nature seeks the sequential in the things that come to be


so he says each has come to be, having philosophized in the form of a
narrative about the natural doctrines. And writing in some commanding voices
of God of each of the things coming to be, doing this also well and in a
manner worthy of God. For everything that comes to be according to some series
and wisdom of God is some voice face to face. Because we do not know what the
substance of God is; but taking into mind the self-wisdom and the self-power,
we believe that we have taken up God in thought. For this reason, when the
whole came to be, before each of the things filling up the whole was shown by
itself, gloom was poured over the all; for the ray of fire had not yet shone
forth, being hidden in the parts of the matter, just as even the pebbles
remain unseen in the dark, even if they have by nature in themselves the
illuminating power, bringing forth fire through their collision with one
another, and when the spark has appeared from them, they too appeared together
with the brilliance of this; so all things were both invisible and unseen,
before the illuminating substance came forward into manifestation. For just
now, at once, in the one inclination of the divine will 73, the all having
come to subsist undistinguished, and all the elements being mixed in one
another, the fire sown everywhere was darkened, being obstructed by the excess
of the matter.

And since its power is sharp and easily moved, at the same time that the
signal toward the generation of the world was given by God to the nature of
the beings, it sprang out before all the heavier nature, and at once all
things were lit around by the light. And what came to be according to the
account of wisdom by the power of the maker, Moses remembered as a commanding
word of God: "God said, Let there be light, and there was light." For upon
God, according at least to our conception, the work is word. Because
everything that comes to be comes to be by a word; and nothing irrational and
coincidental and automatic is thought in the things that have their
subsistence from God. But it is necessary to believe that some wise and
technical account also lies in each of the beings, even if it is better than
our sight. What then did God say, since such a voice is representative of a
word? We will think it, as I suppose, in a manner worthy of God, referring the
saying to the account lying in the creation. For thus also the great David
interpreted such voices for us, saying: "You made all things in wisdom." For
the commanding words of the creation of the beings, which have been written by
Moses from the divine voice, these David named the wisdom contemplated in the
things that have come to be. Whence he also says that the heavens narrate the
glory of God, that is, of the technical contemplation appearing in them
through the well-fitted revolution, coming to be instead of a word for those
who know. For having said that the heavens narrate, and that the firmament
announces, he corrects those who hear the things said more thickly, and
perhaps expecting even a sound of voice and an articulated word from the
narration of the heavens, in which he says that There are no speeches nor
words whose voices are not heard, in order to show that the wisdom
contemplated in the creation is a word, even if it is not articulated. And
again, Moses having said that some extended voices of God had come to be
toward him in the wonder-working of the signs in Egypt, the Psalmist
interpreted more loftily than according to the conception of the many, saying,
"He set in them the words of his signs, and of his wonders in the land of
Ham." For that by some word the working power of each of the things coming to
be is brought into activity, the Psalmist clearly hinted through this voice,
as the word not being in verbal expressions, but the power toward the signs
being so named. Therefore here too the illuminating power ran forward and was
separated from the beings in the swift and easily moved of the nature,
separating itself from the things of other nature, and all that was shone
around was lit through its radiating power. And by what account the substance
of fire works these things, it is of God alone to say, who implanted the
illuminating account in the


nature; and this also the great 76 Moses witnesses through his own writing, in
which he says that "And God said, Let there be light;" giving this through the
things said, as I think, that the work of the light is a divine word, passing
every human thought. For we look toward the thing coming to be alone, and we
receive the wonder by sense. But where the fire, being nourished, is generated
at once; if leaping up from the collision of the pebbles, from which; or from
some other thing rubbed toward itself; and what the power is, which eats
through what has been seized by it, and lights the air with the flame, we can
neither see, nor take some thought about this; but we say that the account of
this paradoxical wonder-working is laid up in God alone, who made, according
to the unspeakable account of the power, the light to be generated for the
fire. As Moses witnesses in his own account, that "And God said, Let there be
light, and there was light; and God saw the light that it was good." For it is
of God alone truly to see how what is so good would come to be; but the
poverty of our nature sees what comes to be, but the account according to
which it come, it is able neither to see nor to praise. For praise is of the
things recognized, not of the things unknown. "God saw" then, he says, "the
light that it was good, and God divided between the light and between the
darkness." Again Moses raises what comes to be necessarily according to the
sequence of nature in some order and harmony into a divine activity; teaching,
I think, through the things said, that all things that would come out
according to the sequential through some necessary order have been thought out
beforehand by the wisdom of God. For the illuminating substance that had been
sown through the all, having run together toward the kindred, and having been
gathered all about itself, the things being obstructed by the remaining matter
of the elements were necessarily shadowed, and the shadowing was darkness.
This then that came to be sequentially, lest someone should raise it toward
some automatic coincidence, Moses says is a work of God, who implanted this
power in the things that have come to be; and yet that the nature of fire is
sharp and upward-borne and ever-moving is clear to everyone from the appearing
things; and what the account proposes to think through this beginning from the
sequential has been written together by Moses historically in the form of a
narrative: "And there was evening, and there was morning." For who does not
know that, the creation being thought in two, into the intelligible and the
sensible, all the zeal of the lawgiver now is not to interpret the
intelligibles, but to show us through the appearing things the ordering in the
sensibles? Since then the fire, at the same time that the all was constituted,
having been shot out from the elements of other nature, as some arrow, in the
upward-borne and light of the motion according to nature sprang out before the
all; and having passed through the sensible substance with equal thought, it
did not have to lead the motion onward in a straight line, the intelligible
creation having no communion toward mixture with the sensibles, and fire being
sensible; for this reason 77 the fire, having come to be at the extreme bounds
of the creation, necessarily makes the motion circular, being driven to be
borne toward the all by the power lying in the nature, while the bearing
according to a straight line has no place; for all the sensible creation is
contained by its own bounds; it travels in the extreme limit of the sensible
nature, in which it is given a way while being moved, the intelligible nature,
as we said before, not receiving in itself the course of the fire. For this
reason Moses, having followed in thought the motion of the fire, says that the
light that came to be did not remain upon the same parts, but, being carried
around the thicker subsistence of the beings, in the violence of the motion it
brings across through the circuit both the beam to the unlit and the gloom to
the lit. And perhaps according to the temporal intervals of such a succession
coming to be about the lower place, I mean of the light and of the darkness;
again Moses ascribes to God the naming of day and night, permitting us to
think that none of the things that have come to be according to the sequential


has had the beginning automatically, or from some other. For this reason he
says: "God called the light day, and the darkness he called night." For the
illuminating power being unable by nature to be still, when the beam passed
through the upper part of the circle, and the bearing was toward the
underlying, of necessity by the running-under of the fire the overlying was
shadowed, the thicker nature, as is likely, obstructing the ray. The
withdrawal of the light, then, he named evening. And again, the fire having
run around the underlying circle, and having brought the ray back to the
things above, he addressed what came to be as morning, thus naming the dawn.
And let us take up the account again a little, so that the things set beside
from the divine Scripture may also work together for us toward the series of
the contemplation given. For the account of the cosmogony said first that "In
the beginning God made the heaven and the earth." And this we supposed, that
the account presents the at-once of the constitution of the beings, indicating
also the within from the encompassing. For in the extremes the middles also
are altogether contained. And extremes as toward human sense are heaven and
earth. Because by these on either side the sight of human beings is bounded.
As then he who said that In his hand are the ends of the earth also took in
the middles together with the encompassing of the ends; so also Moses has made
the indication of the material laying-down of the whole world through the
limits; and we say that the words through the middle work together toward such
a conception. For it is written that "And the earth was invisible and
unconstructed;" so that from this it is clear that in power all things were in
the first impulse of God about the creation, as if some seminal power had been
laid down toward the generation of the all, but in activity the things
according to each were not yet. For the earth, he says, was invisible and
unconstructed. Which 80 is equal to saying that it was, and was not. For the
qualities had not somewhere run together about it; and a demonstration of this
thought is that the account says it is invisible. For the invisible is not
color; and color comes to be as some outflow of the shape according to the
surface, and the shape is not without a body. If then it was invisible, it was
also altogether uncolored. And with this the unshaped is contemplated
together; and with that the unbodied; therefore in the at-once of the
laying-down of the world, the earth was among the beings, as also all the
other things. But it awaited the through the construction of the qualities,
which is to come to be. For by saying that it is invisible, the account
indicates that not even some other quality is contemplated about it. And by
naming it unconstructed, it gives to think that it had not yet been condensed
with the bodily properties. And more clearly such a thought is made plain
through the writing of Symmachus and Theodotion and Aquila; the one saying:
"And the earth was idle and undistinguished;" and the other, "Emptiness and
nothing;" and the other, "Nothing and nothing." For it is made clear through
these, according to my account, through the idle, that in activity it was not
yet; but it had being in power alone; and through the undistinguished, that
the qualities had not yet stood off from one another and been recognized each
by itself peculiarly, but the all was seen in some confused and
undistinguished quality, neither color, nor shape, nor bulk, nor weight, nor
magnitude, nor any other of such things being contemplated by itself according
to its own account in the underlying. And the emptiness and nothing also
indicates the same thought to us. For by the voice of emptiness he hinted the
power capable of receiving the qualities; so as to learn through this that the
creator of the all laid down beforehand a receptive power of the qualities;
and this was empty and had in itself nothing, before it was filled with the
qualities. And the third account, as having been found beside from the
philosophy of Epicurus, I think we should leave uncontemplated. For that one
also says something similar about the first beginning of the beings,
indicating through such voices the empty-voiced and nothing, the insubstantial


nature of the atoms, which is similar to the nothing, and nothing. But let us
go forward again upon the continuous of the contemplation, how, the fire
having once for all gone around the highest bound of the sensible nature, the
firmament comes to be sequentially; which he says is a boundary of both the
overlying and the underlying waters. For I think that no solid and resistant
body, whether some one of the four, or even some other besides these, as the
outside philosophy imagined, is contemplated about the firmament, but the
extreme of the sensible substance, which the nature of fire goes around
according to the ever-moving power, this was said by the Scripture as
firmament, by comparison with the eternal and unbodied and intangible
property.

For who does not know that everything solid has been condensed through some
resistance altogether? And the dense and resistant is not clean of the quality
according to weight; and the heavy according to nature cannot be upward-borne.
And yet the firmament lies above all the sensible creation, therefore 81 the
sequence of the account does not give to think something thick and bodily
about it, but, as has been said, by comparison toward the intelligible and
unbodied, everything as much as is of the sensible kind is called solid, even
if by the natural fineness it escapes comprehension. Therefore sequentially,
as much as was taken through by the circuit of the fire; and the limit of the
material substance was taken through; having been once for all circumscribed
by some bound of its own, it was named firmament because of the material
nature by comparison with the underlying. And heaven it received as name, as
also he set day as name for the light, and night for the darkness. And the
distinction of the waters, which has been separated by the mediation of the
firmament, is neither alien to this conception, and it has sequentially toward
the Scripture. For it is written in the sequence of the things said, after
speaking about the earth, that "Darkness was upon the abyss, and a spirit of
God was borne upon the water." We conjecture then that the spirit of God is as
far from being darkness as it also has itself alienly from every evil. And it
is possible to set beside a thousand voices of the holy Scripture about this,
that God is also true light, and dwells in unapproachable light. And the
spirit of God is the same in nature with God himself; and if the nature of God
and of the spirit is one, and God is light, the spirit of God would altogether
be light also. And the light altogether makes those things in light, upon
which it is borne. And the water upon which the spirit of God was borne is
something other besides this downward-borne nature of the flowing waters,
which is walled off by the firmament toward the heavy and downward-borne
water. And if that also is named water by the Scripture, by which through the
higher contemplation we conjecture the fullness of the intelligible powers to
be signified, let no one be made strange through the homonymy. For God also is
a consuming fire, but the account is clean of the material signification of
fire. As then, having learned that God is fire, you thought him something
other besides this fire; so also, having been taught water borne upon by a
divine spirit, do not think the downward-borne nature that flows down upon
earth. For the spirit of God is not borne upon the low and unstable.
Therefore, so that the thought may be uncovered more clearly for us, let us
take up through brevity the mind of the things said: that the firmament which
was called heaven is a boundary of the sensible creation, and from that some
intelligible creation succeeds, in which there is not form, not size, not
position upon place, not the measure from intervals, not color, not shape, not
magnitude, not any other of the things contemplated under the heaven. And let
no one suppose me through the tropology to bring confusion upon the

things written, and the natural contemplation advocates the letter. And if the
things said have themselves deficiently, there is no envy that the leftovers
be worked out both by your understanding and by each of those who meet with
it. For neither did the contribution that came to be of the two obols from the
widow hinder the gift-bearing of the rich; nor did as many as brought the
skins and the woods and the hairs to Moses toward the tent-making become a
hindrance to those who dedicated the gold and the silver and the precious of
the stones. And we then will be content if the things from us should be judged
in the order of hairs, only may it come to be through your purple, which is
plaited with the gold, that the over-garment be set upon the account, whose
name is Oracle
