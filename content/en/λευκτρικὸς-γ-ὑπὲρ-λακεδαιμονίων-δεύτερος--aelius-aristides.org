I have come forward, men of Athens, to consider with you which of the two
sides I ought to support. And I think you too would be doing something right
if you paid attention not to those who have already judged of old, nor to
those who look to their own interest, but to those who wait upon what will
follow from the calculations. For to give the same counsel to you and to
oneself: this, it seems to me, is to consider the matter in common, without
having been forestalled. Well then, the Lacedaemonians, as you have heard and
as I too affirm, broke the treaty by seizing the Cadmea, and they made a
display of greed no less than the Thebans once did when they entered Plataea.
But the Thebans have become so strong that it has turned out to their
advantage to have been wronged. This being so, the accusation lies with the
Lacedaemonians, but the fear with the Thebans. If, then, we are judging their
case, we vote that the Lacedaemonians have done wrong and have suffered justly
what they have suffered. But if some counsel mixed with a view to ourselves as
well is before us, we shall not aggrandize the Thebans nor make them heirs of
the Lacedaemonians' arrogance. For we shall not consider whether they will
justly destroy those men, but we shall fear this: whether they will be able to
do this same thing to us unjustly. And the example which has now made us share
the Thebans' pain we shall not wish to come upon ourselves, once those who now
pull the other way have been removed. You have many charges, men of Athens,
against the Thebans, and many against the Lacedaemonians, and I will not
dispute against which of the two they are more and greater, except that I know
that if the Thebans had succeeded in one of the things they attempted, we
should not now be deliberating. But I will let pass at least what did not even
come to pass in deed, by some god's great providence. Yet it comes over me
again that of this very thing the Lacedaemonians are not altogether

without blame. For bringing the city to such a point that the Thebans could
make these declarations about it draws them justly into a share of the blame.
But in bringing us to this point the Thebans who fought with them have a share
as well, whereas in sparing us thereafter the Lacedaemonians no longer have
these men as partners. And I will not accept that other point either, that one
Theban said these things. For what they formerly did to Plataea leaves them no
such retreat or pretext, nor does it show that the argument about the vote
concerning us is of that kind. They might say also about the ancient charge
that it did not come from all of them, but that their leaders medized. But if,
for the things in which they betrayed the Greeks, and for the men they
destroyed, and for what they were about to do to us, acting in keeping with
those first deeds, and if they have been arrogant in any way at all, they will
put forward their leaders as a screen for these things, yet whenever anyone so
much as mentions the Piraeus, they will all come forward about things which, I
suppose, one man and a second said: then all of them have been called simple
in vain, being supremely wise. It seems to me unreasonable that, when we have
charges against both, we have gone after the Lacedaemonians and sought to take
vengeance, but have let the Thebans go altogether, and have so exceeded in our
anger at the former that we think we shall do wrong if we do not stand with
these men to the end. For I think we ought to punish the Lacedaemonians only
so far as we shall not let the Thebans go entirely unpunished. And I fear that
by becoming allies of the Thebans we may destroy those we already have for
ourselves. For when I look at the old charges the Greeks brought against us, I
find they had no other content than this, that we did not deal moderately with
some of them.

Now all the things one might have examined in us then, if one put them
together, do not even come near the present undertaking. For to be seen
wishing to destroy the Lacedaemonians: who would say that of all the
unpleasant things toward our subjects then

is not far greater and more grievous? So how those who were then offended at
those things will follow us I for my part cannot see. For even if some of them
hate the Lacedaemonians, as though all had suffered this, and by Zeus perhaps
the majority do, because of the harmosts and the insolence from Lysander then:
up to the point of their not having the power to do the same things they
perhaps hate them, each for their own safety; but they do not seem to me
willing even to see them utterly destroyed, knowing that the city of the
Lacedaemonians has a certain figure in Greece. And indeed you and the Thebans
must not deliberate about these things on equal terms. For it far more befits
them to take the extreme penalty for small charges than for you to take a
moderate one for the greatest. For if one reckons each party's character into
the action, he will judge theirs moderate even if it is most harsh, and yours
the greatest even if it is moderate: so great a surplus of humanity have you,
and they of cruelty. Then too it is an old custom with them to care nothing
for the common interest of Greece, but to look only to where they will get
some advantage; but you, being champions of the common rights and so reputed,
cannot overlook such things for the sake of private anger or complaint. As for
that question, whether it belongs to those from the Piraeus or those from the
city to save the Lacedaemonians, I for my part would have answered: to both,
if they are Athenians. For that other point has no reason either: that we
ourselves have sworn and bound ourselves with strong pledges that we will be
of one mind and not remember wrongs against one another, yet that we should
stir up the memory of these things against the Lacedaemonians so as to destroy
them, things we agreed to bury, and not consider or look to this, that he who
examines these things closely destroys the credit of the covenants. And those
who fear that the Thebans, failing of help from us, will call in the King, do
not fear a fear that has sense. But they seem to me to have suffered something
absurd: fearing lest they get some allies, yet persuading you yourselves to
become so to them,

and not reckoning this, that they would have been better men if they thought
it terrible that some help should accrue to the Thebans in this way,
preventing it rather than furnishing it. But as it is they advise these things
as though one ought to choose those whom one fears someone may treat well, not
those to whom one would pray that others too be added. And yet if they cannot
prevent the King from helping, they could at least have avoided calling you
in. But I do not think the Thebans so insensible or out of their minds as,
while warring with the Lacedaemonians for empire, to come to terms with the
King about slavery. For it is ridiculous to seek to destroy these men so that
they may not stand in their way, and to call that man in upon the ** whom they
know they will have as master. For they will be deliberating so much better
about us and the Lacedaemonians, by calling the King as ally, than they
themselves about themselves, inasmuch as they will leave us at least the
chance to defend ourselves against him, while they, without even raising arms
against him, will at once bow down to him; and as for us, if he should
prevail, he will have prevailed through himself, but he will have them first
through themselves. They would be mad if they wanted these things.

And if anyone thinks they take heart from their old friendship and alliance
with the barbarian, it is these very things they will fear most. For they know
that then they got no profit from all their goods, and yielded what they had
to him, first receiving the King himself, and after that Mardonius his
lieutenant, who sat among them with his cavalry and boundless army and kept
wasting some of their land and reaping the rest, used the city as a camp, and
gave no one even a scrap of free speech: so that nothing was more wretched at
that time than the Thebans, having reproach among the Greeks, and being badly
worn down by the barbarians. They will not wish, then, to come again to the
trial of such misery, nor to ask the King to send down a man who, settling in
Boeotia and becoming leader of the war, will not let them so much as grunt.
Then too, having won such a

victory and become illustrious, they would, I think, shrink from taking so
humble a figure as to go back to the barbarian and the old stain. In general
this suspicion has no reason. For where it is one of two things, either that
if they prevail they have prevailed for another and against themselves, or
that if they are beaten, which is also what happened before, they are left
behind and at our mercy: for which of these would they need the King? And as
it is, if they are struck, they will have some expectation of being saved
again through us, knowing that they themselves formerly and the Lacedaemonians
now have suffered this. They will. But if they call in the barbarian and fail,
they know they will be utterly destroyed. How then will they not choose what
makes the victory clean and the disaster hopeful for them, to fight by
themselves, rather than what makes success profitless and will leave the
matter incurable, taking the King as partner? Then too they have been shut
into a necessity of not going mad even if they wish. For the Eleans and
Arcadians who are now with them, if they perceive them bringing in the
barbarian, it is quite clear they will not stay, but will grow suspicious and
be on their guard, knowing on the one hand that the extreme danger will come
upon them, and on the other the infamy, if they themselves now join the
Thebans in attaching themselves to the man against whom they formerly arrayed
themselves with the Greeks who thought justly. The Thebans seem to me more
likely, because of the allies they now have, to neglect the King than, because
of him, to overlook these men. And in general, men of Athens, if now, while
the Lacedaemonians are still safe, we fear the Thebans may attach themselves
to the King, what shall we do when we no longer have even these as partners,
but are left alone between the Thebans and the barbarians? For if you have
some strong guarantor that if the Thebans destroy the Lacedaemonians you will
not at once be subject to them, and that they will not come against this city:
it is a terrible thing, by Zeus the Liberator at Plataea and the monuments of
those who fell there, common ancestors of both, to look on while the city of
the Lacedaemonians is driven out of Greece, and that by Thebans; nevertheless
let this

impiety be dared in speech: let us give them up, men of Athens, and look on,
making no account of it; do you want me to say we should even join in
destroying them? Or is this by no means so? But if what the Thebans have done
toward us does not yet have such intimacy and familiarity as to be greater and
more than what they formerly had toward the Lacedaemonians, of which they are
now far from remembering; and if there is no satiety of success for men, but
those who have accomplished the greatest things grasp at the greatest; and if,
being desirous of dominion, they are likely to fear our city far more than
that of the Lacedaemonians, both because of pride and because of experience in
affairs and because of sharpness and the unwearied presence in all things; and
if, having formed this opinion, it follows that they seek to put us out of the
way; and if in all things the exact pledge is not that which presents someone
as not wishing to do wrong, but that which presents him as unable even if he
wish: if these things appear so, what prevents us from saving the
Lacedaemonians at once, and putting the city in safety, and gaining in
addition a reputation for humanity, greatness of soul, and courage? For in
saving them without remembering wrongs these three things tend toward honor.

Now as to the point that the Lacedaemonians are not present under the same
fortune as we, and that we must save them by vote but destroy them by arms,
and as to this nicety, or rather this perplexity, for I hesitate to say
paralysis, it is not hard to find a simple and clear argument. If it were
possible, men of Athens, to portion it out, and to treat them badly in the war
only so far as to keep them in a state that still allowed a settlement,
perhaps someone would listen to these things. But it not being in our power so
to cut the war, what shall we do if once we overshoot? Shall we change our
minds? And what good, when the moment has passed? Since even now one battle, I
suppose, has taken everything from them: their allies, the helots, the loyalty
of the perioeci, in a word the station on which they stood. And a stumble for
those who are unsound makes the perception easy. If then the remainder are
beaten in one more battle, what prevents, in a small part of one day,

everything of theirs from being put to the proof, all those of military age
dead, the city taken, and that unfortified, children and women led away, and
the thing, so to speak, accomplished before it comes to counsel and speech?
Since even now the Thebans are calling you, I suppose, for this very thing, to
join in destroying them. For to what else would one say they call, when the
discussion has been about the city itself? We must, then, have already
deliberated, and not wait for what will nowhere come to pass. "By Zeus, we
shall seem untrustworthy to the Thebans if we do not help." Much more so, if
having helped we do not do the things for which we were taken on. What then
prevents us, if those things seem good to them, from agreeing? I mean such
things as razing the city, enslaving, killing all the children and women. For
they call us to these things. I will grant, then, that the matter will wait
for us, and that they will not be destroyed first, and that a vote will be put
forward, as though this were owed to all enemies by some necessary cycle. If
then, when we put a vote to save the Lacedaemonians, the Thebans are not
persuaded and do not yield, and this needs no demonstration, will anything
else be left but to be at variance and in difficulty? This, then, is a
beginning of war and trouble, and the Lacedaemonians have either already
perished, if the Thebans' view prevails, or they will await the decision of
the war, if we are able to snatch them out. For it is necessary either, if
they suffer something contrary to our judgment, that we go against the
Thebans, or, if they are preserved by us, that these men come against us in
anger. Which then is better: to war later with the Thebans over the shadow of
the Lacedaemonians, when they cannot share with us, or have utterly perished,
or to fight the straight and noble war on their behalf while they are still
safe? I think the latter. And if they have not experienced the same fortune as
we, this is most of all in your interest. For that they will come into far
worse if you so wish has been shown to all.

But if you prevent it, you will seem to have taken vengeance by being able,
had you wished, to do it easily, and to have shown greatness of soul by not
having needed to: so that instead of one thing you will have both, both the
vengeance and the seeming to be mild, which is yours from the beginning.

And that they have suffered, men of Athens, things far more terrible than what
they once did to you, I will easily show. We, for our part, doing and
suffering, so to speak, spent our time in many contests and battles treating
one another badly; but they, beaten in one battle, stand stripped of
everything.
It is clear from this too: they deprived us of the hegemony by sea, but they
themselves, having had both, have now thrown them away. So if being stripped
of more is a sign of a greater disaster, the present one of the Lacedaemonians
is greater than that which formerly surrounded us. Nor do I understand the
station of the alliance with the Thebans. For they themselves, as you see,
stand at the head of the war, and they need you in the part of an addition.
Now as to yielding the lead to them, judge yourselves what sort of thing that
is for you, remembering the trophy you once set up at Oenophyta. And as for
claiming the leadership yourselves, let it not be a clear piece of arrogance.
For to dispute about the presidency when called in by them, and when they are
the ones who have accomplished the greatest things, will not seem outside this
charge. Those who press to crush the Lacedaemonians in war and say we must
not let up I want to ask this: if the Thebans do not give us the hegemony, do
you bid us be their allies in the rank of subjects? "By Zeus, but they
will give it."
Not these men, at any rate, so long as they see the Lacedaemonian shields
hanging among them. And if one may guess both ways, from where and how shall
we know the exact truth? For if they give it, one may say there is no more
need to war upon these men; if they do not yield, who would say we must put up
with it? If then it happens that we decide to side with the Thebans, but when
the difference I speak of arises we split, will anything else have happened
than that we have looked on at these men too, and are in collisions and
complaints with those, and can neither go back to these

nor have anything to find that we ought to do? And if this should prevail as
seeming good to all, that each in turn should lead, for instance the Thebans,
Argives, Eleans, Arcadians, you: a fine thing, being Athenians, to draw lots
against all mankind for the hegemony, and to rule the Greeks in rotation, as
though dividing some other share or contribution. And which is better: to
take, if it so happens, the fifth part of the presidency, and for all the rest
to go on being ruled by those I named, or to receive the Lacedaemonians on
half shares and have two parts standing at the head, into which Greece has
been divided from the beginning? For you need not fear that the Phliasians at
least will dispute with you; and I am not yet saying we must receive the
Lacedaemonians on this condition. I do not define this; but I think most of
all they will even yield altogether. And by Zeus I for my part bid it, if they
truly need us and have let down something of their pride; or I would tell
their ambassadors that we shall choose the Thebans if those men submit to
these terms, and if not, it is more moderate, men of Athens, to receive the
Lacedaemonians on equal terms than to undergo such an indiscriminate
partnership with the Thebans. I think this too belongs to you to consider: how
you may not come into the same charges as the Lacedaemonians, nor imitate what
you justly blame. Now it is no small accusation against them that they joined
these Thebans in destroying Plataea and handed it over, and again last of all
that they handed the Ionians over in those treaties with the King. Do not
think, then, that joining the Thebans in destroying Sparta will have a fine
account for you, nor, blaming the Lacedaemonians because they handed over your
colonists to the King, yourselves hand yourselves over to the Thebans. And you
will hand yourselves over if you put out of their way the Lacedaemonians, with
whom they are now occupied. I will add a little more: if the Lacedaemonians,
missing you, will find other allies, what greater shame of all could there be
than first to have sought some others, and then even to have found those who
would help keep Sparta itself, while Athenians exist; but if they will get no
other

helpers, you must not neglect them, whom they have as their only ones. And I
pray for them: if you will fight with them, safety and victory; if you abandon
them, that they perish rather than recover through others, so that our
