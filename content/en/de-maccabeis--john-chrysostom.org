On the Holy Maccabees, and on their mother.
Homily 1.
1. How bright and full of joy is the city for us, and how much more splendid
than the whole year is this present day, not because the sun is sending forth
a brighter ray than usual toward the earth today, but because the light of the
holy martyrs has illumined the whole city for us beyond every lightning-flash.
For these are more brilliant than countless suns, and more manifest than the
great luminaries. Because of them the earth today is more august than heaven.
Do not speak to me of the dust, do not reckon the ash, nor the bones consumed
by time, but open the eyes of faith, and see the power of God sitting beside
them, the grace of the Spirit clothing them, the glory of the heavenly light
wrapping them round. Such rays are not sent forth from the solar circle toward
the earth as are the gleams and radiances leaping from these bodies, which
blind the very eyes of the devil.
For just as robber-chiefs and tomb-breakers, whenever they see royal arms
lying somewhere, a breastplate and a shield and a helmet, all gleaming with
gold, leap away at once, and dare neither to approach nor to touch them,
suspecting a great danger if they should dare any such=50.618= thing; so also
the demons, the true robber-chiefs, wherever they see the bodies of martyrs
lying, take to flight and leap away at once. For they do not look to their
nature, which is still mortal, but to the ineffable dignity of Christ who wore
them. For these arms neither an angel nor an archangel nor any other created
power put on, but the Master of the angels himself. And just as Paul cried out
saying, "Do you seek a proof of Christ who speaks in me?" so these too can cry
out and say, "Do you seek a proof of Christ who contended in us?"
For the bodies are precious, because they received wounds for their own
Master, because they bear the marks for Christ's sake. And just as a royal
crown, adorned on every side with varied stones, sends forth diverse gleams,
so also the bodies of the holy martyrs, marked as with precious stones by the
wounds suffered for Christ, appear more precious and more august than any
royal diadem. Those from without who set contests think it the greatest
display of honor when they bring young and vigorous athletes into the pits and
the contests, so that before the exhibition in the wrestling they may furnish
wonder to the spectators from the good condition of the limbs. But here it is
not so, but quite the contrary. For Christ, setting a contest, not such as
those, but one terrible and full of fear; for it is not a wrestling of men
against men, but a battle of men against demons; setting such a contest for
us, then, he did not bring young and vigor=50.619=ous athletes to the
wrestling, but quite young lads, and with them an old man, Eleazar, and
besides these an aged woman, the mother of the lads. What in the world is
this, Master? Do you bring the unserviceable age into the pits for the
contests? Who ever heard of a woman competing in such old age? No one has
heard of it. But this paradoxical and new thing, he says, never even received
by hearing, I will confirm through their deeds. For I am not such a president
of the games as to leave the whole to the power of the competitors; but I
stand by, and I help, and I stretch out a hand to my athletes, and of

the successes the greater part comes to them from my protection. Whenever,
then, you see a woman trembling, aged, in need of a staff, entering into a
contest, and dissolving a tyrant's madness, prevailing over bodiless powers,
mastering the devil easily, beating down his practice with much authority,
marvel at the grace of the president of the games, be astonished at the power
of Christ. The athletes are not vigorous according to the flesh, but they are
vigorous according to the faith; their nature is weak, but the grace that
anointed them is strong; the bodies are relaxed by old age, but the minds are
compacted by the longing for piety. The contest is not perceptible; do not
then study the athletes from without, but enter with the reason into the good
condition of their soul; study the strength of their faith, that you may learn
that he who boxes with demons does not need a strong covering of body, nor an
age in its prime, but even if he is quite young, even if he has come to
extreme old age, yet if he has a noble and robust soul, he is in no way
injured from his age for the contests.
2. And why do I speak of an old man and a young one, when even women have
stripped for these wrestlings and have bound on themselves brilliant crowns?
For the contests from without, examining age and nature and rank, shut out
from entrance to them slaves and women and old men and lads; but here the
theater has been opened with all freedom to every rank, and to every age, and
to either nature, that you may study both the abundance and the ineffable
power of him who sets the contest, and that you may see that apostolic saying
confirmed through the deeds, "That his power is made perfect in weakness." For
when children and old men have strength beyond nature, through all things the
grace of God who works through them shines forth brilliantly. And that you may
learn that this outward weakness of the athletes makes the crowned ones more
brilliant, come then, leaving the old man and the children, let us bring
forward into the midst the weaker of them, the woman, the aged one, the mother
of seven children; for the pangs of childbirth are no small hindrance of these
wrestlings. What then of her is first worthy of wonder? The weakness of
nature, or the ripeness of age, or the vulnerability of compassion? For these
too are great hindrances for a course that needs so great endurance. But I
have something else greater than these to say, through which we shall see both
the courage of the =50.620= woman and all the malice of the devil. And what is
this? Consider the wickedness of a foul demon: he did not drag her first into
the pits, but after the children he brought her to the wrestlings. Why? In
order that, having first overthrown her mind by the tortures of the seven
children, and having softened the firmness of her thought, her strength having
been spent beforehand through the spectacle of what was taking place in the
punishments of her offspring, he might then easily attack her when she had
become weaker. Do not look at this, that those received the tortures, but
consider this, that she, in the case of each of them, endured more grievous
pains, and in the case of each of them was slaughtered. And this that is said
those women know well who have had experience of birth-pangs and have become
mothers. Often at any rate a mother, seeing a child burning with fever, would
choose to suffer everything, so as to transfer the fire of the disease from
that body to herself; so do mothers think the misfortunes of their children
more unendurable than their own evils. And if this is true, as indeed it is
true, the mother was punished more grievously than the children in the
tortures of the children, and the martyrdom took place more greatly in the
mother than in the children. For if the sickness of one child, only being
heard of, disturbs the inward parts of her who bore it, when not one child but
so great a choir of children is being destroyed, not receiving the sufferings
by hearing but by sight


taking in what was taking place, what would she not have suffered? How did she
not go out of her mind, seeing each of them being destroyed little by little
by various and terrible punishments? How did the soul not break away from the
body? How did she not, from the first sight, leap upon the pyre, releasing
herself from the rest of the spectacle? For even if she was a philosopher, yet
she was a mother; even if she was a lover of God, yet she also wore flesh;
even if she was eager, yet she also shared in woman's nature; even if she
boiled with zeal for piety, yet she was also held by the bond of birth-pangs.
For if we, being men, often seeing a condemned man being led through the
marketplace with the rope at his mouth, being dragged to the pit, having no
claim of friendship toward him, are broken by the sight alone, although we
have a sufficient consolation against suffering any such thing, namely the
wickedness of the condemned; what is she likely to have suffered, seeing not
one condemned man being led away, but seven children at once in a single day,
not by a swift death, but being cut down by varied slaughters? For if she were
of stone, if her inward parts were compacted of adamant, would she not have
been disturbed, would she not have suffered something such as both a woman and
a mother were likely to suffer?
Consider how we marvel at the patriarch Abraham, because, having brought his
son, he bound his feet and led him up upon the altar, and then you will
see well how great was the courage of the woman. O most bitter spectacle, and
that most sweet one; most bitter because of the nature of what was taking
place, but most sweet because of the disposition of her who saw. For she did
not see the blood flowing down, but she saw the crowns of righteousness being
woven; she did not look at sides being dug through, but she looked at
eternal=50.621= tents being built; she did not see executioners standing
round, but she saw angels circling; she forgot the birth-pangs, she despised
nature, she overlooked age; she despised nature, a tyrannical thing, nature,
which is accustomed to master even the beasts.
At any rate many of the hard-to-catch beasts are caught in this way, through
sympathy toward their young taking no account of their own safety, and falling
unguardedly into the hunters' hands; and there is no animal so weak that it
does not shield its offspring; none so tame that it is not provoked when its
young are taken away. But she dissolved both the tyranny of nature stretched
through rational human beings and that through the irrational animals; and not
only did she not leap upon the tyrant's head, nor tear his face, seeing the
cubs being torn apart, but she displayed such an excess of philosophy as even
to prepare beforehand for him the inhuman banquet, and, while the former ones
were still being punished, to anoint those remaining for the trial of the same
terrible things.
3. Let mothers hear these things, let them emulate the courage of the woman,
the affection toward her offspring; so let them rear the children; for it is
not the bearing that belongs to a mother, for that belongs to nature, but the
rearing that belongs to a mother, for that belongs to choice. And that you may
learn that it is not the bearing that makes a mother, but the rearing well,
hear Paul crowning the widow not because of the birth but because of the
nourishment of the children. For having said, "Let a widow be enrolled, having
become not less than sixty years, being well reported of in good works," he
added the chief of the good works. And what is this? "If she has brought up
children," he says; he did not say, "if she has borne children," but, "If she
has brought up children."
Let us then consider what the woman was likely to suffer, if indeed one must
call her a woman, seeing fingers twitching on the coals, a head leaping up, an
iron hand being laid upon another child's head and stripping off the skin, and
him who was suffering these things still standing and conversing. How did she
open a mouth? How did she move a tongue?


How did the soul not fly away from the flesh? How, I say. She was not looking
toward the earth, but she was preparing everything toward the things to come;
she feared one thing only, lest the tyrant spare and break off the contest
beforehand, lest he split the choir of the children, lest some remain
uncrowned. And that she feared this, having all but taken the last with her
hands, she so put him into the cauldron, using toward him, in place of hands,
the exhortation and counsel of words. We do not hear others' evils without
pain, and she looked upon her own without pain. Let us not hear these things
simply, but let each of the hearers take up this whole tragedy upon his own
children; let him sketch the longed-for face, and, drawing his dearest for
himself, let him paint these sufferings upon them, and then he will know well
the power of what is said; or rather not even so; for no speech will be able
to present the sufferings of nature, but experience alone teaches. It is
timely to say toward her that prophetic word after the crown of the seven
children: "But you are as a fruitful olive tree in the house of God." In the
Olympic contests, when a thousand athletes have often entered, the crown is
limited to one only; but here from seven athletes seven crowned ones have come
to be. What land can you show me so fertile? What womb so fruitful? What such
birth-pangs? The mother of the sons of Zebedee became a mother of apostles,
but of two on=50.622=ly; but seven martyrs I do not know one belly to have
brought forth, and herself again added to them, not in the addition of one
martyr only, but in that of many more.
For the children were seven martyrs only; but the body of their mother, added,
was one body, yet filled the place of twice seven martyrs, both because she
was martyred in the case of each of them, and because she also made them such,
having given birth for us to a whole Church of martyrs. She was in travail
with seven children, and she was in travail with none for the earth, but with
all for heaven, or rather for the King of the heavens, having given birth to
them all into the life that was to come. The devil, then, was leading her last
to the wrestlings for the reason I have already said, in order that, her power
having been spent beforehand in the spectacle of the tortures, she might
become easy to handle, receiving the adversary last. For if men, often seeing
blood flowing down, have fallen, fainting, and have needed much effort, so
as to bring back the life failing them and the soul flying away from the body;
she, seeing so many streams of blood flowing down not from another's flesh but
from the flesh of the children, what would she not have endured? How great a
tumult would she not have received in the soul? The devil, then, as I have
already said, for this reason dragged her after the children to the contests,
so as to make her weaker; but the contrary came to be; for she was coming to
the contests with all the more courage.
For what reason, and why? Because she no longer feared, nor was anxious about
any children being left behind for her here, lest perhaps, having been
softened, they be deprived of the crowns; but having stored them all, as into
an inviolable treasury, in heaven, and having sent them to the crowns above
and the unshaken goods, she stripped for the contest with courage, with much
pleasure; and just as one fitting the most precious stone to a crown, she,
having fitted her own flesh to the choir of the children, departed to the
longed-for Jesus, leaving us a very great consolation and counsel, the
exhortation through deeds, so as with a robust soul and a high mind to dare
all the terrible things. For what man, what woman, what old man, what young
man will henceforth obtain pardon, or will have a defense, having feared
dangers brought


upon him for Christ's sake, when a woman, and an aged one, and a mother of so
many children, and having competed even before grace, while the gates of death
were still shut, sin not yet having been quenched, nor death having been
overthrown, is seen enduring so many punishments for God with so much
eagerness and courage? And having reckoned all these things, both women and
men, both young and old, having written the contests and the wrestlings as
upon a tablet of our heart, let us have her endurance stored in our soul as a
continual counsel toward contempt of the terrible things, in order that,
having imitated here the virtue of these saints, we may be able there also to
share the crowns with them, displaying as much endurance in the irrational
passions as they displayed philosophy in the dangers: in anger, and desire of
money, of bodies, of empty glory, and of all the other such things. For if we
should prevail over the flame of these as they did over the fire, we shall be
able to stand =50.623= near them, and to share the same boldness; which may it
come to pass that we all obtain, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord
Jesus Christ, =50.624= through whom and with whom to the Father be glory,
together with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of ages.
Amen.

On the Holy Maccabees.
Homily 2.
1. It is not possible with one tongue to praise all the holy martyrs, but even
if we have countless mouths and as many tongues, we fall short of the praises;
and I suffer the same thing looking at the successes of the seven martyrs, as
if a money-loving man, having come upon a spring flowing gold with seven
mouths, should attempt to draw it all out, but after much and ineffable toil
should go away having left the greater part. For however much of a spring you
draw out, the greater part is left behind. What then? Since we cannot
contribute what is according to worth, shall we be silent? By no means. For
they who receive the gifts are martyrs, and they imitate their own Master in
the judgment of such munificence. And how does that one do it? Whenever
someone offers gifts, not looking to the size of what was offered, but to the
eagerness of him who offered, he measures the return.
So he did in the case of that widow; the woman put down two obols, and she was
preferred to those who had put down much; for God saw not the smallness of the
money, but the costliness of the mind; two obols were the money, but the mind
was more precious than countless talents of gold. Let us then take hold of the
praises with courage, and what we did yesterday, let this, if it seems good,
come to be now also; for yesterday, having taken the mother alone, we spent
the word upon her; and we did this not wishing to split her from the choir of
the children, but to furnish the wealth more securely for us; let us then do
this now also; having drawn off one of the children, let us say a few things
upon him; for there is fear lest ever, like seven rivers, the praises of the
seven martyrs, coming together all at once, flood the word for us. Therefore
let us take one of the youths, not splitting even this one from the choir of
the brothers, but making the load light for us; for even when one is praised,
the crown will be common also to the others, since they all also shared in the
same contests. And in any case the mother will come in upon us today also,
even if we do not touch her; for the sequence of the words will altogether
draw her in, and she will not endure to leave the children; for if in the
contests she did not stand away from the


offspring, neither in their praises will she hold back. Which then of the
seven athletes do you wish us to take? The first, or the second, or the third,
or the last? Or rather no one among them is last; for it is a choir, and of a
choir neither beginning nor end appears; but in order that we may make the one
being praised more recognizable, we speak of the last in age. For the
wrestlings are brotherly, and the successes kindred; =50.624= and where there
is kinship of successes, there is no first and second. Let us then take the
last in age and of like age in mind, not of like age with the brothers only,
but even with the old man himself. This one alone of the brothers was being
led unbound to the tortures; for he did not wait for the hands of the
executioners; but he anticipated their cruelty by his own eagerness, and was
being led unbound.
And of the brothers he had none as spectator; for all had been perfected; but
he had a theater more august than the brothers, the eyes of the mother. Was I
not saying to you that even if we are not eager, the mother too will
altogether come in? See then, the sequence of the word has brought her in. And
this theater was so august and great that it had even the assembly of the
angels themselves, or rather even the brothers watching him, no longer from
the earth but from the heavens. For they sat crowned, just as in the Olympic
contests the judges, not judging the wrestlings, but hastening to receive the
crowned one. He stood then unbound, and uttering words full of philosophy;
for he wished to transfer the tyrant to his own piety; but when he could not,
he did what was his thereafter, he gave himself over to the punishment. And
that one pitied him for his age, but this one wept for him for his impiety;
for the tyrant and the martyr do not see the same things; for both have
the same eyes, but those of the flesh; but those of faith are no longer the
same; but that one was looking at the present life, this one was looking at
the life to come,
toward which he was about to fly up; and the tyrant saw frying-pans, but the
martyr saw Gehenna, into which the tyrant was about to cast himself. And if we
marvel at Isaac, because, having been bound by the father and having had his
feet tied, he did not leap from the altar, nor skip seeing a knife being
brought on; much more must one marvel at this one, because he was not bound,
nor had need of the compulsion from the bonds, nor waited for the hands of the
executioners, but himself became for himself both victim and priest and altar.
For having looked round in a circle, and having seen none of the brothers
present, he was troubled, he was in haste to hurry and overtake, so as not to
be split from this fair choir. For this reason he did not wait for the hands
of the executioners; for he feared the sparing of the tyrant, lest perhaps
having pitied him he snatch him from the communion of the brothers; for this
reason, anticipating, he snatched himself from the untamed loving-kindness.
For many things were sufficient to bend the tyrant, the youth of the age, the
punishment of so many brothers, which was sufficient even to satiate a beast,
but that one was not satiated, the gray hair =50.625= of the mother, that
nothing more had come to him from the punishment of those who went before.
2. Having considered all these things the young man threw himself into a
punishment from which it was not possible to escape, and as into a spring of
cold streams, so he plunged into the cauldrons, thinking them a divine bath
and a baptism. For just as men who are burning throw themselves into a channel
of cold waters, so that one, kindled with desire for the brothers, threw
himself toward that punishment. And the mother also brought exhortation, not
because that one needed exhortation, but that you may learn again the firmness
of the woman; for over none of the seven children did she suffer the things of
mothers, or rather over each she suffered the things of mothers; for she did
not say to


herself: What in the world is this? The choir of my children has been snatched
away from me; this one alone has been left; in this one the things of
childlessness are in danger for me; who will tend my old age henceforth, if
this one goes away? was it not enough for me to have presented the halves, and
if not the halves, the two parts? but even the one left to me alone for
consolation of old age, shall I give him too again? None of these things did
she say, she did not think, but as with hands, having lifted him by the
exhortation of words, she threw the son into the cauldron, glorifying God,
because he had received the whole fruit of her womb, and had rejected none,
but had harvested the whole tree; so that I would say with courage that she
suffered greater things than the children. For for those the greater part of
the pain and the fainting was thereafter cut short, but she, with an
unimpaired mind and an unsullied judgment, was receiving a clearer perception
of what was taking place because of nature. And one could see a triple fire,
that which that tyrant kindled, that which nature set ablaze, that which the
Holy Spirit lit. That Babylonian tyrant did not kindle such a furnace as this
tyrant kindled for the mother; for there the food of the fire was naphtha and
pitch and tow and vine-twigs; but here nature, birth-pangs, affection, the
harmony of children. Those were not so fried lying upon the fire as she was
fried because of affection; but she prevailed because of piety, and nature was
fighting with grace, and the victory was of grace; piety prevailed over
birth-pangs, and fire mastered fire, the spiritual over the natural, and over
that which was burned by the cruelty of the tyrant.
And just as a sea-rock, receiving the assaults of waves, itself remains
unmoved, but dissolving those into foam makes them vanish easily; so also the
heart of that woman, as some sea-rock receiving as waves the assaults of the
pains, itself remained unmoved, but dissolved those assaults with a firm
reasoning full of philosophy; she was ambitious to show the tyrant that she is
truly their=50.626= mother, that those are truly her genuine children, not
through the kinship of nature, but through the communion of virtue; she did
not think she was seeing a punishing fire, but a wedding torch. A mother
adorning children for marriage is not so gladdened as she rejoiced seeing them
being punished; and just as clothing one with a bridal robe, and weaving
crowns for another, and setting up bridal chambers for another, so seeing one
running to the cauldrons, another to the frying-pans, another having his head
cut off, she was exulting. And all things were full of smoke and the smell of
burning, and through all the senses she was receiving the trial of the
children, seeing through the eyes, hearing through hearing the words of the
dearest, receiving with the very nose that smoke of the flesh, that sweet and
unpleasant one; unpleasant to the unbelievers, but to God and to her of all
things most sweet; that smoke which clouded the air, but did not cloud the
mind of the woman; for she stood unbending and unturned, enduring all that was
taking place. But it is time now to stop the word, so that they may enjoy more
of the praises at the common teacher. Let fathers imitate this woman, let
mothers emulate her, and women, and men, those living in virginity, and those
clothed in sackcloth, and those wearing collars; for wherever we arrive of
austerity and philosophy, the philosophy of the woman anticipates our
endurance. Let no one then of those who have come to the peak of courage and
endurance think it unworthy to have the aged woman as teacher; but let us all
pray in common, both those who dwell in cities, and those who live in the
deserts, and those who practice virginity, and those who shine in honorable
marriage, and those who overlook all present things, and who have crucified
the body, to be able,


having taken up the same course as she, to be counted worthy of the same
boldness as she, and to stand near her on that day by the prayers of this holy
woman herself, and of her children, and of Eleazar who completes their choir,
the great and noble old man, who displayed an adamantine soul in the terrible
things. And we shall be able if, together with the prayers of those saints, we
also contribute all that is from ourselves, before wars and the terrible
things, in the time of peace mastering the passions that rule in us, cutting
away the disorderly skippings of the flesh, bruising and enslaving the body.
For thus, if we live in peace, we shall have brilliant crowns of the
exercises; and if the God who loves mankind should think it good for us to
accomplish the same contest, we shall come prepared to the wrestlings, and we
shall obtain the heavenly goods; which may it come to pass that we all obtain,
by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and
with whom to the Father, together with the Holy Spirit, be glory, honor, and
power unto the ages of ages. Amen.

On the Holy Maccabees
Homily 3.
And seeing the eulogy of deeds owed to the martyrs, and seeing the multitude
being cramped =50.626= I hesitate. Therefore, if it seems good, let us
postpone the teaching for =50.627= the present, and let us be eager to imitate
the tension of the martyrs. Therefore first let Eleazar the elder come into
our midst, the beginning of the contests, the foundation of the martyrdom, the
door of the pits, the leader of courage, the forerunner of endurance, the
youthful gray hair, the first martyr of the Old Covenant, the image of Peter
the chief of the apostles; the enemy grew weary flattering and scourging, and
the one being punished did not cease speaking; the elder stood trembling from
old age, but the tyrant sat breathing threat and murder, and the one who was
trembling went away in his prime, but the one who was vigorous went away
defeated; gray hair stood being carded, but youth was judging, growing savage
with authority, and the things of the victory came to be with the gray hair. O
novel manner of victory-prizes; one old man being struck turned a whole army
shooting together. The wonder does not let me pass over to the courage of the
young from the contests of the old man; yet we must go to them who have forced
this; for brilliant also among those are the trophies against the tyrant; for
youth was not about to appear more timid than gray hair. At any rate seven
young men in succession, excelling, were crowned, from one womb having run
into one battle-line. If it seems good, I stop the word, strong choristers of
strong martyrs. But, as I was saying, seven young men in succession,
excelling, were crowned, from one womb having run into one battle-line,
adorned with one brotherhood of birth and with manner, one throwing himself
upon another into the contests.
Now I must remember that evangelical voice upon you, O noble ones: "Blessed,"
he says, "is the womb that bore you, and the breasts which you sucked." And
since I have remembered breasts and womb, it is time for me also to pass over
to the mother of the champions, she who died many times in one body; =50.628=
or rather she who was slaughtered many times, and did not even once feel pain;
the unwounded together and the much-wounded. The first of the children being
dragged to death did not disturb her as the second, who had not yet touched
the contests, frightened her; the death of the second again did not grieve her
as the life of the third child frightened her, having an unknown end; small to
her was both a third and a fourth


child being slaughtered, the fifth not yet having been murdered; nor did the
fall of the sixth conquer the philosophy; there remained thereafter last
toward the contests the seventh, who completed the seven-toned lyre of the
martyrs. What then? Was she bent by the untimeliness of the age, did she share
the pain even with the remnant of nature? She herself then was pushing the
child toward death, if not with the hands, yet with the counsels. Do not cut
off, she says, O child, the number of the crowns; become with the birth-pangs
and the successes a partner to the brothers; imitate the communion of nature

obols in season.
But let us bring to God an offering of both souls and money and bodies, in all
things glorifying Christ, to whom be the glory and the power unto the ages of
ages. Amen.
