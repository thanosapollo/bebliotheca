What would you say of the sacred well? Or is it plain that you blame the
nature of speech, as not reaching far enough to all things, and not able at
times to show what is with sufficient power? This, at least, no single speech
could show, of what sort its beauty and its pleasure are. We are better at
drinking this water, bathing in it, and looking on it with delight than at
having anything to say about it, just as lovers of the beautiful have been
taken by the power of beauty and know of what they are in love, yet if someone
asks them they could not speak of it in its absence, though when it is present
I think they would point it out. We have suffered the same with the well. We
are lovers of it in very great numbers, or rather nearly all of us, but of
what sort it is we cannot say. If someone takes one of us aside and asks, we
would think it right to take him with us, lead him to it, and show it. Even
that will not be enough for him. Once he has tasted and tried it, he will
think he has tasted Homer's lotus, wishing to stay and finding it hard to
consent to leave. Yet we must not, as they say of the thirsty, drink in
silence, but adorn it somewhat with speech as well, and address both the
Savior god, whose work and making this also is, and the Nymphs who hold it and
work with him, granting us the use of the god's grace and serving at his side.
What beginning, then, could there be? Or, just as when we drink from it we set
the cup to our lips and no longer draw it away, but pour it all in at once,
will the speech too have everything said in a rush? Let this stand for us in
place of bringing the cup to the lip: that it lies in the fairest part of the
whole inhabited world. For the place the god chose from all lands as the most
healthful and the purest, and which by the benefits from himself he has made
the most conspicuous of all, surely this is very much

the fairest of all things on earth. For it is not as with other gods, who from
the beginning are native to other places, though these too I think must be
held in honor by the very fact that the gods obtained them as their lot. This
at least is greater: that setting out from Epidaurus itself the god fell in
love with this place most of all, as is clear from his choosing it, remaining
in it thereafter, and preferring it to the rest. And that to which a god, and
the gentlest and most humane of gods, judged it right to give his care, how
can we, and we his servants at that, say anything other than that this is the
best?

It lies, then, in the fairest part of the inhabited world, so one may say.
Further, of the sanctuary itself, as much open ground as can be walked is in
the fairest part, for it is set in the very middle. The water, if you like,
flows from a plane tree, for this too has grown beside it as a kind of token;
or if you like, what is still fairer and more sacred, it flows from the very
foundations on which the temple stands. So this belief and trust underlies for
everyone, that it is carried from a healthful place and a giver of health,
since it starts from the sanctuary and from the feet of the Savior. No water
could flow from more healthful or purer places than this, flowing from these.
Appearing in such a place and starting from such sources, as is likely, it is
the fairest. First it is the finest, nearest to air; next, what follows from
this, the lightest and the gentlest; third, the sweetest and most drinkable,
poured of itself, which when you drink you would not need wine besides. Homer
said the Titaresius flows upon the Peneius, like a man swimming on the surface
from the lightness of the water. This, it seems to me, if you dashed another
water upon it, would rise up against it, and the other would sink like divers
nodding down into the deep from the surface; unless one may even say that it
seems to me it would lift what was poured on it by its own lightness. That we
are not boasting is judged by the scale. Yet what would the offshoot of Styx
say when, set against it, it tips the balance? And there is added to this that

this stream is not an offshoot of Styx, nor has it anything else that is
dreadful. You would call it an offshoot of health, or of nectar, or of
something of that kind. This too is proof: time at least does not touch it,
but the water drawn off and brought outside does the same as that which is
always left in the well; it remains uncorrupted and unharmed. The abundance of
this well, again, is such as, so to speak, of no other well. Those who draw
must be quite unwearied and have sense, so that what closes in on them may not
get there first. This alone of all wells, whether people are drawing or
leaving it, always furnishes the same measure, doing the reverse of the
pierced jar. That is never filled, but this is always near the brim. Being a
servant and fellow worker of the most humane of the gods, it is most ready for
service and is always full. He has no leisure to do anything other than save
human beings, and this, imitating its master, always fills the need of those
who require it. It is as it were some nursling or gift of Asclepius, just as
Homer made the arms and works of Hephaestus move as that god saw fit.

Since I have now come to this point, what water among those used by men could
be compared with this for use? It is not only a drink. The same is also a bath
most pleasant and most harmless. Nor does it turn with the seasons of the
year. In summer it is coldest of itself; in winter it becomes as mild as can
be, always dissolving and comforting the hardships of the present time, as the
sacred spring of Asclepius ought to be. These things are fair and pleasant
both for the one who uses it himself and for one who sees others: in the
summer season standing in a row about the lips of the well like a swarm of
bees, or flies about milk, from dawn seeking to forestall the stifling heat in
place of any other drink of those that check

thirst and hold it back; and again when, with ice frozen hard, someone
stretches out his hand, washes, and becomes warmer than himself and more at
ease. But in other things too the god uses it as some other fellow worker, and
already for many people many times this well has helped them obtain what they
needed from the god. Just as the boys of physicians and of wonder-workers are
trained for their services and, working with them, astonish those who watch
and those who use them, so this is a discovery and possession of the great
wonder-worker who does all things for the salvation of men. It works with him
toward all things and becomes for many a substitute for a drug. Many who
bathed in it recovered their eyes; many who drank were healed in the chest and
recovered the breath they needed; of some it set the feet straight, of others
something else. Already someone drinking sent forth a voice from being
voiceless, just as those who drink of secret waters become prophetic. For some
the very drawing itself has stood in place of another salvation. For the sick
it is thus an antidote and a saving thing, and for the healthy who live with
it it makes the use of every other water not without blame. For all things
after this water become, for those who try them, as if someone after a wine
with the bouquet of flowers should drink some that has gone off. This alone is
the same for the sick and the healthy alike, most pleasant and most profitable
both for each and for both together. You would neither set milk beside it nor
long for wine. It is as Pindar made nectar, poured of itself, drinkable, mixed
enough with some divine blend. So if there were two cups, one of another water
and of the fairest wine, and the other of this water, you would be at a loss
which to take. Further, the other sacred waters have fled the use of the many,
such as that on Delos and if there is any other such anywhere else. This is
sacred by saving those who use it, not by no one touching it;

and the same suffices both for the purifications about the sanctuary and for
human beings to drink, to bathe, and, looking on it, to be glad. I would
neither compare the Cydnus, nor the Choaspes from which the king used to
drink, carrying it about, nor the Peneius, for which the earth sent up the
fairest wreaths about either bank, nor a bottomless spring, nor whatever other
water you may name, with this that is wholly sacred. I would say that it
prevails among waters by as much as the god who is its patron among gods. It
remains to say one thing: that we would also be doing what is holy in so
judging. For the god first cast this vote about it, as they say.
