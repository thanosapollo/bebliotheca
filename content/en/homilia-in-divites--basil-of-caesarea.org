OF THE SAME, HOMILY TO THOSE WHO ARE RICH

1. Things concerning this young man have been spoken to us also before, and
the labor-loving hearer altogether remembers what was then examined: first,
that he is not the same as the lawyer in Luke. For that one was a tempter,
making the questions ironic; but this one, asking soundly, yet not receiving
with ready obedience. For he would not have gone away grieving at such answers
of the Lord, if he had brought the inquiries to him contemptuously. Wherefore
his character appeared to us as it were mixed: the word showing him in one
respect praiseworthy, in another most wretched and altogether desperate. For
to recognize the true teacher, and passing by the boast of the Pharisees, and
the conceit of lawyers, and the crowd of scribes, to assign this title to the
only true and good Teacher, this was what was praised. And indeed to appear
making it a matter of care how he might inherit eternal life, this too is
acceptable. But that remaining thing convicts his whole purpose, not looking
toward the truly good, but surveying what pleases the many: that, having
learned from the true Teacher saving lessons, he did not write them on his own
heart, nor bring the teachings into deed, but went away despondent, darkened
by the passion of love of wealth. And this convicts the unevenness of his
ways, and his disagreement with himself.

You call him Teacher, and you do not do the things of disciples? You confess
him good, and you send away what is given? And yet the good is clearly a giver
of good things. And you ask concerning eternal life; but you are wholly
convicted as bound to the enjoyment of the present life. And what hard, or
heavy, or overweening word did the Teacher hold out to you? Sell your
possessions, and give to the poor. If he had set before you agricultural
toils, or the dangers from commerce, or as many other laborious things as
belong to those who make money, you ought to have been grieved, bearing the
command with difficulty; but if by so easy a way, having no toil nor sweat, he
promises to show you heir of eternal life, you do not rejoice at the ease of
salvation, but you go away with soul in pain and mourning, and you make
useless to yourself all that you have previously labored. For if you have not
murdered, as you say, nor committed adultery, nor stolen, nor borne false
witness against anyone, you make the zeal about these things unprofitable to
yourself, not adding what is lacking, through which alone you will be able to
enter into the kingdom of God. And if a physician promised to set right
mutilations of limbs that belonged to you from nature or from sickness, you
would not have been despondent hearing it; but since the great physician of
souls wishes to make you perfect, lacking in the most vital things, you do not
receive the grace, but you mourn and are downcast. For it is clear that you
are far from that commandment, and that you have falsely testified it to
yourself, that you have loved your neighbor as yourself. For behold, what was
proposed by the Lord convicts you as falling far short of true love. For if
what you affirmed were true, that you have kept from youth the commandment of
love, and have given to each as much as also to yourself, from where is this
abundance of money to you? For the care of those in need is a spender of
wealth: each receiving little toward the necessary attention, and the goods
being divided among all together, and they spending them upon themselves. So
that he who loves his neighbor as himself possesses nothing

more than his neighbor; but indeed you appear having many possessions. From
where these? or is it clear that you make your own enjoyment more preferred
than the consolation of the many. As much then as you abound in wealth, so
much you fall short in love. Since long ago you would have practiced the
alienation of money, if you had loved your neighbor. But now money has grown
upon you more than the members of the body, and the separation of them grieves
you as a mutilation of the vital parts. For if you had clothed the naked, if
you had given your bread to the hungry, if your door had been open to every
stranger, if you had become a father of orphans, if you had suffered with
every powerless person, over what money would you have been grieved? And where
would you even have been distressed putting away what remained, having long
practiced distributing it to those in need? Then, in a fair no one is grieved
letting go the things present and acquiring in return the things lacking; but
as much as he buys the highly prized things at a lesser price, so much he
rejoices, as the exchange having become splendid for him; but you are grieved,
giving gold, and silver, and possessions: that is, providing stones and dust,
that you may acquire the blessed life.

2. But what will you use the wealth for? Will you wrap yourself in highly
prized clothing? Then a little tunic of two cubits will suffice you, and the
wrapping of one cloak will fulfill all the need of garments. But will you use
up the wealth for food? One loaf is enough to fill a belly. Why then are you
grieved? as being deprived of what? of the glory from wealth? But if you do
not seek glory on the ground, you will find that true and splendid one that
leads you forward in the kingdom of the heavens. But the very having of wealth
is beloved, even if no benefit comes from it. That the zeal of useless things
is unprofitable, then, is known to everyone. Yet perhaps what I am about to
say will appear paradoxical to you; but it is more true than anything. Wealth
being scattered, in the manner the Lord suggests, is by nature wont to remain;
but being held together, to be alienated. If you keep it, you do not have it;
if you scatter it, you will not lose it. For "He has scattered, he has given
to the poor; his righteousness remains forever." But it is not for the sake of
garments nor of foods that wealth is much sought by the many, but some device
has been invented by the devil, suggesting countless occasions of expense to
the rich, so that the superfluous and useless are pursued as necessary, and
nothing suffices them toward the invention of expenditures.

For they divide the wealth both toward present need and toward the future; and
they lay up one part for themselves, and the other for children. Then they
also divide the same into occasions of varied expense. For hear what sort
their arrangements are. Let there be, he says, wealth in use, and wealth laid
away; and that which serves the needs, let it overstep the bound of
necessities; let this be present toward the luxuries in the house, let that
serve the outward displays; let one furnish luxury to the traveler, and let
the other, for the one remaining at home, make life splendid and conspicuous;
so that it comes upon me to wonder at the invention of superfluities. There
are countless vehicles, some carrying gear, others carrying themselves about,
covered with bronze and silver. Horses in great number, and these too
genealogized from nobility of fathers, as men are. Some carry them about the
city in luxury; others hunt with them, others trained for travel. Bits and
belts and collars, all of silver, all gold-spangled. Purple rugs, adorning the
horses as bridegrooms; a multitude of mules, divided by color; their drivers,
successors of one another, those running before, those following. Of the other
household servants a number infinite, sufficient for every luxury for them:
stewards, treasurers, farmers, skilled in every kind of art, both the
necessary and that invented toward enjoyment and luxury; cooks, bakers,
cupbearers, hunters,

modelers, painters, craftsmen of every pleasure. Herds of camels, some
burden-bearing, some pasturing, herds of horses, cattle-herds, flocks, swine,
their shepherds, land sufficient for the feeding of all these, and still
increasing the wealth by the revenues; baths in the city; baths in the fields;
houses gleaming round about with marbles of every kind, one of Phrygian stone,
another of Laconian or Thessalian slab; and of these some warming in winter,
others cooling in summer. A floor flowered with mosaics, gold overlaying the
roof. And as much of the walls as escapes the slab is beautified with the
flowers of painting.

3. And when the wealth, being torn into countless things, still overflows, it
is thrust under the earth, and is kept in secret places. For the future is
uncertain, lest some unexpected needs overtake us. Uncertain indeed, then,
whether you will come to the need of the buried gold; but not uncertain is the
damage of the inhumanity of the ways. For when you were not able by countless
inventions to spend out the wealth, then you hid it in the earth. A terrible
madness, while the gold was in the mines, to search out the earth; and when it
has become manifest, to make it vanish again in the earth. Then, I think, it
happens to you that in burying the wealth you bury also the heart with it. For
"Where your treasure is," he says, "there also is the heart." Therefore the
commandments grieve them; for they make life unlivable for themselves, if they
are not occupied with unprofitable expenditures. And the passion of the young
man, or also of those like him, seems to me to be similar, as if some
traveler, with desire of a certain city, having earnestly completed the road
as far as it, then should lodge somewhere there about the inns before the
walls, by shrinking from a small movement both making useless the toil that
went before, and shutting himself out from the sight of the beautiful things
in the city. Such are those who accept to do the other things, but resist the
laying aside of their possessions. I know many who fast, who pray, who groan,
displaying all the inexpensive piety, but not putting forth one obol to those
in affliction. What benefit to these of the remaining virtue? For the kingdom
of the heavens does not receive them; because: "It is easier," he says, "for a
camel to go through the eye of a needle, than for a rich man into the kingdom
of the heavens." But the sentence is so clear, and the one who spoke it is
without falsehood; but those who are persuaded are rare. And how shall we
live, having acquired nothing? he says; and what will the form of life be, all
selling and all acquiring nothing? Do not ask me the mind of the Master's
commands. He who legislated knows also to fit the possible to the law. But
your heart is tested as on a balance, whether it inclines toward the true life
or toward the present enjoyment. Those who reason soberly ought to consider
the use of wealth as a stewardship, not as enjoyment; and putting it away, to
rejoice as being separated from things belonging to another, and not to be
distressed as falling from their own. Why then are you grieved? why do you
mourn down in your soul, hearing: "Sell your possessions"? For if they
followed you into the future, not even so would they be much sought, being
overshadowed by the precious things there; but if they must remain here, why,
having sold, should we not carry off the gain from them? But you, giving gold,
and acquiring a horse, are not despondent; but putting forth perishable
things, and receiving in return the kingdom of the heavens, you weep, you
refuse the one asking, and you shake the head at the gift, inventing countless
pretexts of expenditures.

4. What will you answer to the Judge, you who clothe the walls, and do not
clothe a man? you who adorn the horses, and overlook the brother in
unseemliness? you who let the grain rot, and do not feed the hungry? you who
bury the gold, and despise the one being strangled? And if indeed a
wealth-loving wife also dwells with you, the disease is double; for she both

kindles the luxuries; and she increases the love of pleasures together, and
puts goads into the curious desires, inventing certain stones, pearls and
emeralds and hyacinths, and gold, some forging, some weaving, and through
every want of taste increasing the disease. For the zeal about these things is
not from a side-work, but nights and days have the cares concerning these. And
countless flatterers of some sort, running under their desires, gather the
dyers of flowers, the goldsmiths, the perfume-makers, the weavers, the
embroiderers. She gives the husband no time to breathe from her continual
commands. No wealth suffices serving the womanly desires, not even if it
should flow in from rivers; when the barbarian perfume is pursued among them
as the oil from the market, and the flowers from the sea, the murex, the
pinna, above the wool from the sheep. And gold, binding round the heavy-priced
of the stones, becomes for them some of it an ornament of the forehead, some
of the neck; and another in belts, and another binds the hands and the feet.
For the gold-loving women rejoice being bound with handcuffs, only if it is
gold that binds them. When then will he who serves womanly desires take care
of the soul? For as storms and tempests do to the unsound of the ships, so the
wicked dispositions of women drown the weak souls of those who dwell with
them. The wealth then being dragged toward so many things by man and woman,
overcoming one another in the inventions of vain things, reasonably has no
season to peep out toward those outside. But if you hear: "Sell your
possessions, and give to the poor," that you may have provisions toward the
eternal enjoyment, you go away grieving; but if you hear: "Give money to women
living in luxury, give to stone-cutters, carpenters, mosaic-setters,
painters," you rejoice as acquiring something more precious than money. Do you
not see these walls that have fallen down by time, whose remnants, like
certain reefs, stand up through the whole city? How many poor were there
throughout the city, when these were being raised, who through the zeal about
these things were overlooked by the rich of that time? Where then is the
splendid construction of the works? and where is the one emulated for the
magnificence of these things? Have not the one been confused and vanished, as
the things artfully made in play in the sands by children, and the other lies
in Hades, repenting at the zeal of vain things? Have a great soul; but walls
both small and greater fulfill the same need. When I pass into the house of a
man of no taste and lately rich, and see it gleaming with all kinds of
flowers, I know that this man possesses nothing more precious than the things
seen, but he beautifies the lifeless things, and has the soul unadorned. What
more need, tell me, do silver beds and silver tables provide, ivory couches
and ivory chairs, so that the wealth through these things does not pass over
to the poor, although countless stand at the door, sending forth every
pitiable voice? But you refuse the gift, saying it is impossible to suffice
those who ask. And with the tongue you swear it off, but you are convicted by
the hand; for your hand, being silent, proclaims the false speech, flashing
round about from the bezel upon the ring. How many can one ring of yours set
free from debts? how many falling houses set upright? One chest of your
garments can wrap a whole people shivering; but you endure to send the poor
man away without effect, not fearing the justice of the recompense of the
Judge. You did not show mercy, you will not be shown mercy; you did not open
the house, you will be sent away from the kingdom; you did not give the bread,
you will not receive the eternal life.

5. But you call yourself poor; and I agree. For a poor man is one in need of
many things. And the insatiable of desire makes you in need of many things. To
the ten talents you are eager to add ten others; when they become twenty, you
seek

as many again, and always what is added does not stop the impulse for you, but
kindles the appetite. For as for those who are drunk the addition of wine
becomes an occasion of drinking, so also the newly rich, having acquired many
things, desire more, feeding the disease by what is always being added, and
their zeal is overturned toward the contrary. For the things present, being so
many, do not gladden them as much as the things lacking, whatever they suppose
to be wanting to them, grieve them; so that their soul is always wasting away
with cares, making the contest toward what exceeds. For they ought to be glad
and give thanks, being so much more well-resourced; but they bear it with
difficulty and are in pain, because they fall short of one or two of those who
are exceedingly rich. When they overtake this rich man, at once they contend
to be equaled to the richer; and if they reach that one, they transfer the
zeal to the other. For as those going up ladders, always lifting the foot
toward the step that lies above, do not stand still before they reach the top;
so also these do not cease from the impulse according to power, until, having
been raised, they dash themselves down from a high fall. The Creator of all
contrived that the seleucis bird be insatiable for the benefit of men; but
you, for the harm of the many, the Creator of all contrived to be insatiable;
but you have made your own soul insatiable for the harm of the many. As many
things as the eye sees, so many the greedy man desires. "The eye will not be
filled with seeing"; and the money-lover will not be sated with taking. Hades
has not said: "It is enough," nor has the greedy man ever said: "It is
enough." When will you use the things present? when will you enjoy them,
always being held by the toils of acquisition? "Woe those who join house to
house, and bring field near to field, that they may take something from the
neighbor." And what do you do? Do you not put forward countless pretexts, that
you may take the things of the neighbor? "The neighbor's house overshadows
me," he says, "it puts in noises, or it receives the wanderer," or whatever he
happens to have accused, driving round, and thrusting out, and always
dragging, and tearing, he does not cease before he brings upon them a
necessity of migration. What killed Naboth the Jezreelite? Was it not Ahab's
desire of the vineyard? An evil fellow-dweller in the city, an evil one in the
fields, is the greedy man. The sea knows its bounds; the night does not
overstep ancient landmarks. But the greedy man does not reverence time, does
not recognize a bound, does not yield the succession of inheritance, but
imitates the violence of fire: he seizes all, he feeds upon all. And as
rivers, having started from a small first beginning, then by additions little
by little taking an irresistible increase, sweep away by the force of the
current what stands against them; so also those who have gone forward to a
great degree of power, taking from those already overpowered the power to
wrong more, enslave those remaining by those already wronged, and the
abundance of wickedness becomes for them an increase of power. For those who
have already suffered evil, furnishing them a forced help, labor together at
the harms and injustices against others. For what neighbor, what
fellow-dweller, what dealer is not swept away? Nothing withstands the violence
of wealth. All yield to the tyranny, all cower under the domination, each of
those wronged having more reason not to suffer some additional evil, than to
take justice for the things that have already come. He brings on the yokes of
oxen, he plows, he sows, he reaps what does not belong. If you speak against,
the blows; if you lament, indictments of insolence, you are liable to seizure,
you will dwell in the prison, the sycophants are ready, setting you in danger
over life. You will be content, even giving something more besides, to have
been freed from troubles.

6. I wished you to breathe a little from the works of injustice, and to give

leisure to your own reasonings, so as to consider toward what end the zeal of
the things being done has been arranged. You have so many and so many plethra
of arable land, as many others of planted land, mountains, plains, glens,
rivers, pools. What then after these things? Do not three cubits in all await
you? Will not the weight of a few stones suffice for the guard of the wretched
flesh? For what do you toil? for what do you transgress? Why do you gather
unfruitfulness with your hands? Would that it were unfruitfulness and not fuel
for the eternal fire. Will you never sober from this drunkenness? Will you not
be sound in your reasonings? Will you not become your own? Will you not take
before your eyes the judgment-seat of Christ? What will you plead, when those
who have been wronged, standing round about you, cry out against you before
the just Judge? What then will you do? what advocates will you hire? what
witnesses will you present? how will you persuade the Judge who cannot be
cheated? There is no rhetor there; there is no persuasiveness of words able to
steal the truth of the Judge; the flatterers do not follow, nor the money, nor
the bulk of dignity; deserted of friends, deserted of helpers, without
advocate, without defense, you will be taken in shame, downcast, with face
fallen, isolated, without freedom of speech. For wherever you turn the eye,
you will see the images of the evils clear: from here the tears of the orphan,
from there the groan of the widow, from another side the poor who were struck
under your fist; the household servants whom you tore to pieces; the neighbors
whom you provoked; all will rise up against you; an evil chorus of your wicked
deeds will encircle you. For as the shadow follows the body, so sins accompany
the souls, imaging the deeds clearly. Therefore there is no denial there, but
the mouth even the shameless one is stopped. For the very deeds of each bear
witness, not sending forth a voice, but appearing such as they have been
prepared by us. How shall I be able to bring the fearful things before your
sight? If perhaps you hear, if perhaps you yield, remember that day in which
"Wrath is revealed from heaven." Remember the glorious coming of Christ, when
they shall rise: "Those who have done the good things unto a resurrection of
life, and those who have done the worthless unto a resurrection of judgment."
Then eternal shame to the sinners; "And a zeal of fire about to eat the
adversaries." Let those things grieve you, and let not the commandment grieve
you. How shall I put you to shame? what shall I utter? Do you not desire the
Kingdom? do you not fear Gehenna? from where shall a healing be found for your
soul? For if the fearful things do not frighten, the bright things do not
urge, we are conversing with a heart of stone.

7. Look through, O man, toward the nature of wealth. Why are you so fluttered
about gold? Gold is a stone, silver is a stone, the pearl is a stone, each of
the stones is a stone: chrysolite, and beryl, and agate, and hyacinth, and
amethyst, and jasper. These then are the flowers of wealth; of which you lay
some away hiding them, and covering the transparent of the stones with
darkness; and others you carry about, taking pride in the gleam of the
heavy-priced of them. Tell, what benefit to you to turn about a hand shining
with stones? Are you not ashamed, craving little stones as women do when they
are pregnant? For they too gnaw little stones, and you are greedy about the
flowers of the stones, seeking sardonyxes, and jaspers, and amethysts. What
beautifier was able to add one day to life? whom did death spare because of
wealth? from whom did disease hold back because of money? How long gold, the
noose of souls, the hook of death, the bait of sin? How long wealth, the
occasion of war, for which weapons are forged, for which swords are sharpened?
Through this kinsmen fail to know nature, brothers look murderously against
one another; through wealth the deserts nourish murderers, the sea those who
drown men, the cities sycophants. Who is the father of falsehood? who the
craftsman

of forgery? who the one who begot perjury? Is it not wealth? is it not the
zeal about this? What is the matter with you, men? who has turned your own
things into the plot against you? A help toward living. Were the monies given
as provisions of evils? A ransom of a soul. Were they an occasion of
destruction? But wealth is necessary because of the children. This is a
fair-faced occasion of greed; for you put forward the children, but you fill
the heart. Do not blame the blameless; he has his own master, his own steward;
he received life from another, from him he awaits the occasions of life. Were
the Gospels not written for those who have married: "If you wish to be
perfect, sell your possessions, and give to the poor"? When you asked of the
Lord good children, when you requested to become a father of children, did you
add this: "Give me children, that I may disobey your commandments? give me
children, that I may not arrive at the kingdom of the heavens"? And who will
even be a surety of the child's purpose, that he will use what is given as he
ought? For to many wealth has become a servant of licentiousness. Or do you
not hear the Ecclesiast saying: "I saw a terrible sickness, wealth being kept
by its owner to his harm"? and again, that: "I leave it to the man who will be
after me. And who knows whether he will be wise or foolish?" See then lest,
having gathered the wealth with countless toils, you prepare material of sins
for others, then be found punished doubly, both for what you yourself have
wronged, and for what you have provisioned another. Is not the soul more
intimate to you than every child? Does it not draw near into intimacy more
than all? First render to it the rights of the inheritance, furnish it richly
the occasions of life; and then you will divide the living among the children.
The children at least, not having succeeded from parents, have often made
houses for themselves; but the soul, having been abandoned by you, by whom
will it be shown mercy?

8. What has been said has been said toward the fathers; what fair-faced cause
of stinginess do the childless put forward to us? I do not sell the
possessions, nor do I give to the poor, because of the necessary needs of
life. Then the Lord is not your teacher, nor does the Gospel order your life,
but you yourself legislate for yourself. And see into what danger you fall
reasoning thus. For if the Lord has arranged them for us as necessary, and you
set them aside as impossible, you say nothing other than that you are more
prudent than the legislator. But having enjoyed them throughout all my life,
after the end of life I will make the poor successors of what belongs to me,
having shown them masters of my things by writings and wills. When you will no
longer be among men, then you will become a lover of men; when I see you a
corpse, then let me call you a lover of the brethren. Much thanks to you for
the munificence, that lying in the tomb, and dissolved into earth, you have
become stout in expenditures and great-souled. Of what times, tell me, will
you demand the wages, of those in life, or of those after the departure from
life? But the time you lived, taking pleasure in life and dissolving in
luxury, you could not endure even to look toward the poor; and of one who has
died what deed? and what wage of work is owed? Show the works, and demand the
returns. No one does business after the fair has been dissolved; nor, coming
after the contests, is crowned; nor after the wars plays the hero; so then
neither after life is one clearly pious. You promise the good deeds in ink and
letters. Who then will announce to you the season of the departure? who will
be surety of the manner of the end? How many have been snatched by violent
accidents, not even being allowed by the suffering to break a voice? How many
has fever made out of their mind? Why then do you wait for a season in which
often you will not even be master of your own reasonings? Deep night, and a
heavy disease, and the helper nowhere; and the one lying in wait for the
inheritance is ready, managing everything toward his own use, making your
counsels of no effect. Then

looking about here and here, and seeing the desertion standing round you, then
you will perceive the want of counsel; then you will groan at the folly, unto
what season you were storing the commandment, when the tongue is slack, and
the hand already trembling, shaken by the convulsions, so as to signify the
mind neither by voice nor by writing. And yet even if all had been written
clearly, and every voice had been proclaimed expressly, one letter inserted
was enough to change the whole mind; one seal counterfeited, two or three
unjust witnesses, would have transferred the whole inheritance upon others.

9. Why then do you deceive yourself, now disposing the wealth badly toward
enjoyment of flesh, and promising afterward things of which you will no longer
be master? As the word has shown, the counsel is wicked: while living, I will
enjoy the pleasures; and having died, I will do the things commanded. Abraham
will say also to you: "You received your good things in your life." The narrow
and afflicted way does not receive you, who have not put off the bulk of
wealth. You went out carrying it; for you did not throw it away, as you were
commanded. When you lived, you set yourself before the commandment; after
death and the dissolution, then you preferred the commandment to the enemies.
For lest so-and-so receive, let the Lord receive, he says. And this, what
shall we call it, vengeance upon enemies, or love of the neighbor? Read your
wills. I wished indeed to live, and to enjoy my own things. Thanks to death,
not to you. For if you were immortal, you would not have remembered the
commandments. "Be not deceived; God is not mocked." A dead thing is not
brought up onto an altar; offer the sacrifice living. He who offers from the
surplus is unacceptable. But you offer to the benefactor what has been left
over after all the life. If you do not dare to entertain the glorious from the
leftovers of the table, how then do you dare to propitiate God from the
leftovers? See the end of love of money, you rich, and cease being disposed
passionately toward money. As much as you are a lover of wealth, so much the
more leave behind nothing of what belongs to you. Make all your own, carry all
with you, do not leave the wealth to strangers. Perhaps the household servants
will not even lay you out with the last adornment, but they will discharge the
burial as a form, having already gone over in goodwill to the heirs. Or
perhaps they will even philosophize against you then: "Want of taste," he
says, "to beautify a corpse, and to carry out expensively the one who no
longer perceives. And why is it not better that those still alive be adorned
with the expensive and glorious wrapping, than to rot together with the corpse
the highly prized of the clothing? And what benefit of a notable tomb, and an
expensive burial, and a profitless expense? One ought to use the remaining
things toward the necessities according to life." Such things they will say
both defending themselves against you for the heaviness, and gratifying those
who have succeeded to your things. Anticipating then, bury yourself. A good
burial-wrap is piety. Go away wrapped in all; make the wealth your own

who loved you, who became poor for us, that we by his poverty might become
rich; who gave himself a ransom for us. Either as a wise man who sees together
what is profitable for us let us be persuaded, or as of one who loves us let
us bear with him, or as our benefactor let us repay him. And by all means let
us do the things commanded to us, that we may become heirs of the eternal life
that is in Christ himself; to whom be the glory and the power unto the ages of
the ages. Amen.
