* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

In the notes on imitation published before, having gone through those poets
and historians whom I supposed to be the most distinguished, Quintus Aelius
Tubero, and having shown in brief what virtues of subject-matter and of
diction each of them contributes, and wherein he most becomes worse than
himself in his failures, whether because his purpose does not hunt down
everything according to the most exact reasoning, or because his power does
not succeed in all his works, in order that those who choose to write and to
speak well may have fine and approved models, on which they will make their
exercises in detail, not imitating everything that lies in those men, but
taking their virtues and guarding against their failures; and having taken up
the historians I also set out my views about Thucydides, wrapping them in a
short and summary writing, not through neglect and ease, nor through scarcity
of those who would be able to confirm the propositions, but aiming at the
fitness of what was being written, as I also did concerning the others. For it
was not possible to make an exact and detailed exhibition about each of the
men, when I had chosen to gather the treatise into the smallest bulk. But when
you wished me to compose a separate writing about Thucydides, embracing
everything that needed discussion, putting off the treatise on Demosthenes
which I had in hand, I both promised to do it as you preferred, and having
fulfilled the promise I deliver it.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But as I am about to take up the arguments in detail, I wish to say a few
things first about myself and about the kind of the treatise: not, by Zeus,
for your sake and those like you, who judge affairs from everything that is
best and think no possession more honorable than the truth; but for the sake
of the others, in whom there is much love of finding fault, whether it comes
about through zeal for the ancients, or through contempt of those of the same
age, or through both these passions, which are common to human nature. For I
suspect that there will be some among those who will read the writing who will
blame us, because we dare to declare that Thucydides, the strongest of all the
historians, both misses at times in the purpose of his speeches and grows weak
in his power; and this calculation came upon us, that we shall seem first and
alone to be introducing paradoxical novelties, if we set ourselves to slander
anything of what was written by Thucydides, not only opposing the common
opinions which all have received from long time and hold irremovable, but also
distrusting the private testimonies of the most distinguished philosophers and
orators, who set that man down as the canon of historical work and the limit
of skill in political speeches; of whom neither strong purposes. Wishing,
then, to clear away these reproaches, which have something theatrical and
attractive to the many, about myself I shall be content with saying only this
much, that having guarded throughout my life until now against this
contentious and quarrelsome and randomly barking-at-all quality, and having
published no writing in which I accuse anyone, except one treatise which I
composed on behalf of political philosophy against those who run it down
unjustly, I would not have attempted now for the first time to exhibit against
the most distinguished of the writers the malice that suits neither free
characters nor my own habit. About the kind of the writing I had more to say,
but I shall be content with a few things. And whether I have chosen arguments
true and fitting to myself, both you will judge and each of the other lovers
of letters.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

The wish of my treatise, then, is not an attack on the purpose and power of
Thucydides, nor a reckoning of the faults, nor a cheapening, nor any other
such work, in which I have thought the successes and the virtues worthy of no
discussion, and fasten upon what was not said in the strongest way; but a
certain reckoning of the character of the speeches, embracing everything that
has happened to him both in common with others and differing from the rest. In
which it was necessary that not the virtues only be spoken of, but also the
vices neighboring them. For no nature of a man is sufficient of itself to be
faultless either in speeches or in deeds, and the strongest is the one that
hits the most and misses the least. Referring, then, what will be said to this
hypothesis, let each man not become an accuser of my purpose, but a just
examiner of the works belonging to the character. And that I am not the first
who has attempted to do this, but many both long ago and in our time, having
chosen writings not quarrelsome but contemplative of the truth, though I could
furnish countless witnesses I shall be content with two men only, Aristotle
and Plato. For Aristotle is not persuaded that everything has been said in the
strongest way by his teacher Plato: among which are the things about the Idea
and the things about the Good and the things about the Republic; and Plato
himself wishes to show that Parmenides and Protagoras and Zeno and not a few
of the other natural philosophers have gone wrong; and no one blames him on
this very point, considering that the aim of philosophic contemplation is
knowledge of the truth, from which the end of life also becomes clear. Where,
then, no one blames those who differ about doctrines for their purpose, if
they do not praise everything of the elders, would anyone blame those who have
chosen to exhibit the peculiarity of styles, if they do not testify to those
before them all the virtues, even those that are not present?

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

One part still remains for me that needs a defense, a certain invidious charge
and one pleasing to the many, but able to be easily refuted as not sound. For
it is not the case that if we fall short in power of Thucydides and of the
other men, we have also lost the theoretical grasp of them. For neither are
those who do not have the same virtues as they forbidden to judge the arts of
Apelles and Zeuxis and Protogenes and the other famous painters, nor the works
of Pheidias and Polycleitus and Myron those who are not craftsmen of such
stature. For I forbear to say that in many works the layman is no worse a
judge than the artist, of those things that are grasped through irrational
sensation and by the affections, and that every art aims at these criteria and
takes its beginning from them. Let this be enough of prefaces for me, so that
I may not unawares wear out the argument on these things.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

But as I am about to begin the writing about Thucydides I wish to say a few
things about the other historians, both the older ones and those who
flourished in the same times as he, from which both the purpose of the man, by
using which he differed from those before him, and his power will become
clear. Ancient historians, then, were many and in many places before the
Peloponnesian War; among whom are Eugeon the Samian and Deiochus the
Proconnesian and Eudemus the Parian and Democles the Phygalean and Hecataeus
the Milesian, and Acusilaus the Argive and Charon the Lampsacene and
Melesagoras the Chalcedonian, and a little older than the Peloponnesian events
and stretching as far as the lifetime of Thucydides Hellanicus the Lesbian and
Damastes the Sigean and Xenomedes the Chian and Xanthus the Lydian and many
others. These used a similar purpose about the choice of subjects and had
powers not differing much from one another, some writing up the Greek
histories, others the barbarian ones, and these themselves not joining them to
one another, but dividing them by nations and by cities and publishing them
apart from one another, keeping one and the same aim, as many memories as were
preserved among the natives by nations and by cities, whether writings stored
in sacred places or in profane ones, to bring these into the common knowledge
of all, such as they received them, neither adding anything to them nor taking
anything away; in which there were also certain myths believed from long time
and certain theatrical reversals that seem to those now to have much that is
silly; and as to diction, for the most part all who chose the same characters
of the dialects practiced the same one, the clear and common and pure and
concise and fitted to the facts and displaying no technical contrivance; yet a
certain bloom and grace runs over their works, in some more, in others less,
through which their writings still remain. But Herodotus of Halicarnassus,
born a little before the Persian events, and stretching as far as the
Peloponnesian ones, both brought the purpose in subject-matter to something
greater and more splendid, having chosen to write up neither the history of
one city nor of one nation, but to gather many and different actions from
Europe and from Asia into the compass of one treatise (beginning, at any rate,
from the dynasty of the Lydians he brought the history down as far as the
Persian War, embracing in one composition all the distinguished actions of
Greeks and barbarians that happened in the two hundred and twenty years), and
he also added to the diction the virtues left aside by the historians before
him.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Thucydides, coming after these, neither wished to settle the history upon one
place, as the circle of Hellanicus did, nor to gather into one history the
actions accomplished from every land by Greeks or barbarians, imitating
Herodotus; looking down on the former as cheap and low and not able to benefit
the readers in many things; and on the latter as greater than could fall into
a survey of human reasoning according to the most exact of the manners; but
having taken in hand one war, which the Athenians and the Peloponnesians
fought against one another, he was eager to write this up; being strong in
body and sound in mind and living through the whole of it, and not composing
the actions from chance hearings, but of those at which he himself was
present, from experience, and of those from which he was left out through the
exile, inquiring from those who knew them best. First, then, in this he
differed from the historians before him, I mean in taking a subject neither
altogether single-membered nor divided into many and unconnected heads; next
in attaching nothing mythical to it, nor turning the writing aside into deceit
and bewitchment of the many, as all those before him did, relating certain
Lamias sent up from the earth in woods and glens, and amphibious Naiads coming
out of Tartarus and swimming through the sea, and mixed beasts, and these
coming into company with men, and half-divine offspring from unions of mortals
and gods, and certain other histories unbelievable for the life among us and
seeming to have much that is mindless.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

I was led to say these things not blaming those men, but even having much
forgiveness, if they also took hold of mythical fabrications, publishing
national and local histories; for among all men both in common by places and
privately by cities certain memories were preserved even of such hearings, as
I said, which children succeeding to from fathers made it their care to hand
on to their descendants, and they thought it right that those who wished to
bring them into the common should write them as they received them from the
ancients. For those men, then, it was necessary to variegate the local records
with mythical episodes. But for Thucydides, who chose one subject at which he
himself was present, it was not fitting to mix into the narrative the
theatrical bewitchments, nor to fit himself to the deceit of those who would
read, which those compositions are of a nature to bring, but to the benefit,
as he himself has shown in the preface of the history, writing word for word
thus: /and as to hearing, the non-mythical of them appears rather joyless; but
as many as shall wish to look at the clear of what has happened, and of what
is going to be at some time according to the human, such and similar, it will
be enough to judge them useful; and it is composed as a possession for always
rather than as a prize-piece for hearing on the spot./

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And it is testified for the man perhaps by all philosophers and orators, or if
not, by most at least, that he also made the greatest provision for the truth,
of which we wish history to be the priestess, neither adding to the facts
anything that is not just nor taking away, nor using license in the writing,
but keeping the purpose blameless and pure from every envy and every flattery,
especially in the judgments about good men. For having mentioned Themistocles
in the first book he has gone through the virtues belonging to him without
stint, and having taken up the policies of Pericles in the second book he has
spoken an encomium worthy of the celebrated reputation about him; and being
compelled to speak about Demosthenes the general and Nicias son of Niceratus
and Alcibiades son of Cleinias and other generals and orators, he has shown as
much as was fitting for each. And I have no need to bring examples about them
for those who have gone through his histories. These, then, one might say are
the things in which the writer succeeds about the place of subject-matter,
fine and worthy of imitation. And strongest of all is to tell no falsehood
willingly nor to stain his own conscience.

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

But the things which he constructed more deficiently, and for which some blame
him, are about the more technical part of the subject-matter, that which is
called the economic, and is sought in all writings, whether one chooses
philosophic subjects or rhetorical ones. And these are the things about the
division and the things about the order and the things about the workings-out.
I shall begin from the division, saying first that of the historians before
him, who either portioned the records by places or by times easy to follow, he
approved neither of these divisions. For neither following the places in which
the actions were accomplished did he portion the narratives, as Herodotus and
Hellanicus and certain other historians before him did; nor the times, as
those who published local history chose, portioning the records either by the
successions of the kings or by those of the priests or by the circuits of the
Olympiads or by the magistrates shown forth for the yearly offices. But
wishing to travel a certain new road, unbeaten by the others, following summer
and winter seasons he portioned the history. And from this the opposite of
what he expected has happened to him. For the division of the times has not
become clearer but harder to follow according to the seasons. At which it is
worth wondering how it escaped him that, many things happening at once in many
places, the narrative, being chopped into small cuttings, will not receive
that /far-shining light/ /and pure/, as becomes clear from the facts
themselves. In the third book at any rate (for I shall be content with this
alone), having begun to write the things about the Mytilenaeans, before
filling out the whole narrative he goes off to the deeds of the
Lacedaemonians; and not even having rounded these off he mentions the siege of
the Plataeans; and leaving this too unfinished he mentions the Mytilenaean
war; then from there he leads the narrative to the things about Corcyra, how
they stasis-ed, some bringing in the Lacedaemonians, others the Athenians; and
leaving these too half-finished he says a few things about the earlier
expedition of the Athenians to Sicily. Then having begun to speak of the
Athenians' sailing out against the Peloponnese and of the Lacedaemonians'
expedition against the Dorians he goes over the things done about Leucas by
Demosthenes the general and the war against the Aetolians; and from there he
goes off to Naupactus. And leaving the mainland wars also unfinished he takes
hold of Sicily again, and after this he purifies Delos and the Amphilochian
Argos being warred upon by the Ambraciots [lost] leaves it. And what need to
say more? For the whole book is chopped up in this way and has lost the
continuity of the report. We wander, then, as is likely, and follow with
difficulty what is shown, the mind being disturbed in the tearing-apart of the
facts and not easily nor exactly bringing up the half-finished memories of
what was heard. But the historical treatise ought to be strung together and
undistracted, especially when it comes to be about many facts and ones hard to
learn. And that this canon is not right nor proper to history is clear. For
none of the later writers divided the history by summers and winters. But all
went over to the beaten roads that are able to lead to clarity.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

And some also blame his order, as having taken neither the beginning of the
history which he ought nor having fitted to it the fitting end, saying that no
small part of good economy is both to take a beginning than which there could
be nothing prior, and to wrap the treatise with an end to which nothing will
seem to be lacking; of neither of which he has made the fitting provision. And
the writer himself has furnished them the starting point of this accusation;
for having said first that the Peloponnesian War became the greatest of the
wars before him both in length of time and in conjunctions of many sufferings,
at the end of the preface he wishes first to speak the causes from which it
took its beginning. And having set these down as two, the true one, not spoken
to all, the growth of the city of the Athenians, and the one not true, but
fabricated by the Lacedaemonians, the alliance sent from Athens to the
Corcyraeans against the Corinthians, he has made the beginning of the
narrative not from the true one and the one that seemed so to him, but from
the other, writing word for word thus: /And the Athenians and Peloponnesians
began it, having broken the thirty-year truces which came to be for them after
the capture of Euboea. And why they broke them, I wrote the causes first and
the differences, so that no one may ever seek from what so great a war was set
up for the Greeks. For the truest pretext, and the most unclear in speech, I
think the Athenians becoming great and furnishing fear to the Lacedaemonians
compelled them into the war. And the causes spoken into the open were these.
Epidamnus is a city to the right as one sails into the Ionian gulf. And
Taulantian barbarians dwell next to it, an Illyrian nation./ And after this he
goes through the things about Epidamnus and the things about Corcyra and the
things about Potidaea and the meeting of the Peloponnesians at Sparta and the
speeches spoken there against the city of the Athenians. And having stretched
these as far as two thousand lines, then he renders the account about the
other cause, the true one and the one that seemed so to him, beginning from
here: /And the Lacedaemonians voted that the truces had been broken and to
make war on the Athenians, not so much persuaded by the speeches of the allies
as fearing the Athenians, lest they become able for something greater, seeing
already the greater part of Greece subject to them. For the Athenians came
upon their affairs in such a manner, in which they were increased./ To which
he adds the deeds of the city, as many as they accomplished after the Persian
War until the Peloponnesian, in summary and cursorily in fewer than five
hundred lines. And having recalled that these were prior to the Corcyraean
events and that the war took its beginning not from those but from these, he
writes these again word for word: /And after these already, not many years
later, the things said before come to be, both the Corcyraean and the
Potidaean and as many as were set up as a pretext of this war. And all these
things as many as the Greeks did toward one another and toward the barbarian
happened in about fifty years between the withdrawal of Xerxes and the
beginning of this war; in which the Athenians both established the empire more
firmly and themselves went on to a great power, and the Lacedaemonians
perceiving it neither hindered them, except for a short time, and were quiet
the greater part of the time, being even before not quick into wars unless
compelled, and then also being shut out by wars of their own; except that the
power of the Athenians was clearly being raised and was laying hold of their
alliance; and then they no longer made it bearable, but it seemed that they
must attempt with all eagerness, and that the strength must be taken down, if
they could, by taking up this war./

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

But he ought, having begun to seek the causes of the war, first to have
rendered the true one and the one that seemed so to himself. For nature
demanded that the prior things lead the later, and that the true things be
spoken before the false, and the entry of the narrative would have become far
better if it had met with such an economy. For neither could anyone of those
wishing to defend him say that the facts were small and not worthy of speech,
or common and worn down by those before him, so that one ought not to make a
beginning from these. For he himself has supposed this place, as left out by
the ancients, worthy of history, writing in these very words thus: /And I
wrote them and made the digression of the argument, because this space was
left out by all before me; and they either composed the Greek things before
the Median, or the Median things themselves. And of these, those which
Hellanicus also took hold of in the Attic writing, he mentioned briefly and
not exactly as to the times; and at the same time it also has a demonstration
of the empire of the Athenians, in what manner it was set up./

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

This too, then, was a sufficient proof that the narrative has not been
economized by him according to the best manner, I mean its not having the
beginning according to nature. And there is added to this also its not having
ended the history at the heads at which it ought. For though the war embraced
twenty-seven years, and he lived through all this time until its dissolution,
he brought the history down as far as the twenty-second year, stretching the
eighth book with the sea-fight about Cynossema, and this though he had said
beforehand in the preface that he would embrace all the things done in this
war; and in the fifth book again he sums up the times, from which it began and
as far as which, having gone forward, it was dissolved, having written these
word for word: /and for those who asserted something from oracles this alone
happened firmly. For I always remember, both at the beginning of the war and
until it ended, it being brought forward by many that it must last three times
nine years. And I lived through the whole of it, perceiving with my age and
applying my mind so that I should know something exactly; and it happened to
me to be in exile from my own for twenty years after the generalship at
Amphipolis, and being beside both sides of the affairs, and no less with the
Peloponnesians through the exile, to perceive something of them more in quiet.
I shall therefore relate the difference after the ten years and the confusion
of the truces and the things after, how they were warred./

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

And that he is also less careful about the workings-out of the heads, either
rendering more speeches than needed to things that need fewer, or running more
carelessly over things that need more working-out, though I could confirm it
with many proofs I shall use a few; having begun to write both the first
sea-fights of the Athenians and Peloponnesians about the end of the second
book, in which against forty-seven ships of the Peloponnesians with twenty
ships the Athenians alone [lost] having fought at sea against many times more
of the barbarians they both destroyed some and took some with their men, not
fewer than as many as they sent to the war. And I shall set down his diction
too. /There happened after this also at the river Eurymedon in Pamphylia a
land-fight and a sea-fight of the Athenians and the allies against the Medes,
and the Athenians won both on the same day, Cimon son of Miltiades being
general, and they took triremes of the Phoenicians and destroyed them all, two
hundred./ And similar in him are also the things about the land battles,
either lengthened beyond what is needed or gathered into less than the
moderate. The things done for the Athenians about Pylos, at any rate, and the
things about the island called Sphacteria, in which having shut the
Lacedaemonians in they took them by siege, having begun to narrate in the
fourth book and having narrated alongside in the middle of this war certain
other actions, then turning again to the rendering of what follows, he has
gone through all that happened in the battles by both sides exactly and
powerfully, himself having rendered more than three hundred lines to the
battles, and this though neither those who perished nor those who gave up
their arms were many. He himself, at any rate, summing up the things about the
battle writes word for word thus: /And there died on the island and were taken
alive so many; four hundred and twenty hoplites in all crossed; of these two
hundred and ninety-two were brought alive, and the others died. And of these
the Spartiates were one hundred and twenty of the living, and of the Athenians
not many were destroyed./

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

And having mentioned the generalship of Nicias, when bringing sixty ships and
two thousand Athenian hoplites he sailed against the Peloponnese, and having
shut the Lacedaemonians into the forts he took by siege those in Cythera and
the Aeginetans dwelling in Thyrea and ravaged much of the rest of the
Peloponnese, from which bringing a multitude of captives he sailed away to
Athens, he has spoken thus cursorily, about the affairs in Cythera: /and a
battle having happened the Cytherians stood for a certain short time; then
turning they fled into the upper city and later came to terms with Nicias and
his colleagues, to leave it to the Athenians about themselves except death./
And about the capture of the Aeginetans in Thyrea: /And in this the Athenians
putting in and going at once with the whole army take Thyrea, and they both
burned the city and plundered what was in it, and bringing the Aeginetans, as
many as were not destroyed in hands, they arrived at Athens./ And great
misfortunes having happened about both cities right at the beginning of the
war, through which both desired peace, about the former, when the Athenians,
their land having been cut and the city ruined in its household by plague,
having given up every other help sent an embassy to Sparta begging to obtain
peace, he has spoken neither the men sent nor the speeches spoken there by
them nor those who opposed, by whom the Lacedaemonians being persuaded voted
down the reconciliations; and somehow cheaply and carelessly as about small
and inglorious affairs he has said these things: /And after the second
invasion of the Peloponnesians the Athenians, as their land had been cut the
second time and the disease pressed together with the war, were altered in
their minds, and they held Pericles in blame as having persuaded them to make
war and through him having fallen into the misfortunes; and they were eager to
yield toward the Lacedaemonians, and having sent certain envoys to them they
became unsuccessful./ And about the later one, when the Lacedaemonians, having
set themselves to recover the three hundred taken about Pylos, sent an embassy
to Athens, he has both spoken the speeches spoken then by the Lacedaemonian
and gone through the causes through which the truces were not completed.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

If, then, on the embassy of the Athenians the showing that embraced the heads
of what happened was enough, and there was no need of speeches and
exhortations which the envoys used, the Lacedaemonians having been neither
persuaded nor having received the truces, why in the world did he not keep the
same purpose also on those who arrived from Sparta at Athens? For neither did
those go away having accomplished the peace. And if these needed to be spoken
exactly, why did he leave those aside carelessly? For it was not, surely, by
weakness of power that he was shut out from finding and speaking out the
speeches that were in both. And if indeed by some calculation he chose to work
out the one embassy, I cannot conjecture on what ground he preferred the
Laconian to the Attic, the later in times instead of the former and the
foreign instead of his own and the one that happened on lesser evils instead
of the one on greater.

And being often compelled to write captures and razings of cities and
enslavements and other such misfortunes, sometimes he makes the sufferings
appear so raw and terrible and worthy of laments that he leaves no excess
either to historians or to poets; and sometimes so low and small that not even
a mark of terrible things falls into perception for those who read the man.
And speaking what he has said about the city of the Plataeans and about that
of the Mytilenaeans and about that of the Melians, I have no need to bring
those dictions in which he has worked out their misfortunes from the peak of
his power. But in those in which he runs over and makes the sufferings small
with it [lost] in many places of the history, I shall mention these: /And
about these same times the Athenians, having taken the Scionaeans by siege,
killed those in their prime, and enslaved the children and women and gave the
land to the Plataeans to dwell./ /And the Athenians again crossing into Euboea
with Pericles as general subdued it all; and they settled the rest by
agreement, but having expelled the Hestiaeans they themselves held the land./
/And the Athenians at this same time removed the Aeginetans from Aegina,
themselves and women and children, charging them not least with being causes
of the war for them; and it appeared safer to hold Aegina, lying against the
Peloponnese, by sending their own settlers./

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

And one would find many other things through the whole history either having
met with the peak of working-out and receiving neither addition nor
subtraction, or carelessly run over and having not even the least indication
of that skill, especially in the public speeches and in the dialogues and in
the other orations. Taking thought of which he seems to have left the history
unfinished, as Cratippus too, who flourished with him and gathered what was
left aside by him, has written, saying that they had become a hindrance not
