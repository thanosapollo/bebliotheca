* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

It is certain, as Sallustius Crispus and as the historians Marcus Cato and
Gellius set down in writing by way of a maxim, that all men's virtues are only
as great as the talents of those who describe each man's deeds have wished
them to appear.

Hence it is that Alexander the Great of Macedon, when he came to Achilles'
tomb, sighed heavily:

/Lucky you, young man, who found such a herald of your virtues,/

meaning Homer, who made Achilles as great in the pursuit of virtue as he
himself was great in genius.

Where this is heading, my Celsinus, you perhaps ask. The emperor Probus, under
whose rule the east, the west, the south, the north, and every part of the
world were brought to complete security, we almost no longer know, for want of
writers. For shame! The history of so great a man, and of such a kind as the
Punic wars do not have, nor the Gallic terror, nor the Pontic risings, nor
Spanish cunning, has perished.

But I will not allow it, I who not long ago brought Aurelian alone into the
open, whose life I followed as far as I could, with Tacitus and Florian
already written, that I should fail to rise to Probus' deeds. If life remains,
I mean to tell of all who are left, down to Maximian and Diocletian.

Nor do I now promise talent and eloquence, but the deeds, which I will not let
perish.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

I have used, lest I in any way deceive the friendship dearest to me, chiefly
books from the Ulpian Library, in my day in the Baths of Diocletian, and
likewise from the House of Tiberius. I have used also the registers of the
clerks of the Porphyry Portico, and the acts of the senate and people.

And since the journal of Turdulus Gallicanus, a most honourable and sincere
man, helped me greatly in gathering such a man's deeds, I ought not to keep
silent the kindness of an old friend.

Who, after all, would know Gnaeus Pompeius, shining with three triumphs of the
pirate war, the Sertorian war, the Mithridatic war, and raised high by the
majesty of many deeds, unless Marcus Tullius and Titus Livius had set him down
in writing? Would not darkness hold and cover Publius Scipio Africanus, or
rather all the Scipios, whether Lucii or Nasicae, unless historians, famous
and obscure, had stood forth as their champions?

It would be long to go through everything of this kind that must be taken as
an example even if we keep silent. This only I wish attested: I have written
the matter, which if anyone wishes he may set forth more honourably in loftier
speech.

And it was my purpose not to imitate Sallusts, Livys, Tacituses, Troguses, and
all the most eloquent men in treating the lives of princes and their times,
but Marius Maximus, Suetonius Tranquillus, Fabius Marcellinus, Gargilius
Martialis, Julius Capitolinus, Aelius Lampridius, and the rest, who handed
these and such things down to memory not so much with eloquence as with truth.
For I am one of the curious, which I cannot deny, with you urging me on, who,
though you know much, wish to know much more. And lest I speak longer of what
belongs to my plan, I will take up a great and famous prince, such as our
history has not known.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Probus, sprung from Pannonia, from the city of Sirmium, with a mother of
higher birth than his father, a moderate estate, and no great kinship, shone
as a private man and as emperor by the noblest virtues. Probus' father, as
some have recorded, was named Maximus, who, after commanding companies most
honourably, obtained a tribunate and died in Egypt, leaving a wife, a son, and
a daughter. Many say Probus was a kinsman of Claudius, that best and most holy
prince, which, because it is reported by only one of the Greeks, we shall
leave unsettled. One thing, however, I do say, which I remember reading in a
journal: that Probus was buried by his sister Claudia. As a youth Probus
became so famous for bodily strength that by Valerian's judgement he received
a tribunate when almost beardless. There survives a letter of Valerian to
Gallienus in which he praises Probus while still a youth and sets him forth
for all to imitate. From this it is clear that no one has ever reached the
height of virtues already mature, unless as a boy, formed in a nobler nursery
of virtues, he had already shown something famous.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:

These things, then, are shown by the letters. Now, as much as could be
gathered from the journal: when, already tribune in the Sarmatian war, he had
crossed the Danube and done many brave things, he was publicly decorated in
assembly with four unbarbed spears, two rampart crowns, one civic crown, four
unadorned standards, two gold armlets, one gold torque, and one sacrificial
dish of five pounds.

At that same time he freed Valerius Flaccinus, a young noble, a kinsman of
Valerian, from the hands of the Quadi. For this Valerian bestowed the civic
crown on him. Valerian's words spoken before the assembly:

/Receive, Probus, rewards for the republic; receive the civic crown for a
kinsman./

At that same time he added the Third Legion to him, with a testimonial of this
kind.

Letter about the Third Legion:

/Your deeds, dearest Probus, make me seem both too slow in handing you greater
armies and yet I will hand them over soon. Receive into your charge the Third
Legion Felix, which I have so far entrusted to no one except a man already
advanced. It was entrusted to me at a time when he who entrusted it saw me
already grey and congratulated me. But I do not wait for age in you, since you
shine with virtues/

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

It would be long if I ran through the deeds of so great a man, what he did as
a private citizen under Valerian, under Gallienus, under Aurelian and
Claudius, how often he scaled a wall, tore down a rampart, killed an enemy at
close quarters, earned the gifts of princes, and by his own valour restored
the republic to its ancient state.

A letter of Gallienus to the tribunes shows what Probus was:

/Gallienus Augustus to the tribunes of the Illyrian armies. Even if the fatal
necessity of the Persian war has held my father, I still have a kinsman in
Aurelius Probus, by whose labour I can be at ease. If he had been present,
that pretender, who should not even be named, would never have usurped the
empire for himself. Therefore I wish you all to obey his counsels, he who has
been approved by my father's judgement and by the senate's./

Perhaps Gallienus' judgement may not seem great, as he was a softer prince,
but this cannot be denied: not even a man of loose life commits himself except
to the trust of one whose virtues he thinks will help him. But let it be so:
set Gallienus' letter aside. What of Aurelian's judgement? He handed over to
Probus the men of the Tenth, the bravest of his army, with whom he himself had
done great things, with a testimonial of this kind:

/Aurelian Augustus greets Probus. That you may know/

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

It would be long now to weave in Claudius' judgements of Probus, and Tacitus'.
Yet it is said that Tacitus spoke in the senate, when the empire was offered
to him, that Probus ought to be made emperor. But I have not found the
senate's decree itself.

The emperor Tacitus himself sent Probus a first letter of this kind:

/Tacitus Augustus to Probus. The senate has made me prince by the will of a
wise army. Yet you must know that the republic has now rested more on your
shoulders. Who you are and how great, we all know; the senate knows. Be
present, then, at our needs; claim for your house, as you always do, the
republic. By a decree conferring on you the command of the whole East we have
made your salary fivefold, doubled your military decorations, and decreed you
the consulship with us for next year. For by your virtues the Capitoline
palm-embroidered robe awaits you./

Some say this was an omen of empire for Probus, that Tacitus wrote,

/The Capitoline palm-robe awaits you./

But that phrase was always written to all consuls.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

The soldiers' love for Probus was always immense. For he never allowed a
soldier to do wrong. He even often drew Aurelian back from heavy cruelty. He
went to each maniple, inspected clothes and shoes, and if there was any plunder
he divided it so that he kept nothing for himself except spears and arms.

What is more, when from plunder, whether from the Alans or some other people
(it is uncertain), a horse was found, neither handsome nor huge, which, as the
captives said, could run a hundred miles a day, and keep it up for eight or
ten days running, and all believed Probus would keep such an animal for
himself, he first said:

/This horse suits a runaway soldier better than a brave one./

Then he ordered the soldiers to put names into an urn, so that someone drawn
by lot should receive it. And since there were in the army four other soldiers
named Probus, it happened by chance that the first name to come out was
Probus, though the general Probus' own name had not been put in. But when
those four soldiers disputed among themselves and each claimed the lot, he
ordered the urn shaken again, and again the name Probus came out. When he had
done it a third and a fourth time, the fourth time the name Probus was poured
out. Then the whole army dedicated that horse to the general Probus, even
those soldiers whose names had come out wishing it.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

He also fought most bravely against the Marmaridae in Africa and defeated
them, and from Libya he crossed to Carthage and freed it from rebellions.

He also fought in single combat against a certain Aradio in Africa and laid
him low, and because he had seen a most brave and stubborn man, he honoured
him with a huge tomb, which still stands, a mound of earth raised as high as
two hundred feet by the soldiers, whom he never allowed to be idle. There
remain in Egypt works of his, which he built through the soldiers, in very
many cities. On the Nile he did so much that he alone helped the grain tax. He
built bridges, temples, porticoes, basilicas by the soldiers' labour, opened
many river-mouths, dried most of the marshes, and set crops and fields in
them. He also fought against the Palmyrenes of Odaenathus' and Cleopatra's
party who were defending Egypt, at first with success, later rashly, so that
he was almost captured; but afterwards, with his strength restored, he brought
Egypt and the greater part of the East under Aurelian's power.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

When, then, he shone with so many and so great virtues, Tacitus having been
taken off by fate and Florian seizing the empire, all the eastern armies made
him emperor.

It is no silly or inelegant tale to know how Probus took the empire. When the
news came to the armies, the soldiers' first mind was to forestall the Italian
armies, lest the senate again give a prince. But when among the soldiers there
was talk of who ought to be made, and the tribunes addressed them by maniples
in the field, saying that some prince must be sought who was brave, holy,
modest, merciful, honest, and this was said through many circles, as usually
happens, as if by a divine nod from every side all cried out,

/Probus Augustus, may the gods keep you!/

Then a rush and a turf tribunal, and he was hailed emperor, dressed even in a
purple cloak, which was taken from a statue of a temple, and from there led
back to the palace, unwilling and holding back, often saying:

/It is not to your advantage, soldiers; you will not do well with me, for I
cannot flatter you./

His first letter, sent to Capito the praetorian prefect, was of this kind:

/I never wished for empire and I took it unwillingly. I am not allowed to lay
down an office most exposed to envy. The part the soldier has put on me must
be played. I ask you, Capito, as you would share this with me while the
republic is safe,/

And since we have mentioned the senate, it must be known what he himself wrote
to the senate, and what the most distinguished order wrote back to him.

Probus' first address to the senate:

/Rightly and in order, conscript fathers, it was done in the year just past
that your clemency should give the world a prince, and indeed from among you,
who are and always have been princes of the world and will be so in your
descendants. Would that Florian too had been willing to wait for this, and had
not claimed the empire as if by inheritance; or that your majesty had made him
or someone else. Now, since he seized the empire, the name of Augustus has
been conferred on us by the soldiers, and what had been usurped has even been
avenged upon him by the wiser soldiers. I ask that you judge of my deserts; I
will do whatever your clemency commands./

Likewise a senate decree: On the third day before the Nones of February, in
the Temple of Concord, among other things Aelius Scorpianus the consul said:

/You have heard, conscript fathers, the letter of Aurelius Valerius Probus;
what is your pleasure concerning it?/

Then they cried out:

/Probus Augustus, may the gods keep you. Long worthy, and brave, and just, a
good leader, a good emperor, a pattern of soldiering, a pattern of empire. May
the gods keep you. Champion of the republic, may you rule in good fortune;
master of soldiering, may you rule in good fortune. May the gods guard you
with yours. The senate chose you before, later than Tacitus in years, before
the rest in all else. We give thanks that you have taken the empire. Guard us,
guard the republic. We do well to commit to you those whom you saved before.
You are Francicus, you Gothicus, you Sarmaticus, you Parthicus, you are all
things, and before this you were always worthy of empire, worthy of triumphs.
May you fare well, may you rule fortunately./

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

When, then, he had received this senate decree, in a second address he allowed
the fathers to hear appeals from the judgements of the great judges
themselves, to create proconsuls, to give lieutenants to the proconsuls, to
give praetorian right to the governors, and to consecrate by their own senate
decrees the laws that Probus should issue.

At once thereafter, if any of Aurelian's killers still survived, he punished
them in various ways, yet more mildly and moderately than the army had
earlier, and Tacitus later, punished them. Then he also took notice of those
who had laid snares for Tacitus. He spared Florian's associates, because they
did not seem to have followed some pretender, but their own prince's brother.
He then received all the European armies, who had both made Florian emperor
and killed him.

When this was done he made for the Gauls with a huge army, which after
Postumus' death had all been in turmoil, and after Aurelian's murder had been
seized by the Germans. There he fought so many battles and so successfully
that he recovered from the barbarians sixty most famous cities throughout the
Gauls, then all the plunder by which they were puffed up not only with riches
but also to glory. And when they were already wandering at ease on our bank,
or rather through all the Gauls, he slew nearly four hundred thousand who had
occupied Roman soil and moved the rest beyond the Neckar river and the Alba.
He took from them as much barbarian plunder as they themselves had taken from
the Romans. Opposite the Roman cities he placed camps on barbarian soil and
stationed soldiers there.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

He made fields and granaries and houses and grain-allowance for all those
beyond the Rhine, namely those he stationed on watch. Nor did fighting ever
cease, since every day barbarians' heads were brought to him, already at a
gold piece each, until nine petty kings from various nations came and lay at
Probus' feet. From them he first demanded hostages, who were given at once,
then grain, last even cows and sheep. He is said to have ordered them more
sharply not to use swords, and to wait for Roman defence if they had to be
avenged by anyone. But it seemed this could not be done unless the Roman
frontier were extended and all Germany made a province. Yet most of all, with
the kings themselves consenting, those who had not faithfully restored the
plunder were punished. He also received sixteen thousand recruits, whom he
scattered through various provinces, inserting fifty or sixty among the units
or the frontier soldiers, saying that the Roman should feel, not see, when he
is helped by barbarian auxiliaries.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

After this he made for Illyricum. Before he arrived he left the Raetias so
pacified that he left there not even a suspicion of any terror. In Illyricum
he so crushed the Sarmatians and the other nations that he recovered almost
without war all they had plundered. He then made his way through Thrace and
received all the Getic peoples, terrified by the fame of his deeds and pressed
by the power of the ancient name, either in surrender or in friendship.

When this was done he made for the East, and on the way, having captured and
killed a most powerful brigand, Palfuerius, he freed all Isauria, restoring
peoples and cities to Roman laws. He entered the places of the barbarians who
are among the Isaurians, either by terror or by courtesy. When he had gone
through them he said,

/It is easier to keep brigands from these places than to remove them./

He gave all those places which are approached by narrow ways to veterans as
private land, adding that their sons from the eighteenth year, males only,
should be sent to military service, lest they ever learn brigandage.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

When at last all parts of Pamphylia and of the other provinces neighbouring
Isauria were pacified, he turned his march to the East. He also subdued the
Blemmyes, whose captives he sent to Rome, and they offered a wonderful sight
of themselves, to the astonishment of the Roman people.

Besides this he restored Coptos and Ptolemais, cities snatched from barbarian
slavery, to Roman law. From this he gained so much that the Parthians sent
envoys to him, confessing fear and asking peace, whom he received more proudly
and sent home more afraid. A letter of his is also reported, when he had
rejected the gifts the king had sent, to Narseus of this kind:

/I wonder that you have sent so little of all that will be ours. Keep for now
all those things in which you take joy. If we wish to have them, we know how
we ought to possess them./

When he received this letter Narseus was greatly terrified, and especially
because he learned that Coptos and Ptolemais had been recovered from the
Blemmyes who had held them, and that those who had formerly been a terror to
the nations had been slain to the last man.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

Peace being made, then, with the Persians, he returned to Thrace and settled a
hundred thousand Bastarnae on Roman soil, who all kept faith. But when he had
likewise transferred many from other nations, that is from the Gepids,
Greuthungi, and Vandals, they all broke faith, and while Probus was occupied
with pretenders' wars they wandered almost through the whole world by land and
by sea, and brought no little trouble to Roman glory. These he crushed in
various encounters and various victories, a few returning home with glory
because they had escaped Probus' hands. These things Probus did with the
barbarians.

But he also had serious risings of pretenders. For he overcame Saturninus,
who had seized the empire of the East, by various kinds of battles and by his
well-known valour. When he was defeated there was such quiet in the East that,
as they said in common speech, no one heard of rebellious mice.

Then, when Proculus and Bonosus had seized the empire at Agrippina in Gaul and
were already claiming for themselves all the Britains, the Spains, and the
provinces of trousered Gaul, he defeated them, the barbarians helping him.

And lest you ask more of Saturninus or of Proculus or of Bonosus, I will
insert them in a book of their own, meaning to say a little of them, as is
fitting, or rather as necessity demands. One thing, to be sure, must be known:
that all the Germans, when they were asked for help by Proculus, preferred to
serve Probus rather than to rule with Bonosus and Proculus.

He then allowed all the Gauls and the Spaniards and the Britons to have vines
and make wine. He himself had the Alma mountain in Illyricum near Sirmium dug
by military labour and planted with a choice vine.

He also gave the Romans pleasures, and notable ones, with donatives bestowed
as well. He triumphed over the Germans and the Blemmyes. He led before the
triumph bands of all nations, up to fifty men each. He gave a most ample hunt
in the Circus, so that the people plundered everything.

The kind of spectacle was this: strong trees, torn up by the roots by the
soldiers, were fastened with beams joined far and wide; then earth was thrown
over, and the whole Circus, planted in the likeness of a wood, leafed out with
the charm of new green. Then through all the entrances a thousand ostriches
were sent, a thousand stags, a thousand boars; then fallow deer, ibexes, wild
sheep, and the rest of the grazing animals, as many as could be fed or found.
Then the people were let in; each seized what he wished. On another day in the
Amphitheatre he exhibited in one release a hundred maned lions, who with their
roaring stirred up thunder. All of these were killed from the rear doors,
offering no great spectacle in the way they were killed. For there was not
that rush of the beasts which there usually is when they come out of the
cages. Many besides were killed with arrows, who would not come forward. Then
a hundred Libyan leopards were exhibited, then a hundred Syrian; a hundred
lionesses were exhibited and three hundred bears at once. Of all these wild
beasts it is agreed the spectacle was great rather than pleasing.

Besides this three hundred pairs of gladiators were exhibited, most of them
Blemmyes fighting who had been led in the triumph, most of them Germans and
Sarmatians, some also Isaurian brigands.

When this was done, while preparing a Persian war, as he was marching through
Illyricum, he was killed by his own soldiers through a plot. The causes of his
killing were these: first, that he never allowed a soldier to be idle, since
he finished many works by military labour, saying a soldier ought not to eat
his grain-allowance for nothing. To this he added a saying heavy for them, if
it should ever happen, wholesome for the republic: that soon soldiers would
not be needed.

What had he conceived in his mind who said this? Had he not subdued all
barbarian nations under his feet and already made the whole world Roman?

/Soon,/ he said, /we shall not have need of soldiers./

What else is this to say: there will now be no Roman soldier? Everywhere the
secure republic will reign, it will possess all things. The world will not
manufacture arms, will not furnish grain-allowance; oxen will be kept for the
plough, the horse will be born for peace; there will be no wars, no captivity;
everywhere peace, everywhere Roman laws, everywhere our judges.

I go on further, out of love for the best emperor, than plain speech requires.
Therefore I will add that which especially hastened the fatal necessity for so
great a man.

For when he had come to Sirmium and wished his native soil to be made fertile
and enlarged, he set many thousands of soldiers at once to dry a certain
marsh, preparing a huge ditch, by which, with outlets thrown into the Save, he
might dry places that would benefit the people of Sirmium. Moved by this, the
soldiers killed him as he fled into an iron-clad tower, which he himself had
built very high for a lookout, in the fifth year of his reign. Afterwards,
however, all the soldiers together made him a huge tomb with raised mounds,
with an inscription of this kind cut in marble:

/Here lies the emperor Probus, and truly honest, conqueror of all barbarian
nations, conqueror also of pretenders./

When I compare the emperor Probus with other emperors, with almost all Roman
leaders, in so far as they have been brave, merciful, wise, or wonderful, I
understand that this man was either their equal or, if raging envy does not
object, better. For in the five years of his reign he waged so many wars
through the whole world, and indeed in person, that it is wonderful how he met
all the battles. He did many things with his own hand. He trained most famous
generals. For from his school came Carus, Diocletian, Constantius,
Asclepiodotus, Hannibalianus, Leonides, Cecropius, Pisonianus, Herennianus,
Gaudiosus, Ursinianus, and the rest, whom our fathers admired and from whom
some good princes arose.

Let him now compare, who wishes, the twenty years of Trajan and Hadrian; let
him compare almost as many of the Antonines. For what shall I say of Augustus,
whose years of empire one can scarcely live through? I am silent about bad
princes. Probus' own most famous saying shows what he hoped he could do, he
who said that soon soldiers would not

be needed. He, truly knowing himself, feared neither barbarians nor
pretenders. What happiness would then have shone forth, if under that prince
there had been no soldiers? No provincial would give grain-allowance, no pay
would be issued from the largesses, the Roman republic would have eternal
treasuries, nothing would be spent by the prince, nothing rendered by the
landholder. He was promising a golden time indeed. There would be no camps,
nowhere would the trumpet be heard, arms would not have to be made. That
people of soldiers, who now vex the republic with civil wars, would plough,
apply themselves to studies, be trained in the arts, sail. Add that no one
would be killed in war.

Good gods, what so offended you in the Roman republic, that you took such a
prince from it? Let them go now, who prepare soldiers for civil wars, who arm
brothers' right hands for the slaughter of brothers, who urge children to
wound their fathers, and who deny Probus the divinity which our emperors have
wisely judged should be consecrated in portraits, adorned with temples, and
celebrated with circus games.

Probus' descendants, either from hatred or from fear of envy, fled the Roman
state and set their household in Italy about Verona and Benacus and Larius and
in those regions.

This, to be sure, I could not pass over: when a statue of Probus set in the
territory of Verona was struck by lightning so that its bordered toga changed
colours, the soothsayers answered that the descendants of this family would be
of such distinction in the senate that all would hold the highest honours. But
as yet we have seen no one; descendants, however, seem to have eternity, not a
limit.

The senate took Probus' death most heavily, and the people equally. And when
it was announced that Carus was emperor, a good man indeed but far from
Probus' ways, both senate and people shuddered because of Carinus his son, who
had always lived most badly. For each man feared a harsher prince, but they
feared more a dishonest heir.

These are the things we have learned of Probus, or have judged worthy of
record. Now in another book, and a short one, we shall speak of Firmus and
Saturninus and Bonosus and Proculus. For it was not fitting that a four-horse
team of pretenders should be mixed with a good prince. After that, if life
remains, we shall begin to tell of Carus with his children.
