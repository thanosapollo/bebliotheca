* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Pausanias the Lacedaemonian was a great man, but changeable in every kind
of life: for as he shone forth in virtues, so he was overwhelmed by
vices. His most illustrious battle is that at Plataea. For under his
command Mardonius, a royal satrap, a Mede by nation, son-in-law of the
king, among the first of all the Persians both strong of hand and full of
counsel, with two hundred thousand infantry, whom he had chosen man by
man, and twenty of cavalry, was put to flight by a not so great band of
Greece, and the commander himself fell in that battle. Lifted by this
victory he began to mix very many things and to desire greater ones. But
first he was blamed in this, that from the booty he had set up a golden
tripod at Delphi with an epigram inscribed, in which this was the sense:
that by his own leadership the barbarians had been destroyed at Plataea
and that for the sake of that victory he had given that gift to Apollo.
These verses the Lacedaemonians cut out and wrote nothing other than the
names of those cities by whose aid the Persians had been conquered.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
After that battle they sent the same Pausanias with the common fleet to
Cyprus and the Hellespont, that he might drive out the garrisons of the
barbarians from those regions. Having used equal good fortune in that
matter he began to carry himself more loftily and to seek greater things.
For when, Byzantium having been stormed, he had taken several nobles of
the Persians and among them some kinsmen of the king, he sent these
secretly back to Xerxes, pretending that they had escaped from the public
chains, and with them Gongylus the Eretrian, who should deliver a letter
to the king, in which Thucydides has handed down to memory that these
things had been written: 'Pausanias, commander of Sparta, those whom he
had taken at Byzantium, after he recognized them as your kinsmen, has
sent to you as a gift and desires to be joined with you by affinity:
wherefore, if it seems good to you, give him your daughter in marriage.
If you shall have done that, he promises that with you helping he will
reduce both Sparta and the rest of Greece under your power. If you shall
have wished to do anything about these things, see that you send a sure
man to him, with whom he may speak.' The king, greatly rejoiced at the
safety of so many men so necessary to himself, at once sends Artabazus
with a letter to Pausanias in which he praises him and asks that he spare
no thing for accomplishing those things which he was promising: if he
shall have completed it, he will meet with a refusal of no thing from
him. Pausanias, this will having been known, made more eager for carrying
on the matter, fell into the suspicion of the Lacedaemonians. Which
having been done, recalled home, accused of a capital charge he is
acquitted, yet he is fined with money, for which cause he was not sent
back to the fleet.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But he not much after of his own will returned to the army and there
disclosed his thoughts by a plan not stupid, but mad: for he changed not
only the ancestral customs, but even the dress and clothing. He used
royal equipment, Median clothing; Median and Egyptian attendants followed
him; he feasted in the manner of the Persians more luxuriously than those
who were present could endure; he did not give access of meeting to those
seeking it, he answered proudly, he commanded cruelly. He was unwilling
to return to Sparta; he had betaken himself to Colonae, which place is in
the land of the Troad; there he was taking counsels hostile both to the
fatherland and to himself. After the Lacedaemonians found this out, they
sent envoys to him with a staff, on which according to their custom it
had been written: unless he returned home, they would condemn him to
death. Moved by this message, hoping that even then by money and power he
could drive off the impending danger, he returned home. When he came
hither, he was thrown into public chains by the ephors: for it is
permitted by their laws to any ephor to do this to a king. From this
nevertheless he freed himself, nor for that did he the more lack
suspicion: for the opinion remained that he had alliance with the king.
There is a certain kind of men, which is called Hilotae, of whom a great
multitude tills the fields of the Lacedaemonians and performs the duty of
slaves. These also he was thought to be stirring up by hope of liberty.
But because there was no open charge of these things by which he could be
convicted, they did not think that it was proper for such and so famous a
man to be judged by suspicions and that it must be awaited until the
thing itself should open itself.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Meanwhile a certain Argilian youth, whom as a boy Pausanias had loved
with venereal love, when he had received a letter from him to Artabazus
and it had come into his suspicion that something in it had been written
about himself, because none of those had returned who had been sent to
the same place on such a cause, loosened the bindings of the letter and,
the seal having been taken off, learned that if he had carried it
through, he must perish. There were in the same letter things which
pertained to those things which had been agreed between the king and
Pausanias. These letters he delivered to the ephors. The gravity of the
Lacedaemonians is not to be passed over in this place. For they were not
even by this man's information driven to seize Pausanias, nor did they
think force should be applied before he had informed against himself.
Therefore they instructed this informer what they wished to be done.
There is a shrine of Neptune at Taenarum, which the Greeks think it
impious to violate. Thither that informer fled and sat down on the altar.
Next to this they made a place under the earth, from which it could be
heard if anyone should speak anything with the Argilian. Hither certain
of the ephors went down. Pausanias, when he heard that the Argilian had
fled to the altar, disturbed, came thither. Whom when he saw sitting on
the altar as a suppliant of the god, he asks what is the cause of so
sudden a plan. To him that man opens what he had found out from the
letter. By which Pausanias, more disturbed, began to beg that he not
announce it nor betray him who had deserved best of him: but if he had
given him that pardon and had lifted him implicated in such great
affairs, it would be a great reward for him.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
These things having been learned, the ephors thought it better that he be
seized in the city. When they had set out thither and Pausanias, the
Argilian having been appeased, as he thought, was returning to
Lacedaemon, on the way, when he was already at the point of being seized,
from the face of a certain ephor, who wished him to be warned, he
understood that an ambush was being made for himself. Therefore a few
steps before those who were following him, he fled into the temple of
Minerva, which is called Chalcioicos. That he might not be able to go out
from here, the ephors at once blocked up the doors of that temple and
demolished the roof, that he might more quickly perish under the open
sky. It is said that at that time the mother of Pausanias was living and
that she, already of great age, after she found out about the crime of
her son, was among the first to have brought a stone to the entrance of
the temple for shutting in her son. When this man had been carried
half-alive from the temple, he at once breathed out his life. Thus
Pausanias stained great glory of war with a shameful death. Whose dead
body, when some said it ought to be carried into the same place to which
those who had been given over to punishment, it displeased the more, and
they buried him far from that place where he had died. Thence afterwards
by the response of the Delphic god he was dug up and was buried in the
same place where he had laid down his life.
