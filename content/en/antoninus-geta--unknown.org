* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I know, Constantine Augustus, that both many people and Your Clemency
can raise the question why even Geta Antoninus is related by me,
concerning whom, before I speak of either his life or his death, I
shall discuss why the name Antoninus was also given to him by Severus

his father, for not many things can be said in his life, who was taken
from human affairs before he held the empire with his brother.

Septimius Severus at a certain time, when he had consulted and asked
that it be shown to him with what successor he would die, saw in
dreams that an Antoninus would succeed him.

wherefore he went at once to the soldiers and named Bassianus, the
elder son, Marcus Aurelius

Antoninus. when he had done this,

either

by a father's thought or, as some say, warned by his wife Julia, who
knew of the dream, that by this act he had himself closed the way to
empire for the younger son, he ordered that Geta also, the younger
son, be called Antoninus.

and so he was always spoken of by him in familiar letters

when if by chance he was away he would write,
/Greet the Antonini, my sons and successors./
but the father's precaution availed nothing, for he alone succeeded
him who first received the name of Antoninus. and this concerning the
name of Antoninus.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But he was called Geta either from the name of his uncle or of his
paternal grandfather, concerning whose life and character in the Life
of Severus Marius

Maximus in the first septenary related quite fully. but Antoninus Geta
was also so called for this reason, that Severus had it in mind that
all princes thereafter, just as they were called Augusti, should also
be called Antonini, and indeed

from love of Marcus, whom he always called his brother

and whose philosophy and training in letters

he always imitated. some say that the name of Antoninus was conferred
not only in honor of Marcus, since Marcus had it by adoption, but in
honor of him who was surnamed Pius, namely the successor of Hadrian,

and indeed for this reason, that that man had chosen Severus for the
advocacy of the fiscus from the forensic roll, when the omen of the
first

step or honor given by Antoninus had opened the way for him to such
great advancement, and at the same time because no emperor seemed to
him more fortunate for lending a name

than that prince whose own name had already run through four princes.

Concerning this same man Severus, knowing his nativity, in which, like
most of the Africans, he was most skilled, is said to have said:

/It seems wonderful to me, most beloved Juvenalis, that our Geta is to
be a god, in whose nativity I see nothing imperial/
, for Juvenalis was prefect of his

praetorian guard, and he was not deceived, for Bassianus, when he had
killed him and feared the tyrant's brand from parricide and heard that
the crime could be mitigated if he called his brother a god,

is said to have said,
/Let him be a god, so long as he is not alive./
finally he enrolled him among the gods and therefore the parricide
somehow returned with

his reputation into favor.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Geta was born at Milan in the consulship of Severus and Vitellius,
although others have related otherwise, on the sixth day before the
Kalends of June, of Julia, whom Severus had married for this reason,
that he had learned she had it in her nativity that she would be a
king's wife,

and he was still a private citizen but already of the highest place in
the state. as soon as he was born, it was announced that a hen in the
palace had laid

a purple egg, which when it was brought Bassianus his brother received
and, as a little child, dashed it to the ground and broke it, Julia is
said to have said in jest,
/Accursed

parricide, you have killed your brother./
and that which was said in jest

Severus took more deeply than anyone present, but by the bystanders
afterwards it was approved as poured forth as if by divine
inspiration. there was also another omen: for when on the estate of a
certain Antoninus, a man of the people, a lamb had been born which had
a purple fleece on its forehead, on the same day and hour that Geta
was born, and that man had heard from a soothsayer that after Severus
an Antoninus would be emperor, and he was taking the omen of himself
but nevertheless

feared the sign of such a fate, he put it to the sword, which itself
was a sign that Geta would be slain by an Antoninus,

as afterwards became quite clear. there was also another huge omen, as
afterwards

the outcome taught, of this crime which came to pass:

for when Severus wished to commend the birthday of the infant Geta, a
sacrificial attendant named Antoninus struck the victim.

which then was neither inquired into nor noticed, but afterwards was
understood.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

He was a handsome youth, of harsh manners, but not impious, a handler
of love-affairs,

gluttonous, greedy for foods

and wine variously seasoned. this is related as notable of him as a
boy, that when Severus wished to kill the men of the opposing parties
and said among his own,
/I snatch your enemies from you,/
and Bassianus agreed so far that he said that even their children, if
he took thought for himself, should be killed, Geta is said to have
asked how great was the number of those to be slain;

and when his father had said, he asked,
/Do these have parents, have they kinsmen?/
when the an-

swer was that they had, he said,
/Then more

therefore in

the city will be sad than glad that we have conquered,/
and his opinion would have prevailed had not Plautianus the prefect or
Juvenalis pressed on in hope of the proscriptions, from which they
were enriched. to these things was added the excessive

cruelty of his brother Bassianus, who when he contended and said, as
if in jest as if in earnest, that all of the opposing parties should
be killed with their children, Geta is said to have said to him,
/You who spare no one can kill even a brother./
which saying of his then was nothing, but afterwards stood as a
presage.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

He was in pursuing letters tenacious of the old writers, mindful also
of his father's sayings, always hateful to his brother, more beloved
by his mother than his brother, somewhat stammering

yet resonant. most eager for shining dress, so that his father would
laugh. if he received anything from his parents, he applied it to his
own adornment and gave nothing to anyone.

After the Parthian war, when the father was flourishing with huge
glory, Bassianus having been named partner in the empire, Geta also
received the name of Caesar and of Antoninus, as some say.

It was familiar to him to pro- pose these questions to the
grammarians, that they should say how each animal

emits its voice, as: lambs bleat, piglets grunt,

wood-pigeons murmur, pigs grunt, bears rage, lions roar, leopards
snarl, elephants trumpet, frogs croak, horses neigh, asses bray,

bulls bellow, and to prove these from the ancients. The books of
Serenus

Sammonicus he held most familiar, which that man wrote to

Antoninus; he had also this custom, that he would order banquets and
especially lunches by single letters with knowing slaves, as one in
which there was

goose, boar-flesh, duck, likewise chicken, partridge, peacock, piglet,
fish, ham and whatever kinds of food fell under that letter, and
likewise pheasant, groats, fig and such things, wherefore he was even
held gracious in his youth.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

When he was slain, the part of the soldiers that was uncorrupted took
the parricide very ill, all saying they had promised faith to two
sons, they ought to keep it for two, and with the gates shut the
emperor was long not admitted.

finally unless complaints about Geta had been published and the
soldiers' minds soothed, huge donatives also having been given,
Bassianus could not return to Rome;

after this finally both Papinian and many others were killed, who had
either favored concord

or who had been of Geta's party, so that men of both orders were
struck down both in the bath and dining and in public, Papinian
himself was struck with an axe, Bassianus disapproving that the matter
was not finished with a sword.

at last it came even to a sedition of the urban soldiers, whom indeed
Bassianus crushed with no light authority, their tribune, as some

say, being killed, as others, banished. but he himself feared so much
that, wearing a cuirass under the broad stripe, he even entered the
Senate-house and so rendered an account of his deed and

of Geta's killing. at which time indeed Helvius Pertinax, who
afterwards was slain by the same Bassianus, when Faustinus the praetor
was reciting

and saying
/Sarmaticus Maximus and Parthicus Maximus,/
is said to have said,
/Add also Geticus Maximus,/
as if Gothicus.

which saying sank deeper into Bassianus' breast, as afterwards was
proved by the death of Pertinax, and not only of Pertinax but of
others also, as was said above, here and there

and unjustly. but he held Helvius also suspected of aspiring to
tyranny, because he was in the love of all and son of the emperor
Pertinax, which thing is not easily safe enough for any private man.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

The funeral of Geta is said to have been more carefully done than of
one

who seemed slain by a brother, and he was borne into the tomb of his
ancestors, that is of Severus, which is on the Appian Way on the right
for those going toward the gate, built in the form of a Septizodium,
which that man had adorned for himself while alive.

He wished to kill also Geta's mother, his own stepmother, because she
mourned the brother, and the women whom after his return

from the Senate-house he found weeping. moreover Antoninus was of such
savagery that he would especially flatter those whom he destined for

death, so that his flattery was more feared

than his anger. it seemed indeed wonderful to all that he himself
would weep for Geta's death as often as mention of his name was made,
as often as an image

or statue was seen; but so great was the inconstancy of Antoninus
Bassianus, or rather so great

a thirst for slaughter, that now he would kill Geta's partisans, now
his enemies, whom chance had offered, by which deed Geta was more
longed for.
