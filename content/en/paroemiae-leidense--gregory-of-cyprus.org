PROVERBS COLLECTED BY THE MOST HOLY PATRIARCH KYR GREGORY OF CYPRUS ACCORDING
TO THE ALPHABET.
A.
1.1 An Aphrodisian oath: not punishable. For the gods, being forsworn in
erotic matters, are not angry. 1.2 An eagle's old age, a lark's youth:
inasmuch as even an aging eagle is better than a young bird. 1.3 Do not
despise a rustic orator: that one must not despise even the cheap. 1.4 At
another time to become of another sort and to follow the land: that it is
fitting for one to make himself like the customs of the cities and places in
which he may come to be; and it is said from the octopuses. 1.5 Shake another
oak: of those refusing to give still; they are accustomed to take the request.
1.6 To lift the armpit: instead of to feast. It is said from those dancing in
a rustic way. 1.7 Always jackdaw to jackdaw; and: Always god leads like to
like. 1.8 Horn of Amaltheia: because Zeus gave to Amaltheia his nurse, from a
goat that was feeding him, a horn, through which there gushed forth for her
everything she might ask. 1.9 You teach an eagle to fly: of those wishing to
teach those wiser than themselves. =1.10= A contest admits no excuse, nor does
friendship. =1.11= A contest does not wait for an excuse: of those idle and
careless by nature, or of those not admitting the words of those making
excuses. And Plato mentions it: yet a contest does not seem to me to admit
excuses; and in the sixth of the Laws: but they say a contest does not
altogether admit excuses. And Aeschylus says in Glaucus of Potniae: For a
contest does not wait for men left behind. And Aristophanes in
Thesmophoriazusae 2. =1.12= The cap of Hades: of those hiding themselves by
certain devices. For such are the things of Hades. =1.13= To touch with the
tip of the finger: of things harmful. =1.14= To weep with blood: by hyperbole,
of those weeping exceedingly. =1.15= We were bailing out one, but another
flows in. =1.16= We were once. =1.17= Last year's things are always better.
=1.18= The man who flees does not wait for the sound of the lyre: of those who
ought to do each thing quickly and sharply. =1.19= An insatiable jar: of the
gluttonous. =1.20= A fisherman struck will bring understanding: they say a
fisherman once, having taken with his hand the fishes being caught, struck by
them said this proverb. =1.21= A fox drives the ox: of the crafty and small,
yet overcoming great men who are foolish. =1.22= Every hedgehog is rough: of
the difficult and ill-tempered. Especially useful of the injurious or of
eunuchs. =1.23= Having eaten up a medimnus of salt: of the ungrateful. This
proverb is of the ethical ones. =1.24= The doors of the Muses have been
opened: of those readily rendering the fairest things of the arts by judgment.
=1.25= You think archaic things: that is, simple-minded. =1.26= An Abydene
after-course: the unpleasant, because the Abydenes after dinner bring their
own children to those feasting, shouting and making a din and causing
unpleasantness to the guests. =1.27= The life of Habron: the luxurious. For a
certain Habron became rich; whence also habrodiaitos. =1.28= The landholder is
always rich next year. =1.29= Fearless fear: of those who fear in vain. =1.30=
Agamemnonian wells: for Agamemnon dug wells beside Aulis and all Greece.
=1.31= The sacrifice of Agamemnon: of the disobedient and hard; for when
Agamemnon was sacrificing the ox fled and was barely caught. =1.32=
Barley-cake too is good with bread: of those giving second things to some.
=1.33= Agathonian pipe-playing: the sweetest and most cheering. =1.34= A
hammer-wrought statue: of the insensible. =1.35= The unlaughing rock: of
things that cause grief. On which Demeter sat when she was seeking her
daughter. =1.36= A sea of goods; and: A treasure of goods; and:

Skeins of goods; and: An anthill of goods: it is said of an abundance of
goods. =1.37= Purer than a rudder: inasmuch as in the sea the rudder darting,
† it is clean. =1.38= This is another Heracles: of the strong. =1.39= Enough
of the oak: of those changing to some better way of life. For the ancients,
ceasing from acorns, partook of grain and wine. =1.40= Untimely goodwill
differs in nothing from enmity: Hippolytus said this to Phaedra when she
claimed to love him greatly. =1.41= Eat the tortoises yourselves, if indeed
you had them: fishermen caught a great tortoise and, hauling it up, were
dividing the one at hand. And they called Hermes as he was passing by to the
feast. And he said the saying set forth. =1.42= If he asks for wine, give him
blows: of those asking for good things but receiving terrible ones. =1.43=
Taking, we have been taken: of those who hoped to conquer some, then were
captured by those same. =1.44= The laughter of Ajax: of those laughing madly.
=1.45= With unwashed hands: of those handling the sacred with profane hands.
=1.46= A headless tale: of those speaking incomplete things. =1.47= You sing
in vain: of those laboring in vain. =1.48= Word at once, deed at once: of
things accomplished swiftly. =1.49= To stork in return: to give favors in
return. It is said that storks feed their parents when they have grown old.
=1.50= A human is a human's divinity: of those unexpectedly saved. =1.51=
Worth the whole: of things exceedingly precious. =1.52= Always he casts a
young girl toward an old man: of older men wishing to lie with young women.
=1.53= You are worthy of the shield at Argos: of the well-born. =1.54= Child
of the wind: of the changeable and light. =1.55= To go up onto the roof with
unwashed feet: of those going ignorantly upon certain works. =1.56= From the
starting-line: that is, one must be good from the beginning. =1.57= In place
of a bad dog you demand a pig: of those giving evils in return for evils.
=1.58= You speak things older than the hide: of those narrating foolish
things; for the hide, on which Zeus used to write down the things that
happened, is very ancient. =1.59= You mock me from on high: of those speaking
from a height. As if, you utterly despise me. =1.60= A chorus of the idle: of
those doing evils. =1.61= All things upside down: of those turning the order
about. Menander in The Dagger; and in The Widow: This is now the saying, The
above below, they say, and the below above. =1.62= To move unmovable things: a
proverb by hyperbole, that one must not move even altars or tombs or
boundaries. =1.63= To begin from Hestia: of those who have come into power and
first wrong their own, since it was custom to sacrifice to Hestia first of the
other gods. And a certain myth of this sort is current about her: for after
the rule of the Titans was dissolved they say Zeus, having succeeded to the
kingship, allowed Hestia to take whatever she wished; and she asked for
virginity and the first-fruits from men.
B.
1.64t Touchstone: of those being examined in words, inasmuch as the Lydian
stone tests the gold. =1.65= Royal eye: thus they used to call the one sent
upon some office. =1.66= A Seriphian frog: of the voiceless. =1.67= Throw into
the water: instead of sink it. Of those =1.67= worthy of destruction. =1.68=
In mire you will never find bright water: of those mixing the fairest things
with the most shameful. =1.69= A cough instead of a fart: of those who fart
and pretend to cough or laugh. Which is taken of those pretending to do
something in want. =1.70= An ox in the city: of things marvelled at. For
Lysias set up an ox on the acropolis. =1.71= Water to a frog; and: Fat to a
weasel: of those giving these things, in which those who receive take
pleasure. =1.72= You throw with apples: of those wishing to obtain what they
love. =1.73= Bath-keeper: of the busybody. For these, having leisure, are
busybodies.

G.
1.74t War is sweet to one without experience: of those through inexperience
throwing themselves into dangers. =1.75= Ionian laughter: of the dissolute.
For the Ionians were slandered for this. =1.76= Know yourself: of those
boasting beyond their power. =1.77= Nakeder than a slough: a slough is the
skin of a snake or of the embryo. =1.78= You assign a watch to a naked man: of
those not able to fulfill the things commanded. =1.79= I grow old always
learning many things: of those more experienced through old age. =1.80= The ox
grows old, but the works are many for the ox. =1.81= With the head naked: of
those going shamelessly toward anything that happens. =1.82= Naked as from the
womb: of the destitute. =1.83= A woman's wits: of the senseless. =1.84=
Tongue, where are you going, having set a city right and again overthrowing
it: of those who through words either harm or help. =1.85= An owl flies: the
flight of the owl was held by the Athenians as a symbol of victory. =1.86= A
sweet elbow: of those speaking ironically. And there is a place in Memphis
called Elbow by those sailing, perhaps by antiphrasis, because of the
difficulty. But some apply it to those in good cheer, inasmuch as those
rejoicing set the elbow.
D.
1.87τ Justice begot justice and harm harm: of the litigious and those
stringing lawsuits to lawsuits. And of those falling upon dense misfortunes.
=1.88= Egyptians are clever at weaving devices. =1.89= Old men are twice
children: of those seeming to be more simple-minded toward old age. =1.90=
Daedalian works: of marvel. =1.91= More just than a stachane: of those loving
just things. For the Dorians call the balance-beam stachane because of the
standing. =1.92= A far-sounding evil: of those undergoing some great evil.
=1.93= The vote of Zeus: of things sacred and untouchable. =1.94= Through the
middle both famine flows away and is most finely destroyed: for when the
starving man has been destroyed, the famine too is destroyed. =1.95= Seek a
livelihood, but virtue when there is a living. =1.96= Twice and thrice the
fair: a proverb, that one must speak often about fair things.
E.
1.97t You hide in a threshing-floor: that is, you conceal yourself. Of those
not able to escape notice. =1.98= The trap has caught the mouse: of those
justly caught. =1.99= Drawing upon himself a cloud as the caecias does,
=1.100= Then being rich he no longer takes pleasure in lentil-soup, but
before, under poverty, he used to eat up everything. 2.1 In summer you wear
out the cloak: of those not using necessities in season. 2.2 It came into the
ears of a god: of great affairs. 2.3 One man, no man. 2.4 Counsel in the
night: since the night gives freedom to deliberate. 2.5 The wolf into the
traps: of those who plunder, but have been held. 2.6 You revelled into bees:
of those being harmed all at once. 2.7 In the knees of five judges: for of old
five judges judged the comic poets; or because they had in their knees what is
now written into tablets. 2.8 From a wolf's mouth: of those receiving
something from certain people contrary to hope. 2.9 He differs in nothing from
an elephant: of the insensible. =2.10= You are going to Troezen: of those
having a scarcity of beards. =2.11= A deer-man: the coward. =2.12= More
changeable than a buskin: the buskin is a kind of shoe, fitting both feet.
=2.13= Where the wind allows neither to remain nor to sail: of those fallen
upon unmanageable things. =2.14= To be poor on land rather than to sail being
rich. =2.15= Enemies' gifts are no gifts and not beneficial: of unprofitable
gifts. =2.16= You hide in a threshing-floor: that is, you conceal yourself in
a threshing-floor. Of those not able to escape notice. =2.17= For I am going
already upon the very colophon of the argument: of great and indisputable
things and those having great force.

For it is recorded that when the cities in Asia were holding assembly and
casting votes they had no force as yet, unless the vote of Colophon also being
cast gave authority to the things voted. =2.18= Give a pledge, and ruin is at
hand: of those readily giving pledges and coming off badly. And they say it
was inscribed at Delphi; but some said it is not so, but only pledge is ruin;
and they furnish as witness of this Cratinus the younger saying: Having fallen
into three pledges, I was destroyed. Thus once at Delphi were the letters, the
pledge ruin. But I had a friend-loving habit. And that the one at Delphi is
give a pledge, and ruin is at hand, Plato makes clear in Charmides, saying:
which indeed those who dedicated the later letters seem to me to have
suffered, both nothing in excess and give a pledge, and ruin is at hand.
Z.
2.20t Zeus looked at last into the hides: that he is not without providence.
For they say Zeus writes into hides the things done by men. 2. Life of a jar:
of those living moderately. From Diogenes the philosopher, for whom a jar
became a most pleasant haunt. =2.21= The pot lives, friendship lives.
H.
2.22t The dog in the manger: toward those using neither for themselves nor
letting others. Inasmuch as the dog will not eat barley remaining in a manger,
and does not let the horse.
Th
2.23t. Anger grows old last: it is said on account of the older men. For as
much as they grow old, they have the spirit more vigorous. =2.24= Water from a
torrent to the sea: of those ambitiously adding small things to the great.
=2.25= A Thasos of goods: of those promising to show someone happy and
illustrious. =2.26= Quick-witted: of the quick to learn and well-natured.
=2.27= Thamyris is mad: of those living intelligently, but seeming to some to
be mad. For a certain Thamyris, seeing a household-servant being whipped by
his master in the temple and the master taking no thought of the temple, gave
his substance and went abroad. Those seeing him said: Thamyris is mad. =2.28=
Shamelessness is a god: of those puffed up with shamelessness.
I.
2.29t An Iliad of evils was coming on: of the greatest misfortunes. =2.30=
Perhaps it is an Erinys from tragedy: for the abominable of phantoms fits
tragedians more. =2.31= You teach a fish to swim: of those teaching things
which they know. =2.32= Colder than Ialemus: they say Ialemus is the dirge;
and they say he was a son of Calliope, ill-fated and lacking and an orphan.
=2.33= To challenge horsemen into a plain: of those challenging into strife
those better in certain things and more skilled than themselves.
K.
2.34t From a bad raven a bad egg: because the animal itself is inedible, and
indeed also its egg. Or from Corax the orator, the first who taught rhetoric;
for demanding the fee from his pupil, into the law-court, the same pupil said,
let us go, and if you should win, I have not learned; but if you are defeated,
you will not receive fees. =2.35= Just as the nurses you feed badly. =2.36= A
dog having turned upon his own vomit. =2.37= Even upon the dead to make gain:
of those making gain from the poor and the dead. =2.38= With the

stream he is carried. =2.39= A crab catches a hare: of things impossible.
=2.40= A crab catches a throat: of things impossible. =2.41= Blacker than a
beetle; and: Wiser than a beetle: of the wicked and ill-natured. =2.42= The
bad drink the mist of wickedness: of those punished according to desert.
=2.43= A gathering of evils: of an abundance of evils. =2.44= More easily
molded than wax: of those doing something readily. =2.45= Cadmean victory: the
victory upon harm; Eteocles and Polynices sons of Oedipus, but some of Cadmus,
whose kingship they succeeded to, agreed with one another to rule one after
one time. And Eteocles having ruled did not wish Polynices to rule according
to what had been resolved, and in the war they slew one another and have
conquered upon harm, because the one killed the other. =2.46= Bellerophon
against himself: of those doing something against themselves. =2.47= To
Cercopize: instead of to be enslaved and to deceive. And it has been
transferred from the Cercopes. =2.48= Pray according to an ox: of those
shouting greatly. =2.49= Deafer than a thrush: inasmuch as the animal is
voiceless. =2.50= Shadow of a beetle: of those fearing where one must not.
=2.51= More ugly than Corydeus: this man was slandered for ugliness. =2.52=
The jar has been rolled: of those doing something in vain in imitation of

off there; whence also Cynosarges. =2.57= Reap the fruit which you sowed: of
those suffering such things as they did. And it has been derived from the
verse: But if you should sow evils, you would reap evils. And again: And he

L.
2.58t A Lydian chariot: of those contending in contests and being left far
behind. For Pelops being a Lydian conquered Oenomaus with his own chariot. And
from then the proverb, to run beside a Lydian chariot. The preposition para
signifies also nearness, as, he sat, he sat beside; and the greater relation,
as, I rush, I urge on. It signifies also the outside relation, that outside of
what is fitting, as, lawless, unlawful. As: even if we were on foot running
beside a Lydian chariot; instead of: running outside and far from the Lydian
chariot by comparison; for the Lydian chariots are swiftest. And such is the
great Basil, a Lydian chariot. =2.59= He weaves a thin one: of the poor.
=2.60= A Libyan beast: of the versatile, because Libya is full of beasts.
=2.61= Like Lynceus: the sharp-sighted. =2.62= A lethargic dog: the one
fawning, but biting in secret. =2.63= A Lydian at midday: of the unbridled, as
being unbridled at these hours. For the Lydians are mocked as fulfilling the
things of Aphrodite with their own hands. And this proverb is similar to: A
goatherd in the heat, since at such hours the goatherds are unbridled. =2.64=
A wolf's wings: of things impossible. =2.65= You light a lamp at midday: of
those doing something at an unfit time. =2.66= Words of a wolf: of the one who
wrongs both in words and in deeds. For a wolf seeing a shepherd eating sheep
said: how great a din would have been raised, if I were doing these things.
=2.67= A white rule with a white stone: of those marking unclear things with
unclear and in this understanding nothing. For the white rule on white stones
cannot show because it does not differ, as does that made through red ochre.
As Sophocles in Cedalion: I do not judge by your words, no more than a white
rule with a white stone; and a rule is a carpenter's cord. And the proverb has
been said by ellipse; wherefore it also became unclear; and the whole is of
this sort: a white rule on a white stone.

=2.75= Not to move an evil well laid. =2.76= It has escaped him more than the

3.30τ Fair things are hard: they say Pittacus, laying down the office, said to

flies. =3.34= A Chironian wound: that is, incurable. =3.35= Chthonian baths:

paint a pot:

of things impossible. =3.37= A pot-god: of clay, cheap. =3.38= Colophonian
gold: the Colophonians used to work the fairest gold. For they say the
Colophonian gold differs much from the other. =3.39= ** But I thought I would
work gold: Plato has used the proverb; what then, said Thrasymachus, he works
gold. It took its beginning from here. A certain report once fell among the
multitude of the Athenians, that on Hymettus much gold-dust had appeared and
was being guarded by the warlike ants. And they, taking up arms, ran out upon

another, saying you thought, having collected much dust, you would work gold
and be rich. And they were jeered by the comic poets; Eubulus at any rate

3.40t Psyra Dionysus: Psyra is a cheap and small island, near Chios, not able
to bear wine. We therefore say the proverb of those reclining at a symposium
and not drinking. And it has been said also of those signifying cheapness.
=3.41= A drop: the dew. Thus Antimachus was called, being spitty. And this man
was a poet of lyrics. And he was so called, because he used to sprinkle those
conversing while speaking. And Antimachus seemed to have made a decree, that
it is not permitted to blow the sacred fire. One must mock by name, as not
being clean.
