* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I know that most writers have said nothing of the lesser pretenders, or have
passed them by in a few words. Suetonius Tranquillus, the most correct and
most honest of writers, was silent about Antonius and Vindex, content with
having touched on them in passing. Marius Maximus inserted Avidius of Marcus'
time, and Albinus and Niger of Severus', not into books of their own but into
other men's. We do not wonder at Suetonius, who was always fond of brevity.
But what of Marius Maximus, the wordiest of all men, who even tangled himself
in volumes of mythic history: did he stoop to that kind of description and
care? Trebellius Pollio, on the other hand, showed such diligence and such
care in publishing good princes and bad that he even packed the thirty
pretenders, though briefly, into one book, men of the times of Valerian and
Gallienus and of princes not much earlier or later. So we too, even if our
care has not been so great, have not been without some care. After telling of
Aurelian, Tacitus, and Florian, and of Probus too, a great and singular
prince, when Carus, Carinus, and Numerian still remained to be told, we would
not leave out Saturninus, Bonosus, Proculus, and Firmus, who had been under
Aurelian.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

For you know, my Bassus, what a dispute we had lately with Marcus Fonteius,
that lover of histories, when he said that Firmus, who in Aurelian's time had
seized Egypt, had been a brigand and not a prince. I, and with me Rufius
Celsus, Ceionius Julianus, and Fabius Sossianus, argued the other way: he had
worn the purple and, with coinage struck, had been called Augustus. Severus
Archontius even produced his coins, and from Greek and Egyptian books proved
that in his edicts he had been called
au)tokra/tora

For Fonteius, arguing against us, this was the only argument: Aurelian, he
said, had not written in his edict that he had killed a pretender, but that he
had removed a certain brigand from the state. As if a prince of so great a
name ought, in dignity, to have called a man of the shadows a pretender; or as
if great princes have not always named as brigands the men they killed when
they seized the purple.

I myself, in the Life of Aurelian, before I knew the whole story of Firmus,
did not count him among those who wore the purple, but treated him as a kind
of brigand. I have said this so that no one may think I have forgotten myself.
But lest I load a volume that I promised would be very short, let us come to
Firmus.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Firmus' native city was Seleucia, though most of the Greeks give another, not
knowing that at that very time there were three men named Firmus: one prefect
of Egypt, another commander of the African frontier and also proconsul, and
this third, the friend and ally of Zenobia, who, stirred by the Egyptians'
frenzy, seized Alexandria, and whom Aurelian crushed with the usual good
fortune of his valor.

Much is said of this man's wealth. He is reported to have fitted his house
with square panes of glass set in with bitumen and other compounds, and to
have had so much paper that he often said in public he could feed an army on
papyrus and glue. He also kept the closest alliance with the Blemmyes and with
the Saracens. He often sent merchant ships to the Indians as well. He himself
is said to have had two elephant tusks of ten feet, from which Aurelian had
resolved to make a throne, adding two others, on which a Jupiter of gold and
jewels would sit, wearing the look of a bordered toga, to be placed in the
Temple of the Sun after the lots of the Apennines had been consulted, a god he
had wished to be called Jupiter the Consul, or the Consulting. But these same
tusks Carinus later gave as a gift to a certain woman, who is said to have
made a couch from them. I pass over her name, because she is known even now,
and it will do posterity no good to know her. Thus an Indian gift, consecrated
to Jupiter Best and Greatest, seems through a most evil prince to have become
both the instrument of lust and its price.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Firmus, however, was of huge stature, with eyes standing out, curly hair, a
scarred brow, and a rather dark face; the rest of his body was white but hairy
and bristly, so that most called him a Cyclops. He ate a great deal of meat,
and is said to have eaten an ostrich in a day. He drank little wine and a
great deal of water. He was most firm in mind and strongest in sinew, so that
he surpassed Tritannus, of whom Varro makes mention. For he also steadily
endured an anvil set on his chest while others struck it, himself leaning
back, face upward, and bent onto his hands, hanging rather than lying. Yet he
had a contest in drinking with Aurelian's generals, if ever they wished to try
him. A certain Burburus, one of the standard-bearers and a most famous
drinker, challenged him to drink; Firmus drained two buckets full of unmixed
wine and was sober through the whole banquet afterward. When Burburus said to
him,
/Why did you not drink the dregs?/
he answered,
/Fool, earth is not drunk./

We are chasing trifles when greater things must be told.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

This man, then, took up the imperial power against Aurelian to defend what
remained of Zenobia's party. But when Aurelian was returning from Thrace he
was defeated.

Many say that he ended his life by a noose. Aurelian shows otherwise in his
edicts 1; for when he had conquered him he ordered an edict of this kind to be
posted at Rome:

/Aurelian Augustus greets the Roman people most devoted to him. Now that the
whole world of nations has been pacified on every side, as far as the earth
lies wide, Firmus too, the Egyptian brigand, seething with barbarian risings
and gathering the remnants of a woman's shame, not to speak at too great
length, we have put to flight, besieged, tortured, and killed. There is
nothing, people of Romulus, that you need fear. The grain-levy of Egypt,
which the wicked brigand had interrupted, will come entire. Be at one with
the senate, on friendly terms with the equestrian order, and well disposed
toward the praetorians. I will see to it that Rome has no anxiety. Give
yourselves to the games, give yourselves to the circus. Let public
necessities hold us; let pleasures occupy you. Wherefore, most holy citizens,/
and the rest.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

You ought to know that we have learned these things about Firmus, and they are
worth recording. As for those details which Aurelius Festivus, Aurelian's
freedman, related of him one by one, if you wish to know them you must read
the man himself, especially since he says that this same Firmus swam among
crocodiles, anointed with crocodiles' fat, drove an elephant, sat on a
hippopotamus, and, sitting, was carried on huge ostriches and as it were flew.
But what good is it to know these things? Livy and Sallust are silent about
trivial matters in the lives they took up. For we do not know what kind of
mules Clodius had, or what she-mules Titus Annius Milo, or whether Catiline
sat on a Tuscan horse or a Sardinian, or of what purple Pompey used in his
cloak. So we shall make an end of Firmus and come to Saturninus, who claimed
the imperial power for himself against Probus in the East.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Saturninus was a Gaul by origin, from a nation of men most restless and always
greedy either to make a prince or to make an empire. To this man, among the
other generals, because he certainly seemed to be truly the first of men,
Aurelian gave the command of the eastern frontier, wisely charging that he
should never see Egypt. For that most prudent man was considering, as we see,
the nature of the Gauls, and feared that if Saturninus saw that most turbulent
city, the company of its people would lead him where nature was already taking
him. For the Egyptians, as you know well enough, are windy, raging, boastful,
injurious, and besides vain, unrestrained, and hungry for novelty even to
public songs: verse-makers, epigrammatists, astrologers, soothsayers,
physicians. For among them are Christians, Samaritans, and men to whom the
present times, with their enormous liberty, are always displeasing. And lest
any of the Egyptians be angry with me and believe that what I have put into
writing is my own, I will set down a letter of Hadrian brought out from the
books of Phlegon, his freedman, from which the life of the Egyptians is
thoroughly uncovered:

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Thinking these things, then, about the Egyptians, Aurelian had ordered that
Saturninus not see Egypt, and with a mind indeed divine. For as soon as the
Egyptians saw that a great power had come to them, they at once shouted,
/ Saturninus Augustus, may the gods keep you!/

And he, which cannot be denied, being a wise man, soon fled from Alexandria
and returned to Palestine. There, however, when he had begun to think that it
was not safe for him if he lived as a private man, a purple robe was taken
down from a statue of Venus, and he was clothed in a wife's mantle with the
soldiers standing around, and homage was paid to him. I have often heard my
grandfather say that he was present when that man was being adored. "He was
weeping," he said,

/and he was saying, 'The state has lost a necessary man, if I may say so
without arrogance. I restored the Gauls; I gave back Africa when it was held
by the Moors; I pacified the Spains. But what does it profit? All these
things have perished once the honor has been claimed.'/

And when those who had clothed him in the purple were urging him either to
live or to rule, he spoke in these words:

/You do not know, friends, what an evil it is to rule. Swords hang over the
neck, suspended by a hair; spears on every side, darts on every side. The
very guards are feared, the very companions are dreaded. Food is not taken
for pleasure, a journey not at one's own will, wars not by one's own
judgment, arms not for one's own inclination. Add that every time of life is
criticized in an emperor.

Is the man old? He seems unfit. A youth? It is added that he is mad as well.
Need I now speak of Probus, whom all love? When you wish me to be his rival,
to whom I gladly yield and whose general I desire to be, you drag me into the
necessity of death. I have this consolation in dying: I shall not be able to
perish alone./

Marcus Salvidienus says that this speech was truly his own, and in truth he
was not a little lettered, for he had studied under a rhetorician in Africa
and had frequented the teachers' stalls at Rome.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

And, that I may not go further, I must say what especially concerns this man:
some are in error and think that this is the Saturninus who seized the
imperial power in Gallienus' time, whereas he is quite another man and was
killed though Probus was unwilling that he be punished. It is said, moreover,
that Probus often sent him letters of clemency and promised pardon, but that
the soldiers who had been with him did not believe it. At last, besieged in a
certain fort by those whom Probus had sent, he was stabbed against Probus'
will.

It is tedious to string together every frivolous thing, and hateful to tell of
what stature he was, of what body, of what grace, what he drank, what he ate.
Let others say those things which profit almost nothing as an example. Let us
return to what must be told.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Albingauni was Proculus' native place, set in the Maritime Alps. He was noble
at home, but his ancestors were brigands, and so he was rich enough in cattle
and slaves and in the things they had carried off. It is said, in short, that
at the time he took up the imperial power he armed two thousand of his own
slaves. He had a mannish wife, who drove him into this madness, named Samso, a
name given her later, for before she was called Vituriga. His son was
Herennianus, whom also, if he had completed his fifth year (for so he used to
speak), he would have dedicated to empire: a man, which cannot be denied,
[omitted]

and at the same time most valiant, himself also accustomed to brigandage, who
nevertheless always lived an armed life. For he both commanded many legions as
tribune and performed brave deeds. And since all the most trivial things are
pleasant and have some grace when they are read, it must not be passed over
that he himself also boasts in a certain letter of his, which it is better to
set down itself than to say much about it:

/Proculus greets his kinsman Maecianus. I captured a hundred maidens from
Sarmatia. Of these I entered ten in one night. Yet all of them, as far as was
in me, I made women within fifteen days./

He boasts, as you see, of a foolish and quite lustful thing, and believes he
is held among the brave if he grows callous by the density of his crimes.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

This man, however, although even after his military honors he conducted
himself basely and lustfully, yet bravely, was called to the imperial power at
the urging of the people of Lugdunum, who both seemed to have been heavily
crushed by Aurelian and feared Probus most violently. He was called to empire
almost by a game and a jest, as Onesimus says, which indeed I know I have
found in no other writer. For when at a certain banquet they were playing the
board-game of robbers, and he himself had come out as emperor ten times, a
certain jester of no mean standing said, "Hail, Augustus," and, bringing
purple wool, bound it on his shoulders and did him homage. Then came fear
among those who were privy to the deed, and from that an attempt upon the army
and the imperial power. Yet he was of some service to the Gauls, for he
crushed the Alamanni, who were then still called Germans, not without a
splendor of glory, never fighting otherwise than in the manner of a brigand.
Probus put him to flight to the farthest lands. When he wished to take refuge
with the Franks, from whom he himself said he drew his origin, the Franks
themselves betrayed him (it is their custom to break faith with a laugh), and
Probus conquered and killed him. His descendants even now live among the
Albingauni, and they are accustomed to say in jest that it does not please
them to be either princes or brigands.

These things worthy of mention I remember having learned about Proculus. Let
us come to Bonosus, of whom I have composed much less.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

Bonosus was of a Spanish house, a Briton by origin, yet with a Gallic mother:
the son of a rhetorician, as he himself used to say; of a teacher of letters,
as I have learned from others. He lost his father as a little child and,
reared by a most brave mother, learned nothing of letters. He served first
among the ordinary soldiers, then among the cavalry. He commanded ranks, held
tribunates, and was commander of the Raetian frontier. He drank as no man
among men. Of this man Aurelian often used to say, "He was born not to live,
but to drink." He long held him in honor for the sake of military service,
for if ever envoys of barbarians had come from any nations, they were pledged
wine by him, that he might make them drunk and learn everything from them
through wine. He himself, however much he had drunk, was always calm and
sober, and, as Onesimus, the writer of the Life of Probus, says, even wiser in
wine. He had besides a marvelous thing: as much as he had drunk he would pass,
nor were his chest or belly or bladder ever weighed down.

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

This same man, when at a certain time the Germans had burned the Roman
pleasure-galleys on the Rhine, took up the imperial power from fear that he
might pay the penalty, and held it longer than he deserved. For, defeated by
Probus in a long and heavy struggle, he ended his life by a noose, when indeed
the jest arose that it was an amphora hanging, not a man.

He left two sons, both of whom Probus spared, his wife also being held in
honor and a salary furnished up to her death. For she is said to have been,
as my grandfather also used to say, a woman of singular example and of a noble
family, yet of Gothic race. Aurelian had given her to him as wife for this
reason, that through him he might learn everything from the Goths, for she was
a royal maiden. There exist letters written to the legate of the Thracias
concerning these nuptials and the gifts which Aurelian ordered to be given to
Bonosus for the wedding, which I have inserted:

/Aurelian Augustus to Gallonius Avitus, greeting. In former letters I had
written that you should establish the Gothic noblewomen at Perinthus, with
salaries decreed, not that they should receive them one by one, but that
seven together should have one meal. For when they receive them divided, they
take too little and the state loses too much. Now, however, since it has
pleased us that Hunila be given to Bonosus, you will give her, according to
the list written below, all that we command. You will also celebrate the
nuptials at public expense./

The list of gifts was:

/Hooded violet tunics of part-silk, one part-silk tunic with a gold stripe
weighing a pound, two double-striped under-tunics, and the rest that befit a
matron. To him himself you will give a hundred Philippic gold pieces, a
thousand silver Antoniniani, a million bronze sesterces./

This I hold that I have read about Bonosus. And I could indeed have passed
over the life of these men whom no one was asking for; yet, lest anything of
fidelity be lacking, I have taken care that even those things which I had
learned about these also be made known. There remain for me Carus, Carinus,
and Numerian, for Diocletian and those who follow must be told in a greater
style.
