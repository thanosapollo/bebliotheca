The life of the boy Antoninus Diadumenus, whom the army named emperor
together with his father Opilius Macrinus after Bassianus had been killed
by the Macrinian faction, has nothing memorable except that he was called
Antoninus and that astounding omens of an empire not long to last were
made for him, as indeed it turned out.

For as soon as it became known through the legions that Bassianus had
been slain, a huge grief seized every heart, because they did not have
an Antoninus in the state, thinking that with him the Roman empire would
perish.

When this was reported to Macrinus, already emperor, he feared that the
army might turn to some one of the Antonini, of whom many from the kin
of Antoninus Pius were among the generals. He at once ordered an
assembly to be made ready and named this boy, his son, Antoninus.

The assembly:
/You see, fellow soldiers, both me already advanced in years and the boy
Diadumenus, whom, if the gods are kind, you will long have as prince. I
understand besides that a huge longing for the Antonine name remains
among you. Therefore, since by the condition of human frailty not much
of life seems left to me, I name this boy Antoninus with you as authors,
who will long represent an Antoninus for you./

The shout:
/Macrinus emperor, may the gods keep you. Antoninus Diadumenus, may the
gods keep you. We have all long been asking for an Antoninus. Jupiter
Best and Greatest, life to Macrinus and Antoninus. You know, Jupiter,
Macrinus cannot be conquered. You know, Jupiter, Antoninus cannot be
conquered. We have an Antoninus, we have everything. The gods have given
us an Antoninus. Antoninus is worthy of his father, worthy of empire./

Macrinus the emperor said:
/Have therefore, fellow soldiers, three gold pieces for the empire, five
gold pieces for the name of Antoninus, and the usual promotions, but
doubled. The gods will grant that these things happen more often. We
shall give, through every five-year period, what we have resolved
today./

After this the little boy Diadumenus Antoninus, himself emperor, said:
/Thanks to you, fellow soldiers, that you have given me both empire and
name, if indeed you have judged both me and my father worthy to be named
Roman emperors and to have the state committed to us. My father will
take care that he not fail the empire; I shall strive not to fail the
name of the Antonini. For I know that I have taken up the name of Pius,
of Marcus, of Verus, whom it is very hard to satisfy. Meanwhile,
however, for the sake of the empire, for the sake of the name, I promise
all that my father does and as much again, with the honors doubled, as
the venerable Macrinus my father here present has also promised./

Herodian the Greek writer, passing over these things, says that the boy
Diadumenus was named only Caesar by the soldiers and was killed with his
father.

When this assembly had been held, coinage was at once struck at Antioch
in the name of Antoninus Diadumenus; that of Macrinus was deferred until
the senate's order. Letters were also sent to the senate in which the
name of Antoninus was made known. Wherefore the senate is said to have
received that empire gladly, although others think it was done out of
hatred of Antoninus Caracalla.

Macrinus the emperor had indeed prepared to give the people cloaks of
russet color in honor of his son Antoninus, which were to be called
Antoninianae, as Bassianus' were called caracallae, asserting that his
son would better be called Paenuleus or Paenularius than Bassianus had
been called Caracallus.

He also promised a congiarium by an Antoninian edict, as the edict
itself will be able to show.

The words of the edict:
/I would wish, Quirites, that we were already present; your Antoninus
would give you a congiarium of his own name; he would besides enroll
both Antoninian boys and Antoninian girls, who might spread the glory of
so welcome a name./
and the rest.

When these things had been done, he ordered that the standards in camp
and the banners be made Antoninian, and he made images of Bassianus in
gold and silver, and a supplication of seven days was celebrated for the
name of Antoninus.

The boy was the most beautiful of all, of rather tall stature, with
yellow hair, black eyes, a slender nose, a chin formed for every grace,
a mouth ready for kisses, strong by nature, rather delicate in his
training. When he first received the scarlet and purple garments and the
other camp insignia of empire, he shone out as if starlike and heavenly,
so that he was loved by all for the charm of his beauty. These things
are to be said of the boy.

others and in this man especially, are astounding. On the day he was
born his father, then by chance procurator of the greater treasury,
inspected the purples and ordered those he approved as bright to be
stored in that chamber in which two hours later Diadumenus was born.

Then boys are accustomed to be marked with a natural cap, which midwives
snatch and sell to credulous advocates, if indeed pleaders are said to
be helped by this. But this boy did not have a cap but a thin diadem,
yet so strong that it could not be broken, with fibers running through
it in the likeness of a bowstring. They say, finally, that the boy was
called Diadematus, but when he grew up he was called Diadumenus from the
name of his maternal grandfather, although the name Diadumenus did not
differ much from that token of Diadematus.

They say that on his father's estate twelve purple sheep were born, of
which only one was parti-colored. On the same day he was born it is
established that an eagle brought him, little by little, a small royal
wood-pigeon, set it in the cradle as he slept, and withdrew without
harm.

Birds of good omen nested in his father's house in those days in which
he was born. The astrologers, when they had received his nativity, cried
out that he himself was both the son of an emperor and an emperor, as
if his mother had committed adultery, which rumor kept repeating.

An eagle took a cap from this same child as he was walking in the field
and, when a cry had been raised by the infant's companions, set it on a
king's statue in a royal monument which was next to the villa where the
father was then staying, so as to fit it to the statue's head. Many
thought this ominous and suited to death, but the outcome showed it
illustrious.

He was born besides on Antoninus' birthday and at that hour and with
signs nearly agreeing with those of Antoninus Pius. Wherefore the
astrologers said that he would be both an emperor's son and an emperor,
but not for long.

On the day he was born, because it was Antoninus' birthday, a certain
woman who was a kinswoman is said to have cried out, /Let him be called
Antoninus,/ but Macrinus feared and, because no one of his family was
counted under this name, held back from the imperial name, and at the
same time because a rumor of the force of that nativity had already gone
out.

These and other omens many have put into writing, but this especially:
when Diadumenus was in the cradle, a lion, having broken its bonds, had,
as some say, escaped wild and come to his cradle; it licked the boy and
left him unharmed, while the nurse had thrown herself upon the lion and,
wounded by its bite, had perished, as she who alone by chance had been
found in the little plot where the infant lay.

These are the things that seem worthy of record in Antoninus Diadumenus,
whose life I would have joined to his father's deeds, had not the name
of the Antonini compelled me to publish a special account of a child's
life.

And indeed the name of the Antonini was so beloved in those times that
he who did not rest on that name seemed not to deserve the empire.
Whence some even think that Severus and Pertinax and Julianus should be
honored with the praenomina of the Antonini. Whence afterwards they
think the two Gordiani, father and son, were surnamed Antonini. But it
is one thing when a praenomen is taken on, another when the name itself
is imposed.

For Pius had Antoninus as his true name, Pius as a cognomen. Marcus had
Verissimus as his true name, but when this was taken away and abolished
he received Antoninus not as a praenomen but as a name. Verus had the
name of Commodus, and when this was abolished he received Antoninus not
as a praenomen but as a name. Marcus called Commodus Antoninus and so
published it in the public records on his own birthday.

Now it is well established that Caracalla Bassianus, either because of a
dream which Severus had seen, when he understood that an Antoninus had
been predicted as his successor, was called Antoninus only in his
thirteenth year, when he is also said to have added the imperial power
to him.

Geta, whom many deny was called Antoninus, it is well established was so
named for the same reason as Bassianus, that he might succeed his father
Severus, which by no means happened. After this it is well established
that Diadumenus himself, that he might be commended to the army, the
senate, and the Roman people, when there was a huge longing for
Bassianus Caracalla, was called Antoninus.

There exists a letter of Opilius Macrinus, father of Diadumenus, in
which he boasts not so much that he had come to the empire, who was
second in the empire, as that he had been made father of the Antoninian
name, than which nothing had been more illustrious in those times, even
of the gods.

Which letter, before I weave it in, I wish to insert verses spoken
against Commodus, who had named himself Hercules, so that all may
understand that the name of the Antonini was so illustrious that even a
name of the gods did not seem fittingly joined to it.

Verses spoken against Commodus Antoninus:
/Commodus desires to have the Herculean name;
he does not think the Antonines' is good;
void of human right and of empire,
hoping even to be more illustrious as a god
than if he were prince of an outstanding name:
that man will be no god, nor yet any man./

These verses, composed by some Greek or other, were translated into
Latin by a bad poet, which I thought should be inserted for this reason,
that all might know the Antonini were worth more than the gods from the
love of three princes, by which wisdom, goodness, and piety have been
consecrated: in Antoninus piety, in Verus goodness, in Marcus wisdom.

I return now to the letter of Macrinus Opilius:
/Opilius Macrinus to Nonia Celsa his wife. What good we have gained, my
wife, is beyond measure, and perhaps you think I speak of the empire;
that is no great thing which fortune has granted even to the unworthy. I
have been made father of an Antoninus; you have been made mother of an
Antoninus. O we blessed ones, O fortunate house, now at last the bright
praise of a happy empire. May the gods grant, and good Juno whom you
worship, that he too may fashion the desert of an Antoninus, and that I,
who am father of an Antoninus, may seem worthy in all men's eyes./

By this letter it is judged how much glory he seemed to himself to have
gained because his son was called Antoninus.

He nevertheless, in the fourteenth month of the empire, on account of
his father's uncivil and harsh principate, was killed with his father,
not in his own name; although I find that this one too raged beyond his
years against a good many, as the letters ordered by this same boy to
his father teach.

For when certain men had incurred suspicion of revolt, and Macrinus had
punished them most savagely while his son happened to be away, and this
boy had heard that the authors of the revolt had indeed been killed, but
the accomplices, among whom were the governor of Armenia and likewise
the legate of Asia and of Arabia, had been dismissed because of old
familiarity, he is said to have remonstrated with his father in this
letter, like letters having been sent also to his mother, of which I
thought an example should be inserted for history's sake:

/The son Augustus to the father Augustus. You do not seem, my father, to
have held to your character enough in love of us, you who have reserved
the accomplices of an attempted tyranny, hoping they would either be
friendlier to you if you spared them, or that they should be dismissed
because of old familiarity. Which neither ought to have been done nor
will profit. For first of all, already ulcerated by suspicions, they
cannot love you; then they are crueller enemies, who, having forgotten
old familiarity, have joined themselves to your bitterest enemies; add
that they still have armies.

If no glory of such great deeds moves you,
look to rising Ascanius and the hopes of Iulus the heir,
to whom the kingdom of Italy and the Roman land is owed.

Those men must be struck down if you wish to be secure, for by a vice of
the human race others will not be lacking while these are kept./

Some report this letter as his own, some as of his teacher Caelianus,
once an African rhetor; from which it appears how harsh the young man
would have been if he had lived.

There exists another letter sent by the same to his mother, of this
kind:
/Our lord and Augustus loves neither you nor even himself, who keeps his
enemies. Act therefore so that Arabianus and Tuscus and Gellius be bound
to the stake, lest, if occasion come, they not let it pass./

And, as far as Lollius Urbicus says in the history of his own time,
these letters, betrayed through a secretary, are said to have harmed
that boy much among the soldiers. For when they had killed the father,
some wished to save this one, but a chamberlain came forward who read
these letters to the soldiers' assembly.

When both had therefore been killed and their heads carried about on a
pike, the army inclined to Marcus Aurelius Antoninus from love of the
name. He was said to be the son of Bassianus Caracalla, but he was a
priest of the temple of Elagabalus, the most impure of all men and one
who by some fate disfigured the Roman empire, of whom indeed, because
there is much, I shall treat in its own place.
