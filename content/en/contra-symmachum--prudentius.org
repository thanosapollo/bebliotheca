Paul, herald of God, who first tamed
the wild hearts of the nations with the sacred pen,
sowing Christ through peoples fierce
in harsh rites with a peaceful teaching,
that the untamed pagan race might scorn
its ceremonies, God being known,
had by chance in blackest storms
already suffered the winter sea in a weak ship
and the force of a ship-wrecking south wind.
but when the Lord's right hand had ordered
the battles of the dark-blue gulf to be still,
the floating skiff slides back to harbor
and sets on the soil of the wet shore
the oarsmen cramped by rainy cold,
then from the shore hedges the chilled men
hastily gather arms of dry vine-shoots
from which they may build quick fires:
each piles his own bundle on the flames
awaiting the luxury of a warm pyre.
Paul, while he strives to collect fragile twigs
and pack the heap of the hearth,
thrust an unwary hand into the piles,
where a sluggish viper, numb with ice,

was winding the snares of its body in the brushwood.
which after it grew warm on the smoky fuel
and fiercely loosened its stiff neck,
now easy to bend, brought back to the hand
with vibrating head the points of its teeth.
Paul shuddered, bearing it clinging
by a bite in the wound of his finger and hanging.
others cry out, because they believed
deadly venom was creeping through the livid skin.
but the sad form of so sudden a danger
does not terrify the undaunted apostle.
raising his eyes he looks up at the stars,
murmuring Christ under a silent breast,
and shakes off far the struck asp,
the cast snake beats the air
and looses the points of its open mouth.
soon all the poison and the pain leave
the torn right hand as if with no wound,
and the viperous liquor perishes dried,
while the rush whirls the hydra headlong,
he brought it to burn in the midst of the fires.
so now after winter and the force of the fierce strait,
in which the ship of Wisdom was then tossed,
when terrified under idol-worshipping kings
it could scarcely run with canvas spread
and had carried its afflicted ones in the storm of the age
swimming on raging waves,
the pious law suffered a wounding bite.
for the venom first hid itself withdrawn
and had not put forth its heavy head,
content in wrappings and dens
to press closed silences underneath.
but, while impiety by chance lies hidden stiff,
it had sluggishly bitten the right hand of Justice

seething with the bile of kindled wrath.
alas, how nearly nothing it profited
that the catholic ship had swum by the oarage
of the sacred pen which Paul put forth to various nations!
scarcely had it rested safe in a calm port
victorious, a thousand rages tamed,
scarcely, already bound with its own cables,
had it housed its passengers on stable soil:
a sad danger bursts out suddenly,
for while they kindle very hot little fires for themselves
to loosen both age and frost,
while they burn barren and superfluous twigs
with flames from the vine-shoot of faith,
that the vineyard thick with wandering locks
might put off the filth of a wooded idol,
too much warmth felt out ruin.
an unaccustomed snake begins to creep in windings
and shrewdly to vibrate the head of eloquence:
but the right hand impatient of the wound drove off
the vain breaths of the rhetorical mouth;
the poured venom of talent stood idly
on the outermost skin for the worshippers of Christ.
Savior of the race of Romulus, I pray,
who give pardon to all who perish,
who appoint no mortal not of your work
whom you may raise with easy hand,
if it is possible, now pity
this man headlong into a steep pit.
he breathes unaware with sacrilegious blasts
and unteachable fosters his errors,
I entreat, command that a swift rush
not bring him to burn in the midst of the fires.

I believed that the city, sick with pagan vices,
had already driven off enough the dangers of the old disease
and that no evil remained, after the medicine
of the prince had allayed the immoderate pains in the citadel,
but since a renewed plague tries to disturb
the health of Romulus's sons, the father's healing must be implored,
that he not suffer Rome to grow squalid in ancient lethargy
nor the togas of the nobles to be stained with smoke and blood.
did the renowned father of the fatherland, then, and ruler of the world,
do nothing by forbidding that the old error

the one man whose care it was that the public wound

safe from inward poison?
that had been the medicine of tyrants, to see

how great fruits each province bears and how

let there perhaps be some year a little more unfruitful:
no wonder nor new in the world, the former ones learned
having suffered hunger often, if decaying air
dried thin clouds with a burning star
and a most frequent rain did not pour spring waters
on new and green fruit, if a grown harvest before
it could solidify conceived marrows with tender milk
drew together the juice blasted by a hot east wind
and bore fasting stalks and a sterile wood
of stubbles deceived the vain vows of the farmer.
with these vices, unless I am deceived, the field lay corrupt even before
the Palladium, before Vesta kept the penates
under a Pergamene lar with fire stored,

before the parent of Priam with hired smiths
built walls, before the virgin Pallas founded
her Athens; since in these cities the origin of the Vestal
ember was taken from the first tinder, as they recount,
and either a Phrygian or a Greek consecrated the foster hearths.
the elements slip with ancient errors, and shaken
from a legitimate mode they are often borne
into other chances than the law has or the path of the year.
now devouring rust consumes the crop from the vice
of a malignant air, now in a tepid spring
after west winds the ice of a cold north wind burns the fault
and tings the blasted head of the stalk with soot;
or while the swelling herb of tender seed germinates,
it perishes constrained by continuous and excessive frosts
nor is it able to fasten a thin fiber to the earth;
soon thrown out from the soil as ice settles it is left over
and the naked root is torn from the withdrawn field.
doubtful caltrops come up and the bristling thistle;
dry thirst bears these, drunken moisture educates that.
a temper poured less or more drives those
diseases of the lands and wounds the sick world.
not otherwise the corrupted use of our body
often falls into vice nor stands in right order
and by excess of government affects the limbs;
for there is one status of the world and of this body
which we bear; the same nature sustains both.
things brought forth from nothing grow, and about to be nothing

either they totter with diseases or conquered by time they grow old,
nor does nature lack vice, on which an end presses.
always, believe, the pole has woven years with various yields:
these it enriched flowing with much fruit,
some unhappy it condemned with unfavorable stars,
frustrating rustic care with vain and sterile hope.

we also use the return of the country and the reason of cultivating,

Terminus is broken and is violated with no entrails,

nor yet is the fruit of the little field therefore less
or the glad clemency of a serene season,
or the rainy wind which tempers the cultivated fallows.
but neither is there need of much for those living frugally, and when
the greatest things come forth we are not dissolved into joys
of ample census and we do not exult toward gain with greedy zeal.
for those for whom hope is formed into an eternal age,
every good is thin which the present age heaps.

o too happy, wise and rustic the same,
who cultivating lands and mind spends on both
watchful care, such as those whom the author
Christ imbued, and gave these precepts to assumed colonists:
'when you commit seeds to furrows, beware hard fields
with the leanness of little stones, lest what is sown
fall there, since at first the very fertile germ
luxuriates, soon with juice failing under the heat
of a fire-bearing star thirsting it is roasted and dries;
nor let seeds run into thorny briars,
for harsh bonds weave the rising crop
and the bramble binds fragile stalks with sharp knots;
and lest thrown grains be scattered on the ridge of the way,
because these lie naked open to birds and are everywhere devoured
and lie foul for the filthy sports of ravens.'
with these laws God confirms the farmer; he
does not understand the heavenly right of the Father with the top of the ear,
but at once he arranges both the crop of the heart and of the field,
lest the midriff shine less with inward cult
than when glad acres display their harvests.
for we root out thorny briars from the breast,
lest vicious lashes kill the living germ,
lest a thorny brier of evils impede the fruit and crop
of the soul with frequent sin of crimes,
lest thin gravel with fasting sands dry
faith withering under the heart, lest the heat of the breast
burn and roast the charismata with exhausted veins,
lastly lest cheap care leave God in a worn part
of the liver, lest it desert the hope by which we are fed within
and permit it to be eaten by obscene birds,
and cast-out faith be prey for a winged enemy.

such cunning of ours will bring back hundredfold
fruits of the fields, on which it presses more keenly,

there are also most beautiful rewards for our virgins:
both shame and a face covered with a holy veil,
and private honor and a form not known and public,
and rare and thin feasts and a mind always sober,

hence ten times ten fruits are brought back into granaries,
granaries never liable to a nocturnal thief,
for no thief approaches heaven, heavenly things never

encounters of men and deaths and wounds sold for food

o tender and mild mind! she rises to the blows
and, as often as the victor inserts iron in the throat, she
says they are her delights, and the modest virgin

lest any part of the soul hide in the lowest vitals,

while the pursuer palpitates with sword pressed deeper.
is this that merit, that they are said to continue
watches for the majesty of the Latin Palatine,
that they redeem the life of the people and the safety of the nobles,
because they pour necks with hair well or well gird
temples with little ribbons and add threads to locks,
and because under the ground they slaughter lustral cattle
for witnessing shades into flame and mix murmurs?
or because sitting in a better part of the podium
they watch how a bronze face, how frequent spears
with trident driven in shake, and what part of the sand
the wounded man pours with open wounds
when he flees, and with how much blood he marks his steps?
that golden Rome may now not know that kind of crime,
I pray you, most august leader of the Ausonian kingdom,
and order so sad a rite, as the rest, to be removed.
look, is not that place of paternal merit vacant,
which God and the friendly piety of a father kept
for you to be filled? lest he alone take the rewards of so great
virtue, 'I reserve a part for you, son,'

he said, and left the honor whole and untouched.
seize, leader, the fame delayed into your times,

and what remains for the father, have as successor of praise.
he forbade the city to be stained with the blood of bulls:
you forbid the deaths of wretched men to be offered.
let no one fall in the city whose punishment is a pleasure,
nor let her own virginity delight faces with slaughters.
let the infamous arena now content with beasts alone
sport no homicides in bloodied arms.
let Rome be devoted to God, let her be worthy of so great a prince
and powerful in virtue and unknowing of crime,
and whom she follows as leader in wars, let her follow in piety.
