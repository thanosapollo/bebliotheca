Since then these men, having found ten thousand slanders against him, were
harming the disciples, not persuading them to have the fitting opinion
concerning him, he is forced at last to fall into a narration of his own
praises; for it was no longer safe to be silent. Since then he is about to
recount to us his own contests, and the revelations which he saw, and the
toils which he toiled, wishing to show to all that he does this unwillingly
and under compulsion, and though he sees that there is necessity, nevertheless
he called the thing itself folly, speaking thus: "Would that you would bear
with me a little in my folly." "I am about," he says, "to do a foolish thing,
and to praise and commend; but I am not the cause of these things, but those
who have thrown me into such a necessity; for this reason I entreat you to
bear with me, and to reckon the cause to them."

4. And see Paul's wisdom: having said, "Would that you would bear with me a
little in my folly; but indeed do bear with me; for I am jealous over you with
a jealousy of God," he did not come at once to the narration of the praises,
but having inserted words in between again, he speaks somehow thus: "Again I
say, let no one think me to be a fool; but if otherwise, receive me even as a
fool." And not even so did he touch the narration, but again he adds and says:
"What I speak, I speak not according to the Lord, but as in folly, in this
confidence of boasting." And not even after these words did he dare to let
himself down, but having set out again he draws back and says: "Since many
boast according to the flesh, I also will boast; for you gladly bear with the
foolish, being yourselves wise." Then again he shrinks back and hesitates, and
having said certain other things, he adds again: "Whereinsoever any is bold, I
speak in folly, I also am bold." And then scarcely =51.305= after so many
prefatory corrections did he dare the narration of the praises. And just as a
horse that is about to cross a sheer precipice rushes as if about to overleap
it, but having seen the depth grows numb and draws back, then seeing the rider
compelling him more vehemently, attempts again, and suffers this same thing,
and showing the necessity and the force, stands for a long time neighing on
the lip of the ravine, that having encouraged himself he may dare the descent:
so also the blessed Paul, as if about to cast himself down some precipice, the
narration of his own praises, both once and twice and thrice and many times
shrinks back, speaking thus: "Would that you would bear with me a little in my
folly"; and again, "Let no one think me to be a fool; but if otherwise,
receive me even as a fool"; and, "What I speak, I speak not according to the
Lord, but as in folly, in this confidence of boasting"; and again, "Since many
boast according to the flesh, I also will boast; for you gladly bear with the
foolish, being yourselves wise"; and again, "Whereinsoever any is bold, I
speak in folly, I also am bold."

And having called himself a fool and a mindless man ten thousand times, then
he scarcely dared to come to his own praises: "Are they Hebrews? so am I. Are
they Israelites? so am I. Are they the seed of Abraham? so am I. Are they
ministers of Christ? so am I." And not even here did he catch himself, but
again he sets the prefatory correction, adding thus: "I speak as one beside
himself, I more." And he did not even stop here, but after recounting all his
praises, he says: "I have become a fool in boasting; you compelled me." As if
he were saying, Those men were nothing to me, if your affairs had been sound,
if you had not been turned aside, nor shaken. For even if those men were
speaking ill of us continually, no harm was coming to me through their
slander. But when I saw the flock being corrupted, the disciples leaping away,
I despised a burdensome and grievous thing, and I was forced to become a fool,
speaking my own praises for your sake and for your salvation.

5. For such is the custom of the saints: if they should do something base,
they parade it, and every day they lament and make it manifest to all; but if
something noble and great, they hide it and send it into forgetfulness. This
same saint, at any rate, even when no one was compelling him, was continually
turning over and parading his sins, now saying, "Christ Jesus came into the
world to save sinners, of whom I am first"; and now saying, "I thank Christ
who strengthened me, that he counted me faithful, putting into ministry him
who was before a blasphemer and a persecutor and injurious; but I obtained
mercy, because I did it ignorantly in unbelief"; and again, "And last of all,
as to the untimely birth, he appeared also to me; for I am the least of the
apostles, who am not sufficient to be called an apostle, because I persecuted
the Church of God"; and again, "To me, the least of all saints, was this grace
given." Do you see how he calls himself last not of the apostles only, but
simply of all the faithful, saying, "To me, the least of all the saints, was
this grace given"?

Thus he says he is not even =51.306= worthy of the salvation by which he was
saved; for having said that "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners,
of whom I am first": hear also for what cause he says this: "But for this
cause I obtained mercy, that in me first Jesus Christ might show all his
long-suffering, for a pattern of those who were about to believe on him unto
eternal life." And what he means is this: I was not shown mercy because of a
worthy change of life, do not suppose this; but that no one of those who have
lived in wickedness, nor of those who have warred against Christ, may despair,
seeing the last of all, and after whom there was no other so hostile to
Christ, saved. And Christ says, "He is a chosen vessel unto me to bear my name
before nations and kings"; but this man, puffed up by none of those praises,
remains calling himself wretched after so great a confidence, naming himself
first of sinners, and saying that he was shown mercy for this reason, that no
one of those who have gone on to the last wickedness may despair of his own
salvation, looking to him and to the loving-kindness that came to pass toward
him.

6. The sins then, even when there is no necessity, he parades every day in all
his epistles, pillorying them and making them plain not to the men of that
time only, but also to all who were to be after these things; but the praises,
even though he sees that there is necessity, nevertheless he hesitates and
shrinks from recounting. And this is clear from the fact that he called the
thing folly ten thousand times, and clear also from all the time during which
he kept silent that marvelous and divine revelation; for he had seen it not
then, nor two and three and ten years before, but many more. For this reason
he also sets down the time itself, speaking thus: "I know a man fourteen years
ago caught up as far as the third heaven"; that you may learn that he would
not even then have spoken, if he had not seen a great necessity lying upon
him. For if he had wished to go through his own praises, he would have spoken
it at once, when he saw it, or in the first and second and third year; but as
it is he endured fourteen years and kept silent and told it to no one, but to
the Corinthians only.

And when? When he saw the false apostles attacking, showing that he would not
even then have spoken, if he had not seen so great a corruption coming to pass
among the disciples. But we are not so, but we do the complete opposite: of
our sins we do not remember even one day, but even if we hear others
mentioning them, we are indignant, we are displeased, we reckon the thing an
insult, we wash them with ten thousand revilings; but if we should do some
small good, we turn this over continually, and we feel gratitude toward those
who mention it, and we consider such men to be friends; and yet Christ
commanded the opposite, to forget achievements, but to remember sins. And this
he made clear to us both through the things he was advising the disciples,
saying: "When you have done all things, say, We are unprofitable servants";
and through the parable of the Pharisee, setting the publican before him. For
just as the memory of sins justified this man, so the memory of achievements
destroyed that one. And to the Jews also God advises these very same things,
saying =51.307= thus: "I am he who blots out your sins, and I will not
remember; but you, remember."

7. Such was the character of the apostles, such that of the prophets and of
all the righteous. David, at any rate, was continually remembering his own
sin, but his achievements nowhere, except if ever he was forced. When, at any
rate, that barbarian war overtook Judaea, and all things were full of dangers,
being still young and inexperienced in wars, leaving the sheep, and coming to
the battle-line, seeing all cowering and fearing and trembling, he suffered
nothing human, nor did he become more cowardly seeing his own people humbled;
but having surpassed all that was seen by faith, and having looked toward the
King of the heavens, and having filled himself with much eagerness, he came to
the soldiers and to the brothers, promising to deliver them from the danger
that was holding them. But when the brothers laughed at what was said (for
they did not see the God who was anointing him within, nor that noble soul,
heaven-high, and full of much philosophy), leaving those, he went away to
others.

But when they brought him in to the king himself, and he found him dead with
fear, first he raises up his spirit, speaking thus: "Let not my lord's heart
fall upon him, for your servant will go and fight with this foreigner." But
when that man disbelieved, saying, "You will not be able to go; you are a boy,
and he is a man of war from his youth," David, being then at a loss, is forced
to recount his own praises. For that he did not wish to, he showed through the
former things, saying nothing of his own achievements either to the brothers,
or to the soldiers, nor even to the king himself, until he saw him
disbelieving and in agony and hindering the assault against that man. For what
was he to do then? Keep silent about the praises? But he would not have
allowed him to go, nor to deliver them from the dangers that were holding
them. For this reason, having been silent when he ought, when he saw the time
compelling him to speak, he is silent no longer, but says to him: "Your
servant was shepherding in the flock of my father, and whenever a lion or a
bear came, and took a sheep from the herd, I went out after him, and I struck
him, and I snatched it from his mouth, and I seized his throat, and I killed
him; and your servant smote both the lion and the bear; and this foreigner and
uncircumcised shall be as one of these." Do you see how he made clear for what
reason he spoke his own achievements? Then indeed, then the king taking heart
at last commanded him to go. And he went, and he arrayed himself, and he
conquered. But if he had not spoken praises, the king would not have entrusted
that single combat to him; and not trusting, he would not have let him go to
the battle-line; and not letting him, he would have hindered the achievement;
and the achievement being hindered, neither would God have been glorified
then, nor would the city have been delivered from the dangers lying upon it.
In order then that so many absurd things might not come to pass, nor so great
a work of providence be hindered, David was forced to speak his own contests.
For just as they know how to be silent when there is no necessity, so they
also know how to speak, whenever they see much force lying upon them.

8. And not in this case only, but in the case of Samuel =51.308= also one may
see this same thing having come to pass. For that man, having presided over
the people of the Jews for so many years as God wished, and never having
uttered anything great concerning himself, although he had many things, if he
had wished, to say, the rearing from first youth, the sojourn in the temple,
the prophecy from his swaddling-clothes, the wars after these things, the
victories which he won, not using weapons, but arraying himself with the
goodwill of God, in the former times said none of these things. But when he
was about to withdraw from the presidency, and to put the rule into another's
hands, then at last he was forced to go through his praises, and these in a
lowered way. And having called the whole people, Saul also being present, he
speaks somehow thus: "Behold I have heard your voice, and I have made a king
to reign over you; and I, behold, I have walked before you from my youth, and
until this day, and I have grown old. Answer against me before the Lord, and
before his anointed: whose calf have I taken? or whose donkey have I taken? or
whom of you have I oppressed? or whom of you have I crushed? or from whose
hand of you have I taken a ransom or a shoe, and hidden my eyes in it?"

Say against me, and I will restore it to you. And what necessity was there to
say these things, he says? Much and great. For since he was about to introduce
Saul as ruler to them, in his

own defense, wishing to teach that man how one must preside and care for those
who are ruled, he brings forward the subjects themselves as witnesses of his
philosophy. And he does not do this in the time of the rule, lest someone say
that fearing him and being afraid, they testified things that were not; but
when the things of the leadership were dissolved, and the things of the
presidency had passed over to another, and there was no longer any danger to
the accuser, then he goes to trial with them. And yet if it had been some
other man, he would have borne a grudge against the Jews, and would not have
wished the ruler after him to become gentle and moderate, not through bearing
a grudge only, but also that he himself might be praised more greatly.

9. For this is a terrible disease in rulers: they pray that those who come
after them to the rule be base and wicked. For if they happen to be noble,
they suppose that they themselves will appear more illustrious, the successors
of the rule not being such; and if they are clumsy and corrupted, they suppose
that the wickedness of the ruler after these things will be a defense of their
own wickedness. But this blessed man was not such; but he wished and prayed
and desired that they enjoy a far better presidency; so affectionate was he,
so pure of envy, so delivered from vainglory. And yet he was seeking one thing
only, the salvation of men. For this reason he was also training the ruler for
them in his own defense. For since to call the king and say, "Be gentle and
moderate and unbribable, and force no one, nor do injustice, nor be greedy,"
was burdensome and grievous to the one about to hear these things; and to be
silent again was a betrayal of the people: in the pretext of a defense he
accomplished both these things, and taught that man what sort the one who
reigns ought to be, and escaped the offensiveness from teaching. And he seems
to be contending for the =51.309= things concerning himself; but he is
training that man how and in what manner one must take care of those who are
ruled. And do you consider for me how with all exactness he showed himself
clean of gain. For he did not say, "Did I take anyone's fields of you? any
gold?" but what was cheaper than all, "A shoe?" he says.

Then he also made clear to us another great virtue of his own. For since many
of the rulers, when they steal, are gentle and moderate and mild not from
themselves, but through the necessity of conscience, having been stripped of
confidence by the thefts; and the unbribable are burdensome and grievous, not
they themselves again from themselves, but from a certain vainglory and from
being clean of gain; and one would not easily see both coming together into
one man: this saint, wishing to show that he had prevailed over both, and
mastered gain and anger, having said, "Have I taken anyone's calf of you?" did
not keep silent, but added, "Or have I oppressed anyone of you or crushed
him?" that is, squeezed him? And what he means is of this sort: No one would
be able to say this, that I did not take, but because I did not take, I became
burdensome and grievous and savage and wild. For this reason he was saying,
"Or have I oppressed anyone of you?" What then did those men? "You have
neither oppressed us, nor crushed us, nor taken anything from our hand." And
that you may learn that he was saying these things training the king himself
as well, he added: "The Lord is witness, and his anointed is witness"; and
hinting this very thing to us and showing that the testimony was not a favor,
he called as witness him who knows the secrets of the mind, which is a proof
of a clean conscience. For no one, no one, unless he were exceedingly mad and
out of his mind, would ever call God as witness of his own conscience, unless
he were exceedingly confident in himself. Those men then having testified to
the things said by him, he himself also shows another virtue of his own; and
having recalled all the ancient things in Egypt, and the protection of God,
and the wars after those, he reminds them of the battle that came to pass in
his time and of the paradoxical victory; and having said how often, when they
had been delivered to the enemies because of their own sins, he himself called
=51.310= upon God, and delivered them from the enemies, joining the new things
to the old, he adds and says: "The Lord sent Jerubbaal, Gideon, and Barak, and
Jephthah, and Samuel, and delivered you round about from the hand of your
enemies, and you dwell in confidence."

10. Do you see how it is the custom of the saints not to speak their own
achievements, unless they should ever be forced? For this reason Paul also,
looking toward these, and being trained exactly that for a man to speak about
himself is grievous and burdensome, was saying: "Would that you would bear
with me a little in my folly"; not a great thing, but some little thing. For
not even when there is necessity is he prepared to pour himself out with
abundance into the narration of the praises, but he runs over them in brief;
and this very thing for their sake and for their salvation. For just as, when
there is no necessity to speak one's own achievements, it is the last madness,
so when necessity is lying upon one and force is pushing, it is again a
betrayal to keep silent about the things they have done. But nevertheless
Paul, even seeing that there is necessity, was shrinking, and was calling the
thing folly, that you may learn his wisdom and his prudence and his great
caution. For having said, "What I speak, I speak not according to the Lord,"
he added, "In this confidence of boasting." "Do not suppose," he says, "that I
am saying this altogether." So that for this reason I praise him most and
wonder and call him most wise, that he judged it a matter of folly to praise
and commend himself.

But if this man, seeing that there is necessity, was calling this folly, of
what pardon would they be worthy, of what defense, who even when there is no
necessity speak great things about themselves, or even force others to speak?
Knowing these things then, let us not only praise what is said, but also
imitate and be zealous, and forgetting achievements, let us always remember
the sins, that we may both be able to be moderate, and stretching forward to
the things before, may receive the prize of the upward calling, by the grace
and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the Father together
with the Holy Spirit be glory, power, honor, now and always, and unto the ages
of ages. Amen.
