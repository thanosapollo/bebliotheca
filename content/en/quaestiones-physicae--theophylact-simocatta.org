Of Theophylact Simocatta, of the former prefects, a dialogue concerning
various physical problems and their solutions. The discourse of the dialogue.
I have heard that swallows are taught their song musically by their mothers;
for a mother swallow does not endure to hear an uneducated offspring. They
therefore teach the young to settle in cities and to sing to men and to
chatter around chambers a harmonious song. Come then, come, most musical
assembly, grant eloquence to a child, and teach me to sing to the theatre and
to chatter an Attic song, as one just now learning to enter the chambers of
the Muses. And if I should also fall somewhat outside the musi8 cal rhythms,
echo generously for me what is left, as the cicada was a fellow-worker of the
melody beside Eunomus the Locrian. It seemed good also to Teucer to go to war
and to love trophies and to desire a martial array. Therefore Ajax was walling
with the projection of his shield, and Teucer was a most accurate archer; for
Ajax was the ground of the prowess. Hold up for me, hold up, men teachers,
your goodwill as a shield, and let applause be weapons for me today. And I
know I shall be victorious, even if I have not set foot on barbarian land. And
if I have not yet been allotted a mother art, yet I have a race from which I
am yours; and I have words as a fatherland and the things of Hellas as ours.
The heads of the dialogue. 1. For what reason the diamond is unburnable and
without share in the nature of fire. 2. For what reason the feathers of
eagles, when placed upon the feathers of the other animals, work destruction
upon them. 3. For what reason elephants do not drink before they have muddied
the water. 4. For what reason the torpedo transmits through the midst of the
ropes the affection of its name, and the hunters' hands grow numb. 5. For what
reason deer, having cast their horns, flee the accustomed places. 6. For what
reason rue is hostile to snakes. 7. For what reason oil poured upon the sea
makes a calm. 8. For what reason vultures are with young for three years. 9.
For what reason the octopus turns away from the Pontic sea. 10. For what
reason goat's blood softens the diamond. 11. For what reason superfetation
occurs in hares. 12. For what reason ravens do not drink in the season of
summer. 13. For what reason a flux of the belly befalls ravens. 14. For what
reason a female cicada is by nature without share in song. 10 15. For what
reason the eggs of ibises most readily give birth to basilisks. 16. For what
reason the charadrius turns away from those with jaundice. 17. For what reason
the almond tree bearing well also signifies abundance of the remaining fruits.
18. For what reason waters boiled by fire are more lasting than the other
waters, while those from rains disappear more quickly. 19. For what reason in
Seriphos frogs have been established voiceless. 20. For what reason a fly does
not settle upon Attic honey. The persons of the dialogue: Antisthenes and
Polycrates. Antisthenes, or the Labyrinth. {ANT.} Aristagoras the son of
Timocles I have not seen, Polycrates, for a long time indeed. Where then? Has
he perhaps departed for Sicily to look upon the fire-breathing craters, or is
he swimming round the Indian gulf, that he may procure some science of
philosophy? 11 {POL.} Not at all, Antisthenes, since Apollogenes the
Corinthian, having arrived yesterday somewhere from Athens to us about the
Painted Stoa, said he had seen Aristagoras. And he reported that he had seen
Ariston the natural philosopher and Sosistratus the son of Hermogenes arriving
the same day about the Leocorium. {ANT.} From where then, Polycrates? {POL.}
Having sailed about the Euxine gulf and having spent time in very many

and very great labors they brought us as cargo the ingenious theories, as some
golden fleece; for a certain love of science having struck them goaded both
somehow and bacchicized them toward the grasp of inquiry. For they were eager,
they were eager, to look upon the Maeotis lake, whether it is round in
situation, and whether indeed nine thousands of stades bound it as a circuit,
as geographers proclaim, putting forward a trustworthy demonstration to us.
And they say that Pericles the Corinthian, having met them at the Bosporus,
raised certain proble12 ms of natural philosophy. From there words and
theories surge about the city, and everyone philosophizes how he may say
something piercing, and by elegance of doctrine one may prevail over another
in opinion. The Peripatos has the contest toward the Stoa, the Stoa is
entangled with Pythagoreans, the word of Epicureans is not poured in libation
to Academics, and a certain war without iron has been set in motion throughout
Hellas. {ANT.} Come then, my good man, tell us about this; for as you see I
have been winged in mind toward theory and I track veins of science to look
upon, as certain metal-workers whenever they would look upon the passages of
some most precious material. {POL.} Not about some small thing do tongues and
words and theories strike and are struck; for a certain adamantine war has
armed itself and they unroll the mysteries of nature. For they say that
Pericles said why then has nature created the diamond unburnable and high13 er
than the all-devouring fire. This, friend, disturbs the chorus of the
philosophers and has prepared them to be inspired toward research of the
secret things; for the Corinthian, having wrapped us in such bonds, having
shut us in as in some mythical labyrinth by the hard-to-enter of the theory,
went away. {ANT.} I will tell you a work of nature, Polycrates, since I
perceive you to be an ambitious lover of musical words; for may I have nothing
good unknown to a friend, being persuaded by the Cyrenaic. {POL.} Come then,
come, Antisthenes, let the word lead, by which we are accustomed to share the
purpose with one another. {ANT.} Certain most fine pores have grown in the
diamond because of its extreme solidity; wherefore even iron is weak toward
diamond; for it is unreceptive both of engraving and of cutting because of its
extreme density. From there it does not perceive flame, as if the pores were
barring it, and simply as if they were acting against the stone receiving the
nature of fire. The unmixed things then also stand apart, and of things
standing apart there is no relation, and diamond is unpaired with fire;
therefore it is unburnable and by density has been alienated from the nature
of fire. Iron at any rate and gold receive the nature of fire, whence such
things are melted or softened. Yes, and 14 because of being rather
moistureless the diamond is by nature unburnable; for everything meltable must
of necessity have a share of very much moisture. {POL.} Well done for the
word; with certain adamantine phrases you have variegated the theories about
diamond; yes by him, even if there is something higher than diamond; for truth
is stronger for us than diamond. But come, let us mirror that much-bruited and
notable problem of the Corinthian; for his thought was very much plumed even
upon this. {ANT.} Ask whatever you wish. {POL.} He used to say that the
feathers of eagles, being somehow placed upon and intertwined with the
feathers of the other animals, destroy them, as if even here [the] eagle,
wishing to be tyrant of the other winged things, and being stripped of its
hair, were trying no less to mount royal power. What is the reason,
Antisthenes? Narrate to me works of nature. {ANT.} O the Corinthian, how he is
a sufficient sophist of some mystic science. But at any rate I will say
something to you, Polycrates; 15 and you set your ear quietly upon me and you
will hear a Sirenian voice, as some Ithacan coming upon those very songs
beyond the swans. {POL.} Speak then, my good man. {ANT.} The eagle is a most
ill-smelling bird; from there indeed it would never again go upon the remnants
of its own hunt, nor would it obtain, friend, yesterday's hunt; and they are
uneatable even for the other animals; for they have vanished the same day, as
remnants of a dinner of some Phineus and a Harpy

having become. Wherefore the feathers of eagles are somehow destructive. For
they have a share of some putrefying nature. And certain feathers of others
would not be saved having come near the hair of eagles; for they have perished
more quickly. {POL.} Very thoughtfully, my dear. But look for me at the panels
of Apelles, Antisthenes; I have marveled, man, at the seemliness of the
painting. Look for me at this Indian animal, how the craftsman of the painting
has variegated it most excellently. There is a word that an elephant does not
drink before it has muddied the streams with its trunk. 16 {ANT.} The saying
is not fabricated; an elephant is terrified seeing its own shadow in water.
Wherefore they say the Indians also watch for a moonless night whenever they
ferry this animal through waters. Let the elephant educate the mythical
Narcissus to turn away from his own shadow and to keep away from loves foreign
to nature. {POL.} I admire the power of the theory, how altogether clever you
are at going after the reasons of nature. But come, philosophize for me the
words about the torpedo; and may the haughty tongue of the Corinthian grow
numb, vaunting the problems about the torpedo. He used to say that those who
work the sea, if ever a torpedo is caught in the weel, immediately suffer in
both hands as they draw the weel with the ropes. How then, my good man, does
the animal defend itself against the hunters through the ropes? {ANT.}
Pericles has moved his tongue upon the sea of wonders. Hold your thought
within the bounds of knowledge, as one eager to inquire into an Atlantic
pelagos of theory. And I, Polycrates, will lead your love with certain small
words. 17 {POL.} You may speak, O noble one. For the words seem to me elegant
and beneficial to the people to be set forth. I would not be left behind you
today, no by my best love. {ANT.} Air has been allotted by lot a most
impressionable nature; for it has grown resonant and transparent and permeable
to smell. For thus certain divine cares have created it most excellently, that
somehow sympathies and activities may come about through it throughout this
region around the earth. This then is cause of many affections and activities.
For how have those diseased in the eyes often made those who look upon them
partners of the affection? Yes, and of the surrounding air being in us after
the manner of a mid-space of strength and of baseness. Therefore through the
air that is in them the torpedo also knows how to produce some affection in
those who come near it, even if they should not touch it. And I will tell you
a proof of this too. The magnet, which they also 18 call the Heraclean stone,
stands apart from the iron, and yet through the midst of the air we see the
stone putting forth the activity. And what thought does the sympathy that
comes about also about the palm as a plant not drive out of its wits? What
hearing has it not filled with wonder? Upon the palm as a plant the race of
the females is distinguished one way and that of the males another; then when
the male and the female are near one another, you would see the female letting
down her branches as erotic hair toward the male and swelling with desire of
intertwining. And since her branches sometimes do not reach to the male, the
planters contrive something wise for her toward the love; for they bind her
with ropes to the male, and thus the female comes into perception of the
enjoyment and rises from the bending and has brought the branches back to
their proper shape, as having obtained the conjunction of the male through the
ropes. Nothing strange then that a torpedo also produces an affection in those
hunting through the midst of the ropes. 1 {POL.} Bravo for the word; you seem
to me very much, Antisthenes, to be a brother of nature. But the word loves to

attached to them. {ANT.} Someone might perhaps think, Polycrates, that
Pericles is playing, or is somehow corybantizing and gone mad, putting forward
this piece of chatter as a problem. But I have marveled at the man and I honor
the love of the beautiful

that sailors contrive a calm for the sea by putting in the oil, as if
mechanizing that it will be gracious from there 23 as it surges. This indeed
those who work the sea are also accustomed to do about the catches of the
fishes; ferry me upon this theory by the word, as by some skiff, Antisthenes.
{ANT.} Breath is by nature most fine, Polycrates; and the oil has been
established sticky because of the smoothness. The breath then, slipping
because of the smoothness, is unable to furnish a surge to the waters. From
there the sea smiles a calm, as if lulling its anger with some humane oil.
{POL.} I will still hold you and I would not let go, Antisthenes; for by love
of pleasure I have been deprived of satiety. The Corinthian marvels that
vultures are with young a three-year time, and it seems to me to be foreign to
the laws of nature. For not yet have I heard such

a dramatization. {ANT.} Many races of the birds, Polycrates, do not need the
intertwining of the males toward conception. But among the 24 others the

the whole race campaigns toward offspring, as Lemnian women looking upon a

Antisthenes. But we have been caught and we leap upon the weel of truth. Would

go away having acquired the scien25 ce half-wrought. They say that the fishes

unwillingly rises from its own base. 26 Beginning of the second discourse

elegant in science, nor Plotinus, nor Sotion, nor Alexander, nor Theophrastus
the sea of knowledge, nor Bolus, nor Aelian, nor the wealth of science,
Plutarch, nor Ambron, nor Imbrasius or Damascius or Hierocles the son of
Timagenes. And why then should I put upon you the catalogue of the rest, from
whom having gathered certain premises and beginnings, we have given out as it
were some synopsis to you fond of inquiry as it was possible? For it is not
lawful, Polycrates, to be solemn in another's adornment as some mythical
jackdaw. For there is nothing which has not been accomplished by the ancients.
And to meet with it most readily is not very easy. {POL.} May I have benefit
of your science, Antisthenes, and I go away rejoicing. For I have received as
some well-met lucky find the science of the hard-to-procure words.
