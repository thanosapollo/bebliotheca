* Letter to Serapion on the Death of Arius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Athanasius to Serapion, brother and fellow-minister, greeting in the Lord.
1.1 I have read the things written by your Piety, in which you urged us to
make known to you both the things concerning us that have now come to pass,
and concerning the most impious heresy of the Arians, 1.2 on account of which
we have also suffered these things, and what manner of end of life Arius had.
There being, then, three injunctions, I have eagerly performed the two and
have sent to your Godliness the things which I wrote to the monks; for from
these you can learn both the things concerning us and those concerning the
heresy. But as for the other, I mean that concerning the death, I reasoned
much, being cautious lest someone should reckon me as trampling upon the death
of the man. Nevertheless, since a discussion having taken place among you
concerning the heresy, the inquiry ended in this, whether Arius died after
having been in communion with the Church; for this reason, the inquiry being
resolved by the narrative concerning the death, I was eager of necessity to
make it known, 1.3 counting it the same to signify this and to put a stop
henceforth to those who contend. For I suppose that when the wonder concerning
the death is known, no longer will even those who formerly inquired dare to
doubt that the Arian heresy is hated of God. 2.1 I myself was not present in
Constantinople when that man died, but Macarius the presbyter was present, and
I heard him telling it. Arius had been summoned by the emperor Constantine
through the eagerness of those around Eusebius. And when Arius had entered,
the emperor examined him whether he held the faith of the catholic Church; and
he himself swore that he believed rightly, and he submitted a written
statement of faith, concealing the things for which he was expelled from the
Church by Alexander 2.2 the bishop, but speciously alleging the words of
Scripture as a mask. When he had sworn, then, that he had not held the
opinions for which Alexander expelled him, the emperor dismissed him, saying:
"If your faith is orthodox, you have sworn well; but if your faith is impious,
and you have sworn, may God from the oath judge the matter concerning you." So
then, when he had come out from the emperor, 2.3 those around Eusebius wished
to bring him into the Church by their customary violence. But the bishop of
Constantinople, the blessed Alexander, spoke against it, declaring that the
inventor of the heresy ought not to be received into communion. And then those
around Eusebius threatened that "just as, though you were unwilling, we caused
him to be summoned by the emperor, so tomorrow, even if it is not according to
your mind, Arius will assemble with us in this church." And it was Saturday
when they said these things. 3.1 Bishop Alexander, then, having heard these
things and being greatly grieved, went into the church, and stretching out his
hands toward God he lamented, and having thrown himself on his face in the
sanctuary, lying on the floor he prayed. And Macarius also was present,
praying 3.2 with him and hearing his voice. And he was entreating, saying
these two things: "If Arius is assembled tomorrow, dismiss me your servant,
and do not destroy the pious together with the impious; but if you spare your
Church (and I know that you spare), look upon the words of those around
Eusebius, and do not give your inheritance to disappearance and reproach, and
take away Arius, lest when he has entered into the Church the heresy also
should seem to enter with him, and thereafter impiety 3.3 be reckoned as
piety." Having prayed these things, the bishop withdrew, being greatly
anxious. And something wonderful and unexpected took place: for those around
Eusebius having threatened, the bishop prayed, but Arius, taking courage from
those around Eusebius and talking much nonsense, went into a privy as for a
need of the belly, and suddenly, according to what is written, "falling
headlong he burst asunder in the midst," and falling he immediately expired,
and was deprived of both communion and life. 4.1 Such then was the end of
Arius. And those around Eusebius, being greatly ashamed, buried their
fellow-conspirator; but the blessed Alexander, the Church rejoicing,
celebrated the synaxis in piety and orthodoxy with all the brethren, praying
and glorifying God greatly, not as rejoicing at the death (God forbid; for "it
is appointed for all men once to die"), but because this was shown to be
beyond the judgments of men. 4.2 For the Lord himself, having judged between
the threats of those around Eusebius and the prayer of Alexander, condemned
the Arian heresy, showing it to be unworthy of ecclesiastical communion, and
making it plain to all that, even if it has the patronage of the emperor and
of all men, yet it has been condemned by the Church herself. It has been shown
then 4.3 that the Christ-fighting workshop of the Ariomaniacs is not
God-beloved but impious. And many of those formerly deceived came over. For
not another, but the Lord himself who is blasphemed by them, condemned the
heresy that had been formed against him, and showed again that, even if now
the emperor Constantius forces the bishops on behalf of it, still it is out of
communion with the Church and alien to the heavens. Therefore let the inquiry
among you also henceforth cease; for this was the agreement that was made; and
let no one attach himself to the heresy, but let those who have been deceived
also repent. For what the Lord has condemned, who will accept? And that which
he himself has made out of communion, how shall he who receives it not be
greatly impious and openly a Christ-fighter? 5.1 This then is sufficient even
by itself to put the contentious to shame; nevertheless read also to those who
then inquired the things written in brief against the heresy to the monks, so
that from these also they may still more condemn the impiety and the
wickedness of the Ariomaniacs. Yet do not be willing to give a copy of these
things to anyone, nor to transcribe them for yourself; for I have made this
known to the monks also. But as a genuine friend, if anything is lacking in
the things written, add it, 5.2 and immediately send it back to us. For even
from the letter which I wrote to the brethren you can learn what I suffered in
writing, and know that for this reason also it is not safe for the writings of
a private person to be published, and especially concerning the highest and
most sovereign doctrines, lest what has been said inadequately through
weakness or the unclarity of speech should cause harm to those who read. For
the many do not look to the faith nor to the intent of the writer, but either
through envy or through strife of an opinion which they have preconceived in
mind, they receive as they themselves wish and recast the things written
according to their own pleasure. And may the Lord grant among all, and
especially among those to whom you read these things, that both the truth and
the sound faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ may prevail. Amen.
