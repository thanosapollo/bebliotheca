1.5 Read the New Edition of the Chronicle History of Eunapius after Dexippus,
in fourteen books. He begins the history from the reign of Claudius, at which
Dexippus's history ends, and he finishes at the reign of Honorius and
Arcadius, the sons of Theodosius, making that time the end of the history at
which Arsacius, after John, the golden mouth of the Church, had been driven
out, having been raised to the archiepiscopal throne, was serving as priest,
and the wife of the reigning Arcadius, being with child and having miscarried,
departed from life. This Eunapius is a Sardian by race; for he had Sardis in
Lydia as his fatherland; but being impious in religion, for he honoured the
things of the Hellenes, those who adorned the empire with piety he in every
way and freely abuses and slanders, and especially the great Constantine,
while he exalts the impious, and more than the others Julian the Apostate, and
almost the whole of the history was worked out by him as composed into that
man's encomium. He is elegant in expression, if one should strip away from him
the cock-like quality of his words, and the more deer-like, and the more
swine-like, and also 1.6 the hawk-like and raven-like and ape-like and the
river-like tear and the like; for by these he also spoils and adulterates the
rest of the nobility of his names. And he uses tropes recklessly, which the
law of history does not allow, but the emphasis of the diction for the most
part, and its urbanity, take away what is offensive. In composition and in the
clarity suited to history and in periods he is well-proportioned and fitting;
except that in some places more forensically than historically he fills out
and wraps the discourse. He also innovates not a little in constructions,
except not into the ungraceful nor into giving a handle to the methods. He
composed two treatises containing the same history, a first and a second. And
in the first he sows much blasphemy against our pure faith of the Christians
and glorifies Hellenic superstition, attacking many of the pious emperors; but
in the second, which he also entitles New Edition, he cuts away the great
insolence and wantonness which he scattered against piety, and having joined
together the remaining body of the composition he entitles it, as we said, New
Edition, still showing much of the rage from there. We encountered both
editions in old books, each separately arranged in a different volume and
another; and from these themselves, collecting the difference, we recognized
it. It happens therefore that in the new edition many of the passages are set
forth obscurely because of the cuts that have been made in the statements,
although he is a caretaker of clarity; but in what way I cannot say, not
having fitted the words well according to the cuts in the sec 1.7 ond edition
he spoils the sense of what is being read. And there also is the end.
[Photius Bibl. cod. 77. One might say that he did not write a history, but
transcribed that of Eunapius, differing only in brevity, and that this man
does not, as that one does, slander Stilicho. In the other respects as to the
history he is almost the same, and especially in the slanders against the
pious emperors. It seems to me that this man also, like that one, made two
editions; but of this man I have not seen the earlier one; from the fact that
the one we read he entitled Of the New Edition it was possible to infer that
another had also been published by him, as also by Eunapius. This man is
clearer and more concise, as we said, than Eunapius; and he does not use
tropes, except rarely. [Idem cod. 98, de Zosimo.
