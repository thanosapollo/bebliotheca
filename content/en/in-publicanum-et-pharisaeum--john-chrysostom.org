A discourse on the publican and the Pharisee.
=59.595=
1. Those who have looked upon meadows from afar see indeed the varied delight
of the flowers; but they come to know their beauty when, having drawn near,
they handle the flowers with their own hands. Such, beloved, conceive the
state of the Church to be, since, like unfading flowers, the readings of the
God-inspired Scriptures lie before those who will. For one might see in the
spiritual paradises prophets dancing, apostles marshalling the ranks, martyrs
acting as ambassadors, angels fighting alongside, teachers contending in the
contest of piety, the grace of the Spirit in abundance, rational flocks sealed
with the Master's blood, and what is greater than what has been said, the
summit of goods, the confirmation of hopes, the crown of delight, the
perfection of joy: I mean the Gospels of Christ, in which the perfection of
our life is laid up. Come then, let us behold the Master Christ granting every
enjoyment to his own servants; in which we see passions put to flight, the sea
shuddering, winds falling still, death rebuked, Hades stripped, demons
banished, sinners turning back to their own Master. For these are the gifts
Christ has set forth in the divine Gospels, in which he shows the righteous
shining above the sun, the limbs of the palsied being sinewed, bed-borne
paralytics running, publicans receiving the dignity of evangelists, leprosy
shaken off like dust by his will, fever fleeing at the Master's voice, the
dead easily receiving life, tombs releasing to life those they held, the
height of a Pharisee being humbled, the humility of a publican being exalted.
For those already filled with such great goods, even the welcome through
crumbs is thereafter easy. =59.596= And I am persuaded that you will, as
usual, listen again with forbearance to our words, having filled the
storehouses of your souls with the fullness of the goods already proclaimed.
Therefore, since you too, by the goodwill of God, put up with the poor table,
I again, taking courage from the manner of your love, have set the neck of my
soul under the teaching yoke. Come then, I will set before you what was just
now read; for I have come as a poor host to all you who are rich, mixing the
banquet of the word. Two men, he says, went up into the temple to pray, the
one a Pharisee, and the other a publican. A Pharisee, the boastful worker of
righteousness; a Pharisee who reaped the crop of his achievements untimely;
who is richer in arrogance than in money; who by empty puffing has shaken off
the fruit of the virtues; the possessor of pride; the accuser of all nature;
the unloving reproof; the unforgiving reviler; the storm-wave of the
publican's prayer.
And let no one take my speech as fond of abuse; but having heard the
Pharisee's own words, let him recognize that what is said is true. God, he
says, I thank you that I am not as the rest of men. Do you judge yourself more
righteous than all? I am not as the rest of men. What are you doing, Pharisee?
Do not attend to the common name of nature; for the Lover of mankind also
became man without change; and having thought of him, come down from your
arrogant words. Say at least, As some of men, and not, As the rest of men,
extortioners, unjust, adulterers. Are all extortioners except you, Pharisee?
Are all unjust, and you alone happen to be righteous? Are all adulterers, and
to you alone belongs the achievement of chastity? I am not as the rest of men,
extortioners, unjust, adulterers; or even as this publican. Now =59.597= the
man's mind has begun to grow wings; now it shakes the wing of the soul toward
heaven. Why do you drag him down by the weight of your words, hindering
confession from flying up to the Lover of mankind? Why do you trample on him
who is down? Why do you heap wood upon his fire? Why do you accuse him who is
unceasingly striking his conscience? Or even as this publican.
For what did you see the publican doing, O man? Robbing? Plundering others'
labors? Spoiling what is another's? Reaping what he did not sow? Gathering
where he did not scatter? Spreading a net for travelers? You have seen nothing
of the sort, O Pharisee; but you see a pitiable figure, worthy of pity and of
help. For he lies on his face, he tortures his breast as the workshop of
evils, he does not dare to lift his eyes toward heaven; perhaps he even
expects accusation from the stars themselves, or rather he thinks the
multitude of his offenses has been written in the firmament; as one already
judged he has set himself far off from the temple; he has cried, Have mercy,
as already being condemned. Have you not read what is written, that You shall
not provoke a man in his distress? He is in want; from every side his thoughts
have surrounded him in place of a multitude of accusers; conscience has used
the right hand against the breast, scourging it; he has made an executioner of
himself.
Does he not bend you as he trembles? Does this man, bowing his face downward,
not stir you to compassion? Does he not soften your inhuman mind, not daring
to look up to heaven? For when he was collecting taxes, you ought to have
cried out such words against him; for it was fitting then that he be reviled
not only by you, but by everyone. But when he has condemned his own evils,
when, having seen the wounds of the soul, he has run to the physician who
heals without pay, when, remembering his offenses, he has fled to the Master
who keeps no grudge: then do you trample on him excessively, O Pharisee? Not
even as this publican. For are there not other publicans, who have not yet
loved the sweet lament of repentance? If you have a great desire to accuse
sinners, turn your tongue upon those still entangled in the nets of greed.
Toward this man, however, your ill-will is superfluous. For not only can you
not harm the man, but you will the more provoke the Master to display a
swifter pardon.
2. But the publican, he says, standing afar off, would not even lift his eyes
to heaven; he too surely uttered the voice of Manasseh: I am not worthy to
look up and see the height of heaven because of the multitude of my
iniquities. For many evils were then gathered in the publican's soul: an
insatiable desire for money, a longing for injustice that admits no end,
plunder that has no finish; a publican, a common evil to the nature of men, a
lawful wrongdoer, an unaccused robber, a fearless thief, a blameless brigand,
a harm hard to escape, a wolf of the rational sheep, a beast in human form.
With so many evils the publican went up into the temple. For when he had done
all these terrible things, and had made the burden of offenses heavy upon the
soul, when at last he had broken his neck, then he sought to be lightened of
the weight, and did not know how he should shake off the load; then he had a
swift thought, he found a form of freedom; for he remembered the Master's
voice crying out to sinners: Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden,
and I will give you rest.
Remembering this voice, he came with the freight of evils to the temple of
=59.598= God; and having grown weak at last, and being no longer able to bear
it, he fell at once upon his face, and said to the Master, Be merciful to me
the sinner. I am, he says, barren of every good work; I have been shaken by my
own terrible deeds; my lawless acts have become more than the number of the
sand of the sea; they have multiplied beyond the hairs of my head. I see your
judgment-seat already cried against by those I have wronged; I have heard that
before your thrones, Master, opened books will be set, in which surely even
the groans of the travelers have been written. There is no form of injustice
that has escaped your unbribed knowledge; a time of defense is not at hand for
me, a place of flight is not left me. I do not dare to look up to heaven; I
fear even the earth as having seen my deeds. For even lifeless things accuse
the one who is greatly a sinner.
Therefore, having run to you the Master of all, I have found this supplication
alone for help: God, be merciful to me the sinner. Great is the summit of my
evils; but what is that beside the sea of your goodness? For me to be saved is
impossible with men, but with you, Master, all things are possible. For if you
have come upon the earth for the sake of the righteous, I have run to your
temple in vain, having loathed the tax-office; but if you also take account of
sinners, or rather if for their sake you sojourned in the creation, do not put
to shame my hopes that rest on you beyond hope, but heal my crushed heart, and
give life to me who have been put to death by transgressions. A harlot touched
you, and washed off the filth of her evils; you lent the hem to a believing
woman, and dried up lakes of blood; you drew near to a paralytic lying down,
and made him a worker of the bed; you saw a blind man as you passed, and
restored beauty to the sight; for you anointed the missing part of the body,
correcting with clay the vessel that is from clay; and you opened the entrance
for the light, showing the man the beauty of creation; you saw a woman
weeping, and, having scourged Hades before the time of the resurrection, you
brought Lazarus out from there, snatching the man from death as from a lion's
mouth. Having seen a man falling pitiably before you, dreadfully sunk in the
shipwreck of childlessness, you were bent at once by the supplications, and
you called the girl; and when you spoke, the maiden rose, and death fled. A
Canaanite woman also cried to you, seeing her own little daughter torn by a
demon, and having cried, Have mercy, she did not fail of her hopes; for having
received her faith, you drove the wolf from the sheep, and expelled the raging
inmate; granting sobriety to the girl, you stopped the mother's lament. With
small loaves you fed a multitude in the wilderness. I too hunger with a famine
of righteousness; I seek a small crumb of your loving-kindness; have mercy on
me also as one of those mentioned. I am branded in the soul, as the harlot was
before repentance; I am abominable to all, as the woman with the issue of
blood was thought unclean before the healing; I am palsied in the soul, more
than the paralytic's body; I am maimed in the eyes of the soul, as the man
born blind was in the body; I have been put to death by transgressions, having
the soul shut up in the body, as the tomb had Lazarus; I carry almost the
terrible things of all. But you who had mercy on all, pity me also as I fall
before you. God, be merciful to me the sinner.
What then did the Lover of mankind, the Judge, the generous president of the
contest for those running the course of repentance? He both endured the
Pharisee's boasting, and took delight in the publican's =59.599= repenting;
and since the one by his words, as by violent winds, shook off the fruit of
the soul, while the other, having crushed himself, waited for no other
accuser, he assigned to each what was according to desert. For the publican,
who had become his own accuser, he released free of condemnation, while the
Pharisee, who had set his achievements in the midst, he declared poorer than
the publican. For both the manner of life: for the one a starting-point from
poverty into wealth, for the other a slip from wealth into beggary. For what
does the Master say? Amen I say to you, this man went down justified to his
house, rather than that one. O how great an evil is the disease of pride! The
Pharisee went up in full leaf, as it were crowned with his achievements as
with flowers; the publican journeyed with him naked of every defense. But the
one, having humbled himself in season, was exalted; the other, having hurled
himself toward an untimely height, endured a laughable stripping.
Was it not better to use =59.600= lowered voices? Was it not better to hear
the prophetic voice saying, Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will
exalt you? Was it not more profitable to hold the tongue, and to keep the
wealth which he had acquired with many toils? For the virtues he had gathered
in time, these in an instant's turn he scattered by his voice. Perhaps the
Pharisee had not heard what is written, that Every one who is high-hearted is
unclean before the Lord?
Was he not taught what is well said, What your right hand does, let not your
left hand know? Perhaps he knew both, but the strength of vainglory conquered.
Let us then acquire the publican's humility, and put off the weight of sins;
and let us hate pride, through which the Pharisee lost the wealth of the
virtues; for The Lord opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble. To him
be the glory and the power unto the ages of ages. Amen.
