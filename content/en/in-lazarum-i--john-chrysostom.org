On Lazarus. i.
=62.771=
Every God-inspired teaching is accustomed to gather the benefits of men who
love learning, but most of all the feasts that always come in their season,
imitating the streams of waters, which, being stretched forth to those who
thirst and healing the desire, become all the more longed-for by those who
receive them. What then does my preface mean? Lazarus the four-day dead man
rose in these days of the fasts, when Christ was already hastening toward the
salvation of the Passion, and practicing wonders before wonders, and renewing
a feast before the feast, and displaying a work before the work, so that when
he himself should be given over to the cross, the disciples might not despair
of the resurrection of their own teacher, but might believe that he who had
subjected death for the sake of another can himself suffer nothing terrible
from death.
It is just, =62.772= therefore, granting a favor both to the season, and to
ourselves, and to the coming festival, to share in the gladness at the
resurrection of Lazarus, and to share not in tears only, but also in cheer and
joy, with the good women about Mary, and to honor also our teacher Jesus by
the eagerness of the history. For he who honors his friends sends the gift on
to him; for being a lover of mankind, he is always accustomed to make his own
the honors offered for their sake. He himself is the one who has said in the
Gospels: "He who receives you receives me; and he who receives me receives him
who sent me." "Now there was a certain man sick," it says, "Lazarus, of
Bethany, the village of Mary and Martha her sister." The writer is simple,
bringing forth an unadorned history; for John was a fisherman. Being about,
then, to set forth a =62.773= paradoxical work, he did not flatter the hearer
with diction.
See what I say: he wrote the miracle at Bethany the village, the manner, the
women present and not present, what was said, what was done, where they met
the teacher, how he wept, how they entreated, how, mourning, they accused the
Master, that he was not present to Lazarus when he was dying, nor shut the
gates of death by the word, nor held back the waves of destruction; for if the
steersman had been present, the vessel would have been saved. And he wrote
sitting in Ephesus, as one who through want of possessions and poverty did not
even use ink and paper of his own. John having written, then, all run together
to see a tomb that has lost its dead man: kings, private men, judges, tribes
of barbarians, nations of soldiers. Why do I say a tomb? The coffer of Lazarus
has become a church.
For all hasten to pray there, where Jesus prayed, procuring the benefit by the
sharing of the form. There was a certain man sick, Lazarus of Bethany, the
village of Mary and Martha. Let no one despise a place; for God is everywhere,
and dwells in a village, and in a city, and in mountains; he oversees all
things; for God is everywhere, not attending to places, but to the minds of
the pious. Lazarus was ailing, and was being pressed hard by the disease. Here
the faithful women about Mary and Martha did not mourn at the evil, did not
despair at the assault of the fever, did not seek drugs, did not call
neighbors, did not call a physician; for it was superfluous, having Jesus, the
true physician, to seek another; but they knew from where they must be saved,
and, their brother being sick, they send ambassadors of the death that was
about to come. And you too, then, when a child, or a mother, or a brother, or
an acquaintance, being sick, is being dragged by the executioners below, send
to Jesus, not using a man as servant, but your own prayers, which are much
stronger than men, and imitate the women about Martha.
What do they say? Not, "Be entreated"; not, "Hasten"; not, "Come, give the
grace"; but what do they say? "Lord, behold, he whom you love is sick." Death
has watched for your absence, and is forcing your friend. Stand by Lazarus who
is being stolen, snatched, warred upon, struck with many arrows, by fever and
pains and a cloud of evils; and it is unclear whether you will even find your
friend.
Jesus heard, and said: "This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of
God, that the Son of man may be glorified through it." What then shall I
wonder at? the providence of the things said, the prophecy, the outcome? the
command beyond man, the ineffable love of mankind, the gentleness of the
words, the honor toward the Father? I find all things of God. He foretold as
God, "This sickness is not unto death," and not long after he brought on the
work. He sent up the glory to God, showing that the doxology of Father and Son
is one; he comforted the sisters by the saying, giving them good hopes,
saying: "This sickness is not unto death." Lazarus does not die. For if he is
not given to death, he remains living. But he is given, and he is not given.
For when someone gives back what he has received, in truth he does not
receive.
Having said these things, Jesus, it says, remained in that place two days. Why
did he remain? He spent the time, so that death might clearly take Lazarus; so
that the wave, passing over, might sink the vessel. For if he had been
present, the fever would have fled, and what happened would have been reckoned
to the nature of the disease; and even if to Jesus, still the miracle would
have been inferior. The common death of men, then, took him, and held him as a
prisoner.
After these things Jesus, who was not present, who had not touched, who had
not seen with his own eyes, says to those who did not know: "Lazarus our
friend has fallen asleep. But I go, that I may awaken him." Great consolation
of men: death has been changed into sleep, destruction has been transferred
into salvation by a word. Let no one, then, despair of those who have died;
for all live to God, sleeping when he wills, and rising when he wills. The
disciples say to him, not having understood the word of the riddle: "Lord, if
he has fallen asleep, he will be saved." For natural sleep is a healing for
those who are ailing. Then he says to them with frankness: "Lazarus has died,
and I rejoice for your sake." And who rejoices over a dead man? Who is glad
over a friend who has died? One ought to look gloomy and to grieve at having
lost one's own. But, "I rejoice for your sake, that =62.774= I was not there."
For if I had been, the fever would have been quenched by a word, and the
miracle would have been shown as lesser, or the matter would have been thought
a matter of custom; but now I remained, so that the wolf might wholly snatch
the lamb; for death swallowed, having prevailed, and again God took away every
tear from every face of the earth.
They came, they hastened to Bethany, not running to quench the conflagration,
but to behold the ashes of the conflagration; or rather, when they were able
to find remnants of a shipwreck; for already he stank, being four days. For in
shipwrecks it is possible also to find a keel, and to gather planks, and to
take a sail-yard, and to keep rudders for the use of another ship, and by much
eagerness to save some part of the cargo; but in the case of Lazarus all
things were burdensome, both an ending, and a giving-forth of stench. The
mourning was at its height, and the dead man was held under the stone. "And
many of the Jews," it says, "had come from the city, that they might comfort
the sisters of the one who had died." "I have witnesses," he says, "of the
grace that is to come, so that the work may not be thought a theft, but a
surety of truth."
Jesus came, a steersman after shipwreck, a physician after a dead man. And why
have you come here, O Master? that you may be accused, as having been careless
of your friend, as having overlooked him perishing, as not having taken leave
of him who was about to die? Martha heard, and taking little thought of the
mournful appearance she comes running along the road, and begins to accuse the
Savior mournfully: "Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have
died." For if the shepherd is present, how is a sheep lost? if a general is
fighting, how were gates opened? if the sun is shining, how was the gold
plundered?
"Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died." The word is
unbelieving, and still also weak; for if the potter is present, it was
possible for the vessel that had fallen apart to be formed again. Jesus says
to her: "Your brother shall rise." And she to him, "I know that he shall rise
in the last day." And Jesus said, "I am the resurrection."
Why then do you seek what you have? Wait a little, and you shall see the
grace. Martha heard, she believed, and she raises her sister, and all who were
mourning, those who were comforting the suffering, are borne along at a run.
Again Jesus in a crowd and at a wedding and in mourning, everywhere resolving
the difficulties for those who believe. And when he saw the mourning and the
tears, Jesus says: "Where have you laid him?" Why do you inquire, O Master?
Not being present you knew the death, and being present you do not know the
place, but you say, "Where have you laid him?" "Give me," he says, "those who
buried, those who bound, those who wrapped the dead man. Let these, having
become my witnesses of the things that shall be, recognize their own, so that
when they see the one buried by them risen, they may not think the work to be
a phantasm." He came to the tomb; he who knows all things endured those who
were pointing it out; after these things he also wept. And why do you weep, if
you speak truly what you promised, if you are about to raise him? But he weeps
for the man, showing the humane disposition of his mind; and perhaps also
because of the ingratitude of the Jews, that even after this miracle they
again remained in unbelief.
For some of them said, "Could not this man, who opened the eyes of the blind
man, have caused that this one also should not die?" He could. But why do you
take on the railing? Remain, and see the work. Jesus says to them: "Take away
the stone." The word is paradoxical. He who overthrows gates of bronze, and
crushes bars of iron of death, says, "Take away the stone"? Why do you not by
the word unfasten the stone, Master? "Take away," he says, "you the
slanderers, the stone, you the unbelieving, the deceivers; take it away,
handle it, be fully assured, so that you may not again disbelieve yourselves;
make the sight sure." It is not a shadow that is about to come out, nor a
phantasm; for these can come out even when stones are shut. Martha, the sister
of the dead man, says to him: "Lord, already he stinks; for he is four days."
He being a servant ought not to be made like the Master's gifts.
For the Master according to his own promises rose on the third day; but he is
raised on the fourth; for a servant is one thing, and the Master another. They
say to him, "Lord, he is four days, and already he stinks." These things are
small toward God; not yet dry bones, not yet empty of flesh and sinews, as
once in the case of Ezekiel the army of the dead lying on the ground, and
empty =62.775= of all things, of soul, of sense, of perception, of vital
spirit; he stood beside the tomb already furnishing a clear proof of the
resurrection that was to come. Martha was silent, and the stone was
unfastened, and all were in suspense, living dead men; and perhaps Hades
himself also, the gates of death already being undone. And first Jesus the
Lord begins from a doxology.
For he lifted, it says, his eyes to heaven, signifying the manner of his own
descent, giving thanks for us to the Father, and saying: "Father, I thank you
that you have heard me." Having from himself the supply of life, he does this,
because Mary and Martha were saying to him: "We know that whatever you ask
God, he would give you." Therefore, seeing their understanding weaker, he
says: "You know entirely that if I ask anything of the Father, he will give it
to me; therefore come forward in understanding to me, and to the Father."
And then he adds, saying: "I thank you, Father, that you have heard me. And I
knew that you always hear me; but I said it because of those standing by." Did
you see how he has no need of the prayer? But he does this because of the
unbelief of those present. "You have heard me," he says, "always." Do not seek
the "when?"; for however much we inquire, we do not grasp it. You pray all
things according to nature as Son; but he mentioned it, sending up the honor,
so that he might display one concord of Son and Father to those who do not
know. And when he had made the faith sure, and had fortified the reasonings of
the hearers, then he proceeds to the work. "Lazarus, come forth." He did not
double the word, so that the one alone might rise, and the people of the dead
might not run an unordered course. "I seek one dead man; let not all come; for
it is not yet the time of that nod." "When the dead shall hear the voice of
the Son of man, and those who hear shall live." Let one alone come for now,
let one be released because of the friendship of the one Only-begotten; I need
one prisoner. Let none of those below array himself against, no unseen
gate-keeper, no heavy jailer, nor the key-holders below who have practiced all
to take. Learn first to give back those who are mine. And you too, O Hades, be
silent, be muzzled, =62.776= having suffered nothing strange, obeying the
Master, claiming none of these things.
A man becomes a fugitive from your forecourts. "Lazarus, come forth; conquer
death by my commands, conquer death having plundered the things below." I
still remain bound, already being crowned against him. Lazarus came out bound
and walking; he came out bound in all things, and wholly moving; for he was
wholly bound through and through, feet and hands, according to the local
manner of burial. And he was not yet, then, outside of death and the tomb.
Again Jesus commands a new command: "Unbind him," he says, "and let him go."
Why is he not unbound of himself, he who has fled death, he who has overthrown
the guard below? "You unbind him, you who bound him of old, so that you may
recognize the seals of your own bonds." And when the Pharisees heard these
things, they assemble a strange council, saying, "What do we do, because this
man does such signs?" Great accusations: the dead being raised, death is put
to death, and Lazarus rises from the dead; but the Jews are put to death in
their mind. Do not then sit as an incomplete council, but add the word of the
accusation, and say: "What do we do, because after a few days from above the
sun is darkened, from below the earth trembles not bearing the things that
have been done, from within the temple the veil is torn, a people of the dead
returns to life?" Again say: "What do we do, because a temple is destroyed,
because worship ceases, because the city is set on fire, because we are cast
out, and others are set as heirs in our place?" "What do we do, because all
things are transferred, prophets, apostles, priesthood, fasting, festival,
worship? and God will receive."
Concerning these things consider, concerning these things take counsel; for
the phrases of the words are unprofitable to you, the mind remains; for a word
which you may speak shall not remain in you. Once, then, God has given to
those to whom he has given, always to keep feast, always to keep festival,
each year to renew the word of the festival, in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom
be the glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
