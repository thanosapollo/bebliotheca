According to you two principles, as natural cause, are principles of all
beings, all things will be consubstantial and co-eternal with the principles
according to you. And if they are eternal, they are also unchangeable and
contrary and incommunicable and ruling and not ruled and master and not slave.
But if as productive cause the two principles according to you are principles
of beings, the souls are not from the substance of God nor the bodies from the
substance of matter, but they were brought from non-being into being, and
neither did matter wage war with the light nor snatch a part from it and make
the souls. And how were things contrary and incommunicable brought together
into the making of one world? Or how did the evil one fashion the body,


and the good one the soul, and they completed one living being, the human
being? But if as a prototype of an image the thing imaged is a cause by
imitation, the principles according to you are other worlds, after whose
likeness the world came to be. For there is not one form nor one or two shapes
among beings, but countless. And how are the countless forms images of the two
principles among you? Thus the opinion among you concerning the two principles
is impossible and without standing. For ask the innocent multitude of
children: Is the dyad a principle, or the monad? And they will say that the
monad is the principle of every number. O more childish than children and more
mindless.
11 And this too, when we ask, tell us: Is polyarchy a good and stable and
orderly thing, or monarchy? It is clear to all that polyarchy is anarchy and
factious and a cause of war and battle and of evils and of dissolution, but
monarchy is firm and lawful and a cause of peace and order and calm and
justice and of increase toward the better. If then according to you there are
two unoriginate principles, polyarchy is the principle of beings, and the
principle of all things is evil, and thus evil is one principle. And how do
you also call the two principles, which you yourselves invented, wholly
incommunicable? For if this one is, and that one is, and this substance is an
unoriginate principle and eternal, and that one likewise, they are not wholly
incommunicable. For they share both in being and in being substance and
unoriginate principle and eternal, and in every way your account has been
shown false.
12 And again: If there are two unoriginate principles, each rejoices and
delights in what is its own and reckons its own constitution a good; for to
each the good is what constitutes its own nature. And so both the good one
among you and the evil one among you, each rejoices in himself and delights in
what is his own and reckons his own things goods. Who then is the one who
judged the one good and the other evil? And that one is rather God, greater
than both; for the lesser is judged by the greater and the ruled by the ruler.
13 Say this too: Do the two principles among you share with one another, or
are they wholly incommunicable? Manichaean: They were wholly incommunicable;
for the one was in a fullness of goods, and the other in a fullness of evils,
but after this a mixing came about. Orthodox: Put off for now the account of
the mixing. Tell us first: Is being contrary to being in respect of being, or
non-being to being? Manichaean: Existing evil is contrary to existing good.
Orthodox: I did not ask whether they are contrary in respect of evil and good,
but in respect of being. For beings are not wholly contrary; for in respect of
being itself they share with one another, and non-being is contrary to being.
So that if evil is wholly contrary to the good, and the good is, evil is not.
So that evil is a privation of being and is called evil; and if the good is
substance, evil is without substance, or they are not wholly contrary.
14 Manichaean: What then? Is wickedness not seen among beings? Orthodox:
Complete wickedness is not seen among beings; for neither is God, who truly is
and is cause of being, wickedness, nor is any of the things that have come to
be, insofar as it has come to be, evil, but both the cause of all things is
good and all the things that have come to be by him are good. And all beings
were brought by him from non-being into being, and all things are beautiful
and very beautiful. And evil is nothing other than a voluntary casting-off and
privation of the things given by God to the rational nature, just as poverty
is a casting-off of wealth; for wealth is a possession of existing things,
gold and silver and translucent stones and varied clothing, boys and girls,
beasts and fields and such things, each of which is a substance. Therefore the
one who has acquired these is also called rich, as one of many substances
having much substance about himself. And poverty is a partial, a partial


loss of these, and complete, a complete privation of these, and just as the
integrity of the body is a good, the loss of one member is a partial evil not
existing, but a privation of what exists. And it is seen or thought in what
is, not as being, but as having ceased to be, as the eye and seeing exist and
are, but blindness is not a substance, but a destruction of the eye that
existed. And it is contemplated in the body that is constituted and is, not as
being, but as a privation of an eye that existed. And thus by parts the
addition of the privation of members is called an addition of evil; for evil
is not, but the non-existence of the things cast off is said and contemplated
in the body that remains, not as being but as not being, not as a habit but as
a privation. So also wickedness, that is sin, is a voluntary privation of the
goods given by God to the rational nature; for in the case of the irrational
and the soulless one cannot speak of the voluntary, but in the case of the
rational. Wickedness then is a misuse of the natural powers. For if we use the
powers given by God to the soul for that for which we received them, that is
according to the law of the giver, it is good. But if we do not use them for
that for which we received them, but contrary to the law of the giver, it is
evil; and it is not the powers themselves nor the use of them that is the evil
thing, but the evil manner of use that is contrary to the law of God who gave
them. For if God is good, his law too is good, natural and being from being.
And disobedience to this is a privation of the law that existed for us, as
desire and anger and action and activity and, before these, reasoning are not
evils but goods, if we use them for that for which we received them according
to the law of the giver; and they themselves are not evils, but the using them
not according to the law of the giver. For if we use eating, drinking, sleep
and such things toward the constitution of life, each of these is good. And if
we use desire and love toward God, the only thing desirable by nature, and
toward the neighbor according to the law of God, it is good. And if we use
intercourse with our own wives toward childbearing according to the law given
to nature by God, it is good; but if we use desire toward what belongs to
others contrary to the law of God, it is evil. For God said: "You shall not
desire your neighbor's field, nor his wife, nor whatever is his." Fornication
is not evil because of the joining, but because of being joined to one who
belongs to another. Vainglory is not evil because of loving glory, but because
of not loving the glory from God. Hatred and anger, if against those who
hinder the salvation of the soul and the joining to God through obedience to
him and who make one stand away from God, that is against the devil and his
servants, are good. For he says: "Did I not hate those who hate you, Lord, and
was I not consumed over your enemies? I hated them with a perfect hatred; they
became enemies to me." And the holy martyrs were angry against the tyrants,
being angry justly. And if I am angry against my own reasoning that separates
me from God, it is good. If someone deprives me of salvation, it is good to be
angry and to hate; but if he grieves me according to the world and becomes a
cause of benefit by depriving me of material things, I will love rather than
hate. Murder is evil; for it belongs to God alone to separate the soul from
the body, who also bound them together; but if I kill when commanded by God,
it is good; for everything from the good is good. So it is evil to desire to
snatch what is not ours but God's. Condemnation is evil; for judging belongs
to God alone, and it is evil to snatch the dignity of God, for those to whom
judging has not been given by God. Vainglory and pride are evil; for
exaltation and glory befit God alone. So that wickedness is nothing other than
using the natural powers contrary to nature and the law, which is not a
substance but our own practice.



15 For one must know that both the good and the evil are spoken of in two
ways; for good is spoken of properly as what has been given from God who is
good by nature. Just as every light is lit from the sun or from fire, so every
good has its existence from the unbounded and incomprehensible sea of
goodness, and whatever is from God is good properly and truly. And good is
spoken of not properly but by abuse and according to our custom as what
appears pleasant to our sense and desire, which for a time delights, but later
yields a bitter fruit, coming about contrary to the law of the Creator and
seeming to be good, which is not good but properly evil. And evil likewise:
properly it is and is called evil that which comes about contrary to the law
of the Creator. It is called evil not properly but according to our custom as
what is toilsome and painful according to our sense, which for the present
yields pains, but later yields a fruit of salvation and of everlasting
gladness.
16 Say then: Is the principle of every thing one or two, as the principle and
cause of the soul one or two? Manichaean: One. For if two, how from two
contraries does one and the same come forth? For things wholly contrary have
an incommunicable activity; for how will the immaterial soul be from the
immaterial and from matter? Or how did the same things bring forth the
contraries without agreeing? For things contrary by nature are wholly
incommunicable. Orthodox: You have said well: is there one principle of each
of all beings? Manichaean: In every way. Orthodox: Is being the principle of
the two principles according to you, or are the two principles principles of
being? Manichaean: Toward what does this tend? Orthodox: If being is the
principle of the two principles according to you, see, there is one principle
and not two. For if being is the principle of being, it is in one principle;
for I do not mean being in such and such a way, that is being evil or being
good, but simply being itself. If then being is the principle of the two
principles according to you, there is one principle and not two. But if the
principles according to you are principles of being, there will be two
contraries of the same thing. For just as simply substance, being one, is
receptive of different qualities, and according to this many substances are
spoken of, so also simply being is one, even if, contemplated in different
things, it makes the many beings be named paronymously from itself. I will
define then that the activity of contraries is wholly incommunicable, and that
being is not from two principles, especially contrary ones, but from one. For
being is not the cause of what is; for being did not exist before what is. For
in what was it contemplated? But being, contemplated in the unoriginate one
who is, has subsistence in him and from him as cause. So there is one
principle of being, and the contrary of this is not.
17 And again: Is the principle of the motion of each of beings one or two,
existential and temporal and local and of activity (existential of each one's
coming-to-be, temporal from which time it began, local from which place the
things that begin from a place began, of activity from which activity the
thing acted upon acts), as the first existence of the human being, and from
which time he came to be, and from which place he began to move, to walk
perhaps, and of the walking activity? Manichaean: One. Orthodox: You have said
truly; for it is impossible that there be two principles of the existence of
the one. Manichaean: So it is. Orthodox: Is this contemplated in each of
beings, or not? Manichaean: In each of all beings. Orthodox: What then is
contemplated in each of all will also be contemplated in all commonly, as what
each of humans has, all humanity also has, as living and reasoning, and what
each substance has, the common substance also has, as being self-subsistent
and not having being in another. So then if each of beings is constituted from
one principle, all beings also are constituted from one principle and not from
two, and there will not be two principles but one.


18 Manichaean: Why is God unoriginate? Orthodox: Because non-being does not
have being from itself. How then will that which does not have being from
itself be from itself? And so non-being must receive being from something that
is; and the first that imparts being, having being from itself, will always be
unoriginate.
19 Manichaean: Why one principle and not two? Orthodox: Because a dyad comes
forth from a monad as an offspring of a monad, and a monad is principle of a
dyad, and before the dyad there is in every way a monad, and because the
principle of every thing is one, and if of each of beings there is one
principle, there will also be one principle of all; for the account given in
the case of each of beings will also be given in the case of all.
20 And again: If there are two principles, there is polyarchy; and if
polyarchy, faction; and if faction is the principle of beings, evil is the
principle of beings; for faction is evil. It befits God then to be and to have
what is greater than all things said and thought. Which then is greater, to
make substances from things that are not, in a manner befitting God, or to be
occupied with matter in a human way by the creative, or rather the
carpenter's, art? Manichaean: Wholly, that from things that are not. Orthodox:
Therefore it befits God to make substances from things that are not, and there
was no matter, but it came to be by God.
21 Manichaean: Is God unoriginate in every respect, or in some respect yes and
in some respect no? Orthodox: If he is not unoriginate in every respect, he is
both unoriginate and not unoriginate. But if he is unoriginate in every
respect, he is truly unoriginate and unoriginate by nature. And the
unoriginate is also unbounded; for end too is one form of beginning. So
everything that has a beginning also has an end according to its own nature,
and everything that has an end also has a beginning. And the angels at least,
having a beginning, also have an end according to their own nature, even if by
divine grace they begin again to be and are renewed. For just as the unmoved
body, having begun to move, began from a place, that is the circular, and
reaching the place from which it began, has completed the circumference and
the circle, and begins the motion again and makes a new beginning of motion
and thus remains in motion until the one who commands it to move should will,
so also the angels, moving vitally and reaching the natural end of their own
existence, begin the vital motion, until again at the command of the one who
made them they will cease from the motion. For they always receive from God
being and being moved vitally and intellectually, not having this from
themselves. And just as a wheel, having been moved, is moved and stops (but if
it has perpetual motion, it is always moved, not having the motion from
itself), so also the angels, not having being from themselves, are moved as
far as the one who made them moves them; if indeed that one does not move
them, they cease from the motion. So that what has a beginning has in every
way also an end, and what has an end has in every way also a beginning. And
what is unoriginate by nature is unoriginate in every respect, both in cause
and in time and in place and in authority.
22 The two then will either be in one another or will be circumscribed by
place and are not unoriginate. How then are the light and the darkness in one
another? For the light is destructive of the darkness. If there are not
boundaries of the light and boundaries of the darkness, neither is the light
uncircumscribed, as not being everywhere, nor the darkness. And they are not
wholly unoriginate, having a local beginning. For it is impossible, if there
is no partition and dividing wall, that the light and the darkness be wholly
unmixed. For if at night we light a lamp, the place around the lamp will be
brighter, and at a little distance it will be darker, until the light wholly
fails and there comes to be pure darkness unmixed with light. For either from
the beginning the darkness was mixed with the light, and these are not wholly
contrary and incommunicable, or there was from the beginning some other
dividing wall separating these, and there are no longer two principles but
three. What is naturally so by nature does not change, and what is altered is
not by nature. If then by nature


the light and the darkness are incommunicable, how did they share after this?
How from a good one and an evil one was one living being completed, the human
being? For if they were brought to terms, they shared with one another; for
coming to terms is a sharing of mind. And neither did the good one remain
purely and perfectly good nor the evil one, but both were changed. And what is
changed is not unoriginate nor God; for God, foreknowing all things that are
in our power, defines all things that are not in our power. And again: Is
being contrary to being, or being to non-being? Manichaean: Being to
non-being. Orthodox: Therefore of the two wholly contrary principles according
to you the one will be, and the other will not be.
23 And again: Is being contrary to being? Manichaean: Being evil is contrary
to being good. Orthodox: Therefore the contraries coexist and are
consubstantial in one and the same. And how are they contrary? Manichaean:
Non-being is unnameable; for it must be and thus be named. How then is
non-being contrary to being? For the contraries must first be toward one
another and then be contrary and then be named contrary; for what is not
something is not named. Orthodox: When you said non-being, did you name it or
not? Manichaean: I named it by negation, not by affirmation. Orthodox: Does
affirmation make plain a habit and negation a privation? Manichaean: Yes.
Orthodox: Is the habit contrary to the privation? Manichaean: Yes. Orthodox:
Non-being then is contrary to being as a habit to a privation? Manichaean:
Yes. Orthodox: Is evil spoken of according to habit or according to privation?
Manichaean: According to habit. Orthodox: Is the good spoken of according to
habit or according to privation? Manichaean: According to habit. Orthodox:
Therefore evil is not wholly contrary to the good; for a habit is not contrary
to a habit. Manichaean: privation and deficiency to completeness. Manichaean:
And what do you say? Orthodox: Thus evil too will rather be a privation and
thus contrary to the good as privation to habit; for evil is nothing other
than privation of the good, and complete evil is complete privation of the
good. Therefore we say that evil is rather non-being as a privation of the
natural habit than being.
24 What then do you say matter is, life or corruption and death, motion and
moved or immobility and movable, light or darkness? Manichaean: Corruption and
death, immobility and unmoved, darkness and not light. Orthodox: If then
corruption, how did it bring forth fruits? How did it live? For corruption
does not live nor have life nor impart life; for what one does not have, how
does one impart? And how, being unmoved, was it moved and reached as far as
the boundaries of the light? Who is the one who gave it life and the movable?
If the good one, he is not good nor wise: not good in that he gave life to
evil and moved it, not wise nor knowing in that he furnished wars and troubles
for himself and moved against himself the evil that was at rest and quiet; for
he became a cause of faction and of evils. But if not God, another, and no
longer two but either three; for what one does not have, one cannot acquire
from oneself. Manichaean: But it had living and motion in potency. Orthodox:
If it had life and motion in potency, it was good in potency, if indeed life
and motion are good. Manichaean: The life and motion of evil is not a good but
an evil. Orthodox: What comes from potency into actuality is changeable and
beginning and not unoriginate, and if it had life and motion in potency, it
had actuality later; if its life and motion are evil, it was evil in potency
and not in actuality, and evil will not be unoriginate. But if its life and
motion are a good, it came from evil into good. Manichaean: What then? Is not
the corruption of one the coming-to-be of another? Orthodox: It is not
corruption that becomes coming-to-be, but what was created by God from
non-being into being by the command of God, being changeable as having begun
from change. For change is coming from non-being into being; and those whose
being



began from change are in every way changeable and alterable. Matter then,
being changeable, is given life by the divine command and is corrupted and is
given life again; so that it is not corruption that creates coming-to-be, but
the divine command that gives life and produces coming-to-be.
25 Say this too: Are the boundaries from the light filled with light, or not?
Manichaean: It is clear to everyone, I suppose, that they are filled with
light. Orthodox: Are the light and the darkness receptive of one another, or
unreceptive? Manichaean: Receptive. Orthodox: Therefore they are not by nature
what they are; for what is naturally so by nature does not change, and life by
nature is unreceptive of death. How then did the darkness associate with light
and was not dissolved? Then was the place of the light filled with the good
and with the light, or not? Manichaean: It was filled. Orthodox: How then was
the darkness contained? For a vessel of one medimnus will not contain two
medimni, that is a vessel able to hold one modius would not contain two modii.
26 Then when the fruits of the darkness entered into the boundaries of the
light, what did they do or what did they suffer? Manichaean: They saw the
light and marveled and bounded toward it and came into love of it. Orthodox: O
dark and mindless ones. Is the darkness blind or luminous? Manichaean: Blind.
Orthodox: And how can the blind see, or how will what is wholly evil marvel at
or love the good?
27 Yet are these two principles destructive of one another? Manichaean: Yes.
Orthodox: Therefore not only is evil corruption, but the good one also.
Manichaean: Evil is corruption, but the good one is life. Orthodox: Therefore
evil corrupts the good one and he does not subsist, but the good one gives
life to evil, and evil will subsist, but the good one will perish. As you
said, then, matter is evil and corruption and death. Evil then, if it is evil
to others but good to itself, is constituted for itself, not wholly evil, not
wholly corruption; for constituting itself, according to this it will not be
evil. But if it is wholly evil and wholly corruption, it will also be
destructive of itself and non-existent. Set out for us also the account of
mixing that you mentioned.
28 Manichaean: There was, as I said, God and matter, each in its own
boundaries, the good God, life, light, motion, knowledge, intellection and
every good, and matter evil, corruption, darkness, death, in rest and quiet
and complete immobility. Then after many ages matter rose against itself and
its fruits against one another, and thus some pursuing, others being pursued,
and there was, as I said, God and matter, that is two principles; and of the
light a part is the soul, and of the darkness and of matter a creation is the
body. And the mixing came about thus. And each of them was in a part, and the
good one was in one part, and the evil one in three. Then the evil one was
moved with disorderly impulse from his boundaries and came into the boundaries
of the good one, and the good one, knowing, projected from himself a power
called mother of life, and the mother of life projected the first human being,
and this first human being I mean the five elements, which are air, wind,
light, water and fire. These then he put on and went and made war with the
darkness. And the rulers of the darkness ate a part of his panoply, which is
soul; and they also held the first human being, and he was tortured by them,
being in the darkness, and he cried to the good one, and he projected another
power called living spirit. And it came down and gave a right hand to the
first human being and brought him up. And it left below the part that the
rulers ate, that is the soul; for from the portion of the good one the souls
came to be, and from the substance of evil the bodies.
29 Orthodox: If according to the divine apostle there is no sharing of light
with darkness, how did a mixing of soul and body come about? And experience
itself, which is of



vain words the stronger, teaches. Did the darkness enter into the boundaries
of the light and was not dissolved? And again: How was the darkness contained
in the boundaries of the light? And again: If the good one was wise, how did
he not foreknow the raid of the evil one and fortify himself and his place
beforehand? For if he was not wise nor a foreknower, he was foolish; and how
do you call the foolish one good? And again: If the whole darkness entered
into the boundaries of the light, its place remained empty; but if a part of
it, how did a part of it defeat the one sent by the good one to make war with
it? For the one who sends a weaker against a stronger is unwise. Or the good
one is weak, and the evil one stronger. And again: If the good one willingly
gave over a part of himself, that is the soul, he is not good who gives a good
thing over to an evil one. And how did he himself give the soul over as
surrendered to the evil one? To judge those who serve the evil one? For they
will say to him: You handed us over, the judgment is done away. But if, when
he did not will it, the rulers of evil snatched a part of him, he is weaker
and more ignorant, first having lost a part of himself, second not having been
able to snatch back what was snatched from him; for if from the beginning he
was not able, he will not be able after this either. And how, having taken the
rulers of evil, did he crucify them and from their fleshes make the heavens,
and from their bones the mountains, and did not take back the part of himself
that was snatched? And thus too the judgment is done away. For those who sin
are without blame; for if he himself was not able to receive back his part,
how will the soul that has been overmastered be able not to sin, and it will
be without blame? And if at last the good one will bind evil, he will receive
the soul completely, and a human being will not be punished. And again: If
from the body evil comes to the soul, the soul is without blame. And again:
The body lying dead does not sin. Therefore evil is not from the body but from
the soul.
30 Tell us, O Manichaeans, is the unoriginate evil among you, which you name
matter, incorporeal or a body or composite? And if incorporeal, how is it
matter? For matter is not incorporeal. But perhaps you will say that it is
incorporeal, but it made the body. Tell us then: From its substance or from
non-being? And if you say "from its own substance," you lie. For from an
incorporeal substance a body does not come forth (for what is naturally so by
nature does not change, nor will a substance be turned from incorporeal into a
body), nor from a body into incorporeal, nor from rational into irrational,
nor from irrational into rational. For the substantial quality is unmoved, and
the things that are altered and changed are changed according to accident,
which come to be and cease to be without the corruption of the subject. But if
you say "from non-being," tell us why, being incorporeal and evil, it did not
also make the mixing from evil things, why it did not also make souls evil by
nature as the body too, as you say, is evil by nature, why it mixed with its
own evil a part of the good? For the good one did not do this; so that he is
not perfectly evil, but has a good part. And these things, if you say it is
incorporeal. But if a body, how was it moved? For every body by itself is
without choice and dead. How then was it moved? Who gave it the motion? The
good one? Therefore he is not good, or not wise nor provident, having moved
the evil. But if you say composite, know that everything composite is easily
dissolved, and it is necessary first that the simples be, from which the
composition came about, as it is necessary first that the pitch be by itself
and the wax by itself and then that the composition of wax-pitch come about.
There will then be two principles of evil and not one.
31 Tell us then, O Manichaeans: Are the two principles among you wholly
incommunicable, or do they share in some things and differ in some things,
and, if they are wholly incommunicable, the



one will be and the one will not be. But if they share in being as substance
and accident, the one has its existence in the other; for the accident does
not subsist by itself, as color cannot be constituted without a body. And the
body is substance, and the color accident; and the accident comes to be and
ceases to be apart from the corruption of the subject, and when the color is
changed the body remains the same. Say then which substance you will, and
which accident you will; but if both are substances, know that substance does
not differ from substance, and substance is not contrary to substance except
according to accident. If the good one is, and the evil one is, and each of
them is an eternal and unchangeable substance, active, creative: they are one
in substance both, and they differ in quality. And again: We see the elements
and the seasons, heaven and earth and sea always holding in the same way, and
night and day and the motions of sun and moon and stars and rains and the
fruits of the earth ordered and rhythmically measured according to reason and
in sequence. If then there are two principles contrary to one another, how are
all things carried through in a fitting way, unless they were brought to terms
in a friendly manner? If matter consumed itself, how was it moved? But if it
was moved toward its own consumption, how did it come upon the boundaries of
the light? If matter is downward-tending and its natural place is the lower
and darker things, how, coming upon the boundaries of the light, did it go out
from its own nature? The darkness is blind; how then did it see the light? If
matter is disorderly and mindless, how does it have rulers? And how can it
also make war with what has reason and order? Or even making war will it
become more dominant, so as to snatch something from the wise one and not be
snatched? But if the good God allowed a part of himself to be snatched in
order to check it from advancing into his own boundaries, not being able
otherwise, the evil one is stronger and the good one a weaver of tricks. And
how is he good or God who uses trick, not power? What always holds in the same
way is truth, and truth is a good; and the unoriginate is unchangeable. If
then matter is unoriginate, it will also be unchangeable; and if unchangeable,
always holding in the same way; and if always holding in the same way, it is
truth; and if it is truth, it will be a good. But if matter is changed, from
good into evil, and being good first it later became evil, or into good from
evil, and being evil first it later became good; for change leads to the
contrary. The eternal substance is unchangeable; for it did not come from
non-being into being; that whose existence is not from a progress, the
substance is unchangeable. God then, being good and eternal, does not undergo
change, but is always good, and what he makes is good; for from the good it is
not allowed that evils come to be, yet they are changeable, since he brings
them from non-being into being. Rational things then are receptive of change
because of the bringing from non-being into being, but self-determining
because they are rational. And they remain self-determining, as they came to
be, that is good, slaves and acknowledging and obedient to the one who made
them, which is for the created nature; for virtue is remaining in the
boundaries of one's own nature. Self-determiningly then the human being was
changed, having desired a great thing, but not well; for it is a great thing
to be God, but it is evil for one who is not God to be ungrateful to the one
who made him and to overstep his own nature and to will to be what it is
impossible for him to become; for it is impossible for the one who is not
unoriginate to be God, or for the one who has begun to be unoriginate. The
search for what does not belong then leads the privation of beings.
32 Manichaean: What is the evil of the devil? Orthodox: The rising against the
one who made him. God then gave him being, both so that he might not seem to
fear the rising of the slave and so that he might teach us to be disposed
beneficently even toward the evil. For not only did he make him, but he also
made him ruler of the world, and if he should will to become good, he receives
him and benefits him. Therefore even being able to destroy him he did not



destroy him, but he bears with his folly, beneficently and without envy
furnishing him being, which is his. But his becoming good is not of God alone
but also of him; for what comes about by force is not good nor virtue.
33 But if you say, why did he allow him to make war with humans, I say, toward
what is beneficial; for many are crowned through him. But if you say that many
also perish, I say that just as the devil himself was able without the assault
of another to become evil from his own mind, which is also a worse charge
against him, so the human being too was able from himself, even not having the
one who assaults, to become evil, and the sin would have been worse for him;
for the one who sins from the suggestion of another is counted worthy of more
defense and pardon than the one who sins from himself. For the one who sins
from himself is without defense, just as the one who pursues virtue from
himself is praised more. Therefore the devil, having sinned from himself,
remained unrepentant, but the human being, not having sinned from himself but
from the assault of the devil, came into repentance and was counted worthy of
renewal and of remission of sins. Being then is a good and a gift of God.
34 Manichaean: Why did God, foreknowing that the devil would be evil, make
him? Orthodox: Through excess of goodness he made him. For he said: Since this
one is going to be evil and to destroy all the goods given to him, shall I
also deprive him completely of the good and make him non-existent? By no
means. But even if he himself will be evil, I will not deprive him of
participation in me, but I will give him one good, participation in me through
being, so that, even if he should not will it, he may share in my good through
being. For there is no one who holds together and keeps beings in being except
God; for being is a good and a gift of God.
35 Manichaean: Being belongs to beings from God the good; if then being is a
good for all beings as given from God the good, being is a good for the devil
too. Orthodox: All beings received being from God. All the things given to
each by God, which also are goods; for the things given from the good are
goods. And evil is nothing other than the casting-off of the goods, that is of
the things given by God. Those then who have from God even being are in a
partial participation of the good according to a last echo. It is good then
that the devil be and through being share in the good. Being then is good, and
being well is better. For complete existence is being healthy, and being sick
and being is a partial evil and corruption; for it is not complete
non-existence, but partial existence and partial non-existence. Not being is
complete evil; for complete corruption and non-existence is the completely
not-being. Being then and being well are goods and from God, and evil coming
to be from a good is of the mind of the one who comes to be. God then, through
excess of goodness, has given the goods even to the evil one, being and being
well. 36 You accuse us, O Manichaeans, why God, foreknowing that the devil
would be evil, made him, and we have shown through many things that according
to the natural account of the just he made him well; for according to the
natural account it is better even to be and according to the account of being
to share in the good. For from God, the only one who always is, being itself
is, of a good one and a provider of goods; and what is from the good is in
every way good. But if you say that it was more beneficial for him not to come
to be than to come to be and be punished endlessly, we say that that
punishment is nothing other than a fire of desire of evil and of sin and a
fire of missing the desire. For those who have acquired the unchangeable in
evil do not desire God, but evil, and there there will be no activity of evil
and of sin. For we neither eat nor drink nor



are clothed nor marry nor become rich, nor does envy have force, nor any of
the forms of sin. Desiring then and not having, they are burned by the desire
as
by a fire. But those who desire the good, that is God alone, who is and always
is, and who attain it, are gladdened according to the proportion of their
desire, according to which they attain what is desired. The truly good then is
what is good by nature, even if one shares in some measure according to being
itself in the good. And the seeming good is not good by nature, but evil. To
prefer the enjoyment of creatures to the enjoyment of the Creator, and one's
own will to the will of the maker, is not of a just one nor of a good one, to
prefer what seems good to us, which has rather been shown evil, to what is
good by nature, but that he come to be and have some portion, even the
smallest, of the truly good, rather than through the evil, that is the unjust
appetite and grief, be deprived even of that small portion of the good. For
God does not judge according to our irrational judgment, but according to the
truly good and just, nor does he follow our passions, nor through a supposed
sympathy, which is truly a being-in-passion, does he betray the account of the
just.
37 And again: God calls the things that are not yet as things that are, and he
contemplates and judges the things that will be as already having come to be.
Just as then the judge justly punishes the one who has already sinned (even if
he should not punish, he is unjust) and he himself is not the cause of the sin
or of the penalty of the one punished, but the choice of the one who sinned,
so God, seeing what will be as already having come to be, judged that he come
to be, and, if he should become evil, that he be punished from himself.
Therefore he also made him and made him good; but he from his own choice
became evil and became for himself the cause of the penalty, or rather he
punishes himself, reaching after things that are not. And that the
foreknowledge of God did not become a cause for the devil himself of being
evil is clear; for neither is the physician who foreknows the coming disease
the cause of the disease, but the cause of the disease is the disorder, and
the foreknowledge is a mark of the physician's science, and the cause of the
foreknowledge is that it was going to be thus. And that it is thus, the
apostle says: The one "who calls the things that are not as things that are."
And this too one must know, that as the soul goes out from the body, so it
remains unchangeable for the rest, whether longing for the good or the evil.
38 And upon all these we say that this is good which God wills; for just as
the light is defined from the sun, so also the good from God. Everything then
that the good one wills is good, and this is good which he wills. God is
judge, we are not judges of God. Why then do we examine him? Does God shudder
and cower and fear? A God who fears would not be God.
39 If the sun and the moon have become boats for bringing the portion that
matter snatched from God, there was not before this a sun nor a moon, and the
human being was in darkness.
40 If according to you affliction is evil, in every way pleasure and enjoyment
are good, and, if this, fornication and gluttony and whatever delights and
pleases the one who does it are good, and whatever grieves, self-control,
fasting, vigil and such things, are evils.
41 If you accuse God for the total destruction of the flood and the burning of
the Sodomites and the drowning of Pharaoh, as he disciplines toward what is
beneficial, accuse him rather as he punishes eternally.
42 To bring forth from one's own substance is not a creation, but from things
that are not is a creation. If then evil creates, it brings forth from things
that are not, and no longer from matter



the body; but if from matter, that is from the substance of evil, if evil is
unoriginate, it is also in every way immortal and unchangeable (for what has
not begun does not cease nor is changed; but if it is changed and corrupted,
it is not unoriginate), but if evil is unchangeable, the body from its
substance will also be incorruptible and unchangeable. Whose then was the
corruption and the evil? For corruption in every way corrupts something, or it
is not corruption. If the good is passible, the good one also is passible, and
how is he perfectly good? And the good one, being corrupted by it, will go
into non-existence. But if it corrupted itself, it was corrupted and perished
and is not. But if a part of the good, it also corrupted a part of itself. And
that nature whose part is corruptible, the whole also is corruptible; for it
is impossible that the same nature be both corruptible and incorruptible. For
if we say that the body of the human being is corruptible, being a part of the
human being, and the soul incorruptible, yet they are not of one nature. For
the soul, even if it lives in a body, is not a body, but incorporeal; so that
the soul is of another nature and the body of another, and it is not that a
part of the same nature is corruptible and a part incorruptible, but the one
nature of the human being is corruptible, and the other incorruptible. And
neither is the soul from an unoriginate substance (for it is changeable) nor
the body; for it too is changeable and corruptible. And the human being is
said to be of one nature, not because the soul and the body are of one nature,
but because all humans likewise each have a soul and a body and each has two
natures.
43 Change is an alteration from the contrary into the contrary, from non-being
into being or from beings into non-being or from virtue into vice, which is
from the natural habits into the privation of these. Virtue is a fulfillment
of the law of God, and the law of God is his will (for no one commands what he
does not will), and the will of God is good, unchangeable, always holding in
the same way. So that the good is being, and likewise virtue (for virtue is a
good), and vice is not-being, but a voluntary disobedience and taking-away of
what is, that is of the law of God, coming into subsistence alongside in the
doing according to a compact, and ceasing as soon as the doing has ceased.
44 And this too one must know, that God does not punish anyone in the future,
but each makes himself receptive of the participation of God. And the
participation of God is delight, and the non-participation of him is
punishment; and here God does not bring the trials as punishing, but as
disciplining and healing the evil, so that we may come to know him and turn
and attain his holiness. "For the discipline of the Lord opens my ears."
45 Manichaean: If he is good, why in the coming age does he punish the evil
one and not rather destroy him? Orthodox: God is good, but also just; as good
then he made, whatever he made, good. Just as then it was just that those who
would be good by him and who were going not only to keep the good given to
them but also to work it further should come to be and be counted worthy of
the perfect good and of the eternal delight, so also it was just that those
who would be good by him and who were going not to keep the good given to them
but voluntarily to turn aside through the casting-off of the goods toward evil
should come to be and be deprived of the eternal delight.
46 The seraphim are farther from God than the evil one is from the seraphim.
If then the devil had not come to be, the one before him would have appeared
worse than all and evil in comparison with those before him, and we would have
said: Why did God, having foreknown that he was going to be such, make him?
Likewise also in the case of the one before that one, until we should come
even to the seraphim themselves. For often we contemplate a most pitiable
thing and think there is not another more pitiable than it, and after this we
see another worse. For whatever last evil of those we have seen we may reckon,
we suppose there is not a worse than it.


47 God then made all things very good, each according to its own nature, as he
willed; for he is not subject to a law, but what he wills, this is good, not
what we judge. For we are not judges of God, but just as from the sun or from
fire every light is lit, so from the will of God every good is defined. For if
you say that God made well in this and badly in this, and why he made this and
why he did not make this, you are a judge of God and greater than he and
wiser. He has authority then as God to make what he wills, and this is good,
and he has authority, of the things he makes, to reveal to us the causes he
wills and to hide those he wills. And each of the rational goods that came to
be either remained voluntarily in the good, as it was created, or was changed
voluntarily and departed from the good and lost it. For good is a best order
preserving for each what is proper according to nature (and this is the same
also as virtue), and evil is the dissolution of the order, that is disorder.
All beings, insofar as they are, are also goods and from the good, and insofar
as they have been deprived of the good, they are neither goods nor beings. For
the lifeless, insofar as they did not receive life from the good, are lifeless
as deprived of life, and likewise the insensate and the irrational, insofar as
they received, are goods.
48 God knows the evil as a good, just as he knows fornication as appetite and
friendship and union. And with him even the causes of evils are good-producing
powers; often from fornication vessels of election have been begotten.
49 If evil is an evil, the punishment that drives this out is a good. For
fornication is an evil, not the chastening; for the punishment is not evil,
but making oneself worthy of punishment.
50 If the evil is eternal and creates and is able and is and acts, from where
does it have these? Either from the good, or the good from the evil, or both
from another cause, because the evil also comes into subsistence alongside in
the good, and because it makes even the evils good and its own privation. For
friendship, being good, makes fornication appear a good, because in the good
even non-being somehow contends to be. For God is said not to be according to
the preeminent taking-away of all things.
51 And that nature itself puts forward the principle as one, the number itself
makes plain, not beginning from two but from one. If then a monad is the
principle of number, the principle of beings also is one.
52 If evil is not wholly incommunicable with the good, and the good is being,
evil is not-being; for two beings share in respect of being. If the good is
substance, evil is without substance; for two substances share in respect of
being substance.
53 If the evil was unmoved from the beginning, motion is a good and immobility
an evil. From where then did the evil have the motion? Life is a good and
motion and always was and is in the good, and the good one is living and was.
Death, as you say, is an evil and immobility and corruption and deadness and
no less non-existence. And evil was death, as you say. Just as then the good
one is life and living, the evil is death and deadness and deadened. From
where then did it live again and was it moved? If from itself, life is from
the evil, which is impossible; for corruption does not give life. But if from
the good, the good is not a good, having become a cause of life and of motion
to the evil. And why, having foreknown the evils that would be from it, did he
give it life?
54 The evil, insofar as it is evil, is both incommunicable with the good and
contrary and hostile, and the desire of the good is a good. How then did the
extremely evil desire the good?
55 Evil is unmoved and rather corruption and not coming-to-be. How then did
evil, lifeless and insensate, project its own projections? How then does it
perceive the good and



the projections of the good?
56 What is from the substance of the unoriginate and uncreated is unoriginate
and uncreated. How then do you say that all things are from the substance of
the principles according to you? For if all things are from their substance,
they are unoriginate principles and nothing. But if they are creatures, they
are not from their substance.
57 The evil is corruption and death. The evil then, if it is evil to others
but good to itself, constitutes itself, and is not wholly evil; for it
constitutes itself, and according to this it is not evil. But if it is wholly
evil, it is also evil to itself and destructive of itself and non-existent.
But if also in the corruption of one the coming-to-be of others comes about,
it is not the corruption that is cause of coming-to-be, but the good, through
excess of goodness, works even from the corruption of one the coming-to-be of
another. For matter, being subject to the good, having come to be by him, is
good; for he also brought all things from non-being into being in the
beginning.
58 It is not the same in the same respect good and evil, nor of the same thing
corruption and coming-to-be, the same power in the same respect, nor
self-power or self-corruption. Self-evil then is neither being nor good nor
productive of coming-to-be nor productive of beings or of goods; and the good,
in those in whom it comes to be perfectly, shows them perfect goods, and in
those in whom partially, partial; and in those in whom not at all, they
neither are and they are called wholly evils.
59 Fornication, insofar as it is appetite and friendship and union, is a good,
but insofar as it is appetite of what ought not and of what is forbidden, it
is an evil; for what is not commanded but forbidden is not-being as not owing
to be. Therefore also the appetite of what is not and ought not to come to be
is an evil.
60 The evil is twofold. The one is in truth evil as the falling-away of the
natural habits. For God made the human being upright, but they sought out many
reasonings. And another thing is called evil according to sense as the
afflictions according to the trials; for the name of evil is homonymous. For
it signifies two things: sometimes it signifies the privation of virtue,
sometimes the privation of joy, that is of what is desired. For fornication is
nothing other than privation of temperance and an evil use; for instead of
loving and being mixed with one's own vessel, that is one's own wife, one
loves and is mixed with one who belongs to another. Unbelief, instead of
believing and loving the God by nature, believes in the one who is not God.
This then is wickedness, to use what is not as being, not to furnish to each
what is its own; what is each one's own is what is defined by God. God's own
is the glory and the honor as befitting a creator, and the creatures' own is
the measure that the one who gave being defined for each. And it is in our
power either to use the things given to us by God for that for which he has
given them to us, and it is in our power to use them not for that for which he
has given them, as he gave us the desiring, so that we may desire him, the
only good. If we use the desire for this, it is virtue and good; but if we
love something else besides him, it is not use but misuse. For he made us for
this very thing, that we may share in him, desiring.
61 Matter, he says, was unoriginate, ungenerated, in its own boundaries,
always moved disorderly, always fighting with itself and consuming itself. And
God too, he says, was in his own boundaries, unoriginate, ungenerated,
eternal, incorporeal, wholly light, but matter, he says, being moved
disorderly and without reason, came upon the boundaries of God. And marveling
at the light of God it ceased from the fight against itself, and it set upon
the light, fighting so as even to snatch a part of it into itself; for they
also affirm that matter has rulers as over a hidden palace, whom they also
claim to be world-rulers of the darkness and spiritual things of wickedness


to be. The good God, he says, allowed a part of himself snatched by it to
become captive, so that he might circumscribe it and bound it from advancing
further into his own boundaries. If matter is unoriginate, ungenerated,
incorporeal, without quality, invisible, and likewise the good God, and all
things are from their substance, how are the things that came to be from them
seen? For what comes forth from an incorporeal and invisible substance will in
every way be incorporeal and invisible. And before this, if both substances
are unoriginate and ungenerated and eternal, and we are from them, not brought
by them from non-being into being but from their substance, we are unoriginate
and eternal; for the offspring of each substance is consubstantial with the
substance that generated it: of an eternal, eternal, of an ungenerated,
ungenerated, of an unoriginate, unoriginate. And again: If our constitution is
from the substance of the good one and of the evil one, we are not creatures
but children, and there is no creation in the world. And again: If it is
impossible for the substance of the good one to be turned into evil and wholly
to think or to do an evil thing, and it is impossible for the evil one to be
turned and to think or to do any good (and our soul is of the good), it is
impossible for our soul to think anything base and for our body to do a good.
But we see many souls thinking evil things, as those of the Manichaeans, and
many bodies doing good things, as those of the saints. And again: If the evil
one too is composite from incorporeal and body, and the good one likewise, and
our soul is of the substance of the good one, and the body of the evil one,
our soul too will be composite from incorporeal and body, and our body. And
again: If matter is unoriginate, if it is incorporeal, how did it come forth
from an incorporeal substance? For the unoriginate is in every way
unchangeable; things simple by nature are not naturally united. How then did a
mixing come about? And again: If matter is composite from incorporeal and
body, and the body is contrary to the incorporeal, who is the one who united
the contraries? For the contraries would not associate with the contraries,
being joined in a friendly way into one texture and one hypostasis, unless the
power of the creator brought them together into one. Matter then, being
composite, received being by a divine word. And again: Is matter corruption or
corruptible? For corruption corrupts and is not corrupted, and the corruptible
is corrupted by the corruption; and the corruptible, that is the thing being
corrupted, is matter.
62 What is corrupting, or corruption, or corruptible? For corruption is the
dissolution of the things that have been put together. And the one who brings
the corruption corrupts, and the composite that is dissolved is corrupted. And
the corrupting and the thing being corrupted are substances, and the
corruption is an accident contemplated in the being-loosed of the things put
together, neither pre-existing nor remaining after the being-loosed.
63 What then is matter, corrupting or corruption or corruptible? And if you
say that it is corrupting, say what it corrupts if not the good one? For you
say nothing other than matter and the good one. If then there were two, matter
corrupts, and the good one produces coming-to-be, or matter is constituted,
and the good one is corrupted and perishes. But if it corrupts itself, know
that nothing is destructive of itself; but if it also corrupted itself, it
would have consumed itself and driven itself into non-existence. And how is
what is being corrupted unoriginate? But if it is corruptible, by whom is it
corrupted if not by the good one, and the good one will be a corrupter. But if
it is corruption, by whom is it enacted and is it? For corruption is a
dissolution, as we said. Who then is the one dissolving and what is the thing
being dissolved? And that matter cannot be corruption and a corruptible
substance is knowable from this: for the corruptible substance precedes and
pre-subsists as a hypostasis that has been put together. And the corruption
comes on after the composition, loosing the things that have been put
together. And how was the substance of matter having the corruption concurrent
and consubstantial? But perhaps you will say that it was once not corruption
in actuality but in potency, and likewise corruptible. But know that being
destructive or creative, and not yet enacting the corruption or the



creation, because the things being corrupted or created cannot be co-eternal
with the one corrupting or creating, is true. But you say that nothing has
been brought from non-being into being, but that evil itself is destructive
and consumptive of itself, so that this, having the corrupting and being
corrupted in potency and not in actuality, was incomplete. And what advances
from the incomplete into completeness is not unoriginate; for if the
unchangeable is unoriginate, what is changed is not unoriginate. And again:
What is first of matter, its substance or the corruption? And if the
corruption, it is impossible for it to exist before being, being consumed. But
if its substance pre-existed the corruption, it is rather substance and
existence and not corruption, and there was once incorruption not having
corruption contrary, and the corruption is not unoriginate. But if its
substance always was consuming itself, how did it bring forth the fruits? If
it consumed partially, it is not wholly destructive nor wholly corruptible and
not perfectly evil. But if, being destructive, it ceased from corrupting
itself, either the corrupting is an evil and it was turned from evil into
good, or the corrupting is a good and from good it became evil. If matter is
evil, and it corrupted itself, it is good; for what corrupts the evil is good.
And again say: Was matter light or darkness? And if light, of the substance of
the good; and see, one principle and not two. But if darkness, how does the
darkness contemplate? For if it contemplates, it has light. But if it does not
have light, it is blind,
and how did it see the light? And how will there be darkness in the luminous
and not be dissolved? Or what sharing of light with darkness? And again: Did
matter live or not? And if it did not live, how was it moved? For everything
that does not have life is unmoved. But if it lived, it either has the life
from itself or from elsewhere. And if from itself, it was life and not death.
But if it received the life from elsewhere, either from the good, and how is
he good who gave life to the evil? But if from another, there will not be two

it was unoriginate, it always had being and never had non-being. And how do

give what it does not have? And again: Coming-to-be and corruption are

64 Good is named from all things running exceedingly toward it, which also is

corruption and death, unoriginate and eternal, was it substance or accident?
And if substance, it was either a body, as earth, water, air, fire, or
incorporeal, as God, angel, soul, or composite from body and incorporeal. And
if a body, it was unmoved; for every body is soulless and


unmoved. And how, not being moved, did it come into the boundaries of the
light? Or how did it have motion? Either the one who gave motion to the evil
is good and not good, or another besides the good one gave it the motion, and
there will be three principles and not two. But if you say that matter was
incorporeal, from where the body? For the incorporeal and unoriginate
substance cannot be turned into a body. From where then the countless
multitude of bodies? But if you say that matter is composite from incorporeal
and body, know that it is impossible for a composite to come to be except from
simples, just as it is impossible for two to come to be except from one and
one, and it is impossible for a human being to come to be except from soul and
body, and it is impossible for a body to come to be except from the simple
elements, earth and water and air and fire. The simples then must first be,
and then a composite come to be from the simples. There will then be not two
principles but three. Thus it is impossible for matter to be unoriginate. And
if it is composite from incorporeal and body, why did it not make the human
being from its incorporeal nature and the body?
67 Hear, heaven, and give ear, earth. Give heed, heaven, and I will speak, and
let the earth hear words from my mouth. For I will speak words not of my
heart, but of the darkened and defiled Manichaeans. Hear, peoples, tribes,
tongues, hear. Let heaven and earth become one theater today. Hear what the
accursed Manichaeans say, the ones darkened about the light, the mouth of the
devil. They say that matter made war with God and tore away a part from him.
They call God circumscribed, making war and being warred against and grieving
and being cut off. Is God warred against? Is God cut off? Woe, woe, shall we
not kill them? Shall we not consume them with fire? Shall we associate with
these? Shall we receive these into communion? The one who shares with them is
like them and will be of their portion both in the present and in the coming
age. Brothers, it is called communion: let us die and not receive the
Manichaeans into communion. Let us die, so that we may live; the one who
receives them into communion is a Manichaean, like them. It is better to
judaize and to die a Jew than to share with the Manichaeans. O from the
Manichaeans. Hear then toward God, O men, hear what Manes, destroyed by God,
says. The world, he says, is not of God, but of the devil. They wish to
alienate us from our God. And God, he says, grieved, because a part was cut
off from him, and he suffered. If a part was cut off from him, he is
corruptible; for everything that is cut is corruptible. Evil, he says, robbed
and snatched a part from God, and therefore God sends and robs and snatches
the part robbed from him. Do you introduce robbers of God, O one destroyed by
God? But God, he says, also made machines, twelve buckets and the sun and the
moon, so that he might take what is his own. Then what, so that something last
may come about, so that the good one may send fire and burn the world and
consume all things and restore matter below into its place and the souls that
did not receive Manes? Hear what the adversary of God says, how much power he
gives to matter: He casts down the power of God the Most High. First he says
that he was robbed and stolen from. O powerlessness. Do you call my God
powerless? But he yielded willingly. How then did he grieve, if he was not
forced? No one willingly betrays his own and grieves. But if, so that his
place might not be overmastered, not being able otherwise to get the better of
matter, he grieved, how is he not powerless? Then he grieves, and the part
from him is punished, and he devises and makes machines, so that he may take
what is his own, and he does not even receive back the whole of what is his
own; for the souls that did not follow Manes are punished forever; and last
matter again, having risen in its own places, having afflicted the good one,
and itself not having been afflicted, but the



last having run back toward its own with also the souls that did not believe
Manes. But perhaps it will also rise again.
68 Tell us, O Manichaeans: Was matter, being quiet at first, good or evil? If
evil, later, having risen, it became good. But if first good, later evil.
Then, having risen against itself and having been moved disorderly and having
come into the boundaries of the light, if it beheld the immaterial light, how
is it blind? And how will matter behold immaterial light? But if it thought,
how is it without reason and mindless? But if it also loved according to you,
how is it soulless? And how is it evil? For what loves the good is not evil.
And how is it mindless and unwise, the one that went out from its own, not as
an irrational living being, but as a best and well-ordered general, and having
taken a noteworthy spoil of the light and having returned into its own? And
how do you also call base the one that received the good and contained it? For
the things receptive of good are not contrary nor hostile. And again: If
matter snatched away a part of God, matter is effective and productive, and
the nature of the good is passible and flowing. But if that one desired the
good, and the good one gave a part of himself to the evil, both were changed;
for the evil also desired the good and became good, and the good, having
accepted being mixed with the evil, became evil. And again: Was the good
harmed by the snatching and the mixing, or did it harm, or did it benefit or
was it benefited? If it was benefited, the one that benefited is active and
good, and the one benefited is lesser and passible. But if it was harmed, thus
too the one harmed is passible and the one that harmed more dominant. But if
it harmed, it is evil. But if it benefited, the good one is envious, having
grieved and being mindful of wrongs, having given matter over to fire and, by
excess of remembering wrongs, even a part from himself with it. But if it
neither harmed nor was harmed nor benefited nor was benefited, first it is
without activity; for the good, wherever it comes to be, benefits, if it has a
good activity; and second it is unjust, because, neither having been benefited
nor harmed, he will punish the matter that has done no injustice. But if God
is angry and grieves according to you because matter loved him and shared in
him, let no one love him. But if, as you say, God, knowing that matter was
coming upon him, willingly allowed a part of himself to be snatched by it, so
that, having circumscribed its motion, he might punish it as having melted a
lump into Gehenna, first, for what reason did he not at once melt it and give
it over to Gehenna? But if he was not able except in such a manner, why does
he blame us for not standing against the evil one, himself having betrayed our
souls through powerlessness? Then, saying Gehenna, you will say either that
this is consubstantial with the good or consubstantial with the evil or
another substance, and no longer two but three. For if of the good, how does
it punish? For Gehenna is not good. But if of the evil, it does not punish the
evil; for nothing punishes itself. But if something else besides the two,
three principles and not two, or it was brought from non-being into being. And
if this, why, not coming to the straight, did we not say that all things have
been brought from non-being into being? The unoriginate is unchangeable; for
change is later becoming what one was not first. And those whose being is from
change are changeable by nature; and those whose being did not begin from
change, but is unoriginate, are unchangeable by nature. How then did we come
to be from the unoriginate substances? For if they themselves are not such as
we, they too are humans. But if they are such, how were they changed? We then
are not from the unoriginate substances, but from non-being. If matter is an
unoriginate principle and eternal, and evil has from itself being and whatever
it has, that is the tyranny and the strength and the evil, it will have these
unchangeable. How then will the one who feared it so much as to yield a part
of himself to become captive by it be able at last to get the better of it?
69 Manichaean: Why did God, foreknowing the devil, that he was going to be
turned from


the good into the evil and to cast off the good and to be in non-existence of
the good, which is evil, make him? Orthodox: If he foreknew him, he foreknew
him as in every way going to be; for the foreknowledge of what is not in every
way going to be is not foreknowledge, but ignorance and error. Manichaean: Did
God make him willing? Orthodox: Yes, willing. Manichaean: And why did he will
to make him, being good, the one who would be evil? Orthodox: God made him
beautiful; but he was changed voluntarily and cast off the good. Manichaean:
Why did he cast off the good? Orthodox: Because he came to be
self-determining. Manichaean: And why did he make him self-determining and
changeable? Orthodox: Every creature is changeable by nature; for those whose
coming-to-be began from change, these must by nature be changeable. Or is the
bringing from non-being into being not a change? Only the divine then is
unchangeable by nature as uncreated and always being. And of the creatures, as
many as are rational are changeable by will, being changed by the will, and
the rest according to body. And self-determining, because not from necessity;
for every rational thing is self-determining. For toward what will he use the
rational if he is not self-determining? Or how will he come to be in virtue or
be good if he is not self-determining? For what comes about by force or by
necessity of nature is not virtue; whence the irrational also do not have
virtue. And to do away the virtue of the good because of some slackness is not
of a just one. Manichaean: He ought then to have made only those who were
going to be virtuous, so that only the good might be and only virtue might
live. Orthodox: God alone is perfect good, and by comparison with him all
things are incomplete, being and being called goods according to this, insofar
as they share in him, and by comparison with one another. For what shares more
in the good is and is called more good; and what less, less; and what not at
all, not at all good. And what is not at all good is wholly evil. And being
itself also is a good; for being is from God the good, so that the devil,
being, is good according to being itself.
70 Manichaean: Why does the Lord say: It were good for that man if that man
had not been born? Orthodox: Since it befits the good one to give the goods,
and for the one receiving it is shame and reproach not to keep the goods he
received, not because of the giver but because of himself and his own
slackness. And God cannot not furnish the goods, being good, that is he does
not will; and the one who does not will to receive is himself cause of
himself, making for himself the not-receiving better than the receiving. It is
not just then nor good that, because this one does not will to receive, the
good one be hindered from doing well and giving the goods. For thus evil would
have defeated the good, if God by goodness brings from non-being into being,
and the evil that would be, turning voluntarily from a good, hindered the good
creation of the good. Therefore the Lord did not say: It were beneficial if
that man had not been born, but: It were beneficial for that man. It is good
both according to nature and according to the just that the good one do well
and give the goods, and the one who received and did not keep made the
receiving reproachful for himself; for he himself who does not will the good,
even what good he has, does not will nor is pleased. For being is not in our
power, but from God alone, and being good is from God and from us. God then
gives what is his, being and being well, and we, what is in our power, the
keeping of the goods given, either do or not. And what is of God alone, both
when we will and when we do not will, he himself gives as good, so that the
good may be in us even in some measure.
71 God always furnishes the goods even to the devil, but that one does not
will to receive. And in the coming age God gives the goods to all; for he is a
spring of the goods, gushing goodness for all, and each, as he has made
himself receptive, shares



in the good. Here then, having the appetite about other things and attaining,
we are pleased even in some measure, but there, when God should be all in all,
there being no eating, no drinking nor any fleshly pleasure nor any injustice,
those who neither have the customary pleasures nor are receptive of the things
from God are pained incurably, not God having made a punishment, but we having
prepared the punishment for ourselves, since God did not make death either,
but we drew this upon ourselves. For by nature every composite is dissolved,
but as far as Adam was united to God through contemplation, he had in himself
the life beyond nature giving life to his mortal nature. And when he stood
away from the union toward the life, that is God, he came from the
incorruption beyond nature into the dissolution according to nature of the
composite, which is death.
72 And since some say that God, having foreknown those who would sin and not
repent and be judged, ought not to have brought them from non-being into being
(for it is not of a good one to make and to punish), we say to them first the
word of the apostle: Yes indeed, O man, who are you who answer against God?
For who has resisted his will? For what he wills, this is good; And the potter
has authority to make one vessel unto honor, another unto dishonor. For he
himself is the one who makes both the just and the sinners (but he himself
does not make them just or sinners) or honorable or dishonorable; for the same
apostle says in the letter to Timothy, having numbered the vices beforehand,
he adds saying: Whoever then has cleansed himself from these has become a
vessel of election. For having come to be self-determining we have authority
to cleanse ourselves from the most shameful passions and to defile ourselves.
Yet God made all good. He himself then furnishes both being and being well to
all, as a sun spreading the rays of his goodness upon all his works. And being
is not in our power to receive, but being well is in our power. If then we
will and long, we share in his goodness and will be in light forever. But if
we are slack and blind ourselves and do not long for him, we will be without
share in him. He ought not then, because of our slackness, to have become
unimparting of the gifts of his goodness, of which the first is being, nor
ought our evil to have defeated and made his goodness without activity. For if
this were so, none of beings would have come to be; for none of beings lives
worthily of his goodness. For justly, being compared with him, all things are
unworthy of being.
73 Yet God is not only good but also just and seeing all things not yet come
to be as being. For the one who calls, he says, the things that are not as
things that are. If through goodness he ought not to have made those who are
punished, rather through surpassing goodness he ought both to have made them
and not to punish, but to forgive them as they sin; for he himself is lord of
both, both of making and of forgiving. But if it is just that those who sin be
punished, it was just also that they come to be. For God sees the things that
are not as things that are, and he does not judge from the outcome of the
things, but from the foreknowledge of the things that will be. Since he does
not even know from the outcome of the things that come to be, but he foreknows
the things that will be and according to his foreknowledge he predetermines.
And how does he also foreknow what will not be? For if he foreknew the one who
sins and did not make him, it was not foreknowledge but error; for just as
knowledge is of beings, so also foreknowledge is of the things in every way
going to be.
74 Let us not then judge God; for we do not know his counsel. For who has
known the mind of the Lord? Or who has become his counselor? For he has not
all his counsel


revealed to us, but what it was beneficial for us to know; and what it was not
beneficial for us to know, he hid. Let us not then call God to account; for he
himself is the judge and he himself is the spring of the goods, and there is
no good except what he himself wills, just as there is no light except that
which is sourced from the sun.
75 God himself then, being good by nature and sympathetic, does not will the
sin, does not will the death of the sinner, nor is he delighted at the
destruction of the living, nor is he held by a passion of wrath or does he
punish, but he gushes the goods for all. The one who longs receives, and the
good one attains goods; and in this life there is a certain economy and
governance and unspeakable providence calling those who sin toward turning and
repentance, but after death there is no longer change, no longer repentance,
not that God does not receive repentance (for he himself cannot deny himself
nor does he cast off the sympathy), but the soul is no longer changed.
Therefore even if someone should do all the righteousnesses and, having
turned, should sin and go out from life longing for the sin, he will die in
his sin. Likewise also the sinner, if he should repent and die in his
repentance, his sins will not be remembered. For just as the demons after the
fall do not repent, nor do the angels now sin, but both have acquired the
unchangeable, so also humans after death have the unchangeable, and the just,
longing for God and having him, are always gladdened in him, and the sinners,
longing for the sin and not having the materials of the sin, as being eaten by
fire and worm, are punished having no consolation. For what is punishment if
not privation of what is longed for? According to the proportion then of the
longing both those who long for God are gladdened and those who long for the
sin are punished; for those who attain what is longed for are gladdened
according to the measure of the longing, and those who miss are pained
according to the measure of the longing.
76 And you tell us: What is almighty? Wholly the one who holds all; for the
apostle says: And God who constructed all things. And how is God almighty if
he does not hold matter and the evil? Or how a creator if he himself did not
create the matter? Or how God, being incomplete and having deficient power?
For why did he not destroy and subject the matter, but allowed it rising even
until a turn of a time? If not willing, he is not good; for it is not of a
good one nor of a just one to keep quiet at those who sin. But if not able, a
powerless God would not be God; for you cannot say,
as we have said concerning the devil's coming to be and being, that his being
itself is a good. For we, giving him being from the good, would justly say
that his being is a good; for what is given from the good is a good. But you,
not giving matter existence from the good, but also calling this evil and evil
by nature and wholly without share in good, will not be able to say that its
being is a good. Therefore either God according to you is not good or not
able, not having destroyed it from the beginning. And again: If all things are
from the substance of the good and the substance of the evil, neither is the
good one even a creator nor the evil one, but fathers of beings. And again: If
evil is contrary to the good as being to being and principle to principle and
substance to substance and, to speak according to you, god to god, and it is
in us to do the good and the evil, it is in us to give the prizes of victory
to the good or to the evil, and we are causes of the dominance of each.
77 For we say one God, good, just, creator of all, holding all, all-powerful,
able to do whatever he wills, and that all things have been created by him,
both the visible and the invisible, not from his substance but from non-being
into being



(for the Lord made all things, whatever he willed), and we say that this is
good which he wills; for the things made do not judge the one who made. But
neither do we know the account of his providence; for who has known the mind
of the Lord or who has become his counselor? If his counsel and his will were
known, he would not have been God nor wonderful. For just as his substance is
incomprehensible, so also his willing and his providence. For if no one knows
the things of humans except the spirit of each that is in him, but not even
all his own things does each of humans know (for he does not know the future,
nor what he wishes or what he will will and wish tomorrow does he know), how
then will he know the will of God and his counsel, he who often even comes
into forgetfulness of the things he has counseled?
78 God provides according to his own foreknowledge of all things. And to us
who know neither the things that will be nor the things that have gone before
nor all the things that are present his governance seems uneven, but it is
even and good and just in truth. For foreknowing the things in our power as
being the things that will be, he judges justly, as he himself wills and
wishes; for God will not see as a human sees. For a human looks at a face, but
God at a heart. We often see even what is judged just by all as unjust through
a preconception of evil and through the same thing not being pleasing to all;
for the same is not pleasing to all, but another to another. And God judges
not from a change of mind or from after-knowledge, but from foreknowledge. For
God, having foreknown all things before their coming-to-be, predetermined for
each justly and providentially and beneficially the things not in our power as
corresponding to the things in our power. For knowledge is one thing and
foreknowledge another and after-knowledge another and definition another and
predetermination another. For knowledge is knowing the things that are or that
come to be, and foreknowledge is knowing the things that will be before their
coming-to-be, and after-knowledge is the true knowledge that comes on after a
false knowledge; and definition is a judgment and sentence upon the things
that have come to be, and predetermination a judgment and sentence upon the
things that will be.
79 God then, having foreknown the things that will be by us voluntarily, that
is the things in our power, I mean virtue and vice, predetermines the things
not in our power. And the foreknowing power of God does not have the cause
from us, but the having-foreknown what we are going to do is from us; for if
we were not going to do it, he himself also would not have foreknown what
would not be. And the foreknowledge of God is true and not to be overstepped,
but it itself is not the cause of the thing that will be coming to be in every
way, but, because we are going to do this or that, he foreknows. And he
foreknows many things that are not pleasing to him, and he himself is not
cause of these. As neither is a physician cause of the disease, if he
foreknows that someone is going to be sick, but the being-sick is of another
cause that makes the disease, and the foreknowledge of the physician of the
art in him; so also what will be by us does not have the cause from God, but
our self-determining will; and the self-determining is not an evil, if we use
it as it was given to us and for that for which it was given. And God's
having-foreknown is of his foreknowing power. But neither do our penalties
have God as cause. For the judge is not the cause of the penalty of the evil
one, even if he himself judging wills, but the one who has offended is cause
of his own penalty, and the judge is cause of the justice; and justice is a
good. For if he judges willing, yet not according to a preceding will but
according to the following. A preceding will then is what someone wills from
himself, and a following what is from the cause of the things that come to be;
for precedingly from himself God wills all humans to be saved and to come to
knowledge of truth. And when we sin he wills that we be disciplined toward
what is beneficial. And the preceding will of God is of the goodness, and the



following of his justice.
80 And that God predetermined all things according to his foreknowledge, we
will know thus. The divine is unchangeable and judging not from
after-knowledge but from foreknowledge. If then he judges and defines day by
day, he knows day by day what comes to be, and, as some say, he even changes
his mind and adds years of life, not knowing what they say. For if he changes
his mind and adds, he has both the will and the knowledge changeable, and this
is unworthy of God. But if he is said to have added to Hezekiah according to
the scripture, yet he did not add as not knowing nor as not having
predetermined, but he himself both foreknew and predetermined the things
concerning Hezekiah; and providentially he fashioned a form, economizing our
salvation, so that he might show how much repentance is able. And he is said
to change his mind from the things according to us. For the scripture speaks
more humanly about God toward the childishness of those who hear. For just as
it is said: Why do you sleep, Lord? (but God does not sleep, but he called his
forbearance and his long-suffering and the postponement of his help sleep; for
among us those who sleep do not help), so also a change of mind; for we, when
we give someone wealth and that one uses it badly, change our mind. Thus God
having made the human being and having anointed Saul as king, and they having
sinned, he is said to change his mind, not God having changed his mind (for he
foreknew what would be), but these having done things worthy of a change of
mind. Let us strive then to do the good and to become good, so that we may be
of the goods foreknown and predetermined unto eternal life.

the pleasure according to sense contrary to the aforesaid order, which is not
good but rather evil. And evil too is spoken of in two ways: either the truly
evil, that is what comes about contrary to the natural order and contrary to
the law of the creator according to one's own mind (for natural order is
obedience to the creator, and disorder is disobedience), or the seeming evil,
that which is

is contrary to nature; for to God alone, who is to be glorified by nature, the

are good, and likewise sleep and the use of the wife, knowing one's own
vessel, is good, but to use eating and drinking and women indifferently toward
irrational pleasures in the manner of irrational things is wicked.
85 Through empty glory then and through measureless eating and drinking and
the fornications, love of money, the support of these, and envies and strifes
and wickednesses and falsehood and slanders and snatchings and murders and the
hatred toward the neighbor. For because lawlessness was multiplied, the love
of the many grew cold. And he called many those who have used the lawlessness.
From the natural things then come the things contrary to nature, the devil
assaulting.
86 Toward all these two ways are greatest for us toward help, forcing
ourselves (for the force is a proof of love toward God) and the prayer; for
the prayer is a proof of humility. For coming to know our own weakness we call
upon the power of God. And one must know that not judging also ransoms from
sin; for not judging is a sign of humility. Nothing so calls upon the help of
God as humility. For see, he says, my humility and the toil of my force and
forgive all my sins, and see my humility and deliver me, that is from the
enemy who makes war with me and from my sin and folly. And living with the
virtuous also contributes toward virtue (for the human being is an imitative
living being) and holding off from the association according to sense of the
things that provoke; for as far from the eyes, so far also from the heart. For
through them the memory of the sensible things comes to be for the soul. And
it lies before us to separate the mind from the memory of the sensible things,

thickened by the memory of the sensible things and is dragged down toward them
and stands away from the contemplation and enjoyment of the intelligible
beauty; for no one can serve two lords. Humans accomplish every art by habit
and by persistence and by force. 87 Through force then that comes about from
love, and prayer from humility, and flight from the sensible things, and
fleeing the cohabitation of the more careless, and not judging, and humility,
and the association and obedience of the virtuous, let us work our own
salvation, not hiding the reasonings and the passions in us. For everything
hidden is base, and what is made manifest is light. For a serpent does not
generate in the sun, but in dens, nor does a seed grow lying above earth, but
hidden under earth. So also the wicked reasonings, not being published, bear
fruit unto corruption, and the good unto eternal life. Which may it be that
all of us attain by the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the
Father is the glory together with the all-holy and good and



life-giving and consubstantial Spirit, now and always and unto the ages of the
ages. Amen.
