* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Conon the Athenian entered public affairs in the Peloponnesian war, and in it
his work was of great moment. For both as praetor he was set over the infantry
armies and as prefect of the fleet he accomplished great things at sea. For
which causes especial honor was held for him. For he alone was set over all
the islands, in which power he took Pherae, a colony of the Lacedaemonians.
He was also praetor at the end of the Peloponnesian war, when at the river
Aegos the forces of the Athenians were defeated by Lysander. But then he was
absent, and by that the affair was administered worse: for he was both
prudent of military matters and a diligent commander.
And so to no one in those times was it doubtful that, if he had been present,
the Athenians would not have received that calamity.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
But when affairs were afflicted, when he had heard that the fatherland was
being besieged, he did not seek where he himself might live safely, but from
where he might be a protection to his citizens. And so he betook himself to
Pharnabazus, satrap of Ionia and Lydia and likewise son-in-law of the king
and a kinsman: with whom that he might avail much by favor, he effected it by
much labor and many dangers. For when the Lacedaemonians, the Athenians
having been defeated, did not remain in the alliance which they had made with
Artaxerxes, and had sent Agesilaus to wage war into Asia, most of all driven
by Tissaphernes, who from the innermost of the king had defected from his
friendship and had entered an alliance with the Lacedaemonians, Pharnabazus
was held commander against this man, yet in truth Conon was set over the army
and all things were done by his judgment. This man much hindered the highest
leader Agesilaus and often opposed his counsels, nor indeed was it not open
that, if that man had not been, Agesilaus would have snatched Asia as far as
Taurus from the king. After which man was recalled home by his own citizens,
because the Boeotians and Athenians had declared war on the Lacedaemonians,
Conon none the less kept himself among the prefects of the king and was of
great use to all of them.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Tissaphernes had defected from the king, nor was that so open to Artaxerxes
as to the rest: for by many and great deserts with the king, even when he did
not remain in duty, he availed. Nor was that to be wondered at, if he was not
easily led to believing, remembering that by that man's work he had overcome
Cyrus his brother. For the sake of accusing this man Conon, sent by
Pharnabazus to the king, after he came, first according to the custom of the
Persians approached Tithraustes the chiliarch, who held the second grade of
command, and showed himself to wish to speak with the king. To this man that
one said, "There is no delay, but do you deliberate whether you prefer to
speak or to transact by letters what you have in mind. For it is necessary,
if you come into his sight, that you venerate the king, which they call
prosku/nhsin (for no one is admitted without this). If this is heavy to you,
through me none the less, the mandates having been set forth, you will
accomplish what you strive for."
Then Conon said, "To me indeed it is not heavy to hold any honor for the
king, but I fear lest it be a reproach to my city, if, when I have set out
from that which has been accustomed to command the other nations, I rather
perform the custom of the barbarians than of that one." And so what he wished
he handed over written to this man.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Which things having been known, the king was so moved by his authority that
he both judged Tissaphernes an enemy and ordered the Lacedaemonians to be
pursued with war and permitted him to choose whom he wished for dispensing
the money. That judgment Conon denied to be of his own counsel, but of the
king himself, who ought to know his own best, but that he advised that he
give that business to Pharnabazus.
Hence having been gifted with great gifts he was sent to the sea, that he
order long ships from the Cyprians and Phoenicians and the other maritime
cities and prepare a fleet by which he might guard the sea the next summer,
Pharnabazus having been given as helper, just as he himself had wished. When
that was announced to the Lacedaemonians, they administer the affair not
without care, because they judged a greater war to be imminent than if they
contended with the barbarian alone. For they saw that a brave and prudent
leader would be set over the royal resources and would fight with them, whom
they could surpass neither by counsel nor by forces. With this mind they
gather a great fleet: they set out with Pisander as leader. These Conon,
having attacked at Cnidus, puts to flight in a great battle, takes many
ships, sinks several. By which victory not only Athens, but also all Greece,
which had been under the empire of the Lacedaemonians, was liberated. Conon
came with part of the ships into the fatherland, sees to both walls destroyed
by Lysander, both of the Piraeus and of Athens, being repaired, and fifty
talents of money, which he had received from Pharnabazus, he gives to his
citizens.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
It happened to this man, what happens to the rest of mortals, that he was
more inconsiderate in favorable than in adverse fortune. For the fleet of the
Peloponnesians having been defeated, when he thought he had avenged the
injuries of the fatherland, he desired more things than he was able to
effect. Nor yet were those things not pious and to be approved, because he
preferred that the resources of the fatherland be increased rather than of
the king. For when he had established great authority for himself by that
naval fight which he had made at Cnidus, not only among the barbarians but
also all the cities of Greece, he began secretly to give work that he restore
Ionia and Aeolia to the Athenians. When that had been hidden less diligently,
Tiribazus, who was in charge at Sardis, summoned Conon, pretending that he
wished to send him to the king about a great matter. Obeying this man's
message, when he had come, he was thrown into chains, in which he was for
some time. From there some have left it written that he was led away to the
king and there perished. Against those things Dinon the historian, to whom we
give most credit concerning Persian affairs, wrote that he escaped: that he
doubts, whether it was done with Tiribazus knowing or unaware.
