* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

We neither dread nor fear what we suffer from the ignorant, since we came to
this sect having accepted the terms of its covenant, so that, having pledged
even our lives, we enter these contests, hoping to obtain what God promises
and fearing to suffer what he threatens against the other life. We contend
with all your cruelty, even bursting forth of our own accord, and we rejoice
more when condemned than when acquitted. We have sent this little book, then,
not because we fear for ourselves, but for you and for all our enemies, not to
mention our friends. For our discipline commands us to love even our enemies
and to pray for those who persecute us, so that this may be our perfect and
proper goodness, not a common one. To love friends belongs to all men; to love
enemies belongs to Christians alone. We who grieve at your ignorance, pity
human error, look ahead to what is coming, and see its signs threatened every
day, must therefore break forth even in this way to set before you what you
refuse to hear in public.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

We worship one God, whom you all know by nature, at whose lightnings and
thunders you tremble, at whose benefits you rejoice. The rest even you
yourselves take for gods, whom we know to be demons. Yet it is a matter of
human right and of natural power for each man to worship what he has judged
fit, and one man's religion neither harms nor helps another. But neither is it
religion to compel religion, which ought to be taken up willingly, not by
force, since even victims are demanded from a willing mind. Thus even if you
compel us to sacrifice, you will furnish nothing to your gods. For they will
not want sacrifices from the unwilling, unless perhaps they are contentious;
but God is not contentious. In short, he who is true bestows all that is his
equally on the profane and on his own, and therefore he has also appointed an
eternal judgment of the grateful and the ungrateful. Yet you have never caught
us, whom you think sacrilegious, even in theft, much less in sacrilege. But
all who plunder temples and swear by the gods and worship them are not
Christians, and yet they are caught as sacrilegious. It would take long if we
were to unfold the other ways in which all the gods are both mocked and
despised by their own worshippers. So also we are defamed in the matter of the
emperor's majesty, yet never could followers of Albinus or Niger or Cassius be
found among Christians; but those very men who had sworn by their genii even
up to the day before, who had both offered and vowed victims for their safety,
who had often condemned Christians, have been found their enemies. A Christian
is an enemy of no one, much less of the emperor, whom, knowing that he is
appointed by his God, he must love and revere and honor and wish safe together
with the whole Roman empire, as long as the age stands: for so long it will
stand. We therefore worship the emperor also in such a way as is lawful for us
and expedient for him, as a man second after God, who has obtained whatever he
is from God, and less than God alone.

This he himself will also wish. For so he is greater than all, while he is
less than the true God alone. So he is also greater than the gods themselves,
while they too are in his power. Therefore we also sacrifice for the safety of
the emperor, but to our God and his, but as God has commanded, with a pure
prayer. For God, the founder of the universe, does not need odor or any blood.
For these are the food of demons. But we not only reject demons; we also
refute them and daily expose them and drive them out of men, as is known to
very many. Thus we rather pray for the safety of the emperor, asking it from
him who is able to grant it. And indeed it can be sufficiently clear to you
that we act from the discipline of divine patience, since so great a multitude
of men, almost the greater part of every city, we live in silence and modesty,
known perhaps more as individuals than as a body, and recognizable only by the
amendment of former vices. Far be it from us to take it ill that we suffer
what we wish, or to contrive any vengeance of our own, which we expect from
God.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Yet, as we have said above, we must grieve that no city will bear with
impunity the pouring out of our blood; as also under the governor Hilarianus,
when they had cried out concerning the burial grounds of our dead: Let there
be no burial grounds! they themselves had no threshing-floors! For they did
not gather their harvests. Moreover, what the rains of the past year recalled
to the human race has appeared, namely that there was a flood also of old
because of the unbeliefs and iniquities of men; and what the fires which
lately hung by night over the walls of Carthage threatened, they know who saw
them; and what the former thunders sounded, they know who have grown hard. All
these are signs of the imminent wrath of God, which we must, in whatever way
we can, both announce and preach, and pray that it be meanwhile local. For
those who interpret its examples otherwise will feel the universal and supreme
wrath in its own time. For that sun too in the assembly at Utica, with its
light almost extinguished, was so great a portent that it could not have
suffered this from an ordinary eclipse, placed in its own exaltation and
domicile. You have astrologers. We can equally also set before you the ends of
certain governors, who at the end of their life remembered that they had done
wrong because they had harassed Christians. Vigellius Saturninus, who first
here drove the sword against us, lost his sight. Claudius Lucius Herminianus
in Cappadocia, when, taking it ill that his wife had gone over to this sect,
he had treated Christians cruelly, and alone in his praetorium, wasted by
pestilence, he had boiled with worms at his banquets, used to say, Let no one
know, lest the Christians rejoice or Christian women take hope. Afterwards,
having recognized his error, that by torments he had made some fall away from
their purpose, he died almost a Christian. Caecilius Capella, in that fall of
By(??)mtinor, cried out, Christians, rejoice (??). But those who seem to
themselves to have borne it with impunity will come to the day of divine
judgment. For you also we wish that it was only a warning, that when you had
condemned Mavilus of Hadrumetum to the beasts, at once this harassment
followed, and (??)nc in the same cause a reckoning of blood. But remember for
the future.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

We do not terrify you, we who do not even fear, but I would wish that we may
be able to make all safe by warning μὴ θεομαχεῖν. You can both discharge the
duty of your jurisdiction and remember humanity, even because you too are
under the sword. For what more is commanded to you than to condemn the guilty
who have confessed, but to recall those who deny to the tortures? You see
therefore how you yourselves act against the mandates, in that you compel
those who have confessed to deny. So you confess that we are innocent, whom
you do not wish to condemn at once from the confession. But if you strive to
crush us, then you are already assaulting innocence. But how many governors
both more constant and more cruel have turned aside from causes of this kind?
as Cincius Severus, who at Thysdrus himself gave a remedy, how Christians
should answer, so that they could be dismissed: as Vespronius Candidus, who
dismissed a Christian, as if to satisfy his fellow-citizens would raise a
tumult: as Asper, who moderately harassed a man and immediately threw him
down, and did not even compel him to sacrifice, having first professed among
the advocates and assessors that he grieved to have fallen into this cause.
Pudens also dismissed a Christian sent to him in an indictment, once he
understood the extortion in it; he tore the same indictment, and denied that
he would hear the man without an accuser, according to the mandate. All these
things can be suggested to you both from your office and by the same
advocates, who themselves also have benefits of Christians, although they cry
out what they will. For both the notary of a certain man, when he was being
dashed down by a demon, was set free, and the kinsman of certain men and a
little boy, and how many honorable men (for we do not speak of the common)
have been healed either from demons or from illnesses? Even Severus himself,
the father of Antoninus, was mindful of Christians. For he also sought out
Proculus the Christian, who was surnamed Torpacion, procurator of Euhodia, who
had once cured him with oil, and had him in his palace until his death; whom
also Anto(??)s had known very well, having been brought up on Christian milk.
Severus, knowing most illustrious women and most illustrious men to be of this
sect, not only did not harm them, but even adorned them with testimony and
pala(??) resisted the people raging against us. Marcus Aurelius also in the
German expedition, by the prayers of Christian soldiers made to God, obtained
rains in that thirst.

When have droughts not also been driven away by our kneelings and fastings?
Then the people also, crying out Deus deorum, who alone is powerful, in
Jupiter's name bore witness to our God. Besides these things we do not deny a
deposit, we adulterate no one's marriage, we treat wards piously, we refresh
the needy, we return evil to no one for evil. Let those see to it who falsely
claim the sect, whom we ourselves also refuse. Who finally complains of us
under another name? What other business does a Christian suffer, except that
of his own sect, which no one has for so long a time proved incestuous or
cruel? For so great innocence, for so great honesty, for justice, for
chastity, for faith, for truth, for the living God we are burned, which
neither the sacrilegious nor true public enemies nor so many guilty of majesty
are accustomed to suffer. For even now this name is harassed by the governor
of Legio and by the governor of Mauritania, but as far as the sword, as also
from the beginning it was commanded that punishment be inflicted on such. But
greater contests, greater rewards.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Your cruelty is our glory. See only that we do not, by the very fact that we
endure such things, seem to break forth for this alone: to prove that we do
not fear them, but call them of our own accord. When Arrius Antoninus in Asia
was persecuting insistently, all the Christians of that city offered
themselves before his tribunals in a formed band. Then, after ordering a few
to be led away, he said to the rest, Ὦ δειλοὶ, εἰ θέλετε ἀποθνήσκειν, κρημνοὺς
ἢ βρόχους ἔχετε. If it should please that this be done here also, what will
you do with so many thousands of men, so many men and women, of every sex, of
every age, of every rank, offering themselves to you? How many fires, how many
swords will be needed? What will Carthage itself suffer, to be decimated by
you, when each one shall have recognized there his kinsmen, his companions,
when he shall have seen there perhaps also men of your order and matrons and
all the principal persons and either kinsmen or friends of your friends? Spare
yourself therefore, if not us. Spare Carthage, if not yourself. Spare the
province, which, once your intention has been seen, has been made liable to
the extortions both of the soldiers and of the enemies of each. We have no
master, except God alone. He is before you and cannot be hidden, but one to
whom you can do nothing. For the rest, those whom you think masters for
yourself are men, and they too will die in time. Nor yet will this sect fail,
which you should know is then more built up, when it seems to be cut down. For
whoever, looking at so great endurance, as if struck by some scruple, is both
kindled to inquire what is in the cause, and when he has learned the truth,
himself also follows at once.
