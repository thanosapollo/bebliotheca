* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The paternal stock of Titus Aurelius Fulvus Boionius Antoninus Pius came from
Transalpine Gaul, from Nemausus, that is.

His grandfather was Titus Aurelius Fulvus, who through various honors reached
a second consulship and the prefecture of the city.

His father was Aurelius Fulvus, who was himself consul, a stern and upright
man. His maternal grandmother was Boionia Procilla, his mother Arria Fadilla,
his maternal grandfather Arrius Antoninus, twice consul, a holy man and one
who had pitied Nerva because he had begun to rule. His sister of the same
mother was Julia Fadilla, his stepfather Julius Lupus of consular rank, his
father-in-law Annius Verus, his wife Annia Faustina. There were two sons and
two daughters, a son-in-law through the elder daughter, Lamia Silanus, and
through the younger, Marcus Antoninus.

Antoninus Pius himself was born on the thirteenth day before the Kalends of
October, in the consulship of Flavius Domitian for the twelfth time and of
Cornelius Dolabella, at a villa at Lanuvium. He was reared at Lorium on the
Aurelian Way, where later he built a palace whose remains still stand today.
He spent his boyhood with his paternal grandfather, then with his maternal
grandfather, honoring all his own with religious care, and so far was he
enriched by the inheritances of cousins and of his stepfather and of many
kinsmen.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

He was a man of striking form, famous for his mind, gentle in manners, noble
of countenance, calm in temper, of singular eloquence, of polished letters,
especially sober, a careful tiller of the soil, mild, generous, keeping his
hands from what was another's, and all this with measure and without
boastfulness; in all things, lastly, praiseworthy, and one who by the judgment
of good men is justly compared to Numa Pompilius.

He was surnamed Pius by the senate, either because he supported with his hand
his father-in-law, already weary with age, in the presence of the senate
(which indeed is not proof enough of great piety, since he is more impious who
does not do such things than pious who pays what is due), or because he saved
those whom Hadrian in his ill health had ordered to be killed, or because he
decreed to Hadrian after death, against the wishes of all, boundless and
immense honors, or because, when Hadrian wished to make away with himself, by
great watch and care he made it so that he could not do it, or because he was
in truth by nature most clement and did nothing harsh in his own times. He
also practiced lending at a third of the usual interest, that is at the lowest
rates, that he might help very many from his own estate.

He was a generous quaestor, a splendid praetor, consul with Catilius Severus.
In all his private life he lived most often in the fields, but he was
distinguished in every place. By Hadrian he was chosen among the four men of
consular rank to whom Italy was entrusted, to govern that part of Italy in
which he possessed most, so that Hadrian might provide both for the honor and
for the quiet of so great a man.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

To him, while he governed Italy, an omen of empire was given, for when he had
mounted the tribunal, among other acclamations it was said, /Augustus, may the
gods keep you./

He administered the proconsulship of Asia in such a way that he alone
surpassed his grandfather.

In his proconsulship he also received an omen of empire thus: for when a
priestess at Tralles, who by custom always saluted the proconsuls by that
name, did not say /Hail, proconsul,/ but /Hail, emperor./ At Cyzicus also a
crown was transferred from the image of a god to his statue.

And after his consulship, in a garden a marble bull was hung by its horns from
the growing branches of a tree, and lightning from a clear sky came into his
house without harm, and in Etruria jars which had been buried were found above
the ground, and a swarm of bees filled his statues throughout all Etruria, and
he was often warned in a dream to set an image of Hadrian among his household
gods.

Setting out for his proconsulship he lost his elder daughter. Of his wife many
things were said because of too much freedom and ease of living, which he
checked with pain of heart. After the proconsulship he lived often at Rome in
Hadrian's councils, always showing the milder opinion on all things about
which Hadrian consulted.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

The manner of his adoption is reported thus: when Helius Verus had died, whom
Hadrian had adopted and named Caesar, a sitting of the senate was being held;
thither Arrius Antoninus came, supporting the steps of his father-in-law, and
for that reason he is said to have been adopted by Hadrian. Which cause alone
of the adoption neither could have been at all nor ought to have been,
especially since Antoninus had always administered the republic well and in
his proconsulship had shown himself holy and grave.

Therefore when Hadrian had made public that he wished to adopt him, a space
for deliberation was granted, whether he wished to be arrogated by Hadrian.
The law of the adoption was given of this kind, that just as Antoninus was
being adopted by Hadrian, so he should adopt for himself M. Antoninus, the son
of his wife's brother, and L. Verus, the son of Helius Verus who had been
adopted by Hadrian, who afterwards was called Verus Antoninus. He was adopted
on the fifth day before the Kalends of March, giving thanks in the senate that
Hadrian had so thought of him, and he was made colleague of his father both in
the proconsular power and in the tribunician power. This is reported first of
him, that when he was reproached by his wife as if he were giving too little,
I know not what, to his own, he said:

/Foolish woman, after we have passed over to the empire, even that
which we had before we have lost./

He gave a congiarium to the people from his own means, and those things which
his father had promised, and he contributed very much to Hadrian's works, and
the crown-gold which had been offered because of his adoption he restored in
full to the Italians, and half to the provincials.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

And while his father lived he obeyed him most religiously, but when Hadrian
had died at Baiae he brought his remains to Rome with holiness and reverence
and placed them in the gardens of Domitia; even against the resistance of all
he enrolled him among the gods. He allowed his wife Faustina to be called
Augusta by the senate. He received the name of Pius. He gladly accepted
statues decreed to his father and mother and grandparents and brothers already
dead. He did not refuse circus games dedicated to his birthday, other honors
being declined. He set up a most magnificent shield to Hadrian and established
priests.

Made emperor, he gave a successor to none of those whom Hadrian had advanced,
and he was of such constancy that he kept good governors in the provinces for
seven and nine years. Through his legates he waged very many wars, for he also
conquered the Britons through Lollius Urbicus the legate, another wall of turf
being drawn when the barbarians had been driven back, and he forced the Moors
to ask for peace, and he crushed the Germans and the Dacians and many peoples
and the Jews in revolt through governors and legates; in Achaia also and in
Egypt he repressed rebellions.

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

He often checked the Alans when they were stirring. He ordered his procurators
both to receive tributes with moderation and, if they exceeded measure, to
render an account of their deeds; nor was he ever gladdened by any profit by
which a provincial was oppressed. He willingly heard those who complained
against his procurators.

For those whom Hadrian had condemned he sought pardons in the senate, saying
that Hadrian himself would have done this. He brought the imperial height down
to the highest civility, whence he grew the more, against the will of the
court servants, who, since he did nothing through go-betweens, could neither
frighten men at any time nor sell those things which were not secret. As
emperor he deferred to the senate as much as, when he was a private man, he
had wished another prince to defer to him. The name of Father of the
Fatherland, offered by the senate, which he had at first put off, he received
with great thanksgiving. In the third year of his reign he lost his wife
Faustina, who was consecrated by the senate, circus games being granted and a
temple and priestesses and statues of gold and of silver; and he himself also
granted this, that her image should be set at all circus games. He received a
golden statue offered by the senate and set it up. He made M. Antoninus
quaestor and consul at the senate's request. Annius Verus, who afterwards was
called Antoninus, he designated quaestor before the time. He decided nothing
concerning the provinces nor concerning any acts unless he had first referred
it to his friends, and from their opinion he composed the forms. He was indeed
seen by his friends both in private dress and doing certain household things.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

With such diligence he ruled the peoples subject to him that he cared for all
things and all persons as if they were his own. All the provinces flourished
under him. Informers for fourfold penalties were extinguished. Confiscation of
goods was rarer than it had ever been, so that only one man was proscribed as
guilty of aspiring to tyranny, that is Atilius Titianus, the senate punishing
him; from him he forbade that accomplices be sought, and his son was always
helped in all things. Priscianus also perished, accused of aspiring to
tyranny, but by a willing death; concerning that conspiracy he forbade
inquiry.

The manner of living of Antoninus Pius was such that there was richness
without blame, thrift without squalor, and his table was furnished by his own
slaves, by his own fowlers, fishermen, and hunters. The bath which he had used
he offered to the people without charge, and he changed nothing at all of the
quality of his private life. He took salaries from many whom he saw receiving
them in idleness, saying that nothing was more sordid, nay more cruel, than if
he should gnaw the republic who contributed nothing to it by his own labor.
Whence also he lessened the salary of Mesomedes the lyric poet. He knew
especially well the accounts of all the provinces and of the revenues. He
conferred his private estate upon his daughter, but he gave the fruits to the
republic. Superfluous imperial furnishings and estates he sold, and he lived
on his own farms variously and according to the seasons. He undertook no
expeditions, except that he went to his own fields and to Campania, saying
that the prince's retinue was a burden to the provincials, even if too
sparing. And yet he had immense authority among all nations, since he sat in
the city for this reason, that being in the middle he might more quickly
receive messengers from all sides.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

He gave a congiarium to the people, he added a donative to the soldiers, he
established alimentary girls in honor of Faustina, called Faustinianae. These
works of his remain: at Rome the temple of Hadrian, dedicated to the honor of
his father, the Graecostadium restored after a fire, the Amphitheatre
repaired, the tomb of Hadrian, the temple of Agrippa, the Sublician Bridge,
the restoration of the Pharos, the harbor of Caieta, the restoration of the
harbor of Tarracina, the bath at Ostia, the aqueduct of Antium, the temples at
Lanuvium. He also helped many cities with money, that they might make new
works or restore old ones, so that he helped both magistrates and senators of
the city toward their functions.

He refused the inheritances of those who had children. He first established
that a bequest left for the sake of a penalty should not stand. He gave a
successor to no living good judge except to Orfitus, prefect of the city, and
that at his request. For Gavius Maximus, prefect of the praetorian guard,
reached even to the twentieth year under him, a most severe man, whom Tattius
Maximus succeeded; in whose place when he had died he substituted two
prefects, Fabius Cornelius Repentinus and Furius Victorinus. But Repentinus
was struck by a notorious tale, that he had come to the prefecture through the
prince's concubine. So far was it that under him no senator was struck down,
that even a confessed parricide was placed on a desert island, because by the
laws of nature it was not permitted him to live. A scarcity of wine, oil, and
wheat he allayed at the loss of his own treasury, by buying and giving freely
to the people.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

These adversities came about in his times: the famine of which we have spoken,
the collapse of the Circus, an earthquake by which the towns of the Rhodians
and of Asia fell, all of which he restored wonderfully, and a fire at Rome
which consumed three hundred and forty blocks or houses, and the city of Narbo
and the town of Antioch and the forum of Carthage burned. There was also a
flood of the Tiber, a hairy star appeared, a two-headed boy was born, and from
one birth of a woman five boys were brought forth. In Arabia a crested serpent
larger than usual was seen, which ate itself from the tail to the middle.
There was also a plague in Arabia. Barley grew in Moesia on the tops of trees.
Four tame lions besides offered themselves of their own accord to be taken in
Arabia.

King Pharasmanes came to him at Rome and paid him more honor than he had paid
Hadrian. He gave Pacorus as king to the Lazi. He drove back the king of the
Parthians from the storming of the Armenians by letters alone. By his
authority alone he induced King Abgarus to withdraw from the regions of the
East. He settled royal causes. The royal throne which the king of the
Parthians asked back, which Trajan had taken, he utterly refused. Rhoemetalces
he sent back into the Bosporan kingdom, after hearing the affair between him
and the curator. He sent help to the Olbiopolites against the Tauroscythians
into Pontus and so far defeated the Tauroscythians that they had to give
hostages to the Olbiopolites. No one had so much authority among foreign
nations, though he always loved peace, even to this point, that he often used
Scipio's saying, in which that man said he would rather save one citizen than
kill a thousand enemies.

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

The senate decreed that the months of September and October should be called
Antoninus and Faustinus, but Antoninus rejected this. The marriage of his
daughter Faustina, when he joined her to Marcus Antoninus, he made most
celebrated, even to a donative of the soldiers. Verus Antoninus he made consul
after the quaestorship. When he had called Apollonius, whom he had summoned
from Chalcis, to the Tiberian house in which he was living, that he might give
Marcus Antoninus over to him, and that man had said

/the master ought not to come to the pupil, but the pupil to the master,/

he laughed at him, saying,

/it was easier for Apollonius to come from Chalcis to Rome than from
his own house to the Palace,/

whose greed even in fees he noted. Among the proofs of his piety this also is
held, that when Marcus was weeping for his dead tutor and was being called
away by the court servants from a display of piety, he himself said:

/Permit him, he said, to be a man; for neither philosophy nor empire
takes away affections./

He both enriched his prefects and presented them with consular ornaments. If
he condemned any for extortion, he restored the paternal goods to their
children, yet on this condition, that they should restore to the provincials
what their parents had received. He was most ready toward pardons. Games were
given in which he exhibited elephants and corocottae and tigers and
rhinoceroses, crocodiles also and hippopotami and all things from the whole
world. He also put forth a hundred lions with tigers in one release.

* Caput 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He used his friends in his empire no otherwise than as a private man, because
they themselves never joined with the freedmen to sell empty hopes about him;
inasmuch as he used his own freedmen most severely. He loved the arts of
actors. He delighted himself much with fishing and hunting and with walking
with friends and conversation. He kept the vintage in the manner of a private
man with his friends. To rhetors and philosophers through all the provinces he
conferred both honors and salaries. Most have said that the speeches which are
carried under his name are another's; Marius Maximus says they were his own.
He shared both private and public banquets with his friends, and he made no
sacrifice through a deputy, except when he was ill. When he sought honors for
himself and his sons, he did all things as if a private man. He himself also
frequented the banquets of his friends. Among other things this also is a
chief proof of his civility, that when, visiting Homullus's house and
wondering at the porphyry columns, he had asked whence he had them, and
Homullus had said to him,

/when you have come into another's house, be both mute and deaf,/

he bore it patiently. Of this Homullus he always received many jests
patiently.

* Caput 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

He ordained many things concerning the law and used the jurists Vindius Verus,
Salvius Valens, Volusius Maecianus, Ulpius Marcellus, and Diavolenus.
Seditions made anywhere he crushed not by cruelty but by moderation and
gravity. He forbade the dead to be buried within cities. He established the
expense for gladiatorial games. He lightened the vehicular post with the
highest diligence. Of all things that he did, both in the senate and by
edicts, he rendered an account.

He died in his seventieth year, but was missed as if a youth. His death,
however, is related to have been of this kind: when at dinner he had eaten
Alpine cheese too greedily, in the night he vomited it up and on another day
he was shaken with fever. On the third day, when he saw himself growing heavy,
he commended the republic and his daughter to Marcus Antoninus in the presence
of the prefects, and he ordered the golden Fortune, which used to be placed in
the bedchamber of the princes, to be transferred to him; he gave the tribune
the watchword of equanimity, and so turning as if he were sleeping, he gave up
his spirit at Lorium. Wandering in the fever he spoke of nothing else than of
the republic and of those kings with whom he was angry. He left his private
estate to his daughter, but by his will he followed all his own with fitting
bequests.

* Caput 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

He was handsome with a tall stature, but since he was tall and an old man and
would stoop, he was bound with linden boards placed on his breast, that he
might walk upright. Even as an old man, before the morning callers came, he
ate dry bread to sustain his strength. He had a voice hoarse and sonorous with
pleasantness.

He was called divine by the senate, all striving in rivalry, when all praised
his piety, clemency, mind, and holiness. All honors were also decreed which
had before been conferred on the best princes. He merited also a flamen and
circus games and a temple and the Antoninian sodales, and he alone of almost
all princes lived wholly without civil blood and without enemy blood, so far
as it pertains to himself, and one who may rightly be compared to Numa, whose
happiness and piety and security and ceremonies he always obtained.
