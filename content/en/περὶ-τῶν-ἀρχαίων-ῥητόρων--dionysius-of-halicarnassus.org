* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

It would be just to feel much gratitude toward our own time, most excellent
Ammaeus, both because certain other pursuits are now practiced more finely
than before, and not least because the care of political speeches has made
no small advance toward the better. For in the times before us the ancient
and philosophic rhetoric, being insulted and enduring terrible outrages, was
being destroyed, having begun from the death of Alexander of Macedon to gasp
and wither little by little, and in our own generation having come within a
little of vanishing completely; but another, having come into her place,
intolerable in theatrical shamelessness and ill-bred and sharing in neither
philosophy nor any other liberal discipline, having stolen upon and deceived
the ignorance of the crowds, not only lived in greater abundance and luxury
and splendor than the other, but also attached to herself the honors and
the leadership of the cities, which the philosophic one ought to have had,
and she was altogether vulgar and annoying and in the end made Greece become
like the houses of the prodigal and ill-fortuned. For just as in those
houses the free and temperate wife sits, being mistress of none of her own
things, while some foolish courtesan, present for the ruin of the household,
claims to rule all the property, treating the other with contempt and
terrifying her; in the same way in every city, and no less in the
well-educated ones (for this is the worst of all evils), the Attic Muse,
ancient and native, had taken on a dishonored form, fallen from her own
goods, while she who arrived yesterday and the day before from certain pits
of Asia, some Mysian or Phrygian woman or some Carian evil, a barbarian,
claimed to administer Greek cities, having driven the other from public
affairs, the ignorant one the philosophic, and the mad one the temperate.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But indeed not only is /time the best savior of just men/ according to
Pindar, but also, by Zeus, of arts and of pursuits and of every other worthy
thing. And our own time has shown, whether some god began it or a natural
cycle revolving the ancient order or a human impulse leading many toward the
like, and it has granted to the ancient and temperate rhetoric to recover
the just honor which she also formerly had well, and to the new and foolish
one to cease enjoying a reputation that does not belong to her and
luxuriating in others' goods. And it is perhaps not for this one thing only
that the present time and the men who share in philosophy deserve praise,
that they began to make the better things more honored than the worse (and
yet a beginning is said to be, and is, half of the whole), but also that it
arranged for their change to be swift and their advance great. For except
for some few Asian cities, in which through ignorance the learning of what
is fine is slow, the rest have ceased loving the vulgar and frigid and
insensible speeches, those who formerly prided themselves greatly on them
now being ashamed and little by little deserting to the others, unless some
are altogether incurable, while those newly taking hold of the study bring
those speeches into contempt and make the zeal for them a matter of
laughter.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And the cause, I think, and beginning of so great a change was Rome, ruling
all, compelling all the cities to look toward her, and of this city itself
those in power who administer public affairs according to virtue and from
the best, being altogether well-educated and noble in their judgments, by
whom the prudent part of the city, being adorned, has advanced still more,
and the foolish part has been compelled to have sense. Therefore many
histories worthy of serious attention are being written by those now living,
and many graceful political speeches are being published, and philosophical
treatises not, by Zeus, contemptible, and many other fine works, very
carefully pursued by both Romans and Greeks, have come forth and will come
forth, as is likely. And I would not wonder, such a change having taken
place in this short time, if that zeal for foolish speeches will no longer
proceed further than one generation; for what has been contracted from
everything into the least is easily, from a little, not even to be.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

But as for giving thanks to the time that is changing affairs, and praising
those who choose the best things, and inferring the future from what has
happened, and all things like these, which even a chance person could say, I
will leave them aside, and I will try to say those things from which the
better may still take greater strength, taking a subject of the discourse
that is common and humane and able to benefit in very many ways. And it is
this: who are the most noteworthy of the ancient orators and writers, and
what were their choices of both life and speeches, and what one ought to
take or guard against from each, fine observations and necessary for those
practicing political philosophy, and not, by Zeus, common or worn by those
before. I at least know that I have encountered no such writing, having made
much search for them. I do not, however, assert it as one who knows clearly;
for perhaps there may be some such writings that have escaped me, and to
make oneself the limit of the history of all things and to say that
something of the things possible to have happened has not happened is
altogether arrogant and not far from madness. About these things, then, I
have nothing, as I said, to assert. And of the orators and writers, about
whom the discourse is, being very many and good, seeing that to write about
all of them requires a long discourse I will let it go, and selecting the
most graceful of them I will speak about each according to their ages, now
about the orators, and if it is possible, also about the historians. And the
orators taken up will be three from the older ones, Lysias, Isocrates,
Isaeus, and three from those who flourished after them, Demosthenes,
Hyperides, Aeschines, whom I consider the strongest of the others, and the
treatise will be divided into two compositions, and it will take its
beginning from this one written about the older ones. These things having
been said beforehand, it is time to return to the matters at hand.
