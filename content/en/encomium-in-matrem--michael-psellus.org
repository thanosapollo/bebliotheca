and preparing the infant with the slips. And she who wore the fiction of
temperance also shared the midwifery with them, and the sister rested more
upon her than upon the midwives. About which one of those, having become
jealous: "But from this one," she says, "your hard labor is; for it is not
allowed for pregnant women to give the hands to those in pangs. For this
perhaps the women's quarters legislate." And the sister: "And who then," she
says, "among you here is with child?" And she immediately pointed that woman
out and, taking hold of the tunic, showed forth the belly. Here the sister had
almost expired, and, having forgotten the pangs, she was torn in soul by
unspeakable pains at the word; but not even so had she left the noble spirit,
but she commanded her at once to flee to the ends of the earth, and for
herself immediately the birth was timely and "the infant leapt forth." This
one nature sprouted paradoxically with the first "beauty of the season," and
death took her more paradoxically, having left even in the image the
impossible fairness and not having stripped it from there, I think, not having
been able.
15. I then at that time, it having so happened, was living in the fields
before the city, having gone away a little with a certain man of those very
noble about letters, entrusted to judge not a small part of the western
section. I then first went out of the city and have seen the wall that fences
it, and one might say also the whole open country, being sixteen years old and
having an age greater than the time, just released from hearing the poems and
having peeked into the art of words with grace. And upon my sister, as the
parents later said, a terrible disease strikes about the inward parts and the
liver was at once ulcerous underneath, then also swelling, and the whole body
was being burned with a hidden fire and nature was giving way, yielding to the
one tyrannizing and, not yet consumed by the wasting, she had died still upon
a blooming and well-fleshed body. As many things then as had immediately
happened from those who had begotten at the passion the word will show later,
but for now let it hold to what follows. When then my sister had perished
"before the season" and the event could not be undone for the parents, they
feared no less over me lest, struck by the unexpectedness of the hearing, I
should join a passion to their passion, a greatest no less; for they knew our
breathing-together and that with the part perished the whole almost


is destroyed together. They wished then to "take me in hand" and to surround
me with a circle of words, and then thus to reveal the sting of the blow
itself, whence they also sent me ahead a letter reproaching the longest lack
of communication toward them and mocking the neglect about letters, and that I
do not cease as one beginning, but for one who has leapt sharply from the
starting-line the breath of the course quickly fails, and they added also
something of the customary things about the sister, by which I had often been
hunted. With these then they turn me back toward them. And they thought the
device escaped notice, but I, having been besieged from another side, have
been captured, and before the fair deceit the truth brought me down. And how,
the word will say. I happened then already to have entered the wall and to
have come at that part where the body of the sister had been buried; and she
happened to be at the seventh day from the burial and many of the family were
present both to mourn the one lying and to console the mother. There then
having met one of the relatives, "a man" simple "and noble," who knew neither
the deceit nor that, hunted by those who had begotten, I am being dragged, I
asked both about the father and about those otherwise belonging to me. And he,
having woven or covered nothing of the true: "The father," he says, "is
rendering the funeral laments to his daughter, and the mother is even now
present upon him, inconsolably, as you know, occupied about the disaster." He
then said these things, and I do not know what I had suffered; for as if
burned through with divine fire, I had become dry and speechless. I slipped
then even from the seat about the horse and the rumor about me took the
parents' hearing. Another lament then had been raised and a weeping over me
much more vehement than the former; for upon a kindled fire even a little fuel
thrown under raises the flame and "fans the breath" and doubles the excess of
the conflagration. For when they perceived they ran at me as if mad, and then
first the mother published her beauty, having despised the sight of males.
Coming down then upon me as I lay and taking hold, one from one place and
another from another, they called me back with their own laments; then indeed
also having led me around half-dead they give me to the tomb of the sister.
But O, how shall I even now hold the tear narrating, and how shall I be able
to weave out the word, having been seized in soul by a mist? I will speak
then, as I am able, strengthening myself with better reasonings. When then
having opened the eyes I saw the tomb of the sister and had recognized the
passion and gathered the thinking, having fallen suddenly I poured the
channels of the tears upon the one lying as "libations on the tomb," "O sweet
to me," saying, "not sister only but even if some other name more kindred or
more sympathetic. O impossible beauty, incomparable nature, unmatched virtue,
'living statue,' goad of persuasion, 'siren of words, grace' invincible, O all
things to me and more than soul. But how have you gone having left the
brother, how have you been torn from the one grown-together, how do you even
endure the solitude for long? O, what station received you, what lodging
having obtained rests you, what meadows, what graces? What paradise charms
you? And what is that beauty which you have preferred to my company and sight?
What flower draws you away, what rose-beds, what gushing water? What
nightingales, what cicadas sending a sweet voice? And the beauty of your body,
has nature, having taken it, guard and treasure it, or has the dust consumed
it and the lamp of the eyes been quenched for you and the flower upon the lip
vanished and the composition dissolved and the combination unloosed, or does
your beauty still remain kept in the tomb as a treasury? If then the spirit
that has gone from you has wholly turned toward God and you have nowhere an
account of the things here nor a remembrance, hold to the contemplation there
and the illumination. I do not blame you the unsharing, I do not


reproach the uncommunicating, the participation is above our nature; but if
you have bent even a little also toward the visible, do not grudge us the
sharing upon you, have it having taken and communicate to me of the lot. And
if even here I shall be seen immoderate, yet the tomb at least will not divide
me from your body, but of the dust of both having been dissolved I shall share
with you the co-dissolution even in the worse."
16. These things then and others more passionate than these I wailed at the
tomb of the sister; and the parents groaned, all but dripping drops of bloods
from the eyes. And the whole multitude that had run together also lamented
with them and such was the excess of the mourning that all were almost brought
down by the passion and the symphony of the wailings was reduced to
speechlessness. With difficulty then the parents having torn me from the tomb:
"But pity us," they said, "sweetest inward part, keep for us the remainder of
the breath, destroy no longer to the end, nor let anyone charge you with our
destruction. Yes, by these 'gray hairs that reared you,' by our souls, which
indeed by the breath upon you have been held to these bodies." And they thus,
but for me the lament ended again into lament. For when having seen I had
recognized the mother, having put on a black cloak and having dishonored
herself on every side with "coarse rags," I did not know even what I should
become, but I had almost also perished. For the things concerning her had come
about in this manner. The sister "was being loosed" and "was breathing the
last," having set the head in the mother's bosom, and neither "the soul having
been committed" nor the tongue having been moved nor indeed the breath having
failed, she "paid" naturally and unforced "the debt" of nature to those who
exact it, and the mother, having somehow strengthened herself then, closed the
eyelids and having been "suddenly filled with tears," or rather having been
"wholly burned with unspeakable fire" and having groaned and wept the funeral
things, when she also saw the husband moved by the rush of the disaster, for
he was otherwise also prone toward such passions and I know the character in
the forms of my own soul; when then she saw him so having, having briefly
ordered the soul not to be confused by the passion, O noble and unfloodable
mind and unstormed even in such seas, she philosophizes something higher than
the many and more powerful than the occasion, and having unrolled many words
to the husband about the better change, having taken the passion at once also
as advocate toward the persuasion, she hunts beyond hopes. And she does not
postpone the time, but immediately sacrifices the first-fruits of the transfer
to the Better and changes the dress and shears the hairs and wears the cloak
still having the prime in bloom, not yet having cast off the flower, still
"leafy in foliage," not yet having the petal wrinkled, still growing "the
tender branch," still "sprouting fruits." And the father also agreed with the
things said and thus they carry the sister out upon the burial still being
shone upon by the beauty, still showing forth the bright grace of the face.
They said then that when they had combed out the coils of her hairs and those
who had adorned the corpse hung them from the ends of the bier, for she was
otherwise golden and abundant of hair, and the carrying-out "was aloft" and
visible to those from three-storied houses, there is no one who, having turned
the eye toward her, either had not gone off the horse or had not come down
from the three-story; all then, men and women, followed and shared the dirge
in common. Such for the mother the transfer toward the better life, this for
her the occasion "of the monastic life," this passion transferred her toward
that passionless life, this death becomes for her "a procurer of immortality."
And of what sort also the things after these? For not once


"having planted the step" in the better life and having entered upon the
divine course and "having gone down into the spiritual stadium" was she
confused in soul having seen the theater being filled from angels and humans,
which indeed even the more of the males still suffer, and some turn back from
the very start, others dissolve from the road and depart uncrowned. But having
provided also that the tomb of the sister be dug there where she was going to
be without going out, for it still remains and will remain for philosophic
souls a training-place toward virtue, she shuts herself in there, immediately
having bid farewell both to house and life, and one might say even to "blood
and flesh," except so far as she administered what belonged in a fair way and
provisioned herself with these toward the life to which she was being sent.
And since she had not yet purely "been consecrated to God" nor had the things
of the change been completed under a spiritual leader, she deliberates
something again here more intelligent or wiser; she pre-sacrifices the father
to God and "gives" him again "the privileges" also of the other life. And when
she saw the consecration of the member, she gives over the part of the
whole-burnt-offering to the Better and "the first mystery of the ascent is
completed."
17. And from here I do not know with what words I shall praise her. For just
as if some noble body, having been turned on a lathe into a circle, then
should be moved upon a level place, shows even there the good running and easy
rolling by the fitness of the shape, but when it touches sloping or
precipitous ground, immediately the whole is carried with unrestrainable
motion toward the "downward slope," so then also my mother, being even first
easy to lead in soul toward the better and well-wheeled toward the fair
through the fitness of nature, when she had passed over toward the spiritual
life, which is downward-sloping toward virtue and easy to lead, immediately
with unhindered motion she pressed toward the end of the descent. For no
longer being divided in body at once and spirit, matter and immateriality,
clay and holy earth, nor being split between world-ruler and God, but having
transferred with her every desire, and every longing, to God and having
inclined with them, she was carried toward him with all the powers of the
soul. For immediately she subtracts the flesh and takes down the bulk and
"empties the body," the bond of the soul, and despises every "economy of
nature," and she gathers the soul about herself and "contracts it from the
senses" and subjects to it every desire, and enslaves all the irrational,
destroys outlandish imagination and alien reasoning and "irrational opinion"
and matter-loving sense and dissolving thought, and she lives with mind alone
and uses this as an organ toward "the divine ascent." You ought then to have
been spectators, but not hearers, of her fair things; for so I would have been
released from suspicion and the true would have been seen by you. But now I
have been able to say neither the whole, and even in the brief, of her fair
things <and> I shall be suspected by the many. For as if ashamed that she had
even come down into a body, which is rumored concerning one of those who
philosophized among the Greeks, she showed forth a little part of the face,
and a little of the hands, and no part of her was seen naked, and the skin was
philosophized for her not only as immediately conversing with the body, but
also as being added from outside; and at night she slept upon this alone. She
wished then not even to nourish the body at all, but as some fetter of the
soul to wear it away and make it loose. But since it was not possible to fight
nature completely, she at least nobly resisted the occasion and subtracted
from the measure so much that the measureless had not even a measure. And
approaching God, whether she was immediately snatched up I do not much know,
but so she seemed to all; for she had not been turned, nor had any of her been
moved, not head, not hands, not feet, nor any part of the covering, but she
was as the shadow in boards, changing only so much as the wearying eye
thought.


And she was recognized as living by the groans and the posture. And she often
philosophized to me about the matter, that prayer is a meeting with the Better
and the one meeting must not have been changed, until the conversing has
withdrawn; and she called the unfitness of the soul a withdrawal of the
Divine. "For one must not," she says, "say that that is stretched or
contracted, but it is undeparting from itself, and stands everywhere as light,
as air, as some other of the common and exposed things, as a sounding trumpet.
And those see this and perceive it sounding whose 'eye of the soul' is fitly
disposed toward the seeing; and the sense of the sounding is well-hearing
toward the echo. But those who seeing do not see again or 'hearing do not
hear,' the tension both of the seeing and of the hearing has failed them; for
with the matter the mind perceives the immaterial, whence wherever it should
be separated, this purely both saw the visible and had heard the audible; but
when the matter has been mixed into it it immediately loses the fitness. Thus
then many seemed to hear voices and to perceive an echo, having received a
divinized soul and one bacchic in a temperate way. We must therefore keep the
prayer with unbending and unchanging body. Since not every soul has sunk with
the bodily skiff, but so much of it as was mixed with fleshes ended into
sense, and the other part floats above as a surface-sailor, like a topmost
pendant, a cork as it were light, not dragged down with the nets; this then
some other angelic mind from outside turns toward itself and leads up toward
God." This was her spiritual in truth and divine wisdom, which she was
initiated in from above, which she initiated us below.
18. But about the mother I will speak again, and the word looks again toward
the father. Who was otherwise graceful both in the body and in the soul, but
when he was also "deemed worthy of the rite" and of the ascent and elevation
toward the better, he became still more beautiful and more graceful. He seemed
then released from some bundle and precisely winged and free, having become as
some living dedication to God. He went about then having the soul full of
unmixed pleasure, and: "If some other," he often said, "'kingdom of the
heavens' has been stored up for those who have pleased God, God altogether
knows and 'the one who spoke is unlying.' But even the life which those who
are directed toward him live does not seem to me at all to fall short of the
other and first. For my soul at least has not been baptized together with the
body as before, but as in the bounds of divine precincts it dances and
rejoices. And now its immortality has not been calculated by me, but has been
contemplated. For it has been released from the body and stands, and has been
separated from the connatural, not the activity only, but also the essence.
And it has not been loosed so much as the one who bound it has willed; for the
time being then it purely turns back upon itself and has been turned toward
mind and through mind toward God." Philosophizing these things that man also
dissolved for me the many of the "un-syllogized syllogisms" and gave immediate
intellection, stretching forth from mind and concluding at once and not
needing the middle error. I saw him then becoming other from other and being
raised to the highest by the "anagogic powers" and singing the steps from
Babylon and being led up into Jerusalem. Not much time had yet gone through
and I went to him having been summoned and as I entered: "But now," he said,
"little child, that joyful thing is not quite rooted in me, but some mist has
suddenly wandered over the soul and I know that this has run upon me from the
worse natures. For this is their portion," and he added philosophizing more
about this, what it does, how it enters the soul, and the rest both of their
nature and of the precise observation of those who have experienced, "but I
cannot thrust off the siege, but as if walled-in the soul


lurks within and slips down toward the depth." These things he. And I, having
used eloquence then as needed and having spoken the ways according to which
the spirits run down upon us, and we conquer by turning, and having taken away
his confusion, I settled the soul. And: "O sweetest inward part," he said,
"why have you not feasted me in return, having entertained me with such words?
And how sweet also the thing, father and son living together philosophically
and sharing common salt." "But not as one unwilling, O father, do you guest
me. I have not been released at all, even if you yourself had not invited me
upon your hospitality." The father smiled a little toward the word and: "If
there are some," he said, "animals that receive the offspring again when
troubled into the natural bosoms, I have heard from those who inquire; I at
least would gladly have set you even in the inward parts, having torn them
open." Having then feasted in common with the father that day, when the sun
had long come toward setting, having embraced him with many greetings I
slipped out of the training-place and departed, leaving him bright. But I
myself was not quite cheerful, whence it seemed good to me to go away toward
the mother and to use for her a drug toward the passion of the soul. And she
then received me as if a traveler both joyfully and persuaded me to rest there
for the night.
19. But not yet "midnight" and someone was making a noise at the courtyard
door and the doorkeeper, having known who it was, comes in agitation to the
mother and me; for already the time was calling her toward the dawn hymn.
Coming then: "Your father, my fairest youth," nodding toward me, "having been
suddenly brought down in the evening is being heated with a violent fever."
Immediately then I went toward the father, and she followed "at the foot."
Having entered then the little house we had seen him being burned with fire
without artifice, held by thick panting and showing precisely an inflammation
in the inward parts. And when I also judged the motions of the artery with the
fingers, for the art about this had been studied by me from far off, I had
immediately given up. For the motion had failed the part, so that it moved a
little or rather undulated according to the motions of ants. "Dry then and
struck" I stood not knowing whether to bewail him or to console the mother or
to lament myself. And he had also known me to be troubled and taking me with
the right arm: "I, O child, am going away," he said, "the appointed journey
and which the Word has defined; but you, both exhort yourself not to lament
and become a sufficient consolation to the mother." Then having "held a brief
time" and "opened the eyes" suddenly and whispered something unclearly, at
once he let the eyelids to the eyes and had expired, having committed the soul
to God, and having left me to the license of laments. For since none of those
who had trained with him was yet present, having fallen upon his breast and
kissing the heart: "O father," I said, and this often and again I held; for
the breath was not gathered for me. Then indeed, having gathered and having
panted something deep: "O father," I cried again, "where ever have you gone so
suddenly? How have you flown off having left the son? O sudden dichotomy, O
bitter division. Still the echo of your words before the evening sounds in my
hearing, still we seem to philosophize and to converse about soul. What ever
was that dart that wounded your soul so suddenly? Who sent that spear upon the
inward parts? Who poured into you that death-bearing venom? And I thought I
was drawing out the arrow, perhaps I even tore open the wound of the dart,
taking away the point, perhaps I scraped your blow; and you seemed like one
who had changed and you brought up the soul. Woe is me of those departing


words and of the sending-forth kisses. And you perhaps, O father, were giving
us the last embraces, being about to set out and to be sent toward a long
absence from home, but I thought these were an ambition of nature suddenly
relaxed into affection, as indeed many things are accustomed. I do not know
where I should turn, how I shall bring down the swelling of the soul. Shall I
first console the mother or shall I receive her consoling? You then, O father,
have gone the truly fair journey, or rather you have been sent toward heaven
having given yourself to the air and "have gone up toward God," toward whom
you have transacted the many things, and I have been left behind about this
"wandering life," I do not know to what I shall end or in what I shall harbor
having "crossed the great sea." But O father, this "sweet" and longed-for
"name" to me. If any memory of the things here sits in your divine souls, if
any remembrance has been given by the Better, remember the son. For you will
not cut off the illumination for yourself, nor will you subtract from the
contemplation; for so we hear also about the angels that they are both winged
toward the things above and at once administer the things here. You yourself
have been deemed worthy of an "angelic condition," show us the dignity, show
the inclination unchangeable." I then was groaning these things "holding in an
embrace" the father. And when I looked up a little, then first I knew the
mother drawing me and pulling against with all hands. Turning then I have seen
her; O that soul. But how might I tell the mixture of the passions in her, and
how she had become better than the one tyrannizing? Her inward parts were
fiercely stung and her nature had been brought down; but she seemed with a
better mind rebuking the natural passions and troubling herself. She then
checked the tear flowing down and held the groan drawn up from below and the
paled face blushed with shame about the matter and there was in her a mixture
and a standing of several oppositions. Yet the philosophic in her won
nonetheless. Then indeed also looking intently at me: "Has then nothing," she
said, "child, the outside word helped you toward piety, but in vain your
education, and in vain has the quickness of learning been rumored? For you
have not yet learned what is philosophized by the nurslings of "the
evangelical polity"; for this is our pursuit, to loose the body through
continence and to unloose the soul from this natural mixture and to be with
God. And this is the end for us of the much transaction here and tension,
which your father has now obtained, and now first purely his soul has been
freed. And what you now see is matter formed by nature, composed of elements
and again being separated into them. This body too was once fair and lying in
the soul, but that venom of the serpent has filled it with darkness and mist.
God then wishes to cleanse it again and to take out the venom; for this he has
divided the mixture and divides the compound and pours out the terrible drug,
that he may again remodel and build and set the soul in this again. You will
then see the father again, if you should wish, with the body once connatural,
when a trumpet will sound great according to our traditions and the one who
formed will "give form to the matter" and will shape the dust and will remodel
the human. Educate yourself, child, in "this education" and hold to the better
philosophy, and, if you should wish to lament, bewail yourself still held by
the bond and wandering in the sea of life and not yet having come to harbor."
I was ashamed, how might one say, as the mother went through these things to
me, and, having briefly held the lament, I laid the father in the earth and
turning toward the mother: "But you," I said, "become for me a "teacher of the
better word" and impart your wisdom, which you yourself drew abundantly from
the springs above." . I wish then to transfer the word immediately upon the
mother, but another word pulls me against and forces me to linger about the
father, or rather not a word, but a "night vision," if this too is not a word,


either innate and given up from the soul according to which it knows the
things that are, or from outside and more divine, showing to the soul released
from the body the truth of the things that stand. But I will take on a little.
My reasoning was in travail wishing to learn of what sort of lot the father
had obtained. In the evening then before the vision having prayed much about
this to the Better and having somehow also drawn the father's soul toward
this, and again as if having compelled him to show where he had gone, I went
off to take sleep. How much measure of the night I slept I do not know, I
thought then having looked up suddenly, if indeed I did not think, but what
was seen was true. For the time being then, as if having opened the eyes, I
had seen the father with the form in which he had been buried, exceptionally
more beautiful than before. He had rejoiced then without artifice and with a
pure leaping he was bringing up the soul, and the eyes, some torches were
being sent from there and the cheerfulness had passed the measure; each then
was, both what was thought in him and what was seen, lying above both sense
and mind. He approached me then more humanly and did not flee the touch, but
gave to be touched. And he himself also touched in return and addressing me
with the accustomed voice: "Child," he said, "longed for both of old and now
more, take courage then concerning me. For at once I had died and I had seen
God," thus then also speaking the lot, "and I begged and entreated that
unspeakable nature much concerning you." And the rest, shall I speak, father,
or shall I hide? For I have much preferred, for the word has ambition for me
and most of all that it is also divine and from God, but I am ashamed having
falsified the answer. For the Divine has willed better things for me, but I
throughout act against what has been resolved, whence the voice of the Divine
is not a missing of the truth, but my vice; for we have not been compelled
about the better, father, just as not about the worse, nor have we been forced
from foreknowledge toward this or that life, nor does the foreknowledge alter
the character, but "what is in our power" and choice and the inclination of
the will make the difference of the ways of life. The Divine then is good for
the sake of all, or rather a spring of goodness, and wishing to make all
things good, but I am base about the judgment and have slipped willingly into
vice. And if you are jealous of the divine voice as having been moved at my
inclination, entreat again and this often the Lord who is jealous over us, and
take me from the senses upon intellection.
21. Let the word about the father then having ceased here have an end, and to
the mother the remainder of our debt must be paid. For she even before was in
love with the blessed lot toward God, but when the father also was released,
she was wholly winged toward that life; for she "held it terrible" if while
living with the body they happened to be undivided from each other, but later
having been joined in spirit they should have the difference, and he should
dwell with God, and she should live in the world. She sought then again the

30. But since such a life has been allotted me so as not to suffice for itself
alone, but to lie forth for the others also and to give to draw as some

from outside, not only so much as is contemplative, but also so much as goes
down toward history and poetry. For I also speak about poems toward some of
the companions and about Homer and Menander and Archilochus, Orpheus and
Musaeus and as many as the female also sang, Sibyls and "Sappho the

me about the names in them, so as to know what the breakfast, what the
