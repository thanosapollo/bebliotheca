* On Saint Drosis the Martyr

:CUSTOM_ID: h1

Encomium on the holy great-martyr Drosis, and on remembering death. αʹ. The
labor-loving among shepherds, whenever after a long winter they see a bright
ray, and the day become warmer, leading the sheep out of the fold, take them
to the accustomed pastures; this good shepherd too, imitating these, has led
this sacred flock, even the spiritual flock of Christ, to these spiritual
pastures of the saints. Sheep standing at the manger are filled; but when they
get outside the pens, they harvest more benefit from the fields, bending down
with much delight, and shearing the grass with their teeth, breathing clean
air, and looking toward a clear and bright ray, skipping beside lakes and
springs and rivers; and the earth too brings them a certain delight, being
adorned on every side with flowers. Not only upon those, but also upon us this
has much benefit. For the table of spiritual foods was set full for us even
within, but the going-out toward these saints has a certain both entertainment
and a gain of the entertainment not less, not because we breathe the clean
air, but because we look toward the achievements of these noble ones; not
skipping beside rivers of waters, but beside rivers of gifts; not bending down
and shearing grass with the teeth, but gathering virtues of martyrs; not
seeing earth adorned with flowers, but looking at bodies abounding with
spiritual gifts. Each of the martyr-shrines then furnishes no small gain to
those gathered, but this one most of all; for as soon as one has set foot on
the threshold, a multitude of tombs at once falls upon the eyes from every
side, and wherever one may look, one sees coffins and memorials and chests of
those who have departed.

Not small indeed is the contribution this spectacle of the tombs makes for us
toward a part of philosophy. For the soul through this sight, even if it is
sluggish, is quickly contracted, and if earnest and awakened, becomes more
earnest; and if someone bewails poverty, from this =50.684= sight he at once
receives consolation, and if he is proud upon wealth, he is humbled and
brought down. For the sight of the tombs forces each of those looking, even
unwilling, to philosophize the things concerning his own end, and persuades
him to think none of the present things to be firm, neither painful nor good;
and he who has been persuaded of these things will not easily be caught by the
snare of sin. For this reason a certain wise man also exhorts, saying: In all
your words remember your last things, and unto the age you will not sin; and
another too utters things in harmony with this, counseling thus: Prepare your
works for the going-out, and make ready toward the way; not discoursing about
the sensible way, but about the departure from here. For if we continually and
according to each day look ahead to the uncertainty of the end, we shall not
quickly sin; for neither will the splendid things of life be able to puff us,
nor the painful things to cast us down and throw us into confusion, both
having an uncertain end.

For often the one living today does not even remain as far as evening. Now
then, spending time within in the city, it is not very likely to practice and
philosophize such things; but having gone out outside the walls, and having
come to these tombs, and having seen the multitude of those who have departed,
there is every necessity both willing and unwilling to receive these thoughts
from the sight, and having received them to become higher, and to be released
from the sympathy toward the affairs of life. And we shall not only receive
such thoughts, but we shall also have sufficient exhortation to hasten and
prepare ourselves toward our eternal fatherland, and to set all that is ours
in order toward the departure there, knowing that whatever of our things we
leave behind here will bring us loss by having been left. For just as a
traveler going a long road and pressing toward his own fatherland has
altogether lost, and been damaged, whatever he leaves in the inn; so too we,
as many of our things as we leave here and go away, have been damaged in all
of them; wherefore it is necessary to send some with ourselves, and some
=50.685= before ourselves, there. For the present life is a road, and has
nothing stationary, but we pass by both its painful things and its good
things. For this reason especially of all I love this very place, because not
only in an assembly, but also often without an assembly arriving here, I have
continually remembered these words, my eyes surveying the tombs in much
solitude with quiet, and sending the soul on toward those who have departed
and the state there.

βʹ. For these reasons I also admire this noble father, that seizing a calm day
he has led us out here, with blessed Drosis going before and guiding, whose
memory we are celebrating. For besides what has been said there is another
greater gain to harvest from the place. For when, having passed by the other
coffins, we come to the chests of the martyrs, our mind becomes higher, the
soul more vigorous, the eagerness greater, the faith hotter. Whenever
therefore we reckon up the toils and the contests and the prizes and the
awards and the crowns of these saints, we find again another greater occasion
for humility.

For even if someone has achieved great things, he will think he has done
nothing great, comparing his own virtue with their wrestlings; and if he has
done nothing great and good, he will not despair of his own salvation, having
taken from their courage an exhortation toward a change of virtue, and having
thought to himself that it will perhaps sometime happen, the loving-kindness
of God deeming him worthy, that he too should leap such leaps, and suddenly go
up to heaven, and obtain that greatest freedom of speech; and these things and
many more than these it is possible, having philosophized from here, to go
away. For the death of martyrs is an exhortation of the faithful, a freedom of
speech of the Churches, a confirmation of Christianity, a dissolution of
death, a proof of resurrection, a mockery of demons, an accusation of the
devil, a teaching of philosophy, an exhortation to the contempt of present
things, and a way of desire for the things to come, a consolation of the
terrible things that hold us, and an occasion of endurance, a starting-point
of perseverance, and the root and spring and mother of all good things; and if
you wish we will prove each of these, and will say how it is an exhortation of
the faithful, a freedom of speech of the Churches, a proof of resurrection,
and all the rest which I have now said. Whenever therefore contests and
battles about doctrines are stirred up for us toward Greeks, and they slander
our faith, among the other things let us also put this forward against them, I
mean the death of the martyrs, saying: Who persuaded these to despise the
present life? For if Christ died and did not rise, who accomplished these
things beyond nature? For that it is not of human power to persuade so many
myriads over so long a time, not of men only, but also of women and unwed
virgins and small children, to look =50.686= down on the present life, and to
dare against beasts, to laugh at fire, to trample every kind of punishment and
vengeance, and to press toward the life to come, they will not even need our
proofs, but they will ask themselves, and will take a sufficient demonstration
of what is said.

For from the time Christ came, there have been unbelieving emperors, and there
have also been believing ones, but the greater part of the unbelieving ones
led the faithful to pits and pyres and cliffs and seas and the madness of
beasts and manifold punishments and vengeances, trying in every way to pry the
faith out of their soul, and they accomplished nothing, but went away mocked,
while those who believed were being cut down in every way, and the faith was
increasing the more. But no pious emperor ever chose to punish and avenge an
unbelieving man, compelling him to stand off from error. And yet even so it
flows down of itself and perishes, that you may learn both the strength of the
truth and the weakness of the lie, the one perishing of itself even with no
one troubling it, the other increasing through those who hinder it and being
raised to an unspeakable height; and the cause is that Christ both lives and
works in the souls of the martyrs. Whenever therefore they say that he did not
rise, let us ask them these things: And who accomplished these things? The
dead man, tell me? But so many dead men have come to be, and no one has worked
such things. But he was a magician and a sorcerer? But many magicians and
sorcerers and deceivers have also been born, and all have been silenced, and
there is no remnant of them anywhere, but their sorcery too was quenched
together with their life; but the things of Christ increase every day, and
very reasonably; for what happened was not happening by sorcery, but by divine
power, therefore it is not dissolved either. Rather I make the demonstration
of the power not only from this, that it increases, but that it is also for
good, and for the salvation of our life. For after his presence those who
inhabit the earth have become men from beasts, or rather all who attend to him
with truth have become angels from men. But the martyrs were deceived and
cheated, he says, and for this reason they despised the present life. Then did
the first not persuade the second, nor the second the third, but as far as the
things of the persecutions went forward, so far also this achievement
advanced, and no one, in so long a time, perceived the deceit?

And how would these things have reason? And if they had been deceived, how do
the demons fear their dust? how do they also flee the tombs? For it is not
because demons fear dead men that they suffer this. For see, there are myriads
of dead throughout the earth, and they sit by those, and one may see many
demoniacs spending time in deserts and tombs; but where the bones of the
martyrs have been buried, they flee as from some fire and unbearable
punishment, proclaiming with a clear voice the power within that scourges
them.

γʹ. From this, then, it has been shown that the death of the martyrs is a
proof of the weakness of the demons; and that the matter is also an accusation
of their insensibility is clear from the following. For when these, I mean the
martyrs, being subject to body and natural necessities, and having a great
crowd of pains and sufferings standing round them, and living the perishable
life, and spending time upon earth, appear thus looking down on the present
life because of love for the God who made them; while those, both having been
released from flesh and from all this pain and suffering, appear thus
insolent, and have become thankless toward the benefactor, what defense will
they have? what pardon? None from anywhere, since the virtue of these condemns
the wickedness of those from a great surplus. For it is not only that more
earnest men condemn the more sluggish men, but already even the demons
themselves this earnestness of theirs condemns. And Paul made this clear,
saying: Do you not know that we shall judge angels? how much more things of
this life? meaning the angels of the devil, the apostates. And how shall we
judge them, he says? Not sitting and demanding accounts of them, but by our
earnestness condemning their sluggishness.

Which very thing he also made clear when he said: And if the world is judged
in you; he did not say upon you, but through you. Just as when he says: Men of
Nineveh will rise up, and will condemn this generation, not as if the
Ninevites demand an account from the then-unbelieving of the Jews, but as if
their faith condemns the unbelief of these. It is possible also to profit very
greatly toward virtue and toward contempt of present things from the martyrs.
For when you see these despising the whole of life, even if you are more
insensible than all and most sluggish, you will receive a most high mind, and
will laugh at luxury, look down on money, and desire the sojourn there; and if
you are in illnesses, you will take a very great occasion for endurance, the
sufferings of the martyrs; and if you are pressed by poverty, or by anything
else of the most difficult things, looking toward the greatness of the
tortures inflicted on them you will have sufficient consolation for all the
terrible things that have seized you. For this reason especially I love the
memorials of the martyrs, and I love and greet them all, but most of all when
women happen to be competing. For the weaker the vessel, the greater the
grace, the more brilliant the trophy, the more conspicuous the victory, not
because of the weakness of the nature of the athletes, but because even by
those through whom the enemy prevailed, by these he has now been captured. For
through a virgin, at any rate, the devil formerly killed Adam, through a
virgin after these things Christ overthrew the devil, and the sword which had
been sharpened for him against us, this cut off the dragon's head, which also
happened in the case of David. For just as then that righteous man ran and cut
off Goliath's head with the barbarian's own sword, so also now: through a
woman he prevailed, through a woman he has been defeated. This was formerly a
weapon for him, this has now become for him an instrument of slaughter, this
an unconquerable vessel. The former sinned and died, this one died that she
might not sin; that one then, puffed up by hope =50.688= of a cold promise,
trampled the laws of God; this one even despised the present life, so as not
to renounce the faith toward the benefactor.

What defense then could there still be for men who grow soft, and what pardon,
when women play the man? when they thus nobly strip for the contests of piety?
For neither nature, nor age, nor anything else could hinder, when eagerness is
present, and zeal, and a faith on fire; and through all these we draw to
ourselves the grace of God, which indeed also happened in the case of this
blessed woman. For her body was weak, and her nature easy to insult, and her
age more incomplete, but grace coming upon the weakness of all these hid it,
since it found a noble eagerness, and an undoubting faith, and a soul prepared
for dangers.

δʹ. For there is not, there is not anything more powerful than the one who has
the fear of God rooted with much earnestness, but even if fire, even if iron,
even if beasts, even if anything else is threatened, he looks down on all with
much ease, just as also this blessed Drosis. For when the tyrant lit the pyre;
for he did not lead her to a pit, nor cut off her head, lest the shortness of
the punishment make the contest easier for her, but wishing to strike her mind
with terror, and to master the unenslaved soul by the sight of the pyre, he
threw her into the midst of it; when therefore he kindled the pyre, and the
furnace was burning, and was raised to a great height, seeing these things the
blessed martyr was herself kindled with eagerness, and boiled with the fire of
longing for Christ, having remembered the three children, and having reckoned
with herself that she shares in those contests, and bears the same crowns as
they. And just as the mad see none of the things seen as they are, but even if
they see a sharpened sword they tumble easily, even if a pyre, even if a pit,
even if a cliff, even if a sea, even if anything else, they throw themselves
fearlessly against all; so also she, having gone mad with a madness not of
that kind, God forbid! but another more august than all prudence, and having
been dyed with the longing for Christ, saw none of the things seen, but having
transferred her station to heaven and moved her soul there, laughed at all the
terrible things, and thought the fire not fire but dew.

For this reason I call that pyre both a spring of most pure waters, and a
certain wonderful dye, and a smelting-furnace. For just as gold in a
smelting-furnace, so also the soul of that blessed woman through this pyre
became purer. For flesh was melting, and bones were being roasted together,
and sinews were being burned, and the fluid of the body was flowing down from
every side; but the faith of the soul was becoming firmer and brighter. And
those seeing these things thought she was perishing, but she was rather being
purified, and just as gold, the layman standing and seeing it melting and
flowing down and being mixed with the ash thinks it has perished and been
destroyed, but the craftsman who knows these things accurately knows that by
this =50.689= it becomes purer, and after the firing he draws it up from every
side gathering it bright; so also in her case the unbelievers, seeing her
flesh melting and flowing down, thought it was becoming ash and dust, but the
faithful knew very accurately that as it melts it puts off every stain, and
comes up brighter, having received back incorruption.

And in the pyre itself, before the resurrection, she was not prevailing over
the opposing powers in an ordinary way; for the flesh, being dissolved by the
fire and producing a noise, was turning them with a great surplus. And just as
a noble soldier, having put bronze weapons around himself, even by the very
noise of the weapons strikes the more cowardly of the adversaries with terror;
so also then the blessed Drosis by the noise of the skin put those powers to
flight, and not only in this way, but also in another again no less than this.
For as soon as she set foot on the pyre, smoke going up on high and occupying
the air choked all the demons flying through the air, drove away the devil,
wiped clean the very nature of the air. For since it was being polluted by the
smoke of the idols, * another smoke rose up against it, cleansing the stain
that came to it from those. And the image of a spring would also fit that
pyre. For just as at a spring, having taken off a garment and brightening the
body, so in that flame, having put off her own flesh more easily than any
garment and making the soul bright, she was pressing toward the bridegroom,
angels torch-bearing for her. For if angels led away that Lazarus who was
covered with sores to the bosom of Abraham, much more they went escorting this
woman, taking her from the furnace as from some sacred bridal chamber and
nuptial room, and leading her up to the bridal chamber above. And for what
reason did I also call that pyre a dye?

Because just as in some wonderful dye she became a royal purple and was sent
to the king above, and with much freedom of speech she set foot on the
heavenly vaults, Christ himself with an invisible hand holding the holy head
of the martyr, and as in water baptizing her with the fire. O wonderful pyre!
What a treasure it had within, that dust and that ash more precious than all
gold, more fragrant than all myrrhs, more valuable than all stones! For as
much as wealth and gold cannot do, so much do the relics of martyrs. For gold
has never yet driven out a disease, nor put death to flight, but bones of
martyrs have worked both these things, some in the case of our ancestors, and
some even in our own. And these things not we only, but also the righteous who
came to be before the presence of Christ know how to philosophize with
accuracy, when all were going out of Egypt, and some carrying gold, and some
silver, Moses instead of all wealth took the bones of Joseph and carried them,
bringing home with himself a very great treasure full of myriads of good
things.

εʹ. But perhaps someone might say: And for what reason were they transferred
from =50.690= Egypt into Palestine? For in a memorial of martyrs it is
especially necessary to examine such things. For many, being precise about
burial, and charging their relatives that even if they happen to die
elsewhere, those should bring them home and bury them there, then being mocked
by us for smallness of soul because of this, have put forward this history
against us, and when we say that it makes no difference whether one happens to
have burial at home or in a foreign land, they said: Well then, for what
reason, if it makes no difference, did Moses take the bones of Joseph from
Egypt and bring them to Palestine? But I will say even something greater, that
not only did Moses take them, but that he too when dying commanded it. For
this indeed is the greater thing: For God will visit you with a visitation, he
says, and you shall carry up my bones. For what reason then did that one
speak, and this one listen? For the saying is indeed worthy of inquiry. What
do you say? The patriarch who despised even the present life and looked down
on all, of whom the world was not worthy, the stranger and sojourner, who
imagined the things in heaven every day, who looked toward the Jerusalem
above, this man, while he lived, because of the fear of God fell out both of
fatherland and of freedom, and dwelt in a prison, and did not turn back toward
those plots; but when he was about to die, is he so petty as to make so great
earnestness about the transporting of the bones, and from so great a distance
command the relics to be brought up? And who would say these things? For what
was the gain, or what the benefit to the departed from the transfer of the
bones? For what reason then did he charge it? Not caring for the bones, but it
is possible to say that he also feared the impiety of the Egyptians.

For since he had worked many and great good things, and had become their
nourisher and protector, and had found a very great consolation of the famine,
and what had been clear to no one, these things he first and alone unfolded
and brought into the open, and having interpreted the dreams he not only
foretold the famine, but also laid up beforehand a sufficient medicine for it,
thus also filling the storehouses of Egypt, so that no one perceived his
presence; in order therefore that he might not be thought a god after death
because of the greatness of the benefaction, those barbarians easily making
gods from men, taking away every occasion of impiety, he commanded the bones
to be carried home. This then is one reason; but it is possible to say also
another that is indisputable; for it is possible to confirm it from the
Scriptures. What then is this? He knew, having heard from his father, that one
having learned a word brought down to him from the grandfather, that the
Egyptians would enslave them and afflict them many years. For your seed will
be a sojourner in a land not its own, and they will enslave it, and afflict it
four hundred years, God said to Abraham. In order therefore that they, having
given up at the length of the time, and not bearing the misery, might not
despair of the return, and slacken, giving them a very great pledge of hope he
spoke beforehand about the bringing-up of the bones, so that they, reckoning
with themselves that if the righteous man had not very much and with =50.691=
accuracy persuaded himself that We shall all return, he would not have given
command about the bones, might have an indisputable demonstration and a firm
hope of the return to the land that bore them. And that this is true, and that
for this reason he spoke beforehand about his bones, not caring about burial,
but correcting their unbelief, hear what Paul says: By faith Joseph when dying
remembered the exodus of the sons of Israel, and gave command concerning his
bones. What is, By faith? Instead of: he foresaw the things that would happen
long afterward, and that the descendants would altogether receive back their
own.

In order therefore that he might show this, he spoke both these things
beforehand, and it was possible to see a wonderful and paradoxical thing at
the exodus, the bones being carried. For Joseph who led them down into Egypt,
he himself also went before them again as they went up, anointing them for
endurance and the hope of the things to come. For seeing the relics before
their eyes, then being reminded from this of that whole history, and reckoning
with themselves that he was plotted against by the brothers, that he was
thrown into a pit, that he was in danger of the last things, that he dwelt in
a prison, and all the other things that happened to him; then, that after all
those things he became king and first of Egypt, and guardian and protector of
so many, they had sufficient hopes concerning deliverance from the terrible
things always seizing them, being taught through the bones of the righteous
man that no one of those who have believed in God, and have waited for the
alliance from him, was ever abandoned. For even if some painful and unwanted
things come in the middle of the promises, and fall in between, they will
never be able to knock those who hope out of the end, but that which was
foretold will altogether come out according to the vote from above, making
more splendid those who with endurance wait for all the things decreed by God.
For these reasons he gave command concerning his bones.

ʹ. Let us not then be so precise about burial at home, nor let us tremble at
death, but at sin. For death did not beget sin, but sin generated death for
us; and death has become a medicine of sin. For that one must not fear death,
but sin, hear what the prophet says: Precious in the sight of the Lord is the
death of his holy ones; and elsewhere, The death of sinners is evil. Do you
see that those who attend to themselves can harvest the greatest things from
it, but the neglected and sluggish, these are the ones who receive the matter
in the rank of a condemnation? And I do not move this word simply, but since I
often hear many speaking about manners of death, and being ashamed of those
that are not disgraceful, but not slandering those that are worthy of the
greatest accusation, for this reason I want also to distinguish this word
today. For this manner of philosophy too is timely on a martyr's day. For I
have heard many saying, So-and-so has died more dishonorably than a dog in a
foreign land, none of his intimates being present, nor giving him over to the
earth, but scarcely a few neighbors calling one another, wrapped from some
collection, thus he was given over to burial. In order therefore that these
things may not pain us, it is necessary also to correct this =50.692=
suspicion. For this is not to die more wretchedly than a dog, O man, but to
die more wretchedly than a dog is to die in sin, not to end life in a foreign
land. Do not tell me of this man carried on the gold-wrought couch, the whole
city escorting him, the peoples acclaiming, having silk and gold-wrought
garments heaped with much abundance; for this is nothing other than to set a
more abundant table for the worm. Do not then show me this man, but show me
this same man who is escorted with so great honor, on that day when Christ
sits as president on a high tribunal, being led in, being called, being
demanded accounts of what he said, of what he did, of what he thought.

For then none of this multitude will stand by him, nor will rescue him from
the punishment and the vengeance, nor will these shouts and acclamations stand
by, but bowing down, without freedom of speech, ashamed at the accusations of
the deeds, he will be led away, dragged by the wicked powers to the immortal
punishments with the most terrible gnashing of teeth, thenceforth lamenting
and beating himself uselessly from the unbearable pains. And the things there
are such, nor indeed are the things here tolerable either. For after those
acclamations in the midst, those bought ones, or happening from some fear, he
will hear all accusing, both in the streets and in the marketplace, and in the
house and in the taverns, and in other workshops, and on the road and in the
country, and everywhere each of those traveling relating to his neighbor with
much fear and saying what evils will succeed him, what vengeances will meet
him, what tortures now await him. What gain has come to him from the present
life? What more did he harvest from the greed? Having left the money to others
he went away, but he himself, having taken the sins, has been buried, and many
accusers from every side sharing the pain with those who were wronged, even
those who have suffered nothing terrible. For just as also in the case of
those being benefited, even those who have suffered nothing rejoice with those
who have, and praise the one who did well; so in the case of those being
insulted even those who have not been wronged share the pain with those who
have suffered badly, and abuse the one who did it. For this reason he says:
The death of sinners is evil, both because of the accusations here, and
because of the punishments there. This man then is the one who dies more
dishonorably than a dog, but in the case of the righteous it is not so; but
even if in a desert, even if with no one wrapping him, even if with no one
present he ends life, having taken a sufficient shroud he goes away, the
freedom of speech toward God; he has a wonderful funeral, angels being present
and guiding the soul, just as I already showed in the case of Lazarus, leaving
behind myriads of praisers of his own life; and if he leaves children, all who
inhabit the city will be their protectors and guardians, repaying them the
goodwill toward the father.

But he who has died in sins and greed, even if he goes away leaving children,
has left them heirs of his own hatred =50.693=, and has sent them off among
enemies; and if he ends life childless, through the buildings, through the
other possessions, which he had from robbery and greed, he leaves an immortal
accusation of himself. But the righteous man is not so, but even having gone
away he gains the greatest things, by the memory of his own virtue benefiting
all the living and making them better, just as the wicked man is punished in
this way too. For not only living, but also having died they harm many,
leaving proofs of their own greed everywhere. Knowing these things then, let
us not count wretched those who die in a foreign land, but those who die in
sins, nor let us count blessed those on the house and those on the couch, but
those who go away with virtue, and let us take care of virtue, and flee
=50.694= vice. For the one benefits both the living and those who have gone
away, but this harms from both sides, both putting these to shame and leading
them away to immortal punishments. And may God, who also deemed this blessed
woman who has gathered us today worthy to strip and wrestle and conquer and be
crowned, deem us also, all in common, worthy on that day, having finished the
present life with his commands and laws, to be able to come beside the tents
of this holy woman, and to enjoy the immortal goods; which may we all obtain,
by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the
Father be glory, together with the Holy Spirit, unto the ages. Amen.
