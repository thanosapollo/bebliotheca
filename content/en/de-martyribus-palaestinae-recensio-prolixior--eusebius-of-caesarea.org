parts themselves, and to lay so many blows upon his face and neck that, his
face having been destroyed, he was no longer recognized as who he was. =4.12=
The martyr of God, then, like some adamant, strengthened both in soul and in
body still more as a divine power breathed upon him, when the judge asked many
things, confessed nothing more than that he was a Christian; then being asked
who he was and from where, and where he was staying, he confessed nothing else
than that he was a servant of Christ. And the other, already going into
madness and being moved at the unconquered voice of the martyr, orders them to
wrap his feet with linens soaked in oil and to kindle fire beneath. And as the
torturers were accomplishing what was commanded, and the martyr had been hung
high, it was a fearful sight to those who saw: his sides so torn, so swollen
and altered in the form of his face, and his feet being burned for a long time
with much fire, so that the flesh melted and ran like wax, and the fire
penetrated within the bones as if they were dry reeds. =4.13= But none of
these things concerned the one who was suffering; for within he had as helper
the God in him, furnishing to all to see his help and presence clearly, as if
some light. Through which the martyr was filled with greater courage and was
full of more boldness. With a very great voice, then, he cried out, and by
word he proclaimed the confession toward the God to whom he was bearing
witness, and he testified that the power of Jesus our Saviour was present with
him, and that these paradoxical sights were being shown as in a very great
theater. For they were raging like demons, and being pained in their souls, as
if they themselves were suffering the terrible things, gnashing their teeth
and burning in their reasonings, they were forcing him to say who he was and
from where and where he was staying, and to sacrifice and to obey the things
appointed. But he, looking toward them all, saw them as men drunk, and not
even deeming them worthy of an answer, he used for the questions one voice
only, confessing Christ and testifying that he knew only his Father and the
Holy Spirit as God. When the enemies had already been defeated and had given
up, he was taken away again to the prison, and on the following day being
brought to the judge and


having witnessed the same confession, he is ordered to be given over to the
depths of the sea. =4.14= And the things done after this I well know will be
disbelieved by those who have not taken in the deed by sight, for ears are by
nature more unbelieving to men than eyes; yet it is not just on that account
for us to give over the wonder to forgetfulness, and the witnesses of the
history for us have been, so to speak, all who inhabit the city of the
Caesareans; no age, at any rate, was left out of this paradoxical sight.
=4.15= For as they were throwing the man of God into the depth of the sea,
having bound his feet with stones, no ordinary disturbance and an earthquake
at once, and a boiling, held both the sea itself and all that surrounded it,
and a very great movement was shaking the whole city. And together with the
paradox, the dead body of the divine martyr, as if it could not bear it, the
sea casts up before the gates of the city. There lay, then, a grievous sight
right at the very gateways of the city, the corpse, and there was a great
earthquake from God hanging over the whole, spreading through it, threatening
a terrible wrath upon all. And when what had happened was announced to those
inhabiting the city, all together at a run went forth before the gates to the
spectacle: children, men, old men, and likewise every age of females even to
the obscure and those still kept in chambers and those still remaining
virgins; all, both men and women, confessed the one and only God of the
Christians. Such, then, was the end that the drama concerning the wonderful
Apphianus obtained. His memory is celebrated on the second of the month
Xanthicus, four days before the Nones of April. 5.2 And kindred things, a
little later in time, his brother by the same father was suffering, Aedesius
by name. This man, even before the brother's impulse according to God, had
already given himself to philosophy. For he partook of all kinds of learning
and laid hold not only of the education of the Greeks but also of that of the
Romans, and he shared in the school of Pamphilus for a longer time. And this
man, after very many confessions and long-lasting prison sufferings, is first
given over to the copper mines in Palestine. 5.3 Then after the hardship in
these, he comes to the city of the Alexandrians, and happening upon Hierocles,
who was administering all Egypt by his own authority, as he was judging
Christians, when he saw him wantonly outraging the martyrs of God beyond the
fitting laws and giving over holy virgins of God to brothel-keepers for
wantonness and outrage of the body, not enduring the sight of the things being
done, he throws himself upon the same deed as his brother. Filled, then, with
a god-inspired zeal he comes forward and by word and deed puts Hierocles to
shame, with his own hand laying blows upon his face, and throwing him on his
back on the ground, striking him and at the same time exhorting him not to
dare against nature against the servants of God; and having said many other
things as well, and upon these having endured very steadfastly the tortures
laid upon his body, he carried off the brotherly end, being given over to the
sea. But this man at Alexandria, a little later in time, contended this
contest. And of the martyrs in Palestine, after Apphianus, Agapius was coming
forward to the contest. 7.1 Already the persecution against us had been
stretched to a fifth year, and it was the month Xanthicus, the 2nd day of it,
which would be four days before the Nones of April. And a certain holy and
all-holy maiden of those from Tyre, remaining a virgin for the child of God,
not even of eighteen years in all, approaches prisoner confessors of God who
were sitting before the governor's courts and were about to be presented at
once to the judge, greeting them, and she asked them to remember her when they
had attained the goal. 7.2 And when she had done this, as if she had done
something unlawful and unholy, soldiers seize her and at once bring her to
Urbanus; for he was still then administering the rule of the Palestinians. But
he, I know not what he suffered, as if he had been most greatly wronged by the
girl, is at once filled with wrath and rage,


and having ordered her to sacrifice, when he found her refusing, the most
beast-like man all the more lays terrible tortures upon her sides and upon her
breasts, and the pitiless one went even within the bones themselves and the
inward parts, punishing the child persistently as she received the tortures in
silence. And while she was still breathing he asked, commanding her to
sacrifice. But she, having opened her mouth and looking sharply and fixedly
with her eyes, with a slightly smiling face (and the beauty of her prime was
also blooming upon her), says: "Why then do you go astray, O man, not knowing
that I am now acting according to my prayers, when I have been counted worthy
to obtain communion with the martyrs of God?" And he, when he perceived that
he had become a laughingstock of the girl, no longer being able even to
maltreat her with greater tortures than before, condemns her to the depths of
the sea. And having passed from her he went to the remaining confessors, for
whose causes the things shown have been done for the girl, and he gives them
all together over to the copper mines in Palestine, having said nothing nor
laid on any compulsion; for the champion of them all, having taken up their
labors and having unstrung the cruel judge by vigor and strength of soul,
established him as a coward even toward those after these things. It was the
Lord's day on which these things were done at Caesarea in the month mentioned
and in the year shown. 11.1 And now the time calls, besides all, to recount

and quite children, some of the Egyptians with them, and others in their

of so many and such martyrs, in which, although they were not many in number,
nevertheless nothing was lacking of the ranks found among men. As, at any
rate, in a many-stringed lyre composed of unlike strings, high and low, and
the slackened and the taut and the middle, all well fitted by the musical art,
in the same way also upon these, youths at once and old men, and slaves
together and free, both learned and unlearned, both without glory according to
what seems to the many and of good repute, both faithful and catechumens
together, and deacons with presbyters, all as if by one all-wise musician, the
only-begotten Word of God, having been struck in varied ways and each having

having rendered the most brilliant and well-tuned and harmonious and
concordant notes of the confession at the courts, they fulfilled to the God of
all, under one and the same end, the most pious and all-wise melody through
the perfection of martyrdom. η And it is worthy to wonder exceedingly also at
the number of the men, showing some prophetic and apostolic gift; for it
happened that they were twelve in all, as many as we have received patriarchs
and prophets and apostles to have been. ι Nor should one pass over the
much-enduring acts of courage of each in part, the scrapings against the sides
and the rubbings through a texture of goat hairs against the scraped parts of
the body, and the incurable scourges and the many-formed and exchanged
tortures and the terrible and hard-to-endure wrenchings which, at the judge's
command, the spear-bearers, stretching them by hands and feet, were compelling
the martyrs by force to do something of the things forbidden. k What need is
there to tell the ever-memorable voices of the divine men, in which, having
less care for the pains, with a bright and cheerful face they answered the
judge's questions, laughing manfully at the very tortures and with a serious
manner ironically treating his questions? For when he asked from where they
were, leaving aside to declare the city on earth, they made known their true
fatherland, announcing themselves from Jerusalem; and they were indicating,
then, according to their mind, the heavenly city of God, toward which they
were also hastening. λ And other things of such a kind, unknown and not to be
seen together by those without taste of the sacred things, they were putting
forward most clearly to themselves alone and to those set in motion from the
divine faith. At which things especially the judge, being vexed and very
angrily convulsed and at a loss in his reasoning, was inventing varied devices
against them, so that he might not be defeated. Then, falling from his hope,
he at last yielded to each to carry off the prizes of the victory. m And
varied also was the manner of their end, two of the catechumens among them
having been perfected by the baptism through fire, and another having been
given over to the figure of the saving passion, and those about the name dear
to me having been crowned with differing prizes. These things, then, one might
say who remembered them more generally. 11.2 And going through each in part
one would reasonably bless the


leader of the chorus. This was Pamphilus, the man truly dear to God and truly
a friend and addressable to all, proving true his surname, the ornament of the
church of the Caesareans, since being a presbyter he also glorified the seat
of the presbyters, adorning and being adorned together by the ministry there.
And in the other things too he was truly divine and partaking of a divine
inspiration, since throughout his whole life he also happened to have been
distinguished in every virtue, having said a long farewell to luxury and
abundance of wealth, and having dedicated his whole self to the word of God.
Having sold, at any rate, the things that had come to him from his ancestors,
he distributed all to the naked, the maimed, and the poor, and he himself
lived in a life without possessions, pursuing the god-inspired philosophy
through most enduring ascesis. He set out, then, from the city of the
Berytians, where his first age had been nourished in the schools there. And
when the things of understanding were advancing for him toward complete men,
he passed from these to the science of the sacred words, and he took up a
manner of a god-inspired and prophetic life, and he presented himself even
before the last end of his life as a true martyr of God. 11.4 Such, then, was
Pamphilus. And second after him there came forward to the contest Valens,
honored with aged and holy-seeming gray hair, a reverend and sacred old man
even in his very appearance, and moreover also, if anyone else, knowing the
divine scriptures. So many memories of them, at any rate, had he stored in his
breast, that the recitations of the sacred learnings preserved for him through
memory fell short in nothing from reading from letters. And he was a deacon,
though being such, of the church of the Aelians. 11.5 Third among these was
numbered Paul, a most fervent man and boiling in the spirit. He was known from
the city of the Jamnites, who even before the martyrdom had contended the
contest of the confession through endurance of cauteries. 11.6 While these
were being worn down in the prison for a time of two years, the occasion of
the martyrdom became the arrival of Egyptians, of those also perfected with
them. These, having brought those being worn down in the mines throughout
Cilicia as far as the places, were returning to their own. And at the
entrances of the gates at Caesarea, having been asked by the guards who they
were and from where they had come, and having hidden nothing of the truth, but
having said that they were Christians, they were seized in the manner of
evildoers caught in the act. And they were five in number. 11.7 And these,
having been brought to the ruler and having spoken freely before him, are at
once given over to bonds, and on the next day, the sixteenth of the month
Peritius, and according to the Romans on the fourteenth before the Kalends of
March, they bring these very men together with those about Pamphilus to
Firmilianus. 11.8 And he first made trial of the Egyptians alone, exercising
the men with all kinds of tortures. Bringing their spokesman, then, into the
midst, he asked who he was and from where, then having heard some prophetic
name instead of the proper name, and this was also done by the rest, they
having put upon themselves prophetic surnames instead of the idolatrous names
given them from their fathers. You would have heard them, at any rate, naming
themselves Elijah and Jeremiah and Isaiah and Samuel and Daniel, and showing
themselves by their very deeds not only, but also by voices properly brought
forth, the Jew in secret and genuine Israelite. Having heard, then, some such
name from the martyr, yet not having attended to the force of the word, he
asked second what his fatherland was. 11.9 And he lets forth a second voice in
accord with the former, saying that Jerusalem was his fatherland, meaning that
one concerning which it has been said by Paul, "but the Jerusalem above is
free, which is our mother" and "you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of
the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem." =11.10= And he was meaning this. But
the other, having thrown his mind upon the ground and down to earth, was
inquiring exactly what this was and


where on earth it lay, and indeed he also applied tortures, so that he might
confess the truth. But he, being wrenched with both hands behind and being
broken at the feet by certain engines, was affirming that he had spoken the
truth. =11.11= Then when he asked again these same things and many times, what
it was and where lay the city Jerusalem that had been named, he said it was
the fatherland of the Christians alone; for that no others had a share in it
than these alone; and that it lay toward the east and toward the light itself
and the sun. =11.12= And he again through these things was philosophizing
according to his own mind, turning toward nothing of those around who were
maltreating him with tortures, and as if some fleshless and bodiless one he
seemed not even to perceive the pains. But the judge, being at a loss, was
convulsed, thinking that the Christians had perhaps established for themselves
a city hostile and at war with the Romans, =11.13= and he was much upon the
tortures and searching out the city that had been shown and examining the
country toward the east. And when, having torn the young man still more with
scourges, he saw him unchanging from the things said by him before, he brings
forth against him the capital vote unto death. And the things concerning this
man received such a dramaturgy. And having exercised the rest of the Egyptians
with similar wrestlings, he dispatches them in the like manner. =11.14= Then
passing from these to those about Pamphilus, he was informed that they had
already before been tried with very many tortures. And having reckoned it out
of place to throw the men around with the same maltreatments and to toil in
vain, he inquired only this, whether they would even now obey; and having
heard from each the last voice of the testimony, having similarly brought
forth a capital punishment against them, he lays it on. =11.15= And not yet
had the whole word been said by him, and from somewhere a youth of the
household service of Pamphilus cries out, having come forward into the midst
from the midst of the multitude of those circling about the court. And he was
crying with a great voice, asking for the bodies for burial. It was the
blessed Porphyrius, a genuine nursling of Pamphilus, not even of eighteen
years in all, skilled in the science of calligraphy, and for the sake of
temperance and manners covering all praises, as one trained under so great a
man. When he learned the vote against his master, he cried out from the midst
of the crowd, asking that the bodies be given over to earth. =11.16= And the
other, not a man but a beast and wilder than every beast, neither accepting
the reasonableness of the request nor assigning pardon to the newness of the
age, when he learned this only, that he confessed himself a Christian, orders
the torturers to use all their strength against him. =11.17= And when at the
command to sacrifice the wonderful one refused, he orders them to maltreat him
no longer as human flesh but as stones or wood or some other of the lifeless
things, even to the very bones and the inward parts in the depth, and to tear
his whole body. And when this had been going on for a long time, he discerned
that he was attempting in vain, the body of the noble martyr having become all
but voiceless and lifeless. =11.18= And the judge, possessing a persistent
pitilessness and inhumanity, orders them to scrape and rub still more the
sides flayed by the tortures with woven textures of hairs, then, as if having
taken his fill and having been filled with the madness, he gives sentence that
he be thrown to a long and gentle fire. This man, then, before the perfection
of Pamphilus, having come forward last to the contest, outstripped his master
by the release from the body. =11.19= And it was possible, then, to behold
Porphyrius in the disposition of a sacred victor who had won in the
pancration, his body covered with dust, but bright in countenance, and with a
confident and exultant spirit walking toward death, truly full of a divine
spirit. And being clothed in a philosopher's fashion with the cloak about him
in the manner of an exomis, looking up and despising all the mortal and human
life, with an unshaken soul he approaches the


pyre. And already as the flame was drawing near to him, as if no painful thing
were present to him, with an untroubled and sober reasoning the hero was
arranging concerning his own affairs with his acquaintances, still then
preserving his face bright and unchanged. And when he had taken leave
sufficiently with his acquaintances, he was already hastening toward God. The
pyre, at any rate, having been kindled in a circle about him from a distance,
from here and from there he was snatching the flame with his mouth, himself
urging himself on toward the journey set before him. And this he was doing
calling upon no other than Jesus. =11.20= Such also was the contest of
Porphyrius. And Seleucus, having become a messenger to Pamphilus of the
perfection concerning him, is at once counted worthy of the lot with them. At
once, at any rate, as he announced to Pamphilus the end of Porphyrius and had
greeted one of the martyrs with a kiss, the soldiers seize him and lead him to
the governor. And he, as if hastening that he become a fellow-traveler of
those before, orders him to be punished with capital punishment. =11.21= This
man set out from the land of the Cappadocians, and having been most splendidly
distinguished among those in the armies, he had laid hold of no small rank
among the Roman advancements. Moreover also in age itself and strength of body
and greatness and excellence of force he surpassed the rest by very much, and
in his very appearance he was notable to all, and in his whole form admirable
for the sake of size and beauty. =11.22= At the beginning of the persecution,
then, through endurance of scourges he was distinguished in the contests
according to the confession. And after the release from the army, having
established himself as a zealot of the ascetics of godliness, he has been
shown a genuine soldier of Christ, as some overseer and caretaker looking
after orphaned desolate ones and unsupported widows and those worn down in
poverty and weakness, and in the manner of a father and guardian recovering
the labors and hardships of all the cast-off. Whence reasonably, by the God
who rejoices in such things rather than in sacrifices through smoke and blood,
he was counted worthy of the perfection according to martyrdom. =11.23= This
man, a tenth athlete with those mentioned, was perfected on one and the same
day, on which, as it seems, a very great gate of the heavens having been
opened for the martyrdom of Pamphilus, there came about with him an easy and
ungrudging entrance of the kingdom of God. =11.24= In the track, then, of
Seleucus, Theodulus, a certain reverend and godly old man, was coming forward,
counted worthy of first honor of the gubernatorial household for the sake of
manners and age and because he had been established as father of a third
generation and still more because of the goodwill he preserved toward his
guardians. Having done, then, the similar thing to Seleucus and having greeted
one of the martyrs with a kiss, he is brought to his master, and having
sharpened him to wrath more than the others, he received the same martyrdom of
the saving passion, being given over to a cross. =11.25= Upon these, one still
remaining who might fill up the twelfth number for those mentioned, Julian was
present to fill this up. Having arrived from a journey at that very hour, not
even yet having entered the city, at once as he was from the road, having
learned from someone and having rushed upon the spectacle of the martyrs, when
he beheld the bodies of the saints lying on the earth on the ground, having
become full of joy, he embraces each, greeting them all with a kiss. =11.26=
And while he was still doing this, the ministers of the murders seize him and
bring him to the ruler. And he, accomplishing things following his purpose,
gives this man too over to a long fire. Thus, then, Julian also, leaping and
bounding over, and with a great voice confessing thanks to the God who had
counted him worthy of such great goods, was taken up in the choruses of
martyrs. =11.27= And this man too was by race of the Cappadocians, and in
manner full of piety, full of faith, a gentle man and reasonable, and for the
rest serious and breathing the fragrance of the Holy Spirit. So great a
company of a traveling-band with


the blessed Pamphilus was counted worthy of the perfection of martyrdom.
=11.28= For four days, then, and as many nights, by command of Firmilianus,
the all-holy bodies of the martyrs of God were laid out for food for the
flesh-eating beasts. And when nothing approached them, not a beast, not a
bird, not a dog, having been taken by the economy of God safe and unharmed,
having obtained the fitting honor and care, they were given over to the
customary burial, being laid away in most beautiful houses of temples and in
sacred oratories, delivered to be honored in unforgettable memory by the
people of God.
