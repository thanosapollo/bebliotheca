OF GREGORY, BISHOP OF NYSSA, AGAINST USURERS
The life of men who love the beautiful, choosing to live according to
reason, has been regulated by good laws and commandments, in which the
purpose of the lawgiver is seen tending toward two general aims, one
having the forbidding of things forbidden, the other urging to the
doing of the beautiful; for in no other way can a life be governed
well and temperately, unless one, fleeing wickedness so far as lies in
his power, pursue virtue as a son his mother. Having come together
then also today, that we may hear the commandments of God, we have
listened to the prophet slaying the interests, the wicked children of
the loans, and removing from life the use of money for gain. Let us
receive the command with ready obedience, lest we become that rock on
which the seed, having fallen, remained dry and fruitless, and lest it
be said to us what was once said to the hard-hearted Israel: Hearing
you shall hear and shall not understand, and seeing you shall see and
shall not perceive. But I beg those who are about to hear by no means
to cast their vote against my boldness or my folly, if I, after a man
unique and renowned for philosophy, exercised in all words by
education, who excelled in this very subject and left the discourse
against usurers as a possession to life, have also yoked a chariot of
asses or oxen to the prize-winning horses and entered the same
=9.196= contest. For the small things are always set beside the
great, and the moon shines while the sun is shining, and when a
merchant ship of ten thousand burdens sails, driven by the violence of
the winds, the little ferry boat follows, crossing the same deep; and
again, while men contend by athletic laws, children also dust
themselves in the same games. So then let this request have pardon.
But you, to whom the word is addressed, whoever you may be, hate the
huckster's trade; being a man, love men, and not silver. Stop the sin
at this point. Say to your interests, once dearest to you, the voice
of John the Baptist: Brood of vipers, depart from me. You are
destructions to those who have you and take you; you delight for a
little, but after times the poison from you becomes a bitter poison to
the soul; you block the road of life, you shut the doors of the
kingdom, having delighted the sight and rung in the hearing for a
little, you become causes of eternal grief. Having said these things,
bid farewell to excess and interests, and join yourself to love of the
poor. "And him who would borrow of you do not turn away." He entreats
you through poverty and sits at your doors; being in want, he flees
for refuge to your wealth, that you may become his helper in need. But
you do the opposite; you become an enemy instead of an ally; for you
do not work with him, so that you might free him from the necessity
pressing upon him and he might repay you the loan, but you sow evils
in the constrained man, stripping the naked, wounding the wounded,
adding cares to cares and griefs to grief. For he who receives gold at
interest receives a pledge of poverty, bringing destruction into the
house under the pretext of kindness. For as he who gives wine, as it
were out of philanthropy, to the =9.197= fevered man, burning with
heat and held by fiercest thirst and constrained to ask for drink,
delights for a little <the> one drawing the cup, but after a short
time works in the sick man a fiercer and tenfold fever, so he who
offers gold begetting itself to a poor man does not cease the
necessity, but tightens the misfortune. Do not then live a
misanthropic life under the pretext of philanthropy, nor become a
man-killing physician, having the pretext of saving, as that one has
through his art, for the sake of wealth, but using his choice for the
destruction of the one entrusted to you by yourself. Idle and
grasping is the life of the usurer; he does not know the labor of
farming, nor the contrivance of commerce, but sits in one place,
feeding beasts at his hearth. All things he wills to grow for him
unsown and unplowed. He has for a plow the reed, for a country the
paper, for seed the black ink, for rain the time, secretly growing for
him the increase of his money, and the demand is to him the sickle.
The house is a threshing floor, on which he threshes out the substance
of the oppressed; he looks on all things as his own. He prays for
necessities and misfortunes for men, that they may depart to him
constrained. He hates those sufficient to themselves and counts those
who have not borrowed enemies. He haunts the courts, that he may find
the constrained man among the exactors, and follows the bailiffs as
vultures follow battle lines and wars. He carries about the purse and
shows to the drowning the bait of the hunt, that they, choking through
need =9.198=, may swallow down with it the hook of the interests.
Daily he counts his gain and is not filled with desire. He is vexed
with the gold laid up in the house, because it lies idle and
unworking. He imitates the farmers who sow straightway from the heaps
of summer: he received, he gave. He does not let the wretched gold
rest, but moves it from hands to hands. You see then the rich and
much-golden man often having not even one coin in the house, but his
hopes in papers, his substance in acknowledgments, having nothing and
holding all things, having used for his life the opposite of the
apostolic writing, giving all things to those who asked, not from a
philanthropic judgment, but from a silver-loving manner. For he
chooses a temporary poverty, that the gold, toiling like a slave, may
return again with the wages. You see how the hope of the future
empties the house and makes the much-golden man temporarily without
possessions.
What then is the cause of this? The writing in the paper, the
acknowledgment of the constrained: I will pay with the labor, I will
complete with what comes to be. Then, I beg you, the debtor, though he
is needy, is believed on account of the writing, but God, though rich
and promising, is not heard. "Give, and I will repay," he cries,
having written in the gospels, in the public handwriting of the
inhabited world, which four evangelists wrote instead of one notary,
whose witnesses are all the Christians from the times of salvation.
You have Paradise as a deposit, a trustworthy pledge. But if you seek
it also here: the whole world is the possession of the grateful
debtor. Work out wisely the wealth of the one who seeks the labor, and
you will find riches; every land is a gold-bearing possession of your
debtor, every =9.199= mine of silver and bronze and of the materials
after these is a part of his lordship. Look to the great heaven, mark
the boundless sea, measure the breadth of the earth, count the living
creatures nourished upon it: all are slaves and possessions of him
whom you despise as needy. Be sober, man; do not insult God, nor hold
the bankers of less honor, whom you trust without wavering when they
give surety. Lend to a surety who does not die, believe a handwriting
that is not stolen nor torn. Do not ask about the repayment, but give
the kindness without huckstering, and you will see God repaying you
the favor with addition. But if the word seems strange to your
hearing, I have the testimony ready, that God, multiplying manifold,
repays the reward to those spending piously and doing good. For Peter
asking and saying: "Behold, we have left all things and followed you;
what therefore shall we have?" "Amen I say to you," he says,
"everyone who has left houses or brothers or sisters or father or
mother or wife or children or fields shall receive a hundredfold and
shall inherit eternal life." Do you see the zeal? Do you see the
goodness? The utterly shameless lender toils that he may double the
capital, and God freely gives the hundredfold to him who does not
oppress his brother. Trust then God advising, and you will receive
interests without sin. Why do you melt yourself away in numbered days
with sinful cares, counting the months, pondering the capital,
dreaming of the additions, fearing the time limit, lest it prove
unfruitful as a summer struck by hail? The lender busies himself about
the debtor's doings, his absences abroad, his movements, his changes
of place, his trades. And if some grim rumor comes, that so and so
fell in with pirates, or that from some circumstance his wealth was
changed =9.200= to poverty, he sits, having bound both hands, he
groans continually, he sheds many a tear, he unrolls the handwriting,
he laments the gold in the letters, carrying about the document as the
garment of a son who has died; from then on the passion burns hotter.
But if the loan also be a maritime one, he sits by the shores, he
worries the movements of the winds, he continually questions those
landing, lest perhaps a shipwreck has been heard, lest perhaps those
sailing were in danger; his soul grows numb when he sees the sea
raging; he sees dreams, he imagines the soul out of the remains of the
day before's care. To such a one then it must be said: Cease, man, the
perilous care, rest from the hope that wastes you away, seeking not
interests, lest you destroy for yourself the capital. You seek
revenues and additions of wealth from a poor man, making it like one
who would wish to take a wheat rick from a land dried by the hottest
drought, or a multitude of grape clusters from a vine under a
hail-bearing cloud, or the increase of children from a barren womb, or
the nourishment of milk from women bearing none. No one attempts the
things against nature and impossible, since besides achieving nothing
he incurs laughter. God alone is almighty, who finds the resources out
of the impossible and works the things beyond hope and expectation,
now bidding a spring pour from a rock, again raining bread from
heaven, unwonted and strange, and again sweetening the bitter Marah by
the touch of wood, and making the barren Elizabeth's womb fruitful,
and giving Samuel to Hannah and to Mary her firstborn in virginity.
These are works of the almighty hand alone. But you, do not seek
interest from bronze and gold, the barren materials, nor force poverty
to do the things of those who are rich, nor =9.201= to give excesses
to the one asking back the capital. For either you do not know that
the need of a loan is a fair-faced petition for mercy? Wherefore also
the Law, the introductory letter of piety, everywhere forbids
interest: "If you lend money to your brother, you shall not press
him." And grace, abounding at the fountain of goodness, legislates the
forgiveness of debts, in one place being gracious and saying: "And if
you lend, to those from whom you hope to receive"; and elsewhere in a
parable bitterly punishing the hard servant, who was not broken toward
his fellow servant bowing before him, nor forgave the trifling debt of
a hundred denarii, he himself having received the forgiveness of ten
thousand talents. And our Savior and teacher of piety, introducing to
the disciples the rule and simple pattern of prayer, put this also
among the words of the supplication, as most needed and first
sufficient to entreat God: "And forgive us our debts, as we also have
forgiven our debtors." How then will you pray, O usurer? With what
conscience will you seek from God a good thing, you who take all
things and have not learned to give? Or do you not know that your
prayer is a reminder of misanthropy? What have you let go, and do you
ask forgiveness? Whom have you pitied, and do you call upon the
merciful One? But if you sometimes give alms, whence do you give? Not
from your bitter and misanthropic tax? Not from others' misfortunes,
full of tears and groans? If the poor man knew whence you offer the
alms, he would not accept it, as about to taste of his own flesh and
of the blood of his own brothers, and he would say to you a word full
of wise freedom: Do not feed me, man, from the tears of brothers;
=9.202= do not give to a poor man bread made from the groans of the
wretched. Pay back to your fellow man what you wickedly exacted, and I
will acknowledge the kindness. What good do you do, making many poor
and comforting one? If there were not a multitude of usurers, there
would not be the multitude of the poor. Dissolve your band, and we
shall all have self-sufficiency. All bring accusation against the
usurers, and there is no cure of the evil: law, prophets,
evangelists. Such at least the divine Amos says: "Hear, you who
trample the needy, to make the poor of the land cease, who say: When
will the month pass by, and we shall trade?" For not even fathers so
rejoice at the begetting of children as the usurers are glad when the
months are being filled.
But they call the sin by reverent names, greeting the gain as
philanthropy, in imitation of the Greeks, who address certain demons,
haters of men and murderous, as Eumenides instead of their true
name. A philanthropist indeed: the tribute of interest, exterminating
houses, spending wealth, making those born to good fortune live worse
than slaves, delighting for a little at the beginnings and preparing
the life afterward bitter! For as the birds plotted against by the
fowlers delight in the seeds being thrown to them and make the
dwelling in those places familiar and dear to themselves, where food
becomes abundant for them, but a little later, caught in the traps,
they are destroyed, so those receiving the interest of the loans,
having become wealthy for a little time, later fall out of the
paternal hearth itself =9.203=. But pity †is cast out of the souls of
the defiled and silver-loving ones, and, seeing the house of the
debtor with goods for sale lying before them, they do not lament, but
rather they hasten the sale, that, receiving the gold sooner, they may
bind another wretch by loan, according to the manner of the capable
and insatiable hunters, who, having surrounded one valley with their
nets and caught all the beasts in it, again move their nets to the
neighboring ravine, and from that one to the other, and so far, until
they empty the mountains of their game. With what eyes then do you
look up to heaven, O such a one? And how do you ask forgiveness of
sins? Or do you perhaps say in your insensibility, praying, what the
Savior taught: "Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our
debtors"? O how many for the sake of interest have laid hold of the
noose and given themselves up to the streams of rivers, and judged
death lighter than the lender, and have left children orphans, having
poverty for an evil stepmother! And the noble usurers do not even then
spare the desolate house, but they drag the heirs, as having inherited
only the rope of the noose, and demand gold from those who procure
their bread by begging; and when, as is likely, they are reproached
over the death of the debtor, and some have reminded them of the noose
in entreaty, they are neither covered with shame at the deed nor
smitten in soul, but from a bitter judgment they speak shameless
words: "And is this wrong our doing, if that wretched and ungrateful
man, having drawn a base birth, was led by the necessity of fate to a
violent death?" For the usurers even philosophize and become disciples
of the Egyptian astrologers, whenever they must make a defense
concerning their unholy deeds and murders =9.204=. It must be said
then to one of such: You are the base birth, you the evil necessity of
the stars; for if you had lightened the care, and forgiven a part of
the debt, and received a part with abatement, he would not have hated
the toilsome life, nor would he have become his own executioner. With
what eyes then will you see the murdered man at the time of the
resurrection? For you will both come to the tribunal of Christ, where
not interests are reckoned, but lives are judged. What will you say,
being accused before the judge who takes no bribes, when it is said to
you: You had the law, the prophets, the evangelic commands; you heard
all things at once crying with one voice love and philanthropy, some
saying: "You shall not lend at interest to your brother," others: "His
money he did not give at interest," others: "If you lend to your
brother, you shall not press him"? And Matthew cried in parables,
announcing a masterly word and saying: "Wicked servant, I forgave you
all that debt, since you entreated me; was it not necessary for you
also to have mercy on your fellow servant, as I also had mercy on
you? And being angry, the Lord delivered him to the torturers until he
should pay all that was owed to him." Then the unprofitable repentance
will seize you, and heavy groans will seize you and punishment without
reprieve; no gold helping, no silver defending, but the giving back of
the interests more bitter than bile. These are not words to be feared,
but true things attesting the criterion before the trial, which it is
beautiful for the prudent man to keep, and for him who has forethought
of the things to come. And that I may benefit the hearers somewhat by
narrating something between the judgments of God and the things done
in our own times, hear the word, and perhaps the many will recognize
the story as known =9.205=. There was a certain man in this city (but
I will not say the name, guarding against mocking the departed one by
name), having for his craft the making of loans and the increase from
the defiled interests. But being held by the passion of the love of
silver, he was sparing also of his own expense (for such are the
silver-lovers), not setting a sufficient table, not changing a garment
continually or as needed, not providing the children the necessary
livelihood of life, not quickly taking a bath for fear of the fee and
the three obols, but devising every means by which he might advance
the number of his money to the more; yet he did not consider anyone a
trustworthy guardian of the purse, neither child, nor slave, nor
banker, nor lock, nor seal, but, thrusting the gold through the holes
of the walls and smearing the mud outside, he kept the treasure
unknown to all, changing places for places and walls for walls, and
contriving with skill to escape the notice of all. He departed this
life all at once, having disclosed to none of his own folk where the
gold was <buried>. He was indeed robbed of the denarii he had hidden;
but his children, hoping to become the most splendid of all in the
city through wealth, searched everywhere, asked one another, examined
the servants, dug up the floors of the houses, hollowed under the
walls, pried much into the houses of neighbors and acquaintances; but
having moved, as the word has it, every stone, they found not even an
obol; and they pass their life homeless, without hearth, poor, cursed
many times each day for the vanity of their father. Such then, O
usurers, is your friend and comrade, having ended his life worthily of
his character, a money-maker of the winds, toiling with pains and
hunger, and having gathered as an inheritance =9.206= for himself the
eternal punishment, and for those from him the poverty. But do you not
know for whom you gather or toil? The circumstances are many, the
informers ten thousand, the liers in wait and the robbers trouble land
and sea. See to it, lest you also earn the sins and fail to hold the
gold. But "he is burdensome to us," they say (for I know your
mutterings under your teeth), "and constantly †stationed at the bench
he plots against those being benefited and those in need. For behold,
we shall no longer make loans; and how will the constrained live?"
Worthy of the deeds are the words, fitting is the objection for those
darkened by the fog of the money; for they have not the judgment of
their mind sound so as to understand the things said, but on the
contrary they hear the counsel of those who admonish; for as I was
saying that one must not lend, so muttering under their breath they
threaten to shut the doors against those in need. But I first proclaim
and command the giving of gifts; then I also encourage lending (for a
loan is a second kind of gift), to do this not with interests nor with
excess, but as the divine Word commanded us; for alike liable to
punishment is the one who does not give a loan and the one who gives
with interests, since the one is condemned for misanthropy, the other
for huckstering. But those who desert to the opposite extreme,
promising to hold back the giving altogether: this is a shameless
objection, a mad contentiousness against justice, toward God strife
and war: "Either," he says, "I will not give, or, lending, I will set
the contract at interest." To the usurers then the discourse has
contended sufficiently, and the matters of the accusation have been
accomplished for me as in a courtroom =9.207=, and may God give them
repentance; but to those lending readily and casting themselves
recklessly around the hooks of the interests I shall make no
discourse, judging the counsel sufficient for them, which our divine
father Basil in his own book wisely wrought, having made a longer
discourse to those borrowing inconsiderately than to those lending
with greed.
