THIRD THEOLOGICAL ORATION ON THE SON

1. Such then are the things one might say, checking their readiness and speed
about the Word, and the danger of haste in all matters, but especially in the
discourses about God. But since to rebuke is no great thing; for it is very
easy and belongs to anyone who wishes; whereas to bring in one's own judgment
in return is the part of a pious man and one who has a mind: come, trusting in
the Holy Spirit, who is dishonored by them but worshiped by us, let us bring
into the light our own conceptions concerning the Godhead, whatever they may
be, as some noble and ripe offspring; not having been silent at other times,
for in this alone we are youthful and high-minded, but now speaking the truth
even more freely; that we may not by shrinking back, as it is written, be
condemned as not well-pleasing. And since every discourse is twofold, one
establishing what is one's own, the other overthrowing the opposing, we too,
having first set forth our own, will then try to overthrow the things of the
adversaries; and both as briefly as possible, that what is said may be easy to
take in at a glance, just as they themselves invented an introductory
discourse for the deception of the simpler or more naive, and that the
thoughts may not be scattered by the length of the discourse, like water not
confined in a pipe, but poured out over a plain and dissolved.

2. The highest opinions about God are three: anarchy, polyarchy, and monarchy.
The first two were the sport of children of the Greeks, and let them be sport.
For what is without rule is disorderly; and what has many rulers is factious,
and thus without rule, and thus disorderly. For both come to the same thing,
disorder, and disorder to dissolution; for disorder is a practice of
dissolution. But for us monarchy is what is honored; yet a monarchy not that
which one person circumscribes; for even the one, if it is at variance with
itself, can become many; but that which an equality of nature constitutes, and
a symphony of mind, and identity of motion, and a convergence of those from it
toward the one, which is impossible in generated nature, so that even if they
differ in number, they are not divided in essence. Therefore the Monad, moved
from the beginning to a Dyad, came to rest at a Triad. And this for us is the
Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit; the one Begetter and Projector, I
mean passionlessly, and timelessly, and bodilessly; and of the others, the one
is offspring, the other projection, or I do not know how one might name these
things, stripping them entirely of things seen. For we will not dare to speak
of an overflow of goodness, which one of those who philosophized among the
Greeks dared to say, as though some mixing-bowl overflowed, speaking thus
clearly, in the things in which he philosophizes about the first cause and the
second; lest we introduce the generation as involuntary, and as some natural
and hard-to-restrain surplus, least of all fitting to conceptions about the
Godhead. Therefore standing upon our own terms we introduce the unbegotten,
and the begotten, and that which proceeds from the Father, as somewhere the
God and Word himself says.

3. When then were these things? Beyond the when are these things. And if one
must also speak a little youthfully: when the Father was. And when was the
Father? There was not when he was not. This then is true also of the Son, and
of the Holy Spirit. Ask me again, and I will answer you again. When was the
Son begotten? When the Father was not begotten. And when did the Spirit
proceed? When the Son did not proceed, but was begotten timelessly and beyond
reason; even if we cannot present what is beyond time, wishing to escape a
temporal impression; for "when," and "before this," and "after these things,"
and "from the beginning," are not timeless, even if we force them as much as
possible; unless we take as the interval stretched alongside the everlasting
things the age, which is not divided and measured by any motion or by the
course of the sun, which is what time is. How then are they not
co-unoriginate, if they are co-everlasting? Because they are from him, even if
not after him. For what is unoriginate is also everlasting; but the
everlasting is not altogether unoriginate, so long as it is referred to a
beginning, the Father. They are not then unoriginate as to the cause; and it
is clear that the cause is not altogether older than the things of which it is
cause; for neither is the sun older than the light. And they are unoriginate
in a way as to time, even if you scare the simpler with bogeys; for the things
from which time is are not under time.

4. How then is the generation not with passion? Because it is bodiless. For if
the bodily is with passion, the bodiless is without passion. But I will ask
you in return: how is he God, if he is a creature? For what is created is not
God; not to say that even here there is passion, if it is taken bodily, such
as time, desire, forming in thought, care, hope, grief, danger, failure,
correction; all of which and more than these belong to creation, as is clear
to all. And I wonder that you do not dare this too, to think of some pairings,
and times of pregnancy, and dangers of miscarriage, as though it were not
possible to beget unless he has begotten thus; or again, enumerating some
generations of birds and of land-animals and of water-animals, to subject the
divine and unutterable generation to one of these generations, or even to do
away with the Son from the novel hypothesis. And you cannot even see this,
that he whose generation according to the flesh is different, for where among
your things did you know a God-bearing virgin?, for him the spiritual
generation also differs; or rather, he whose being is not the same, for him
begetting also is different.

5. Who then is a Father who has not begun? Whoever did not even begin to be;
but for whom being began, for him being a father also began. He is not then
Father later, for he did not begin; and he is Father properly, because he is
not also Son; just as the Son is Son properly, because he is not also Father.
For our things are not properly, because we are both; for we are not this more
than that; and we are from both, not from one, so as to be divided, and we
become humans little by little, and perhaps not even humans, and such as we
were not willed to be, and leaving and being left, so that only the relations
are left orphaned of the things. But "he begot," he says, itself, and "he has
been begotten," what else do they introduce but a beginning of generation?
What then if we do not even say this, but "he was begotten from the
beginning," that we may easily escape your over-curious and time-loving
objections? Will you bring a writing against us, as falsifying something of
Scripture and of the truth? Or is it clear to all that many of the things said
temporally are brought forward with the times exchanged, and especially in the
custom of Scripture, not only as many as belong to past time, or to the
present, but also as many as belong to the future? As this: Why did the
nations rage? for they had not yet raged; and, Through the river they will
pass on foot; which is, they have crossed. And it would be long to enumerate
all such expressions, which have been observed by the industrious.

6. This then is of that sort. And of such a sort is that of theirs too, so
very contentious and shameless: Willing, they say, he begot the Son, or not
willing. Then they bind us, as they think, with knots from both sides, not
strong, but even very rotten. For if not willing, they say, he has been
tyrannized. And who is the one who tyrannized? And how is the one tyrannized
God? But if willing, the Son is a son of will; how then is he from the Father?
And they fashion a novel mother, the will, in place of the Father. This then
is one charming thing of theirs, if they say this, that having withdrawn from
passion they take refuge in will; for will is not a passion. And second let us
see their strong point, what they say. And it is best to wrestle with them
first more closely. You yourself who say easily whatever you wish, did you
come to be from your father willing, or not willing? For if from one not
willing, he has been tyrannized by violence; and who is the one who tyrannized
him? For you will not say nature; for that has also self-control. But if from
one willing, your father has been lost to you through a few syllables. For you
have appeared a son of will, but not of a father. But I will pass over to God
and the creatures, and I will bring your question to your wisdom. Did God
establish all things willing, or forced? If forced, here too is the tyranny,
and the one who tyrannized. But if wishing, the creatures too have been
deprived of God, and you before the others, who invent such reasonings and
sophisticate such things. For they are walled off by a will in the middle from
the Creator. But willing, I think, is one thing and will another, begetting
and generation, speaking and word, unless we are drunk. The one is the one
moved, the other as it were the motion. The thing willed then is not of will;
for it does not altogether follow; nor is the thing begotten of generation,
nor the thing heard of utterance, but of the one willing, and of the one
begetting, and of the one speaking. And the things of God are even beyond all
these, for whom generation is perhaps the will to beget, but there is nothing
in the middle, if indeed we will admit this at all, and not rather that the
generation is even better than will.

7. Do you want me to sport a little with the Father too? For from you I have
it to dare such things. Is the Father God willing, or not willing? And how
will you escape your own ambidextrous argument? If then willing, when did he
begin to will? For not before being; for there was not anything prior. Or was
one part of him that which willed, and another that which was willed? Then he
is divisible. And how is he too not, according to you, a projection of will?
But if not willing, what is it that forced him into being? And how is he God,
if he has been forced, and that in nothing other than being God itself? How
then has he been begotten? How has he been created, if indeed he has been
created according to you? For this too belongs to the same difficulty. Perhaps
you would say, by will and word. But you do not yet say the whole. For how did
the will and the word have power of a work? It remains still to say. For a
human being is not thus.

8. How then has he been begotten? The generation would not be great if it were
grasped by you, who do not even understand your own generation, or have
grasped some small part of it, and as much as you are ashamed to say; and then
you think you know the whole? You would toil much first, before you found the
reasons of compaction, of formation, of manifestation, the bond of soul to
body, of mind to soul, of word to mind, motion, growth, assimilation of food,
sense, memory, recollection, the other things of which you are composed; and
which belong to the compound of soul and body, and which are the divided
things, and which they receive from one another; for of those things whose
perfection is later, the reasons come with the generation. Say what they are;
and not even then philosophize about the generation of God; for it is not
safe. For if you know your own, you do not altogether know also that of God;
and if not even your own, how that of God? For by as much as God is harder to
conjecture than a human, by so much also is the generation above more
ungraspable than your generation. And if because it has not been grasped by
you, therefore he has not even been begotten, it is time for you to strike out
many of the things that are, which you have not grasped, and before all God
himself; for you cannot say what he ever is, even if you are very daring, and
high-souled about superfluities. Cast down your fluxions, and your divisions,
and your cuttings, and thinking of the bodiless nature as about a body; and
perhaps you may think something worthy of the generation of God. How has he
been begotten? For again I will utter the same, being impatient. Let the
generation of God be honored with silence. It is a great thing for you to
learn that he has been begotten. But the how, not even for angels to conceive,
much less will we grant it to you to think. Do you want me to present the how?
As the begetting Father knows, and the begotten Son. And what is beyond these
is hidden in a cloud, fleeing your dim-sightedness.

9. Did he then beget one who was, or one who was not? What nonsense; these
things are about me and you, who were something, as Levi in the loins of
Abraham, and have become something; so that ours is in a way from things that
are, and from things that are not; conversely concerning the primordial
matter, which clearly came to be from things that are not, even if some
fashion it unbegotten. But here being begotten is concurrent with being, and
from the beginning; so where will you put this two-cliffed question? For what
is older than from the beginning, that we may put there the sometime being of
the Son, or the not being? For either way the from the beginning will be
dissolved. Unless for you the Father too, when we ask again, from things that
are, or from things that are not, should be in danger either of being twice,
one preexisting, the other being, or of suffering the same as the Son, of
being from things that are not, because of your toys of questions, and the
buildings from sand, which do not even stand against breezes. I then accept
neither of these, and I say that the question has the absurdity, not the
answer the difficulty. But if it seems to you necessary that one of the two be
true of everything, according to your dialectical assumptions, accept a small
question of mine. Is time in time, or not in time? If then in time, in what
time is this? And what is it besides this? And how containing? But if not in
time, what is the superfluous wisdom of introducing a timeless time? And of
this, Now I am lying, grant one of the two, either to be true only, or to be
false; for we will not grant both. But it is not possible. For either lying he
will speak truth, or speaking truth he will lie; every necessity. Why then is
it wonderful if, just as here opposites happen, so there both are false, and
thus your cleverness will appear foolish? Solve still one of my riddles: were
you present at your own being born? And are you present now? Or neither? For
if you both were present and are present, as who, and to whom? And how have
the one become both? But if neither of the things said, how do you separate
from yourself? And what is the cause of the sundering? But it is uneducated to
busy oneself about the one, whether he is present to himself or not. For these
things are said of others, not of oneself. More uneducated, know well, is it
to examine the one begotten from the beginning, whether he was before the
generation, or was not. For this account is about things divisible by time.

10. But, he says, the unbegotten and the begotten are not the same. And if
this, neither is the Son the same as the Father. That this argument openly
casts the Son out of the Godhead, or the Father, what need is there to say?
For if the unbegotten is the essence of God, the begotten is not essence; and
if this, not that. What argument will contradict? Choose then of the impieties
whichever you wish, O empty theologian, if indeed you have altogether been
eager to be impious. Then how do you say the unbegotten and the begotten are
not the same? If you mean the uncreated and the created, I too accept. For the
unoriginate and the created are not the same in nature. But if you say the one
who has begotten and the one who has been begotten are not the same, it is not
rightly said. For they must altogether be the same. For this is the nature of
offspring, to be the same as the one who has begotten according to nature. Or
again thus: how do you say the unbegotten and the begotten? For if
unbegottenness itself and generation, they are not the same; but if those of
whom these things are, how are they not the same? Since also the unwise and
the wise are not the same as one another, but they are about the same, the
human being; and it does not cut essences, but is cut about the same essence.
Or also the immortal, and the harmless, and the unchangeable is essence of
God. But if this, there are many essences of God, and not one. Or the divine
is composed from these. For these are not without composition, if indeed they
are essences.

11. These things they do not say, for they are common also to others. But what
is of God alone and proper, this is essence. They would not concede that the
unbegotten is of God alone, they who also introduce matter and the idea as
unbegotten. For let us throw farther the darkness of the Manichees. Yet let it
be of God alone. But what of Adam? Is he not the only formation of God? And
very much so, you will say. Is he then also the only human? By no means. Why
ever? Because humanity is not the forming; for the one begotten is also a
human. Thus neither is the unbegotten alone God, even if it is of the Father
alone, but accept also the begotten to be God. For he is from God, even if you
are very much a lover of the unbegotten. Then how do you call essence of God
not the positing of what is, but the denial of what is not? For the account
makes clear that generation does not belong to him, it does not present what
the nature is, nor what belongs to that which does not have generation. What
then is the essence of God? This belongs to your madness to say, you who busy
yourself even about the generation. But for us it is a great thing if we
should ever even later learn this, when the gloom and the thickness have been
dissolved for us, as the promise of the unlying One. Let this then both be
thought and hoped by those being purified for this. But we will dare to say
this much, that even if it is great for the Father to have sprung from
nowhere, it is not less for the Son to be from such a Father. For he would
share in the glory of the uncaused, because he is from the uncaused, and there
is added the thing of the generation, a matter so great and so venerable to
those not altogether groveling and material in mind.

12. But if the Son is the same as the Father according to essence, they say,
and the Father is unbegotten, this the Son will be also. Well, if the
unbegotten is the essence of God, so that there may be some novel mixture,
begotten-unbegotten. But if the difference is about essence, why do you say
this as a strong point? Or are you also father of your father, that you may
lack nothing of your father, since you are the same according to essence? Or
is it clear that, the property remaining unmoved, we will seek the essence of
God, whatever it is, if indeed we will seek? And that unbegotten and God are
not the same, you might learn thus. If they were the same, it would be
necessary altogether, since God is God of some, that the unbegotten also be of
some; or since the unbegotten is of none, that God also not be of some. For
things altogether the same are also expressed similarly. But the unbegotten is
not of some, for of whom? and God is God of some, for of all. How then would
God and unbegotten be the same? And again, since the unbegotten and the
begotten are opposed to one another, as habit and privation, it is necessary
that essences also be introduced opposed to one another. Which is not granted;
or since again the habits are prior to the privations, and the privations are
destructive of the habits, not only that the essence of the Son be older than
that of the Father, but also that it be destroyed by the Father, so far as
your hypotheses go.

13. What argument is still left them of the inescapable ones? Perhaps they
would take refuge at last in that: that if God has not ceased from begetting,
the generation is incomplete, and will sometime cease; but if he has ceased,
he has altogether also begun. Again the bodily people, the bodily things. But
I do not yet say whether being begotten is everlasting for him, or not, until
I examine accurately Before all the hills he begets me. But I do not see what
the necessity of the argument is. For if according to them what will cease has
begun, what will not cease has not altogether begun. What then will they
declare about the soul, or the angelic nature? If it has begun, it will also
cease; but if it will not cease, it is clear that according to them it has not
even begun. But in fact it both has begun, and will not cease. Therefore
according to them what will cease has not begun. Our account then is: just as
of horse, and ox, and human, and each of the things under the same species,
there is one account, and whatever shares in the account, this is also said
properly, and whatever does not share, this is either not said, or not said
properly. Thus also of God there is one essence, and nature, and appellation,
even if with certain conceptions being divided the names also are divided
along with them; and whatever is said properly, this also is God; and whatever
is according to nature, this is also truly named; if indeed the truth for us
is not in names, but in things. But they, as if fearing to move all things
against the truth, confess that the Son is God, when they are forced by the
argument and the testimonies, but equivocally and sharing only in the
appellation.

14. And when we retort to them: What then? Is the Son not properly God, just
as neither is the painted thing an animal? How then is he God, if he is not
properly God? For what prevents, they say, that these also be equivocal, and
both be said properly? And they will bring us the dog, the land one, and the
sea one, being both equivocal, and said properly, for there is also some such
species among equivocals, and if anything else uses the same designation, and
shares in it equally, being distant in nature. But there, my good man, setting
two natures under the same designation, you introduce neither as better than
the other, nor the one prior and the other later, nor the one more and the
other less being that which it is called. For nothing is yoked that furnishes
them this necessity. For neither is the one more a dog, and the other less a
dog than the other, as the sea one than the land one, or the land one
conversely than the sea one; for why, or according to what account? But in
things equal in honor and different is the sharing of the appellation. But
here, yoking to God the venerable, and being above every essence and nature,
which is of God alone and as it were the nature of Godhead, then giving this
to the Father, but stripping the Son and subordinating him, and assigning him
the second things of honor and of worship, even if in the syllables you grant
the like, in the thing you cut around the Godhead, and you pass over
maliciously from the equivocity that has equality to that which binds together
things not equal; so that your painted man and the living man come nearer to
the Godhead than the dogs of the example. Either grant to both, just as the
sharing of the appellation, so also the equality of honor of the natures, even
if you introduce them as different; and you have dissolved your dogs, which
you invented against the inequality. For what is the use of the equivocity, if
those divided by you should not have equality of honor? For not that you might
show things equal in honor, but that you might show things unequal in honor,
you took refuge in the equivocity and the dogs. How could one be more
convicted both fighting with himself and with Godhead?

15. And if when we say that the Father is greater than the Son by the cause,
they take in addition the premise The cause is by nature, and then conclude
the Greater by nature: I do not know whether they sophistically deceive
themselves, or those to whom the discourse is. For not simply as many things
as are said of something, these will also be said of the subject of this; but
it is clear of what, and what things. Since what prevents me too, having made
this premise, that the Father is greater by nature, then taking in addition
But by nature he is not altogether greater nor father, from there to conclude
the Greater is not altogether greater; or, The Father is not altogether
father. And if you wish thus: God is essence; but essence is not altogether
God; conclude the rest yourself: God is not altogether God. But I think this
paralogism is from the in some respect and the simply, as is customary to
those who are technical about these things. For we giving the greater to the
nature of the cause, they bring on the greater by nature; just as if when we
said that so-and-so is a dead human, they simply brought on the human.

16. And how shall we pass by that, being no less admirable than the things
said? The Father, he says, is a name of essence, or of energy? As if they will
bind us from both sides: if we say of essence, that we will agree that the Son
is of another essence, since there is one essence of God, and this, as these
say, the Father has preoccupied; but if of energy, that we will clearly
confess a made thing, but not an offspring. For where the one acting is, there
altogether also is the thing acted upon. And how is the thing made the same as
the one who has made, they will say they wonder. I myself would have been very
much in awe of your division, if it were necessary to accept one of the two,
and not, fleeing both, to say a third thing more true: that Father is neither
a name of essence, O most wise, nor of energy, but of relation and of how the
Father is toward the Son, or the Son toward the Father. For as among us these
appellations make known the genuine and the kindred, so there too they signify
the sameness of nature of the one begotten toward the one who has begotten.
But let it be, for your sake, that the Father is also something of essence; he
will bring in the Son with him, he will not alienate him, according to the
common conceptions and the force of these appellations. Let it be also of
energy, if this seems good; not even thus will you catch us. He would have
effected this very thing, the consubstantial, even if otherwise the assumption
of an energy about this is absurd. Do you see how, even when you wish to fight
badly, we escape your twists? And since we have known your invincibility in
reasonings and in twists, let us see also your strength from the divine
oracles, if perhaps you will accept to persuade us from there too.

17. For we have both grasped and we proclaim the Godhead of the Son from great
and high voices. Which are these? That of God, that of Word, the one in the
beginning, the one with the beginning, the beginning: In the beginning was the
Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God; and, With you is the
beginning; and, Who calls it beginning from generations. Since he is
only-begotten Son: The only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father,
he has declared him. Way, truth, life, light: I am the way, and the truth, and
the life; and, I am the light of the world. Wisdom, power: Christ the power of
God, and the wisdom of God. Effulgence, impress, image, seal: Who being the
effulgence of the glory and the impress of his hypostasis; and, Image of the
goodness; and, For him the Father has sealed, God. Lord, king, the one who is,
the Almighty: The Lord rained fire from the Lord; and, A rod of uprightness is
the rod of your kingdom; and, Who is, and who was, and who is coming, and the
Almighty. Things said clearly about the Son, and as many as are of the same
force as these, none of which is acquired, nor later added to the Son, or to
the Spirit, just as neither to the Father himself. For the perfect is not from
addition. For there was not when he was without Word, nor was there when he
was not Father, nor was there when he was not true, or unwise, or powerless,
or lacking life, or brightness, or goodness.

18. But you enumerate against these the sayings of ingratitude: my God and
your God, the greater, he created, he made, he sanctified. And if you wish,
also the slave, and the obedient; he has given, he learned, he has been
commanded, he has been sent, the not being able to do anything from himself,
or to say, or to judge, or to give, or to will. And still also these: the
ignorance, the subjection, the prayer, the asking, the progress, the
perfection. Add, if you wish, also as many as are humbler than these: the
sleeping, the hungering, the being weary, the weeping, the being in agony, the
hiding himself. And perhaps you would also reproach the cross, and the death.
For the rising and the ascension you seem to me to pass by, since something
for us too is found in these. And you might still gather many besides these,
if you wished to compose your equivocal and interpolated God, but for us true
and equal in honor with the Father. For each of these is not hard, even going
through them part by part, to interpret for you toward the most pious sense,
and to cleanse the stumbling-block in the letters, if indeed you truly
stumble, and do not willingly do mischief. And in one heading: bring the
higher things to the Godhead and to the nature better than passions and body;
and the humbler things to the compound, and to him who was emptied and
incarnate for you, and it is no worse to say, also humanized, then also
exalted, that you, having dissolved the fleshly and groveling of your dogmas,
may learn to be higher, and to rise together with Godhead, and not to remain
in the things seen, but to be lifted with the things thought, and to know what
is the account of nature, and what is the account of economy.

19. For this one now despised by you, there was when he was also above you;
the one now human was also uncompounded. What he was, he remained; what he was
not, he took on. In the beginning he was without cause; for what is the cause
of God? But also later he came to be for a cause (and it was that you the
insulter might be saved, who for this despise Godhead, because he accepted
your thickness), conversing with flesh through the medium of mind, and
becoming a human, the God below; since he was mixed with God, and became one,
the better prevailing, that I might become as much God as that one became

manger, but he was glorified by angels, and indicated by a star, and worshiped
by magi. How do you stumble at what is seen, not considering what is thought?
He was fled into Egypt, but he puts to flight the things of the Egyptians. He
had no form nor beauty among the Jews, but to David he was fair in beauty
beyond the sons of humans, but on the mountain he flashes, and becomes more
light-formed than the sun, initiating into the things to come.

20. He was baptized as a human, but he loosed sins as God; not himself needing
purifications, but that he might sanctify the waters. He was tempted as a
human, but he conquered as God; but he commands to take courage, as having
conquered the world. He hungered, but he fed thousands, but he is living and
heavenly bread. He thirsted, but he cried: If anyone thirsts, let him come to
me, and drink; but he also promised that those who believe would gush with
springs. He was weary, but he is rest of those who labor and are heavy-laden.
He was weighed down with sleep, but he is lightened upon the sea, but he
rebukes winds, but he lightens Peter being baptized. He pays tax, but from a
fish, but he is king of those who demand. He hears Samaritan and having a
demon, yet he saves the one going down from Jerusalem and falling among
robbers, yet he is recognized by demons, and he drives out demons, and he
sinks a legion of spirits, and he sees the ruler of the demons falling as
lightning. He is stoned, but he is not caught. He prays, but he hears. He
weeps, but he stops a tear. He asks where Lazarus has been laid, for he was
human; but he raises Lazarus, for he was God. He is sold, and very cheaply,
for for thirty silver pieces, but he redeems the world, and at a great price,
for of his own blood. He is led as a sheep to slaughter, but he shepherds
Israel, and now also all the inhabited world. As a lamb voiceless, but he is
Word, proclaimed by the voice of one crying in the wilderness. He is weakened,
and wounded, but he heals every disease, and every infirmity. He is led up
onto the wood, he is fastened, but he restores by the wood of life, but he
saves even a robber crucified with him, but he darkens all that is seen. He is
given vinegar to drink, he is fed gall; who? The one who changed the water
into wine, the dissolver of the bitter taste, the sweetness and wholly desire.
He gives over the soul, but he has authority to take it again, but the veil is
torn, for the things above are shown, but rocks are split, but the dead are
raised. He dies, but he makes alive, and he dissolves death by death. He is
buried, but he rises. He goes down into Hades, but he leads up souls, but he
goes up into heavens, but he will come to judge living and dead, and to
torment such arguments. If these furnish you the occasion of the error, those
dissolve your error.

21. These things from us to the riddlers, not willingly; for chatter and
opposition of words is not sweet to the faithful, for even one opponent is
enough; yet necessarily because of those who fall upon us, since also because
of diseases the medicines, that they may know they are not altogether wise nor
unconquered in the superfluous things that empty the gospel. For when we put
forward the power of the word, leaving off believing, and dissolve the
trustworthiness of the Spirit by inquiries, then the word is defeated by the
greatness of the things, and it will be defeated altogether, starting from a
weak instrument, our mind, what happens? The weakness of the word of the
mystery appears; and thus the cleverness of the word is shown to be an
emptying of the cross, as also seems good to Paul. For faith is the fullness
of our word. And may he who announces bonds, and looses things held, who also
brought it to our mind to dissolve the knots of violent dogmas, especially
even converting these make them faithful instead of technicians, and
Christians instead of what they are now named. This then we also exhort; we
beg on behalf of Christ; be reconciled to God, and do not quench the Spirit;
or rather, may Christ be reconciled to you, and may the Spirit shine forth at
last. But if you should be very contentious, yet may we at least keep the
Trinity for ourselves, and be saved by the Trinity, remaining sincere and
without stumbling, until a more perfect manifestation of the things longed
for, in Christ himself our Lord, to whom be the glory unto the ages; Amen.
