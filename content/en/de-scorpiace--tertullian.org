* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The earth throws up a great evil from a little scorpion, as if by a festering.
As many poisons as there are kinds, as many destructions as there are species,
as many pains as there are colors. Nicander writes of them and paints them.
And yet one movement of assault is common to them all: to harm from the tail,
and the tail will be whatever is prolonged from the hindmost part of the body
and strikes. Accordingly that succession of knots in the scorpion, a thin
poisoned veinlet inside, rising with a bow-like bound, draws tight a barbed
sting at the end after the manner of an engine. From this they also name from
the scorpion the war-machine that by drawing back gives force to its missiles.
That sting is also a pipe, open in its thinness into the wound, and it pours
out the venom where it pierces. The familiar time of danger is summer;
fierceness sets sail with the south wind and the southwest. Among remedies the
natural ones do most; magic also binds something around; medicine meets it
with iron and cup. For some also drink beforehand, hastening a protection; but
intercourse drains it off, and they thirst again. For us faith is a defense,
if it is not itself also struck by distrust, in immediately signing and
adjuring and anointing the heel of the beast. In this way, finally, we often
aid even the heathen, having been given by God that power which the apostle
dedicated when he despised the viper's bite.

What then does this pen promise, if faith is safe from its own store? That it
may be safe from its own store at other times also, when it suffers its own
scorpions. These too have a bitter smallness, and a varied kind, and they are
armed in one way, and they are fitted out at a definite time, and no other
than one of heat. This among Christians is persecution. When therefore faith
burns and the church is on fire after the figure of the bush, then the
Gnostics break out, then the Valentinians creep forth, then all the opponents
of martyrdoms bubble up, themselves also hot to strike, to pierce, to kill.
For because they know that many are simple and untrained, then weak, and a
great many Christians with the wind and if it please them, they never judge
they should be approached more than when fear has opened the entrance of the
soul, especially when some atrocity has already crowned the faith of the
martyrs. So first, still drawing the tail from the feelings, they apply it, or
they lash as if in empty space: Must innocent men suffer these things? So that
you may think it a brother or one of the better heathen. Must a sect that
troubles no one be treated so? Then they drive it in: Men perish without
cause. For to perish, and without cause, is the first piercing. Then they
already strike to kill. But simple souls do not know what is written and how,
where and when and before whom one must confess, except that this is not even
simplicity but vanity, rather madness, to die for God, as for one who would
save me. If he kills, who ought to save? Once Christ died for us, once he was
slain that we might not be slain. If he demands a return, does he too look for
salvation from my death? Or does God demand the blood of men, especially if he
refuses that of bulls and goats? Surely he prefers the repentance of the
sinner to his death. And how then does he not desire the death of those who
are not sinners? These things, and whatever other inventions of heretical
poisons there are, whom will they not pierce even into scruple if not into
ruin, or into bile if not into death?

But you, if faith is awake, dash the scorpion on the spot with a curse as with
a sandal, and leave it dying in its own stupor. But if it has filled the
wound, it drives the poison in and hastens into the bowels; at once all the
former senses grow dull, the blood of the mind freezes, the flesh of the
spirit wastes, a nausea of the name grows sour. Already the mind itself seeks
a place to vomit, and so, once for all, the weakness by which it was struck
breathes out wounded faith either into heresy or into the world. And now the
present state of things is the mid heat, the very dog-star of persecution,
from the dog-headed one himself, of course. The fire has tried some
Christians, the sword others, the beasts others; others hunger in prison for
martyrdoms already tasted meanwhile with clubs and claws besides. We
ourselves, like hares, the appointed hunt, are hemmed in from far off, and the
heretics range after their custom. So the time has warned me to mix with the
pen our antidote against our own little beasts. You who read will drink. Nor
is the draught bitter. If the utterances of the Lord are sweeter than honey
and the honeycomb, the spices are from there. If the promise of God flows with
milk and honey, those things taste of this which make toward that place. But
woe to those who turn sweet into bitter and light into darkness! For in the
same way those who oppose martyrdoms, interpreting salvation as destruction,
recast sweet into bitter as they do light into darkness, and so, by preferring
this most wretched life to that most blessed one, they put bitter for sweet
and darkness for light.

* Chapter 2

:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But not yet about the good of martyrdom, unless first about the duty of it,
nor about its usefulness before we must learn its necessity. Divine authority
goes first, whether God has willed and commanded anything of the kind, so that
those who deny it is good may not be persuaded that it is fitting until they
have been subdued. It is proper that heretics be compelled to duty, not
enticed. Hardness must be conquered, not coaxed. And certainly that will be
judged quite good enough which will be shown to have been appointed and
commanded by God. Let the Gospels wait a little, while I press out their root,
the Law, while I draw the will of God from the place where I also recognize
him himself: I am, he says, God, your God, who brought you out of the land of
Egypt. You shall have no other gods besides me. You shall not make for
yourself an image of those things which are in heaven and which are on the
earth below and which are in the sea under the earth. You shall not adore
them, nor serve them. For I am the Lord your God. Likewise in the same Exodus:
You yourselves have seen that I have spoken to you from heaven. You shall not
make for yourselves gods of silver, and gods of gold you shall not make for
yourselves. To the same effect also in Deuteronomy: Hear, Israel, the Lord
your God is one, and you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and
with all your strength and with all your soul. And again: Do not forget the
Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt from the house of
bondage. You shall fear the Lord your God, and him alone you shall serve, and
to him you shall cling, and in his name you shall swear. You shall not go
after strange gods and the gods of the nations which are round about you,
because the Lord your God is also a jealous God among you, and lest he be
angry and indignant and destroy you from the face of the earth.

But also, setting before them blessings and curses: Blessings, he says, if you
hear the commandments of the Lord your God, whatever I command you today, and
do not wander from the way which I have commanded you, so as to go away and
serve other gods whom you do not know. And about rooting them out in every
way: You shall destroy with destruction all the places in which the nations
whom you shall possess by inheritance served their gods, upon the mountains
and hills and under dense trees; and you shall dig up their altars, you shall
overthrow and break in pieces their standing images, and you shall cut down
their groves, and you shall burn with fire the graven things of those gods
themselves, and you shall destroy their name from that place. He still presses
it, when they had entered the land of promise and had destroyed its nations:
Take heed to yourself, lest you follow them after they have been destroyed
from before your face, lest you inquire after their gods, saying, How do the
nations serve their gods, that I also may do so. But also, If a prophet
himself, he says, arise among you, or a dreamer of dreams, and give you a sign
or a wonder, and it come, and he say, Let us go and serve other gods whom you
do not know, you shall not hear the word of that prophet or dreamer, because
the Lord your God is proving you, whether you fear God with all your heart and
with all your soul. You shall go after the Lord your God, and him you shall
fear, and you shall keep his commandments, and you shall hear his voice, and
you shall serve him, and to him you shall be joined. But that prophet or
dreamer shall die; for he has spoken to turn you away from the Lord your God.
But also in another chapter: If however your brother from father or mother, or
your son, or your daughter, or the wife who is in your bosom, or a friend who
is as your own soul, ask you secretly, saying, Let us go and serve other gods,
whom you do not know, nor your fathers, of the gods of the nations which are
round about you near or far, you shall not be willing to go with him, and you
shall not hear him. Your eye shall not spare him, nor shall you desire him nor
save him; announcing you shall announce concerning him. Your hands shall be
upon him first to kill him, and the hands of your people last, and you shall
stone him, and he shall die, because he has sought to turn you away from the
Lord your God.

He adds also concerning cities, that if it appeared that one of them, by the
persuasion of unrighteous men, had gone over to other gods, all who dwelt in
it should be killed, and all its things should become devoted, and all its
spoil should be gathered into all its exits, and they should be burned with
fire in all its ways, even with all the people, in the sight of the Lord God,
and it shall not be habitable, he says, forever; it shall not be built again
any more, nor shall anything of its devoted spoil cling to your hands, that
the Lord may turn from the indignation of his anger. He has also committed the
order of curses from the execration of idols: Cursed is the man who shall make
a graven or molten thing, an abomination, the work of the hands of a
craftsman, and shall set it in a secret place. But in Leviticus: Do not
follow, he says, idols, and molten gods you shall not make for yourselves. I
am the Lord your God. And elsewhere: The children of Israel are my household
servants. These are they whom I brought out of the land of Egypt. I am the
Lord your God. You shall not make for yourselves things made by hand, nor
shall you set up a graven standing image for yourselves. Nor shall you set a
marked stone in your land. I am the Lord your God. And these things were first
spoken by the Lord through Moses, belonging of course to whomsoever the Lord
God of Israel shall likewise bring out of the Egypt of a most superstitious
world and out of the house of human bondage. But thereafter also every mouth
of the prophets sounds with the voices of the same God, heaping up the same
law of his by a renewal of the same commands, and announcing first nothing so
principally as to beware of every making and worship of idols; as through
David: The gods of the nations are silver and gold; they have eyes and do not
see, they have ears and do not hear, they have noses and do not smell, a
mouth, and they do not speak, hands, and they do not handle, feet, and they do
not walk. Like them shall be those who make them and who trust in them.

Nor should I think it needful to discuss whether God worthily forbids his own
name and honor to be given over to a lie, or whether he worthily refuses to
let those whom he has torn from the error of superstition go back again into
Egypt, or whether he worthily does not suffer those whom he has chosen for
himself to depart from him. So neither will that wait to be treated by us,
whether he has wished the discipline which he wished to institute to be kept,
and whether he justly avenges the desertion of that which he wished to be
observed, since he would have instituted it in vain if he had not wished it
kept, and would have wished it kept in vain if he had been unwilling to avenge
it. It follows, then, that I prove these definitions of God against
superstitions, both as overcome and as also punished, since from these the
whole account of martyrdoms will stand. Moses was away with God on the
mountain, when the people, impatient of so necessary an absence of his, seek
to bring forth gods for themselves, which he himself will rather destroy.
Aaron is pressed, and he orders the earrings of their women to be brought
together into the fire. For they were about to lose, as a judgment upon
themselves, the true ornaments of the ears, the voices of God. The wise fire
casts for them the likeness of a calf, taunting them with having the heart
where they have the treasure also, in Egypt of course, which among the other
animals consecrated a certain ox as well. So three thousand men slain by their
nearest kinsmen, because they had offended so near a kinsman, God, dedicated
both the beginnings and the deserts of the transgression.

In Numbers, when Israel had turned aside at Sethim, they go away to the
daughters of Moab to gratify lust, they are invited to the idols, so that they
may fornicate in spirit also; they eat, finally, of their polluted things,
then they also adore the gods of the nation and are initiated to Beelphegor.
For this idolatry too, sister of adultery, twenty-three thousand cut down by
household swords made a libation to the divine anger. After the death of
Joshua son of Nave they forsake the God of their fathers and serve the idols
of Baalim and Ashtaroth, and the Lord in anger delivered them into the hands
of spoilers, and they were spoiled by them and sold to enemies, and they could
not stand at all before the face of their enemies. Wherever they went forth, a
hand was upon them for evil, and they were crushed greatly. After which God
set over them Critas, whom we understand as Censors. But they did not persist
in obeying even these. As soon as one of the Critas had died, they went on to
offend beyond their fathers, by going after the gods of others and serving and
adoring them. So the Lord was angry. Since indeed, he says, this nation has
transgressed my covenant which I arranged with their fathers, and they have
not heard my voice, I also will not take care to remove a man from before
their face out of the nations which Joshua left when he departed. And so,
through almost all the annals of the Critas and thereafter of the Kings, with
the strength of the surrounding nations reserved, he dispensed wrath to Israel
by war and captivity and the yoke of foreigners, as often as they wandered
from him, especially into idolatry.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

If therefore it is established from the beginning that this has both been
forbidden, from so many and so great commands, and has never been committed
with impunity, from so many and so great proofs, and that no crime is counted
so proud before God as a transgression of this kind, we ought of ourselves to
understand that the aim of the divine denunciations and executions already
then patronized martyrdoms, not only as not to be doubted, but even as to be
endured, for which of course he had made room by forbidding idolatry. For
otherwise martyrdoms would not come about. And certainly he had built his
authority in advance, willing those things to come about for which he had made
room. For now we are stung concerning the will of God, and the scorpion
doubles the blow, denying this will, accusing this will, so that he either
insinuates another god whose will this is not, or none the less destroys ours,
whose will is of this kind, or altogether denies the will of God, if he cannot
deny him. But we, fighting elsewhere about God and about the rest of the body
of each heretical doctrine, now draw definite lines for one form of encounter,
defending that will which made room for martyrdoms as belonging to no other
God than(??) Israel's, as much from the commands of an always forbidden as
from the judgments of a punished idolatry.

For if a command observed suffers violence, this will be, in a manner, a
command of observing the command, that I suffer that through which I shall be
able to observe the command, namely the violence, whatever threatens me as I
beware of idolatry. And certainly he who imposes a command extorts obedience.
He cannot therefore have been unwilling that those things should come about
through which obedience will stand. It is prescribed to me that I name no
other god, lest even by speaking, no less with the tongue than with the hand,
I fashion a god, and that I adore no other or in any way venerate any besides
that only one who so commands, whom I am also ordered to fear, lest I be
forsaken by him, and to love from my whole substance, that I may die for him.
Serving as a soldier under this oath, I am challenged by the enemy. I am equal
to them if I surrender to them. In defending this I fight in the line, I am
wounded, I am cut down, I am killed. Who wished this end for his soldier, if
not he who sealed him with such an oath?

* Chapter 5

You have therefore the will of my God. This sting has been met. Let us look to
another blow concerning the quality of the will. It is long work to show that
my God is good, which the Marcionites have already learned from us. For the
present it is enough that he is called God, so that it is necessary he be
believed good. For who will presume an evil god? He will not be able to stand
in both: he will have either to deny as God him whom he has thought evil, or
to hold as good him whom he has pronounced God. Good, therefore, will also be
the will of him who, unless he is good, will not be God. The goodness of the
thing itself which God has willed will also prove this, of martyrdom I mean,
because none but a good one has willed what is good. I contend that martyrdom
is good with the same God by whom idolatry is forbidden and punished. For
martyrdom strives against and opposes idolatry. But nothing can strive against
and oppose evil except good. Not as if I denied that there is rivalry both of
evils among themselves and of goods as well; but the condition of this title
is different. For martyrdom does not contend with idolatry from some common
malice, but from its own grace; for it delivers from idolatry. Who will not
pronounce that good which delivers from evil? What else is the opposition of
idolatry and martyrdom than that of death and life? Life will be counted to
martyrdom as much as idolatry to death. He who shall have called life an evil
has death, which he may call a good. There is also this perversity of men, to
throw off what is saving, to take up what is deadly, to avoid all dangerous
medicines, or finally to desire to die sooner than to be cured. For there are
also many who flee the aid of medicine, many fools, many timid and wrongly
modest.

And there is plainly a kind of cruelty in medicine, from the lancet and from
the cautery, from the burning of mustard; yet to be cut and burned and
stretched and bitten is not on that account an evil, because it brings useful
pains, nor will it be refused because it only grieves, but because it
necessarily grieves it will be applied. The fruit excuses the horror of the
work. In short, that man howling and groaning and bellowing in the physician's
hands will presently load those same hands with a fee and will proclaim them
the best operators and will now deny that they are cruel. So martyrdoms also
rage, but unto salvation. It will be lawful for God also to cure unto eternal
life by fires and swords and all bitter things. But you will admire the
physician even in this, that he generally applies like qualities of remedies
against the qualities of complaints, when he aids, as it were, from the
reverse, helping by those things through which the suffering comes. For he
both checks heats by loading heats more heavily, and quenches burnings rather
by wasting them with thirst, and gathers the excess of bile by bitter little
draughts, and recalls a flow of blood by letting a vein besides. But you will
think God, and a jealous God at that, must be blamed, if he has willed to
contend with the cause and to do good by rivaling the injury, to dissolve
death by death, to scatter killing by killing, to shake off torments by
torments, to evaporate punishments by punishments, to confer life by taking it
away, to help the flesh by hurting it, to save the soul by snatching it. The
perversity which you think is reason; what you count cruelty is grace.

So, as God cures eternal things from momentary ones, magnify your God for your
good; you have fallen into his hands, but you have fallen happily. He too has
fallen upon your sicknesses. Man always first makes work for the physician; in
short, he has drawn the danger of death upon himself. He had received from his
Lord, as from a physician, a discipline useful enough according to the law of
living, that he should eat of all things indeed, and refrain from one tree
only, which the physician himself knew as untimely for the present. He heard
the one he preferred, and he broke abstinence. He ate the forbidden and,
filled with transgression, he ripened into death, most worthy, in good faith,
to perish altogether, who so wished. But the Lord, having borne the fever of
the offense until in time the medicine might be mixed, gradually composed the
remedies, all the disciplines of faith, themselves also rivals of the vice,
cutting away the word of death by the word of life, filing down the hearing of
transgression by the hearing of devotion. So even when that physician commands
one to die, he drives out the lethargy of death. Why is man now burdened to
suffer from the remedy what he was not then burdened to suffer from the vice?
Does it displease him to be killed unto salvation, whom it did not displease
to be killed unto ruin? Will he nauseate at the antidote who gaped at the
poison?

But if God had set martyrdoms before us under the name of a contest, through
which we might try it with the adversary, so that the man who was gladly
thrown by him may now steadily throw him, here too the liberality of God
stands over it more than his harshness. For he wished to make the man already
plucked from the devil's throat by faith also his trampler by virtue, so that
he might not only have escaped the enemy but also have conquered him. He who
had called into salvation loved to invite also to glory, so that we who were
glad at being freed may also exult at being crowned. How much favor the world
gives those games, the contentious solemnities and superstitious contests of
the Greeks, both of religions and of pleasures, Africa too has now made clear.
As yet the several cities still disturb Carthage with congratulations, granted
a Pythian contest after the old age of the stadium. So from of old it has been
thought most worthy to commit an experiment of studies, to match arts, to
express the present state of bodies and of voices, with the prize as reporter,
the spectacle as judge, pleasure as the sentence. Where the fights are bare,
the wounds are not none; fists shake, heels ram, the cestus tears, whips rend.
Yet no one will insult the president of the contest because he exposes men to
violence. Actions for injuries lie outside the stadium. But he will look to
how much those bruises and gore and weals trade for, namely crowns and glory
and a dowry, public privileges, civic stipends, images, statues, and, such
eternity as the world can furnish, from fame, a resurrection from memory. The
boxer himself does not complain that he hurts, for he wishes it; the crown
presses the wounds, the palm hides the blood; victory swells more than the
injury. Will you think this man hurt, whom you see glad? But not even the
vanquished himself will reproach the giver of the contest for his fall.

Will it be unbecoming for God to bring his own arts and disciplines out into
the open, into this space of the world, into a spectacle for men and angels
and all powers? to prove flesh and soul from constancy and endurance? to give
this one the palm, this one honor, that one citizenship, that one pay? even to
reject some, and to remove them chastised with disgrace? For you prescribe to
God, no doubt, at what times or by what modes or in what places he should
judge concerning his own household, as if it were not also fitting for a judge
to give a prior judgment. What now, if he had not set faith forth into
martyrdoms under the name of a contest, but of its own advancement, ought it
not to have some heap of hope, on which it might compel its zeal and hang its
vow, by which it might strive to ascend, when earthly offices also burn toward
grades? Or how are there many mansions with the Father, if not according to
the variety of merits? How also will star differ from star in glory, except
according to the diversity of rays? Further, if some furthering of loftiness
and brightness was therefore fitting for faith, that gain ought to have been
of such a kind as would cost great labor, torment, torture, death. But look at
the compensation, when flesh and soul are paid down, than which nothing in man
is dearer, the one the hand of God, the other his breath: the very things
whose advancement it is are paid down in the advancement; the very things
which become the gain are expended; the same are the prices which are also the
wages.

God had also foreseen other weaknesses of the human condition, the snares of
the adversary, the deceits of things, the nets of the world, that faith would
be in danger even after the washing, that a great many would perish again
after salvation, who had soiled the wedding garment, who had not prepared oil
for their torches, who would have to be sought over mountains and woods and
brought back on the shoulders. He therefore set second comforts and last
defenses, the fight of martyrdom and the washing of blood thereafter secure.
Of whose happiness David says: Blessed are they whose iniquities have been
forgiven and whose sins have been covered. Blessed is the man to whom God will
not impute offense. For strictly nothing can any longer be reckoned against
the martyrs, by whom in the washing life itself is laid down. So love covers a
multitude of sins, which, loving God of course with all its strength, with
which it contends in martyrdom, with all its soul, which it lays down for God,
forges a man into a martyr. Will you call these remedies, counsels, judgments,
even spectacles of God, atrocity? Does God desire the blood of man? And yet I
would dare to say so, if man also desires the kingdom of God, if man desires a
sure salvation, if man also desires a second regeneration. No compensation is
invidious in which the account of either grace or injury is common.

Let the scorpion still strike, waving a murderer-God; I shall plainly shudder
at the foul breath of blasphemy stinking from a heretical mouth, but I will
also embrace such a God from confidence of reason, by which reason he himself
also pronounced himself more than a murderer from the person of his own
Wisdom, by the voice of Solomon. Wisdom, he says, has slain her sons. Sophia
is wisdom. She has slain wisely, of course, while it is unto life, and
reasonably, while it is unto glory. O the genius of parricide! O the craft of
crime! O the argument of cruelty, which kills for this reason, that he whom it
has killed may not die! And therefore what follows? Wisdom is sung with hymns
in the exits; for the exit of martyrs also is sung. Wisdom treats of constancy
in the streets; for she slays her sons well. But upon the highest walls she
speaks with confidence, when indeed according to Isaiah this one cries, I am
God's; and this one shouts, In the name of Jacob; and another writes, In the
name of Israel. O good mother! I myself also wish to be put among her sons,
that I may be killed by her; I wish to be killed, that I may become a son. But
does she only slay her sons, or also torture them? For I hear God also saying
elsewhere: I will burn them as gold is burned, and I will prove them as silver
is proved. Of course through the torments of fires and punishments, through
martyrdoms that examine faith. The apostle also knows what kind of God he has
ascribed, when he writes: If God did not spare his own Son, but delivered him
up for us, how has he not also with him granted us all things? You see how
divine Wisdom has slain even her own proper first-born and only-begotten Son,
who is of course going to live, nay, also going to bring the rest back into
life. I can say with the Wisdom of God: It is Christ who delivered himself up
for our offenses. Already Wisdom has butchered herself also.

Words savor not by sound alone but also by sense, and they are to be heard not
only with ears but also with minds. He who does not understand believes God
cruel. And yet even for him who does not understand a sentence has been set
which may restrain the rashness of understanding otherwise. For who, he says,
has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counsellor, to instruct
him, or who has shown him the way of understanding? But indeed it has been
lawful in the world for Diana of the Scythians or Mercury of the Gauls or
Saturn of the Africans to be appeased by victims of men, and in Latium to this
day human blood is tasted by Jupiter in the midst of the city, and no one
treats it, or does not presume it by some reason or by the incalculable will
of his own god. If our God too had demanded martyrdoms for himself under the
name of his own victim, who would have reproached him with a deadly religion
and mournful rites and an altar that is a pyre and an undertaker-priest, and
would not rather have counted more blessed the man whom God had eaten?

We stand therefore on one step, and we challenge on this alone, whether
martyrdoms have been commanded by God, so that you may believe them commanded
by reason, if you have learned that they have been commanded, because God has
commanded nothing without reason. Since the death of his own religious ones is
honored with him, as David sings, not, I think, that common death and the debt
of all (rather that one is even disgraceful from the charge of transgression
and the desert of condemnation), but that death which is entered in him from
the testimony of religion and the battle of confession for righteousness and
the sacrament. As Isaiah, See, he says, how the righteous man perishes, and no
one takes it to heart, and righteous men are taken away, and no one notices;
for from the face of unrighteousness the righteous man perishes, and there
shall be honor of his burial. Here too you have both a preaching and a reward
of martyrdoms. For from the beginning righteousness suffers violence. At once,
as soon as God began to be worshipped, religion received ill-will as its lot.
He who had pleased God is slain, and that by a brother. That ungodliness might
the more readily hunt the blood of a stranger, it began the pursuit from its
own, and finally not only that of the righteous but even of the prophets also.
David is harassed, Elijah is put to flight, Jeremiah is stoned, Isaiah is
sawn, Zechariah is butchered between the altar and the house, assigning to the
flints the lasting stains of his blood. That very close of the law and the
prophets, called not a prophet but an angel, is cut down by an insulting
slaughter for the hire of a dancing girl. And certainly those who were led by
the Spirit of God were directed by him into martyrdoms, so that they already
had to suffer also what they had preached.

So also the threefold brotherhood, when the dedication of a royal image was
pressing the city with the duty, were not ignorant of what faith, which alone
in them had not been captive, required, namely that one must die against
idolatry. For they remembered also Jeremiah writing to those upon whom that
captivity was impending: And now you shall see the gods of the Babylonians, of
gold and silver and wood, carried upon shoulders, displaying fear to the
nations. Beware therefore lest you also be like the foreigners and be seized
with fear, while you look at the crowds adoring behind them and before, but
say in your mind: You, Lord, we ought to adore. So they spoke with confidence
conceived from God, when with vigor of soul they shake off the king's
conditional threats: We have no necessity of answering this command of yours.
For there is our God, whom we worship, able to deliver us from the furnace of
fire and from your hands, and then it will be plain to you that we will
neither serve your idol nor adore your golden image which you have set up. O
martyrdom perfect even without suffering! They suffered enough, they were
burned enough, whom God for that reason covered, lest they should seem to have
lied about his power. For the lions' pent-up and accustomed savageness would
at once, of course, have devoured Daniel also, a suppliant of none but God and
therefore accused and demanded by the Chaldeans, if the worthy presumption of
Darius concerning God had been bound to be deceived. For the rest, every
preacher and worshipper of God who, when challenged to idolatry, had refused
obedience, ought to have suffered, according also to the standing of that
reason by which the truth ought to have been commended both to those then
present and to those coming after, for which the suffering of its own
defenders would speak trust, because no one would have wished to be killed
unless possessed of the truth. Such commands and examples from the beginning
show faith to be a debtor of martyrdom.

It remains, lest antiquity should perhaps have had the sacrament as its own,
to review Christian newness, as if it too were from another God and therefore
rival also in discipline, whose wisdom does not know how to slay her own sons.
Clearly, in Christ both divinity and will and school are other! He will have
commanded either no martyrdoms at all, or ones to be understood otherwise,
being one who would urge no one to a risk of this kind, who would promise
nothing to those who have suffered for him, because he does not wish them to
suffer; and therefore, drawing out the first of the commands, Blessed, he
says, are they who suffer persecution for righteousness, because theirs is the
kingdom of heaven? This indeed absolutely to all; then properly to the
apostles themselves: Blessed shall you be when they have disgraced you and
persecuted you and said all evils against you for my sake. Rejoice and exult,
because your reward is very great in heaven; for so their fathers used to do
also to the prophets: so that he even foretold that they themselves also would
have to be killed after the example of the prophets. And yet even if he had
destined all this conditional persecution to the apostles alone then,
certainly through them, with the whole sacrament, with the shoot of the name,
with the layer of the Holy Spirit, the discipline of undergoing persecution
would have looked to us also, as to hereditary disciples and shoots of the
apostolic seed. For so again he directs to the apostles: Behold, I send you as
sheep in the midst of wolves, and: Beware of men; for they will deliver you up
to councils and in their synagogues they will scourge you, and you will be led
to governors and to kings for my sake, for a testimony to them and to the
nations, and the rest.

But when he adds: And brother will deliver brother and father son unto death,
and children will rise against parents and will put them to death, he has
plainly pronounced this unrighteousness upon the rest, which we do not find in
the apostles. For none of them suffered either a brother or a father as
betrayer, which very many of ours already have. Then he recalls it to the
apostles: And you will be hated by all for my name. How much more we, who must
be delivered even by parents? So, by disposing this very betrayal now to the
apostles, now to all, he pours out the same end of the name upon all in whom
the name has settled, with the law of its hatred. But he who has endured unto
the end, this one will be saved. By enduring what, if not persecution, if not
betrayal, if not killing? For to endure unto the end is nothing else than to
suffer the end. And therefore: The disciple is not above the master,
immediately follows, nor the servant above his lord, because when the master
and lord himself has endured persecution and betrayal and killing, much more
will servants and disciples have to pay out the same, lest they seem as if
superior, exempted from unrighteousness, when this itself ought to suffice
them for glory, to be equaled to the sufferings of the Lord and Master;
building them toward the endurance of which he warns that those are not to be
feared who kill the body only, but are not able to kill the soul, but that
fear is rather to be consecrated to him who can both kill and destroy body and
soul in gehenna. Who then are these slayers of the body only, if not the
governors and kings aforesaid, men I suppose? Who also is the lord of the
soul, if not God alone? Who is that, if not the threatener of fires, if not he
without whose will not even one of two sparrows falls to the earth, that is,
not even one of the two substances of man, flesh or soul? because with him the
number of the hairs also is recorded. Do not therefore fear. When he says
above: You are worth more than many sparrows, he does not promise in vain,
that is, not without profit, that they will fall to the earth, if we choose to
be killed by men rather than by God.

Everyone therefore who has confessed in me before men, I also will confess in
him before my Father who is in the heavens. And everyone who has denied me
before men, I also will deny him before my Father who is in the heavens. The
definition and the reason, as I think, are plain, of confession as of denial,
even if the arrangement is different. He who confesses himself a Christian
bears witness that he is Christ's; he who is Christ's must of necessity be in
Christ. If he is in Christ, he certainly confesses in Christ when he confesses
himself a Christian. For this cannot be, unless he is in Christ. Further, by
confessing in Christ he also confesses Christ, who is in Christ, while he
himself also is in him, as being a Christian. For if you have said day, you
have shown the thing of light which furnishes the day, even if you have not
said light. So even if he has not directly pronounced, Who has confessed me,
the act of daily confession is not different from the sense of the Lord's
pronouncement. For what he is who confesses himself, that is, a Christian, he
also confesses that through which he is it, that is, Christ. Likewise he who
has denied himself a Christian has denied in Christ, by denying that he is in
Christ while he denies that he is a Christian; and by denying Christ in
himself, while he denies himself in Christ, he will deny Christ also. So both
he who has denied in Christ will deny Christ, and he who has confessed in
Christ will confess Christ. It would therefore have been enough even if the
Lord had pronounced only about confessing. For from the form of confession a
prior judgment would be made also of its contrary, that is, of denial, that
denial is repaid by the Lord with denial, just as confession with confession.
And therefore, since in the form of confession the condition of denial also is
understood, it appears that what the Lord pronounced otherwise about it than
about confession, by saying, Who has denied me, not, Who in me, belongs to
another mode of denial. For he had also foreseen that this violence would for
the most part follow in the storming of the name, so that he who had denied
himself a Christian would also be compelled, by blaspheming, to deny Christ
himself. As not long ago, alas, with the whole faith of certain ones, we
shuddered at a struggle waged in this way. So it will be in vain to say: Even
if I have denied myself a Christian, I shall not be denied by Christ, for I
have not denied him himself. For he will be held by that denial just as much,
by which, denying himself a Christian, denying Christ in himself, he has
denied him also. There is more, because he also threatens shame with shame:
Who has been ashamed of me before men, I also will be ashamed of him before my
Father who is in the heavens. For he knew that denial is formed especially
from shame; that the state of the mind stands in the forehead; that the wound
of shame is prior to that of the body.

But those who think that confession has not been appointed here, that is, not
within this circuit of earth, nor through this passage of life, nor before men
of this common nature, what a presumption it is against the whole order of
things that have to be undergone in these lands and in this life and under
human powers! No doubt when souls have gone out from bodies and have begun to
be examined through the several stories of the heavens concerning reception,
and to be asked those secret sacraments of the heretics, then one must confess
before the true powers and the true men, the Teleti of course and the
Abascanti and the Acineti of Valentinus! For our men, they say, not even the
Demiurge himself steadily approved as men, whom he counted a drop of a bucket
and dust of a threshing-floor and spittle and locusts, and even equaled to
irrational beasts. Clearly, so it is written. Yet not therefore is another
kind of man to be understood besides us, which, because it is established to
exist, could take on a comparison, the property of the kind and the uniqueness
being saved. For if the life has been tainted, so that judged to contempt it
might be compared to things despised, the nature has not at once been taken
away, so that another might be counted under its name. Rather the nature is
kept, even if the life is put to shame, nor does Christ know other men than
those of whom he says: Whom do men say that I am? And: As you wish that men
should do to you, so also do you to them. See whether he has kept the kind
from whom he both expects a testimony of himself and upon whom he commands the
return of righteousness. But if I should demand that those heavenly men be
shown me, Aratus will more easily sketch Perseus and Cepheus and Erigone and
Ariadne among the stars.

But who prevented the Lord from determining clearly that confession of men was
also to be made there where he openly pronounced that his own would be, so
that it would have been put thus: Who has confessed in me before men in the
heavens, I also will confess in him before my Father who is in the heavens? He
ought to have snatched me from this error of an earthly confession, which he
would not have wished to be undertaken, if he had commanded a heavenly one,
because I knew no other men besides the inhabitants of the earth, not even man
himself having then yet been seen in the heavens. What further credibility of
things is it, that after departure, lifted to the things above, I should be
proved there, where I would not be set unless already proved, that I should be
examined there concerning a command, where I could not come unless I were to
be admitted? To the Christian heaven lies open before the way; because there
is no way into heaven except for him to whom heaven lies open; he who has
reached it will enter. What doorkeeper powers do you affirm to me according to
Roman superstition, a certain Carnus and Forculus and Limentinus? What powers
do you set in order at the railings? If you have ever read in David, Lift your
gates, you princes, and let the everlasting gates be lifted, and the King of
glory will enter, if you have likewise heard in Amos, Who builds his ascent
into the heavens, and founds his outpouring upon the earth, know that that
ascent has from then been levelled by the footsteps of the Lord and the
entrance from then opened by the strength of Christ, and that no delay or
questioning will meet Christians on the threshold, who will not have to be
discerned there but recognized, not interrogated but admitted. For even if you
still think heaven shut, remember that the Lord left its keys here to Peter
and through him to the Church, which each one interrogated and having
confessed here will carry with him.

But the devil asserts that one must confess there, so that he may persuade one
to deny here. Fine documents, to be sure, I shall send ahead, good keys I
shall carry with me, the fear of those who kill the body only, but do nothing
to the soul: I shall be commended by the desertion of this command, I shall
stand honorably in the heavenly places, who could not stand in the earthly, I
shall endure the greater powers, who yielded to the lesser, I shall finally
deserve to be admitted, already shut out. It is still possible to say: If one
must confess in the heavenly places, one must also deny here. For where the
one is, there is both. For rival things run together. Persecution also will
have to be stirred in the heavens, which is the matter of confession or
denial. Why then do you delay, most audacious heretic, to transfer the whole
order of the Christian shaking into the things above, and first of all to set
there the very hatred of the name, where Christ sits at the Father's right
hand? There you will also place the synagogues of the Jews, fountains of
persecutions, before which the apostles endured scourges, and the peoples of
the nations with their own circus, where they readily cry together: How long
the third race? But you ought also to exhibit there our brothers and fathers
and sons and mothers-in-law and daughters-in-law and those of our household,
through whom betrayal has been arranged; likewise kings and governors and
armed powers, before whom the cause must be fought. There will certainly also
be a prison in heaven, lacking the sun or ungratefully luminous, and bonds
perhaps from the zones, and the very axis which turns as a rack. Then if a
Christian is to be stoned, hailstones will be at hand; if to be burned,
thunderbolts are ready; if to be butchered, the office of armed Orion will
work; if to be ended by beasts, the north will send forth bears, the zodiac
bulls and lions. He who has endured these unto the end, this one will be
saved.

Is there then also an end in the heavens, and suffering, and killing, and the
first confession? And where is the flesh necessary for all these? Where the
body, which alone has to be killed by men? Unerring reason has commanded us
these things even in the manner of a play, nor will anyone thrust out the bar
of this prescription, so as not to be compelled to transfer the whole order of
persecution, the whole firm preparation of that cause, there where he has
given confession a forum. Since confession is led from persecution and
persecution is ended in confession, they cannot but follow together which
arrange both the entrance and the exit, that is, the beginning and the end.
Further, the hatred of the name will be here, and persecution breaks out here,
and betrayal brings forth here, and interrogation compels here, and the
butcher's work rages here, and confession or denial finishes this whole order
on the earth. Therefore if the rest are here, neither is confession elsewhere;
if confession is elsewhere, neither are the rest here. But in truth the rest
are not elsewhere, and so neither is confession in heaven. Or if they wish the
reason of a heavenly interrogation and confession to be another, they will
certainly also have to construct for it its own order, far other and diverse
from this arrangement which is noted in the scriptures. And we may be able to
say: Let them see to it, provided this order of earthly interrogation and
confession, running from the matter of persecution and of public discord
against the name, is kept safe to its own faith, so it must be believed as it
is also written, so it must be understood as it is heard. Here I endure the
whole order, the Lord himself not destining another region of the world. For
what does he add after the term of confession and denial? Do not think that I
have come to send peace on the earth, but a sword, certainly upon the earth.
For I have come to divide a man against his father and a daughter against her
mother and a mother-in-law against her daughter-in-law, and a man's enemies
will be those of his own household. For so it is brought about, that brother
delivers brother unto death and father son, and children rise against parents
and make them die. And he who has endured unto the end, let this one be saved.
So this whole order of the Lord's sword, not sent into heaven but into the
earth, sets confession also there, which by enduring unto the end will suffer
death.

By the same form, therefore, we defend that the rest also belong to the
standing of martyrdom. He who has made even his own soul of more account than
me, he says, is not worthy of me, that is, he who has preferred to live by
denying me rather than to die by confessing, and, He who has found his soul
will lose it, but he who has lost it for my sake will find it. For likewise he
finds it who denies, making a gain of life, but he will lose it in gehenna who
thinks that by denying he makes a gain of it. But he who is killed for
confessing will lose it for the present, yet will also find it unto eternal
life. Your life! they say, and, Do not lose your soul! How would Christ speak,
if not as the Christian would be treated? But when he forbids meditating an
answer at the tribunal, he instructs his servants, he promises that the Holy
Spirit will answer, and when he wishes a brother to be visited in prison, he
commands care for those about to confess, and when he affirms that God will do
vengeance for his elect, he consoles their sufferings, even in the parable of
the word after the turf dried up he figures the heat of persecutions. If these
things are not received as they are pronounced, without doubt they savor
beyond what they sound, and there will be one thing in the voices, another in
the senses, as allegories, as parables, as riddles.

Whatever wind of argumentation, therefore, these scorpions have caught,
against whatever sharpness they have driven themselves, there is now one line:
they will be challenged to the facts themselves, whether they are transacted
according to the scriptures. Since then something else will be signified in
the scriptures, if that very thing is not found in the facts. For what has
been written must come about. Further, what is written will then come about,
if something else does not come about. But see, we are also hated by all men
for the name's sake, as it is written, and we are delivered even by those
nearest, as it is written, and we are led to the powers, and we are
interrogated, and we are tortured, and we confess, and we are butchered, as it
is written. So the Lord ordained. If he ordained these things otherwise, why
do they not come about otherwise than he ordained, that is, as he ordained?
And yet they do not come about otherwise than he ordained; therefore as they
come about, so he ordained, and as he ordained, so they come about. For it
would not have been permitted to come about otherwise than he ordained, nor
would he himself have ordained otherwise than he wished them to come about. So
these scriptures will not signify something else than what we recognize in the
facts; or, if those things which are preached are not yet being done, how are
those being done which have not been preached? For these which are being done
have not been preached, if other things are what are preached, and not these
which are being done. But now, because the very things are in the facts which
in the voices are believed to have been said otherwise, what would happen if
they were found done otherwise? But this will be the perversity of faith, not
to believe what has been proved, to presume what has not been proved. To which
perversity I will also oppose this, that if these things which are so done as
they have been written will not be the very things which are preached, those
also ought not to be so done as they have been written, lest they themselves
also, by the example of these, be in danger of being shut out, since there is
one thing in the voices, another in the facts, and it is left that they should
not even seem to have been preached when they have come about, if they are
preached otherwise than they have to come about. And how will those things be
believed which will not have been preached as they come about? So heretics, by
not believing what is preached as it has been proved, believe those things
which have not even been preached.

Who now would know the marrow of the scriptures more than the school of Christ
itself? whom the Lord both adopted for himself as disciples, to be taught all
things of course, and ordained for us as masters, to teach all things of
course. To whom would he rather have declared the figure of his own voice than
to him to whom he revealed the likeness of his own glory, to Peter, John,

martyrdom? Or do they also write otherwise than they think, masters of deceit,
not of truth? Peter indeed to those of Pontus, For how great, he says, is the
glory, if you endure not as being punished for offenses? For this is grace,
unto this you have also been called, because Christ also suffered for us,
leaving you himself as an example, that you should follow his steps. And
again: Beloved, do not be terrified at the burning which is taking place among
you for a trial, as if something new were happening to you. For inasmuch as
you share the sufferings of Christ, rejoice, that you may also rejoice
exulting in the revelation of his glory. If you are disgraced in the name of
Christ, you are blessed, because glory and the Spirit of God rest upon you,
provided none of you suffer as a murderer or a thief or an evildoer or a
watcher of another's affairs; but if as a Christian, let him not blush, but
let him glorify the Lord in that name.

John indeed exhorts that we should even lay down our souls for our brothers,
denying that there is fear in love. For perfect love casts fear outside,
because fear has punishment, and he who fears is not perfect in love. What
fear is it better to understand, if not the author of denial? What perfect
love does he affirm, if not the putter-to-flight of fear and the giver of life
to confession? With what punishment does he punish fear, if not that which the
denier will bring back, to be killed with body and soul in gehenna? If we must
die for the brothers, how much more does he teach that we must die for the
Lord, sufficiently instructed from his Apocalypse also to persuade these
things? For the Spirit had commanded to the angel of the church of the
Smyrnaeans: Behold, the devil will cast some of your number into prison, that
you may be tried ten days. Be faithful unto death, and I will give you the
crown of life. Likewise to that of the Pergamenes concerning Antipas, a most
faithful martyr, killed in the habitation of Satan. Likewise to that of the
Philadelphians, that he would be delivered from the last trial who had not
denied the Lord's name. Thereafter to each conqueror he promises now the tree
of life and pardon of the second death, now hidden manna with a white pebble
and an unknown name, now the power of an iron rod and the brightness of the
morning star, now to be clothed in white and not to be blotted from the book
of life and to become a pillar in the temple of God inscribed with the name of
God and of the Lord and of the heavenly Jerusalem, now to sit with the Lord on
his throne, which was once denied to the sons of Zebedee. Who then are these
so blessed conquerors, if not martyrs properly? For theirs are the victories
whose also are the fights, but theirs are the fights whose also is the blood.

But also in the meantime under the altar the souls of the martyrs rest in
peace and feed patience with the confidence of vengeance, and clothed in robes
they take the white brightness of glory, until others also fill up the
fellowship of their glory. For again a countless multitude is revealed,
clothed in white and marked with palms of victory, triumphing of course over
Antichrist, as one of the elders says, These are they who come out of that
great distress and have washed their garment and made it white in the blood of
the Lamb. For the clothing of the soul is the flesh. Uncleanness indeed is
washed away by baptism, but stains are made white by martyrdom. Because Isaiah
also promises out of red and scarlet the snowy and the woolen. When great
Babylon also is described drunk with the blood of the saints, without doubt
her drunkenness is served by the cups of martyrdoms, whose fear, what it will
bring back, is equally shown. For among all the reprobate, nay before all, are
the fearful. But to the fearful, he says, then to the rest, a portion in the
lake of fire and sulphur. So fear, which in his epistle love casts outside,
has punishment.

But Paul the apostle, from being a persecutor who first shed the blood of the
church, afterward changing the sword for the pen and turning the dagger into a
plough, a ravening wolf of Benjamin, then himself bringing food according to
Jacob, how he now commends martyrdoms as desirable even for himself! When
rejoicing over the Thessalonians, So that, he says, we may glory in you in the
churches of God for your endurance and faith, in all the persecutions and
distresses which you endure, a showing of the just judgment of God, that you
may be counted worthy of his kingdom, for which you also suffer. As also to
the Romans: And not only so, but even exulting in distresses, sure that
distress accomplishes endurance, but endurance proving, but proving hope, but
hope does not put to shame. And again: But if children, then also heirs, heirs
indeed of God, but joint-heirs with Christ; if indeed we suffer together, that
we may also be glorified with him. For I reckon that the sufferings of this
time are not worthy toward the glory which will be revealed in us. And
therefore afterward, Who, he says, will separate us from the love of Christ?
distress, or anguish, or hunger, or nakedness, or danger, or the sword?
according to what is written: For your sake we are put to death all the day;
we have been counted as sheep of slaughter, but in all these things we more
than conquer for him who loved us. For we are persuaded that neither death nor
life nor power nor height nor depth nor any other condition will be able to
separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord.

But also to the Corinthians, enumerating his own sufferings, he certainly
predetermined that one must suffer: In labors more abundantly, in prisons very
much, in deaths more often, from the Jews five times I received forty save
one, three times I was beaten with rods, once I was stoned, and the rest.
Which if they will seem more inconveniences than martyrdoms, yet again, For
which reason, he says, I count it good in weaknesses, in injuries, in
necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ. Also in the earlier
parts: Who in all things are afflicted, but not straitened, and are in want,
but not in utter want, since we are driven by persecutions, but not forsaken,
who are cast down, but do not perish, always carrying about the dying of
Christ in our body. But even if, he says, our outer man is being spoiled, the
flesh of course by the force of persecutions, yet the inner is renewed day by
day, the soul of course by hope of the promises. For what is at present
temporal and light of our distress, through excess upon excess, accomplishes
an eternal weight of glory, for us who look not at the things which are seen,
but at the things which are not seen. For the things which are seen are
temporal, speaking of inconveniences, but the things which are not seen are
eternal, promising rewards. But writing to the Thessalonians from bonds he
certainly affirmed as blessed those to whom it had been given not only to
believe in Christ but also to suffer for him. Having, he says, the same
contest which you both saw in me and now hear. For even if I am poured out
upon the sacrifice, I rejoice and rejoice with you all, likewise you also
rejoice and rejoice with me. You see how he defines the happiness of
martyrdom, for which he acquires a solemnity from mutual joy.

When at last he has come very near his wish, how he writes to Timothy,
exulting from the prospect of it: For I am already poured out, and the time of
departure is at hand; I have fought the good fight, I have finished the
course, I have kept the faith; there remains a crown, which the Lord will
render to me on that day, namely of the passion. Enough had he himself also
addressed above: Faithful is the word. For if we have died together with
Christ, we shall also live together; if we endure, we shall also reign
together; if we have denied, he also will deny us; if we do not believe, he is
faithful, he cannot deny himself. Do not therefore be ashamed of the martyrdom
of our Lord, nor of me his bound one; because he had said before: For God has
not given us a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of a sound mind.
For we suffer by power from love toward God, and with a sound mind, when we
suffer for innocence. But also, wherever he commands endurance, for what more
than for sufferings does he provide it? wherever he tears away from idolatry,
what more than martyrdoms does he tear away for it beforehand?

Do good, and you will receive praise from it. It is therefore a ministry of
God to you unto good. But if you do evil, fear. So he does not command you to
be subject to the powers on an occasion of frustrating martyrdom, but on an
appeal to live well, even under their regard, as being as it were assistants
of the tributes of righteousness, as being as it were handmaids of the divine
judgment which even here prejudges concerning the guilty. Then he also follows
out how he wishes you to be subject to the powers, commanding to render to
whom tribute, tribute, to whom custom, custom, that is, the things that are
Caesar's to Caesar, and the things that are God's to God; but man is God's
alone. Peter had of course already said that the king indeed is to be honored,
yet so that the king is then honored when he attends to his own things, when
he is far from divine honors; because father and mother also will be loved
along with God, they will not be compared. Besides, it will not be lawful to
love even the soul above God.

Now then the letters of the apostles also are well known. And are we
everywhere simple souls, and only doves, gladly going astray? I think from
desire of living. So be it then, that the senses recede from their letters.
Yet what we know the apostles suffered is a clear doctrine. This only I
understand as I run through the Acts; I seek nothing. The prisons there and
the bonds and the scourges and the stones and the swords and the onsets of the
Jews and the gatherings of the nations and the indictments of tribunes and the
audiences of kings and the tribunals of proconsuls and the name of Caesar have
no interpreter. That Peter is struck, that Stephen is overwhelmed, that James
is sacrificed, that Paul is torn apart, have been written in their own blood.
And if the heretic should wish the credit of a public record, the instruments
of the empire will speak, as would the stones of Jerusalem. We read the lives
of the Caesars: Nero first stained with blood the rising faith at Rome. Then
Peter is girt by another, when he is bound to the cross. Then Paul obtains a
birth of Roman citizenship, when there he is reborn by the nobility of
martyrdom. Wherever I now read these things, I learn to suffer; nor does it
matter to me which teachers of martyrdom I follow, the senses or the deaths of
the apostles, except that in the deaths I also recognize the senses. For they
would have suffered nothing which they had not first known must be suffered.

When Agabus had prophesied Paul's bonds by gesture also, the disciples,
weeping and praying that he would not commit himself to Jerusalem, prayed in
vain. For he, animated by what he had always taught, Why do you weep, he says,
and sadden my heart? But I would wish not only to suffer bonds at Jerusalem,
but even to die for the name of my Lord Jesus Christ. And so they yielded,
saying: Let the will of the Lord be done; trusting of course that sufferings
belong to the will of God. For they had tried to hold him back not by a
counsel of dissuasion but of love, as desiring the apostle, not as dissuading
martyrdom. But if even then Prodicus or Valentinus had stood by, suggesting
that one must not confess on earth before men, still less indeed lest God
thirst for human blood, and lest Christ demand a return of the passion, as if
he too were going to obtain salvation from it, he would at once have heard
from the servant of God what the devil had heard from the Lord: Depart, Satan,
you are a scandal to me. It is written, You shall adore the Lord your God, and
him only shall you serve. But even now he will have to hear it, seeing that
long after he has poured these poisons, which will not easily harm any of the
weak even so, if anyone in faith has drunk this draught of ours beforehand, or
even drunk it afterward.
