* 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

A stranger out of Atarneus asked Pittacus
of Mytilene, Hyrras' son, this:
/Old father, two marriages call me. One bride
is my match in wealth and blood;
the other has gone past me. Which is better?
Come, take counsel with me: which do I lead
to the wedding song?/
He spoke. The old man lifted his staff,
that weapon of age:
/Look there. Those boys will tell you the whole word./
They were whipping swift tops in the wide
meeting of three roads.
/Go in their tracks,/ he said. The stranger stood near.
And they were crying: /Drive one that matches you./
Hearing that, he forbore to seize the greater house.
He took the children's omen

and brought the lesser bride home, as that man did.
So you too, as you go, take the one that matches you.

Someone told me of your death, Heraclitus,
and it brought me to tears. I remembered how often
we two sank the sun in talk. And you, I suppose,
stranger of Halicarnassus, are ash four ages gone.
Your nightingales live on. On them Hades,
who snatches all, will never lay a hand.

I am a conch, Zephyritis, older than most, but now
you hold me, Cypris, Selenaia's first offering,
a nautilus that used to sail the open sea:
if the winds blew, I stretched my own sail
from the forestays; if Galene, that sleek goddess,
then I rowed hard with my feet, so name
and work keep company, until I fell
beside the Iulian beaches, that I might become
your much-looked-at plaything, Arsinoe,

and no longer in sea-chambers as before
(for I am breathless) should the damp kingfisher
lay her egg. Grant grace to Cleinias' daughter.
She knows how to do what is noble, and she comes
from Aeolian Smyrna.

I am the labor of the Samian who once took
the divine singer into his house. I tell of Eurytus,
all he suffered, and of blonde Ioleia. They call me
a Homeric writing. Dear Zeus, that is no small thing
for Creophylus.

A boy was crowning his stepmother's stele,
a little stone, thinking life and temper had changed.
It leaned from the tomb, fell, and killed the child.
Stepchildren, flee even a stepmother's grave.

A short speech, Dionysus, for a poet who does well.
The longest word he says is /I win/.
The man on whom you do not breathe kindly,
if someone asks /How did it go?/, says
/Hard is what comes./
Let that saying belong to the man who plots
what is not just. For me, lord, the short syllables.

Does Charidas rest under you? /If you mean the son
of Arimmas of Cyrene, he is under me./
Charidas, what is below? /Much darkness./ And the way up?
/A lie./ And Pluto? /A story./ We are lost.

/This is my true word to you. If you want the sweet one:
a great ox in Hades goes for a Pellaean./

Lycus of Naxos did not die on land, but in the sea
he saw ship and life go under together,
a merchant sailing from Aegina. He is a corpse
in the wet, and I, a tomb that holds a name
and nothing else, cry this all-true word:
/Sailor, do not mix with the sea
when the Kids are setting./

At dawn we buried Melanippus, and as the sun
went down the girl Basilo died by her own hand.
She could not bear to live after she had set
her brother on the fire. The house of Aristippus

and all Cyrene bowed its head, seeing the house
rich in children left empty.

You who pass my tomb, know that I am both child
and father of Callimachus of Cyrene.
You would know both. One once led the arms
of his country. The other sang things stronger
than spite. No wonder: those the Muses looked on
as children, not with a sidelong eye,
they do not put away as friends when the hair is gray.

Callignotus swore to Ionis he would never hold
a dearer friend than her, man or woman.
He swore. But they tell the truth who say
that oaths in love do not sink into the ears
of the deathless. Now he is burned with a male fire,
and of the poor bride, as of the Megarians,
there is neither word nor count.

that carries the crowd this way.
I hate also a beloved who walks the streets, and I
do not drink from the fountain. All public things
turn my stomach.

can say it clearly, someone says /another has him/.

going through frost and snow. But if someone says

what runs, and he flies past what lies in the middle.

and afterwards, man, of Dindymene,

in a good old age in their hands. Go, and farewell.

Half of my soul still breathes. The other half I do not know
whether Love or Hades snatched, only that it is gone.
Did it go again to some of the boys? I have forbidden it

If I came reveling of my own will, Archinus, blame me
a thousand times. If I came unwilling, let the rashness go.

Unmixed wine and Love compelled me. One of them
dragged me; the other would not let the rashness go.
I came, and I did not shout who I was, or whose son.
I kissed the doorpost. If that is a wrong, I do wrong.

The stranger had a wound and hid it. How painfully
he drew breath through his chest: /Did you see?/
When he drank the third cup, the roses shedding petals
from the man's garlands all went to the ground.
He is roasted, and not a little. By the gods I do not guess
off the beat. A thief has learned a thief's tracks.

There is something hidden, by Pan, there is something here,
by Dionysus, fire under the ash.
I am not bold. Do not wind yourself about me. Often
a quiet river eats a wall from below, unnoticed.
So now I am afraid, Menexenus, lest this silent creeper
slip in and throw me into love.

What a good charm Polyphemus found for the lover.
By Earth, the Cyclops was no fool.
The Muses thin love down, Philippus.
Wisdom is the all-heal of every hurt.
This, I think, is the one good hunger has
against trouble: it cuts out the boy-loving sickness.
We have both, unsparing, against Love:
/This, boy, this would I say. Clip your wings, little child;/
/we do not fear you even so much as a leaf of orach;/
/for both charms against the hard wound are here at home./

Simos son of Miccus asked for good learning, giving me

to the Muses. They, as they did with Glaucus, gave

/the lock is holy/, my dream, to me.

So may you sleep, Conopion, as you make me
sleep beside these cold porches.
So may you sleep, most unjust, as you put your lover
