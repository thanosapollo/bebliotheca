DISPUTATION OF JOHN THE ORTHODOX WITH A MANICHAEAN

1. ORTHODOX. If the body is <from> matter, and the soul, according to you, is
from the substance of the good God, and these are opposites, and opposites are
not composed without some craftsman, as black and white by a painter and the
hot kinds with the cold by physicians, who is the one who brought together the
opposites, soul and body, and made one living being?

2. MANICHAEAN. The good God, when matter was moving in disorder, taking a
certain portion of his own power sent it to matter, wishing to bait it; and
it, having received it, swallowed it. And thus from the mixture of both the
composites came to be.

3. ORTH. These are certain myths, having more of the improbable than of the
impiety; first, that no one composes himself toward the generation of another;
and besides this, God is found from this to be passible and divisible, if
indeed taking a part of himself he threw it to matter; but also matter will be
freed from being evil, having fallen in love with the good and swallowed the
light from it. And besides, answer me as I ask: is God incorporeal?

4. MAN. Yes, incorporeal.

5. ORTH. How then does the incorporeal and simple undergo cutting and
division?

6. MAN. But I do not say that a division or cutting of the divine substance
has taken place, but a projection consubstantial with the one who projects,
and I do not think that you, being Christians, will contend against this. For
you do not say that the Son of God and Word is a fragment of the Father's
substance, and yet he is confessed even among you to be consubstantial with
the Father and is proclaimed by the holy Scripture to have been sent into the
world for the salvation of men; so then, no cutting having taken place, the
good God projected a certain divine power in order to check the disorder of
matter.

7. ORTH. And even so you will be refuted, falling under the charge of cutting.

8. MAN. Refute, and I will be persuaded by what comes from you.

9. ORTH. I know that matter and God have been divided in their own places,
having often heard from the Manichaeans, and that the south is assigned to
matter, while the north and east and west are assigned to the good God.

10. MAN. For so we confess.

11. ORTH. How then do you not fear the excess of so great a blasphemy,
introducing the divine as circumscribed, whom you confess to be good and
almighty, and this while he is circumscribed by the worst matter? For you give
a limit to God, placing him in a place; for place is the limit of the
containing, insofar as it contains the contained; but God, being contained, is
altogether circumscribed, and the place itself that contains God is
circumscribed according to that end at which it touches the place of matter.
And thus God will be circumscribed by a circumscribed place according to you,
who confine God in some place. But let this be set aside; tell me, do you say
that God, remaining in his own place, projects the power from himself and
sends it forth to the place of matter?

12. MAN. Yes, this I say.

13. ORTH. If then the projection from God came to be in another place, while
God remained in another region, how was it not divided from the Father's
hypostasis? For things that stand apart in places must altogether also be
divided from one another; and so then the power of God


according to you came to be not in God, but outside his hypostasis? And that
which exists outside of something is altogether also divided from its
hypostasis; but we do not follow your impieties, nor do we say two principles
divided in places, but saying one creator of the all and one principle of all
things, we dogmatize that Father and Son fill all things, confessing Father in
Son and Son in Father according to the saying that declares: I am in the
Father and the Father in me, and All things that the Father has are mine.
Wherefore also John the God-bearer, as the Holy Trinity fills all things, says
concerning the Word of God: He came unto his own, and his own received him
not. But according to you Manichaeans he did not come unto his own, but rather
unto what is alien, if indeed he came upon the place of matter, wishing
according to you to save the souls bound in an alien place, so that even
through the things by which you recalled the cutting, which you have often
spoken of, thinking to escape the division of the divine substance, not even
so have you escaped; for you have fallen into the same impiety. But I return
again to the point from which I departed in the argument. Answer then: is
matter in itself, without the mixture with God, simple or composite?

14. MAN. Composite.

15. ORTH. And does the composite come to be from simples?

16. MAN. So it is. 17. ORTH. If then matter is composite, it is clear that it
has been composed by some other; for no one brings himself to composition. How
then, having been composed by another, is it without beginning? And besides,
composites are composed from different things, either preexisting or also
contemplated together with one another, from preexisting things as electrum
composed from gold and silver, <from> things contemplated together as some
head from bones and flesh and membranes and sinews; for these came together at
once, being different, and in no way did each preexist the head composed from
them. Matter then, if it too is composite, will no longer be one principle,
but many more, being composite in itself even without the composition toward
God.

18. MAN. Do you then say that matter is simple?

19. ORTH. I do not say that matter is simple, but I wish to show that it is
not without beginning, nor ungenerated as you say, and that it was brought
forth together with the qualities by the creator, the qualities about it
always being changed and passing from one into another whenever it should
please the God who brought this forth. For if it is simple, how from one
simple thing, I mean matter, have composite bodies come to be? And how in the
one simple thing, matter, do we find two opposites, generation and corruption?
But if bodies are from it, it too will of necessity be body; for that which
has been generated from some body is body; but if matter is body, it is also
composite; and being composite, it is not ungenerated, as has been
demonstrated.

20. MAN. From where then are diseases and deaths and such evils? For if these
do not come from matter without beginning and ungenerated and evil by nature,
God will be the cause of their generation. 21. ORTH. Why do you consider
diseases and death to be evils? And yet death frees the soul from the body and
releases it from the bond of matter, while disease again melts the body, which
body you consider to be evil. And often disease also prevents one from
committing sins which the sick man would have done if he had not been bound
by the disease. And besides, answer me as I inquire: Must one hate wickedness?

22. MAN. Yes.

23. ORTH. How then do you honor most greatly this which you pretend to hate,
saying it is like the good God, eternal and without beginning and ungenerated?


24. MAN. We do not make it like the good God. For we know God as most high,
good, light-formed, almighty. Even if then wickedness is without beginning,
yet it falls far short of God.

25. ORTH. You seem not to know that the goodness and light-form of God reaches
also to the holy angels, and to us men, by participation or rather gift of
grace. What however is characteristic of the divine nature, being without
beginning and ungenerated, this is contemplated in none of the others.
But if you give to wickedness the things that are properly and primarily
characteristic of the divine nature, how will it not be of equal honor with
God? And tell me how you call God almighty, if according to you he has not yet
mastered matter and the things from it?

26. MAN. But there will be a time when he will master even it. 27. ORTH. Then
he is not yet now almighty, since he has not yet mastered the matter that is
eternally with him, and later what he does not now have will accrue to him
according to you; for being now almighty in potency, later he will become so
in act, coming from the incomplete to perfection. And answer me how he is
not now almighty, as not being able or as not willing? If as not being able,
how is he almighty at all? For the almighty must be always also all-powerful.
But if as not willing, first he grudges the salvation of men, and then he is
also found to be the cause of wickedness, being able, but not willing, to
destroy it.

28. MAN. From where then are the evils?

29. ORTH. Your question has not come about by any logical sequence. For one
must know if there is evil, and what evil is, and then thus seek from where it
is constituted.

30. MAN. Let us inquire thus.

31. ORTH. Of beings some are substance, some quality, some aptitude, some
energy. And substance is I, you; quality is white, black, sweet, bitter, and
all such things as are contemplated in underlying bodies; aptitude is that
toward which the things that have come to be are naturally fitted, being such
in potency, as the hands are apt to work crafts, the tongue to serve speech,
the feet toward walking, a child toward technical learning; and energy is the
action according to nature, that of the irrational as irrational, that of the
rational as rational. Which then of the things said do you define evil to be?

32. MAN. Energy; and it is clear that every energy is energy of a substance.

33. ORTH. Every energy is indeed energy of a substance. But I ask you: can
wickedness, being energy, also be substance?

34. MAN. So I say.

35. ORTH. First you are contrary to yourself, saying that the energy comes to
be from substance and again supposing the energy to be substance; whence you
will not escape being sick with an excess of all ignorance even among those
most inexperienced in dialectic; but I ask you, is every energy evil?

36. MAN. Not altogether.

37. ORTH. What kind of energy then do you call evil?

38. MAN. Murder, adultery, injustice, and all such things.

39. ORTH. If murder according to you is evil by nature, do the judges do evil
in putting to death tomb-robbers and man-slayers? And again: why does the
one who mixes with his own wife not sin, but the one who does this with
another's does sin, and this though the mixing is one energy? I think that
through these things even unwillingly you will confess that every energy is
intermediate, and is varied by the intention of the one who pursues it,
becoming virtue or vice; for the


one mixes with his own temperately, the other with another's intemperately.

40. MAN. Is desire not evil?

41. ORTH. Not altogether; for this too is intermediate, and it is possible for
it to be ordered by the oversight of the mind. Indeed one may also desire good
things; for one is a lover of the poor, another a lover of money, and one uses
mixing for the sake of begetting children, another for the sake of pleasure.

42. MAN. Tell me this, did the corruption of bodies and the rotting come to be
from the good and incorruptible God?

43. ORTH. "From something" is said in many ways; for from me is my son
consubstantial with me, from the carpenter the bench, from the earth the
fruits, from the king confiscations. We then do not say that corruption is
from the substance of God, but that it is worked by his will beneficially for
us. For if, things being corruptible, we are so mad about them as to set in
motion courts and wars against one another, what would we not have dared, if
he had brought these forth incorruptible? And how were we going to receive a
perception of the gift of the incorruptible life, which our Lord bestowed on
us, if we had not first come into experience of this corruptible and
perishable life, into which he judged it good for us to contend beforehand?
And besides, if corruption is evil, generation is altogether good, but
generation and corruption concern the same body, therefore the body is not
evil by nature, as you insist, dogmatizing that the body is of wickedness. And
why do you contemplate and speak of the things you consider to be evils of
bodies, but pass by the goods from them? For first we are nourished and
constituted from the fruits yielded from the earth; and from stones and wood
we construct houses, through which we are freed from rain and frost, and we
also turn aside the burning of the sun, whom you are not ashamed to make a
god, and with iron we not only plow the earth, but also guard against enemies
coming against us. But if, being refuted by these things, you should persist
putting forward rotting and stench, perhaps you do not know that the things
that have partaken of soul rather undergo the rotting and the stench. And
according to you the soul is substance of the good God; the good God then will
be cause of the things you consider evils, of the rotting and the stench.

44. MAN. If there are no evils, why do laws punish them?

45. ORTH. This especially refutes your derangement; for if it were not in our
power to do evils and goods, the laws would not punish the evildoers, which
often do not look at the act, but examine the disposition of the one who did
it; as, someone threw a stick and unwillingly killed someone, they do not
judge the one who did it for murder; someone mixed unwillingly with some other
woman, finding her sleeping on his bed, as mixing with his own wife, they
acquit the accused of adultery, if this should be shown. And answer me, why do
you worship the sun?

46. MAN. Because it is a luminary of the world, an offspring of the good God.

47. ORTH. Is it body or incorporeal?

48. MAN. Incorporeal.

49. ORTH. And how is the incorporeal seen bodily? For the incorporeals are
contemplated intellectually, but the sun is grasped by the eyes of sense
and by circumscription of body. And besides, is there any of the
incorporeals that has bodily qualities?

50. MAN. I do not think so.

51. ORTH. But the sun has bodily qualities; for it is both hot and dry; and
these are qualities of bodies; therefore the sun too is body. And how do you
not blush, reverencing what you ought to hate as doing things contrary to your
doctrine?


52. MAN. Why contrary?

53. ORTH. Because it both nourishes and holds together the bodies, which you
say belong to wickedness. And no one will contradict these things, unless
fighting against experience. And tell me this too, do you confess a judgment?

54. MAN. Yes.

55. ORTH. Who is the one judging and who is the one judged? 56. MAN. The good
God judges, and the soul that has sinned is judged.

57. ORTH. But God is found punishing himself having sinned, if indeed the soul
being judged is from his substance. And how is it judged at all, not having
worked the evil voluntarily? For you say that evils come to be by necessity of
nature and not by choice.

58. But since you say there are two principles, God and matter, and that God
is good, and matter evil, and you dare to assign to each nature a separated
and peculiar place and you also attach to these other myths, that matter,
having seen the light of God, came into desire, and rushed toward the light;
which the good God, seeing it preparing for war, having cut off a part of
his own substance threw it into this, and it, having received it, generated
all the bodies, since then you say these things, I will make a brief
refutation of them.

59. First then one must examine the act, whether it is fitting and appropriate
to the nature of God, then the purpose of the act, then the end. Let us see
then first concerning God, whether what was done by him fits the nature of
God. What then is according to nature for God? Clearly the ungenerated and the
incorruptible and the good and, according to you Manichaeans, remaining in his
own place. But the act, according to your hypothesis, is shown contrary to the
nature; for it injures all the things said. For neither does the one good God
remain one ungenerated, when he is divided into two or even more ungenerated
things. Nor indeed does the one who has been divided remain incorruptible, nor
yet good. For how is he good who has made himself an enemy to himself, and has
betrayed a part of himself to the enemies? And how would he be good, freeing
himself through the making of the world, but doing this for the destruction of
the things created? But neither did he remain wholly in his own place; for a
part of him passed over to the place of matter.

60. Second one must inquire whether the act is consistent with the purpose.
What then is the purpose? Clearly the victory. But the act is shown to stand
contrary also to the purpose; for it is clear that the united is stronger than
the divided, and the one according to nature more powerful than the one turned
contrary to nature, and the one remaining in his own place than the one who
has passed over into another place. What then is consistent and fitting to the
purpose? Rather to keep the strength, and not make it weaker by the
separation.

61. And let us see also the third, whether the act is appropriate to the end.
What then do you say is the end from the victory? For you will altogether say
either the guarding of the constitution of God, or the freedom, or not to have
changed from his own place, or that matter be destroyed, or that it be changed
toward the better. But the act suggests thinking the contrary of these. For
neither did the constitution of God remain in what is according to nature, the
part having been betrayed, nor the freedom; for he was enslaved to the
generation of the world, putting a part from himself into the things created.
Nor did he remain in his own place, the part having changed over to matter.
Nor was the nature of matter destroyed or changed; for it remained what it was
from the beginning, unchangeable, so that from every side the hypothesis which
you composed while mythologizing is shown stale and foolish.


62. And it is possible also from the energies and from the cause and from the
places which you suppose, to refute the absurdity of your impiety. From the
energy then one must overturn thus: if the natures are opposite, the energies
also are opposite. If then the energy of God is confessed to be productive,
that of matter will be passive. But if this, then matter is subject to God and
serves; but if it is subject and serves, then it is not ungenerated. And
besides, either the two make, or the two suffer, or the one makes and the
other suffers, or they themselves both make and suffer. But if the two make,
they will be gods, if indeed making belongs to God, together with also
conceiving matters. But if both suffer, the two will be matters, and another
maker will be sought. But if the one suffers and the other makes, the one
suffering is generated, the one making ungenerated. But if the two suffer and
make, insofar as they make they will be gods, and insofar as they suffer,
matters. The same then will be gods and matters, generated and ungenerated.

63. And from the cause too one must refute you. What do you suppose is the
cause of matter being evil? For either because it is ungenerated and a
principle, or because it is matter, or because it is body, or because it is
divided and changed? But if because it is ungenerated and a principle, God too
is such. But if because it is matter, and it is called matter because it
underlies for the generation of the things from it, then according to the same
hypothesis God is not good; for he underlies for the generation of the souls
and angels from him, as you Manichaeans dogmatize. But if because it is body,
the cause of being body is not itself, but God, if indeed before he threw the
part from himself into matter it was not body. But if because it is divided
and changed, God too according to you will be held by the same evils; for if
he is divided into generations of angels and souls, and the things from him
are not gods, it is clear that he was not only divided, but also changed. But
if you say none of these is the cause of matter being evil, but that it sins,
one must know that nothing of the things that come to be according to nature
by necessity is a sin; for every sin comes from choice. And choice is from
soul, and the soul is substance of God according to you; from God then is the
sin and not from matter, according to your most impious doctrine.

64. And from the places too, which you suppose, assigning one to God and the
other to matter, I will refute you as dogmatizing nothing sound. For if God
and matter are ungenerated, and each is in a place according to you, and the
place is other in substance than the things contained in it, it will follow
that the places are ungenerated together with those in them. And thus you
will find not two ungenerated things, but four, God and matter and the
place of each.
But if you say the places are older than those in them, God and matter will no
longer be ungenerated, but rather the places preexisting them. But if you say
God and matter are before the places, then God and matter were not separated,
but existed in one another; for there was not yet a place that divided. And
thus the account of the opposition among you will be dissolved; for the nature
of opposites cannot subsist in one another, so that from every side your
mythical wonder-talk has been refuted. 65. Do not then impale yourselves and
others on the doctrines of impiety, but running to the light of the truth,
cleanse the gloom of ignorance from your souls. For by self-determination and
not by necessity of nature we men do what seems good in choice.
