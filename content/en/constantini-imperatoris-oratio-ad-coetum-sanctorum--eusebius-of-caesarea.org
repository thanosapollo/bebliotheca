1.t Oration of the Emperor Constantine which he wrote to the assembly of the
saints. Pin.1 αʹ. The preface recalls the Pascha, and how the Word of God,
having benefited all, was variously plotted against by those who were
benefited. Pin.2 βʹ. Address to the church and to the hearers, to pardon and
to correct the faults. Pin.3 γʹ. That God is both father of the Word and the
same creator of creation, and that the things that are could not have been
constituted if their causes were different. Pin.4 δʹ. Concerning the error
according to the idols. Pin.5 εʹ. That Christ the Son of God created all the
things that are and defined the quantity of being in each. Pin.6 ʹ. Concerning
fate, that the account of it is shown false both from the laws among men and
from the things according to creation, which move not disorderly but according
to order, displaying through the order the bound of the creator. Pin.7 ζʹ.
That concerning the incomprehensible things of the creator one must glorify
the wisdom, and not blame another nor an automatic impulse. Pin.8 ηʹ. That God
supplies the needful things to men abundantly, but the things toward delight
moderately, profitably in either case for those who enjoy them. Pin.9 θʹ.
Concerning the philosophers, who through wanting to know all things also
failed in their opinions, and some encountered dangers, and concerning the
doctrines of Plato. Pin.10 ιʹ. Concerning those who spit upon not only the
scriptural doctrines but also the philosophical ones, and concerning the need
either to believe the poets in all things or to disbelieve all. Pin.11 ιαʹ.
Concerning the presence of the Lord according to flesh, who he is and for what
things he has come to be. Pin.12 ιβʹ. Concerning those who did not know the
mystery, and that they were ignorant willingly, and what good things await
those who knew, and especially those perfected through confession. Pin.13 ιγʹ.
That the difference of the parts of creation is necessary, and that the
inclination toward the good and the bad is of the judgment of men, whence also
the judgment is necessary and reasonable. Pin.14 ιδʹ. That created nature
stands infinitely apart from the uncreated essence, but man becomes near it by
the life according to virtue. Pin.15 ιεʹ. What things the Savior taught and
worked as wonders and benefited those who endured. Pin.16 ι ʹ. That the
presence of Christ was foretold to the prophets, and came to be for the
overthrow of the idols and of the idolatrous cities. Pin.17 ιζʹ. Concerning
the wisdom of Moses emulated by the wise outside, and concerning Daniel and
the three children. Pin.18 ιηʹ. Concerning the Erythraean Sibyl making known
in an acrostic of the verses of the oracle the Lord and the passion; and the
acrostic is: Jesus Christ Son of God Savior Cross. Pin.19 ιθʹ. That the oracle
concerning the Savior has been invented by none of those of the church, but is
of the Erythraean Sibyl, whose books Cicero, before the advent of Christ,
translated into Latin, and that Virgil mentions her and the virgin birth,
having hymned the mystery through riddles from fear of those in power. Pin.20
κʹ. Other verses of Virgil Maro concerning Christ and the interpretation of
these, upon which it is shown through riddles that the mystery was indicated
as by a poet. Pin.21 καʹ. That it is not possible for these things to be said
concerning a mere man, and that those who disbelieve, by ignorance of piety,
do not even know whence being is for them. Pin.22 κβʹ. Thanksgiving to Christ
ascribing the victories and the remaining goods of the emperor, and refutation
of the tyrant against them, Maximinus, who by the magnitude of the persecution
procured greater glory for piety. Pin.23 κγʹ. Concerning the polity of the
Christians, and that the divine rejoices in those who live in virtue, and one
must expect judgment and

recompense. Pin.24 κδʹ. Concerning Decius and Valerian and Aurelian who ended
life wretchedly because of the persecution of the church. Pin.25 κεʹ.
Concerning Diocletian who laid aside the empire with shame and was struck by
lightning because of the persecution of the church. Pin.26 κ ʹ. That God is
the cause of the emperor's piety, and that one must seek the successes from
God and reckon them to him, but ascribe the faults to our own slackness.

1.t Oration of the Emperor Constantine which he wrote to the assembly of the
saints. 1.1 Constantine Augustus to the assembly of the saints. The light more
far-shining than day and sun, prelude of resurrection, and new joining of
bodies that once toiled, both ballast of promise and a path leading to eternal
life, the day of the passion is present, O most beloved teachers, and
remaining friends, all you men together; it happens far more blessed by the
great multitude of those who worship, and being addressed to the God of the
worship himself through the inward senses of each who hymn without cease
through instruments of utterance with oracles. 1.2 And you, O all-mother
nature, what such thing have you ever accomplished for the world? Or what work
of yours at all, if indeed the cause of all things is also of your essence?
For this one adorned you, since the life according to nature is the kosmos of
nature. For it has prevailed not moderately that no one should worship the God
of all according to worth, things contrary to nature, and that all things
should be thought to have been constituted not from providence but as it
chanced, disorderly and faultily, 1.3 and these things while divine
inspiration was announcing them in each case through prophets, whom, though it
was necessary to obey, impious injustice opposed with all kinds of devices,
being slanderous toward the light of truth, but embracing the hard-to-refute
of darkness. But not even this without violence and cruelty, especially that
the mind of those in power joined with the unforeseen rush of the vulgar
peoples, or rather this itself led the untimely madness. Wherefore such a
life, strengthened through many generations, became cause of the greatest
evils to those then. 1.4 But when the sojourn of the Savior shone at once,
justice arose from unjust works, and calm from every kind of surge, and all
things whatsoever had been foretold through prophets were fulfilled. Therefore
being lifted aloft into the paternal hearth, having set the inhabited world
about with the splendors of reverence and temperance, he founded the church
upon the earth as a certain holy temple of virtue, everlasting, incorruptible,
in which both the things due to the most exalted Father God and the things
befitting himself were accomplished with piety. 1.5 What then after these
things did the foolish wickedness of the nations contrive? 1.6 It practiced
casting out the graces of Christ and plundering the church constituted for the
salvation of all, but it overturned its own superstition, so that again
factions, wars, battles, an intractable provision of life, love of money,
which is not even by nature proper to wickedness, might at one time charm with
beautified hopes, at another strike with fear. But let this lie on the ground,
defeated by virtue, as is lawful, being torn and rent by repentance. But for
us now the things befitting the divine Word must be spoken.

2.1 Hear then, O shipmaster possessing purity and virginity, church, nurse of
unripe and untaught age, to whom truth is a care, and philanthropy is a care,
from which eternal spring a saving drink drips. And you also would hear with
good omen, you who worship God purely and for this have been a care to him,
applying the mind not to the

phrasing rather than to the truth of the things said, nor to me the speaker,
but to the piety of consecration. 2.2 For what grace of words would there be,
if the disposition of the speaker is left unexamined? I dare then perhaps
great things, but I blame for the daring the inborn affection toward the
divine. For this even forces shame. Wherefore especially I join to myself as
helpers those skilled in the divine mysteries, so that if some fault should
happen about the words, you may accompany and correct, not longing for the
extreme of education, but accepting the fidelity of the attempt. 2.3 And may
the greatest inspiration of the Father and of the Son who does good be present
to me as I utter these things, whatever it itself may phrase and attach to the
mind. For if anyone without God, pursuing rhetoric or some other science, has
supposed himself sufficient to make the work exact, he himself has been found
incomplete and the thing pursued as well. Yet one must not shrink nor delay,
for those who have ever been fortunate in the divine inspiration. Wherefore we
too, having asked pardon for the length of the delay, attempt the completion
of the aim.

3.1 The good for which all things yearn, God who is always above essence, 3.1
has no generation, therefore not even a beginning; for he himself is beginning
of all things in generation. And the one who has from that one the reference
to that one is united again, the separation and comparison with that one
coming to be not locally but intellectually; for the thing begotten was not
constituted by some loss of the paternal inward parts, as indeed the things
from seeds, but by an ordinance of providence contriving an overseer and
savior for the sensible world and for those in it. Therefore for all things
whatsoever are encompassed by the world, the cause of being and living is from
there; and from there also soul and every sense and the organs through which
the things signified by the senses are accomplished. 3.2 What then does the
Word make clear? That there is one overseer of all the things that are, and
that all things have been subjected to the lordship of that one alone, both
the heavenly and the earthly and the natural and organic bodies. For if the
mastery of these things, being numberless, happened to be not of one but of
many, lots and distributions of elements and ancient myths and envy and greed,
prevailing according to power, would have shaken the harmonious concord of all
things, many administering differently the portion allotted to each, 3.3 and
neglecting that the whole world always have itself according to the same
things and in the same way. And who would have known the creator of all
generation? And prayers and supplications toward whom first or last? And whom,
if I served especially, would I not have been impious toward the rest? Or
perhaps even, needing some thing of life, I would have given thanks to the one
who helped, but blamed the one who opposed. And to whom, having prayed, would
I have thought it right to know the cause of the circumstance and to obtain
the release? 3.4 Let us set then that it has been answered to us by oracles
and responses, that these things are not of their own authority and that they
belong to another god; what pity then, and what providence of God toward man?
Unless perhaps the one disposed more philanthropically toward the one having
no relation helped by force. And wrath and faction and reproach, as of those
not minding their own business nor being content with the things that fall to
them through greed, and last the confusion of all things would have followed.
3.5 What then the things after these? It is clear that the faction of the
heavenly things would have ruined the things under heaven and the earthly, the
ordinance of the seasons having vanished, and the change of times and the
enjoyment of the fruits growing according to the times, and day having
vanished and the nightly rest that succeeds it. And enough of these, and let
us return again to the unrefuted account.

4.1 Everything that has a beginning also has an end; and the beginning
according to time is called generation; and all things from generation are
corruptible, and time dims form. How then would those from corruptible
generation be immortal? And some such opinion of the unreasoning peoples has
gone about, that among the gods marriages and begettings of children were
thought lawful. But if those begotten are immortal, and they are begotten
always, the race must flood. And when addition has come about, what heaven,
and what earth, would have contained so great a swarm of gods coming on? And
what would one say concerning men joining heavenly brothers into the communion
of marriage and charging adultery and incontinence? Let us say then boldly
also that their honors and the prizes from men toward them have been mixed
with insults and wantonness. For already someone examined in words, having
composed hymns in meter or even without meter, and a statue-maker, having
taken some form in mind beforehand, crafts an artful plaything, and in the
meantime, as if forgetfulness has fallen, he flatters his own fashioning,
worshipping it as an immortal god, confessing himself to be a mortal father
and creator of the statue. 4.3 And they themselves display tombs and chests of
those immortal ones, and they honor the departed with immortal honors, not
knowing that the truly blessed and incorruptible is in need of no extra honor
from the corruptible. For that which is visible to mind and comprehensible to
thought neither longs for a form through which it would be recognized, nor
endures a shape as of an image or type. And all these things come to be for
the favor of the departed; for they were men, when they lived, being partakers
of body.

5.1 But why do I stain the tongue with polluted words, being about to hymn the
God who truly is? I wish then first with a pure drink as it were to rinse a
salty mouth; and the pure drink is poured through an eternal spring of the
virtues of the God hymned by us, and my own proper work is to hymn Christ
through life and the. thanksgiving owed to him from us in return for many and
great benefactions. 5.2 I say then that this one established the beginnings of
this universe and discovered a foundation of men, legislating by word, and at
once settled those newly born into a certain blessed and flowery place heavy
with various fruits, and willed them at the start to be untaught of goods and
evils, but assigned as an end the seat upon earth fitting for a rational
animal alone, and then already as to rational animals opened the knowledge of
goods and evils. Then also he commanded the race to increase, so that at some
time the whole, bounded by the circuit of ocean, might be inhabited. 5.3 And
as the race of men thus multiplied, arts useful to life were discovered, and
the generation of irrational animals multiplies no less, a certain exceptional
natural power being found in each kind; of tame animals the gentleness and
obedience to man, of wild ones strength and speed and a certain natural
provision of salvation from dangers. 5.4 And he commanded a certain care of
all tame things to men, but toward the wild a certain contest, having
legislated. And after these things he constituted the generation of winged
things, much in number but different in nature and habits, outstanding in
variety of colors and partaking of an inborn harmony of music, and having
distinguished all the other things whatsoever the world, having encompassed
them, holds together, and having defined for all these a fated ordinance of
life, he adorned the most perfect completion of the wholes.

6.1 But most men, being foolish, blame nature for the ordering of all things,
and some of them fate or the automatic. Reckoning the authority of these
things to fate they do not even understand, when they

address fate, uttering a name, but making clear no action nor any underlying
essence. For what would fate be itself by itself, if nature has generated all
things? Or what would nature be thought to be, if the ordinance of fate exists
untransgressable? But even to say that some ordinance is fated makes clear
that every ordinance is a work of the one who ordained. Fate then, if indeed
it is an ordinance, would be a discovery of God; all things therefore have
been subjected to God, and nothing is without share of that one's power. 6.2
And we accept that fate is and is thought to be a choice of God; but how are
justice and temperance and the remaining virtues according to fate? And from
where the things contrary to these, both injustice and incontinence? For
wickedness is from nature and not from fate, and virtue is successes of
character and manners. And the faults of good and right choice, or again the
successes, turning out now one way now another either according to chance or
according to fate, and all the just and what is assigned to each according to
worth, how according to fate? 6.3 And laws and exhortations 6.3 toward virtue
and turnings away from the things that are not due, and praises and blames and
punishments and all things whatsoever call toward virtue and lead away from
wickedness, how are they said to have been constituted not from justice, which
is proper to God providing, but from chance or the automatic? For because of
the quality of the life of men he also hangs the according to worth, plagues
at times and factions and dearths and abundances following, clearly and
expressly almost sending forth a voice, that all such things have been fitted
to our lives. 6.4 For essence rejoices in good justices and turns away from
all impiety, and welcomes the moderate mind, but hates daring and the
boastfulness raised above the animal; of which, even if the proofs are most
clear and lie before the eyes, they shine more clearly then, whenever, having
gathered into ourselves and as it were contracted the mind, we reckon their
cause with ourselves. Wherefore I say one must live moderately and mildly, not
raising the mind above nature, and taking thoughts that the God who oversees
the things done is always present to us. 6.5 And still one must test even thus
whether what is said is true, that the ordinance of all affairs was
constituted from chance and the automatic; is it then also the heavenly things
and the stars, earth and sea, and fire and winds, water and air, and the
change of the seasons, the hours of summer and winter, all these things one
must be persuaded have happened unreasonably and as it chanced rather than
been created? 6.6 For some, not having mind, say that men have invented most
of such things for their own use, nature supplying all things abundantly. Let
it be that this opinion has partaken of some account concerning the earthly
and corruptible; is it then also that the immortal and unalterable things are
discoveries of men? For of these and of all such things, whatsoever have been
separated from our sense, but happen to be comprehensible by mind alone, not
an enmattered animal, man, but the intelligible and eternal essence, mother of
God, is generator. 6.7 But indeed also the account of the ordinance is a work
of providence: that the day shine, being lit by the sun, that night succeed
when he sets, and having succeeded not be left wholly dim because of the dance
of the stars. And what would one say concerning the moon, being filled from
the opposite distance toward the sun, but being thinned through the near
converse with him? Do these things not make clear an inward account and
shrewdness of God? 6.8 And the fitness of the solar flame, ripening the
fruits, and breaths of winds preparing a good year, and the cooling of rains,
and the harmony of all these, according to which reasonably and in good order

all things are administered, and the everlasting of the ordinance, the planets
being restored into the same place at the due and customary times, is this not
a clear command of God, and a complete service of the stars obeying the divine
law? 6.9 And heights of mountains and hollow depths of places and evenness of
opened plains, do they seem to be without providence of God? Of which not only
the sight is delightful but also the use delightful. And measures and
intervals of water and earth, sufficient both for farming and for the
conveyance from abroad of the things in need, how do they not clearly
demonstrate the exact and measured forethought of God? For mountains have
water, and evenness, having received this and having been watered the
sufficient for recovery of the earth, thrusts the rest out into the sea, and
the sea in turn hands it over to the ocean. =6.10= And do we still dare to say
that all these things come to be automatically and as it chanced? Not making
clear in what sort of shape and form the automatic is characterized, having no
subsistence either intellectually or sensibly, but only that a sound of an
unsubstantial name buzzes about the ears.

7.1 For the automatic is truly a tag of men thinking as it chanced and
unreasonably and not grasping their account, but through weakness of grasp
thinking unreasonably that those things have been ordained whose account they
do not have to speak. Yet there are some things whose true grasp of the truth
lies in depth, having obtained a certain wondrous nature, of which sort also
the nature of hot waters happens to be. For no one would have the cause of so
great a fire ready to speak, but it is wondrous that, being surrounded in a
circle by cold water, it still does not depart from the inborn heat. 7.2 These
things indeed seem to be a rare thing and easily numbered throughout all the
inhabited world, so that, as I persuade myself, the power of providence may be
able to become easily recognized among men, decreeing that two most contrary
natures, heat and cold, be filtered from the same and one root. 7.3 Many then
and numberless things have been given to men by the better for consolation and
enjoyment, and especially the fruit of olive and of vine, the one holding the
account of recovery of soul and of brightness, the other being suitable
besides enjoyment also for therapy of bodies. And an extraordinary wonder also
is the continual and uninterrupted flow of rivers by night and by day, a
symbol of eternal and uninterrupted life. And the nightly succession is of
equal power.

8.1 And let all these things have been said by us toward confirmation that
nothing has come to be unreasonably nor unintelligently, but that the Word and
the providence are God; who also, having commanded the usefulness of metals to
assist the wants, stored the nature of gold and bronze and of the remaining
things with the fitting measure, commanding some, of which the need was going
to be much and of every kind, to be supplied abundantly, but the things toward
delight of kosmos and abundance alone both magnanimously and sparingly,
between thrift and bounty. 8.2 For if even of the things made toward kosmos
the same abundance were granted, through greed those mining, having despised
the useful toward farming and building and preparation of temples, such as
iron and bronze, would neglect the gathering of them, but would take thought
beforehand of the things toward luxury and useless surplus of wealth.
Wherefore they say the finding of gold and silver is harder and of greater
toil than of all the other metals, so that the intensity of the toil may be
set against the intensity of the desire. 8.3 And how many other works of the
divine providence is it possible to number upon all things, which abundantly

it has given us, expressly turning the life of men toward temperance and all
the other virtues, and leading away from untimely greed. To track out the
account of all of which is a greater work than according to man. For how would
the thought of a corruptible and weak animal reach the truth according to
exactness? How would it understand the pure will of God from the beginning?

9.1 Wherefore one must attempt the possible things and those according to our
nature. For the persuasive of the inquiries that come to be in the dialogues
leads most of us away from the truth of the things that are, which indeed has
happened also to many of the philosophers, talking idly about the words and
the examination of the nature of the things that are. For whenever the
magnitude of the affairs overpowers their examination, they hide the true with
certain distorted methods; and it happens to them to opine contrary things and
to fight with one another's doctrines, and these while pretending to be wise.
Whence factions of peoples and harsh judgments of those in power, thinking the
paternal custom to be destroyed; and destruction of those very ones has often
followed. For Socrates, being lifted by dialectic and making the worse
accounts better, and playing in each case about the antilogical accounts, has
been taken away by the envy of those of the same tribe and of the citizens.
Yet Pythagoras too, pretending especially to practice temperance and having
lied a silence, was caught; for the things spoken beforehand long ago by the
prophets, having sojourned in Egypt, he proclaimed to the Italians as things
opened to himself as his own by God. 9.3 And Plato himself, the mildest above
all the others, who first accustomed the thoughts of men to look up from the
senses to the intelligibles and the things always having themselves in the
same way, and taught to look up to the things aloft, first set forth God who
is above essence, doing well, and subjected also a second to this one, and
divided two essences in number, there being one perfection of both, the
essence of the second god having existence from the first; for he himself is
the creator and administrator of the wholes, clearly having gone up above, and
the one after that one, having served the commands of that one, sends the
cause of the constitution of all things up to that one. 9.4 One then according
to the exact account would be the God who makes the care of all things and
provides for them, having ordered all things by Word; and the Word himself
happens to be God himself and child of God; for what other name would one,
putting it upon him besides the address of the child, not err the greatest
things? For the father of all things would justly be thought father also of
his own Word. 9.5 Up to this then Plato was temperate; but in the things next
he is found missing the truth, introducing a multitude of gods and putting
forms upon each, which also became a contributing cause of the greater error
among the unreasoning of men, not looking toward the providence of the highest
God, but worshipping their images transformed into human types and of other
animals. And it has happened that a certain greatest nature and education
worthy of great praise, mixed with some such faults, have themselves not
purely and not unmixedly. 9.6 And it seems to me that the same one, taking
hold of himself, corrects the error, in the things in which he clearly
confirms that God has breathed his own Word into us, making clear that the
spirit of God is a rational soul, and dividing all things into two kinds,
intelligible and sensible, the one .... and the other composed from a joining
of body, and the one comprehensible by mind, the other opinable by opinion
with sense; so that that which partakes of the Holy Spirit, as being
uncompounded and insoluble, is both eternal and has obtained the everlasting
life, but the sensible, being wholly dissolved according to the account also
according to which it

was constituted, is without share of the everlasting life. 9.7 And he teaches
wondrously also in the things next, that those who have lived well, souls that
is of the holy and good men, after the withdrawal from the body are
consecrated in the most beautiful things of heaven. But also usefully for
life; for who, having been persuaded by him and expecting this good fortune,
will not practice the best life, justice and temperance, and turn away from
vice? And following these things he brought on that the souls of the wicked,
being carried in the streams of Acheron and Pyriphlegethon in the manner of
shipwrecks, wander.

10.1 There are however some so damaged in thought, that whenever they meet
with these very things, they neither turn nor fear, but despise and laugh as
hearing certain fashioned myths; and they praise the variety of fluency, but
turn away from the solid of the doctrine, and they believe poetic myths, and
they cry throughout all Greece and all barbarian land with stale and false
reports. 10.2 For the poets say that children of God, men, after the end judge
the souls, hymning judgments and courts of justice and setting overseers of
the departed, but the same poets announce battles of daimons and certain
warlike laws, and they publish their fates, and they show some bitter by
nature, others alien to the care of men, and some difficult. And they
introduce them also lamenting the slaughters of their own children, as if it
were not possible for them to help not only strangers but not even the
dearest, and they introduce them as having human passions, singing wars and
woundings and joys and lamentations. 10.3 And they are trustworthy when they
speak. For if they pursue the poetic by divine inspiration, it is fitting to
believe them and to be persuaded concerning the things they say while being
inspired; and they speak passions of gods and of daimons. The passions of
these then have altogether touched truth. But someone will say it is permitted
to poets to lie; for to charm the souls of the hearers is proper to poetry,
but the true is, whenever the things said do not have themselves otherwise
than as they are said. 10.4 Let this be proper to poetry, to snatch the truth
at times; but those who speak the false things do not lie in vain; for they do
this either for the sake of gain and benefit, or, being conscious of some bad
action to themselves, they cover it because of the danger standing from the
laws. For it was possible, I think, for those relating nothing beside the
truth concerning the better nature neither to lie nor to be impious.

11.1 Therefore if anyone is unworthy of a good life and is conscious to
himself of having lived life faultily and disorderly, if he changes and looks
toward the divine with the eye of the soul, having been purified and become
alien to the former worst way of life and having obtained the therapy from
God, he will live into all the age. And it is fitting to give thanks for this
to the God who both saved that one and saves all. And they would be saved more
easily, if with pure faith they should receive the therapy of the soul and not
waver both ways, as those who at one time entrust themselves to the healings
and at another run away, but enduring steadfastly and nobly the cure of
justice and temperance and of the remaining things. We at any rate according
to the possible contend to fill those uninitiated in such accounts with good
hope, having called God as helper to us toward the practice; for it is not a
moderate work, the thoughts of those subject to us, if they happen to be good,
to turn toward piety, but if bad and ungrateful, to lead over to the contrary,
accomplishing them useful instead of useless.

rejoicing then in these very practices and thinking it the work of a good man
to hymn the Savior, I send away all things whatsoever the worse constitution
of fortune unreasonably inflicted by a symptom of ignorance, having thought
repentance the greatest salvation; and I would have prayed that this
revelation had been given me long ago, if somewhere blessed is the one
constituted from youth both in the knowledge of the divine things and having
been gladdened by the beauty of virtue. And let these things have been said by
us moderately; for even if the good of men do not become wise from the first
of age nor from swaddling-clothes, as they say, yet it is still desirable if
even in the age of prime they are fortunate in the wisdom. 11.2 And no
education from hu11.2 mans has ever helped us, but all the gifts are of God,
whatsoever are well reputed in characters and manners among those who have
mind. And I have also, as I have thrown forward against every poison,
whatsoever the wicked one crafts, a not moderate shield, the knowledge of the
things pleasing to God, from which having selected the things useful toward
the present account I will hymn the Father of all. 11.3 And you yourself,

13.1 And already some even play the youth in blaming God. Why then, having

commanded different and for the most part contrary things to generate
