OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS GREGORY THE WONDERWORKER A METAPHRASE OF THE
ECCLESIASTES OF SOLOMON.
CHAP. I.
These things says Solomon, the son of David the king and prophet, to all the
Church of God, 989 a king most honored beyond all men, and a prophet most
wise. How empty and unprofitable are the affairs of men and their pursuits, as
many as are human. For no one can say that any profit is attached to these
things which men, creeping upon the earth, hasten to accomplish with bodies
and souls, overcome by things of the moment, and not wishing to look even a
little, with the noble eye of the soul, above the stars. And the life of men
is worn away day after day, and by circuits of hours and years, and by the
bounded courses of the sun, some arriving, some departing. And the matter is
like the passage of winter-torrents falling into the unmeasured deep of the
sea with much turmoil. And the things that have come to be by God for the sake
of men remain the same, as: to be born from earth, to go away into earth, that
the earth itself stands, that the sun, going round the whole of it, runs back
again to the same bound, and the winds likewise, and that so many rivers give
themselves into the sea, and the winds falling upon it, neither force it to
overpass its own measures, nor do they themselves transgress. And the things
that contribute thus to this life of ours have been so fixed. But the words
and deeds contrived by men have no measure. And there is a great crowd of
words, but no profit from the wandering chatter; but the race of men is
insatiable, both toward speaking and toward hearing another speak; and still
they also long to look with idle eyes at each of the things that fall in their
way. What could come later that has not already been accomplished, or would be
done by men? What new thing that has not yet come into experience, of which it
is even worth making mention? For I think nothing; and whatever one may say is
recent or new, he will find, as he reckons, that it was not unknown even to
those of old. And just as the former things have been hidden by forgetfulness,
so also the present things will in time be dimmed for those afterward. And I
am not improvising these things now as I speak in assembly; but to me,
entrusted with the kingdom of the Hebrews in Jerusalem, all things have been
sufficiently considered. And having sought with care, and having wisely
considered all the nature around the earth, I knew it to be most various, how
it has been given to man to toil upon earth, rolling about at one time and
another on a different pretext of labor into nothing. And all things below are
full of a strange and abominable spirit, so that it is not possible to recover
them, nor even to call to mind at all how great an absurdity has seized the
affairs among men. For having once reasoned with myself, and having supposed
that I was then wiser than all who had come to be here before me, 992 I knew
how to understand parables and the natures of things. But I thought that I was
being carried in vain upon this, and that knowledge follows wisdom, and that
toils attend upon knowledge.
CHAP. II.
1 Having thought that this was so, I knew to turn to another form of life, and
to give myself over to luxury, and to take trial of various pleasures. Now
then I have understood that all such things are vain; and I checked laughter
that is borne along at random, and I chastened pleasure toward temperance,
having rebuked it bitterly.


And having reasoned that the soul is able to stay a nature of the body that is
drunken and flowing like wine, and that self-control enslaves desire, I was
eager to see what might be set before men as something serious, and truly
good, which they will accomplish through this life. For I have gone through
all the rest of the things that are thought admirable, both raisings of houses
exceedingly high and plantings of vines, and still constructions of gardens,
and possessions and cares of fruit-bearing trees of every kind; and where also
great reservoirs were constructed for receptions of waters, assigned for
abundant watering of the plants. And I also surrounded myself with a multitude
of household servants, men-servants and maid-servants; some acquired from
outside, and others I enjoyed as born in my own house. And herds of
four-footed animals, many of cattle, many of flocks, came under my authority
beyond any of those of old. And treasures of gold and of silver flowed in, I
having made the kings of all both my spear-bearers and tribute-bearers. And
choirs were assembled in full multitude, of males and of females together for
my delight, working out a wholly harmonious song. Banquets too and cup-bearers
I appointed as a part of this luxury, chosen from either race of men, nor
would I be able to recount them, so much did I surpass by these those who
reigned in Jerusalem before me. Then it came about that the things of wisdom
were diminished for me, and the things of desire that is not good were
multiplied. For giving way to every bait of the eyes, and to unmixed impulses
of the heart falling upon me from every side, having given myself over to
hopes of pleasures, and with all wretched luxuries I bound down my own choice.
For so my thoughts were carried into misfortune, as to think that these things
were good, and that it belonged to me to accomplish these. Then once having
sobered up and looked up, I saw the things I had in 993 my hands, both
wretched and wholly hard, works of a spirit that is not good. For nothing
whatsoever chosen by men now appears to me acceptable and eagerly sought by
right reasoning. Having therefore reckoned together both the goods of wisdom
and the evils of folly, I would reasonably wonder enough at such a man,
whoever, being carried along without reason, then taking hold of himself,
turns back to what is due. For there is a great interval between prudence and
folly, and the difference of both is as great as that of day toward night. So
then the one who has chosen virtue is like one who looks at each thing clearly
and upward, and who has made the season of* his own course one of brightest
light; but the one entangled in vice and in every kind of error is like one
wandering as in a moonless night, being blind in his looking, and deprived by
the darkness of affairs. And having considered the difference of the end of
each of these lives, I found nothing, and as having made myself a partner of
fools, I shall receive the wages of folly. For what good is there of those
sophisms, or what profit of the many words, where the streams of chatter start
as from a spring of folly? And there is nothing common to the wise man and the
fool, not according to the memory of men, not according to the recompense of
God. And of the affairs among men, while they still seem to be beginning, the
end already takes hold of them all. But a wise man never shares the same end
with one without understanding. I hated then also my whole life, the one spent
in vanities, which I passed clinging to the toils around the earth. For to
speak in short, all things have been toiled out by me painfully, having become
works of an unreasoning impulse; and another will succeed to them, whether
wise or simple, to the cold after-fruits of my labors. And when I had taken
these things back and thrown them away, the things that are truly good, set
before man, appeared: both knowledge of wisdom and possession of courage. And
if anyone has not taken thought of these, but is fluttered after other things,
such a one has chosen evil things instead of good, and has gone after vice
instead of goodness, and toil instead of quiet, dragged about by various
tumults, held always both by night and by day, by toils of body


pressing, and by unceasing cares of soul, his heart convulsing for the sake of
strange affairs. For the perfect good does not settle around eating and
drinking, even if the foods come to men from God in the highest degree; for
nothing of the things given us toward salvation exists outside his providence.
But the good man, having obtained wisdom from God, has obtained heavenly
gladness; but the wicked man, driven by God-sent evils, being sick with greed,
both hastens to gather many things, and is eager to reproach the one honored
by God before the Master of all, stretching forth useless gifts, having made
the pursuits of his own wretched soul both deceitful and vain. 996
CHAP. III.
1 And this time is full of all contrarieties: of births, then of deaths; of
sprouting of plants, then of overthrow; of healings and of slayings; of
setting houses upright and of pulling them down; of weepings and of laughters;
of beatings of the breast and of dances. Now one gathers the things from the
earth, and now he has thrown them out; and at one time he has been mad for a
woman, and at another he loathes her. And now one has sought something, and
now he has lost it; and now he has kept it, and now he has let it go; at
another time he killed, at another he was murdered; he spoke, then was silent;
he loved, then hated. For the affairs among men are at one time warred upon,
and at another are at peace, the affairs falling over swiftly from things that
seem to be good into things agreed to be evil. Let us then cease from
unprofitable toils. For all these things, as it seems to me at least, have
been set to sting men with venomous goads. A certain wicked watcher of times
has gaped round this age, straining beyond measure to make the fashioning of
God vanish, having chosen to war against it from beginning to end. I am
persuaded then that the greatest goods for man are good cheer and well-doing,
and moreover that this temporary enjoyment has come from God alone, if
righteousness should lead the actions. And of the eternal and incorruptible
things, as many as God has firmly appointed, it is possible neither to take
anything away nor to add anything. To whomsoever then they belong, yet they
are; those things are both fearful and wonderful; and the things that have
come to be stand; and the things that will be have already come to be
according to the having been known beforehand. And the one who is wronged uses
God as helper. I saw in the parts below a pit of punishment receiving the
impious, and another place given over to the pious. And I reasoned that all
things are thought and judged to be alike before God, that the just and the
unjust are the same, the rational and the irrational. For time has been
measured out alike to all, and death has been hung over them, and the tribes
of beasts and of men are the same before God, and they differ from one another
only by the articulated voice; and all things happen to them nearly alike, and
death goes, no more upon the rest of the animals than also upon men. For there
is a like spirit for all, and there is nothing more in men, but all things in
a word are vain, having taken their constitution from the same earth, and
having their dissolution into the same earth. For it is unclear 997 both
concerning the human souls, whether they will fly up above, and concerning the
rest which the irrational creatures possess, whether they will flow away
below. And what seemed to me to be no other good, except luxury and use of the
present things. For I thought it not possible again to come to the enjoyment
of these, once a man has tasted death.
CHAP. IV.
1 And turning myself away from all these thoughts I considered and turned away
from all forms of calumnies that wander among men, from which some who are
wronged weep and lament, struck down by force, those who would defend them, or


those who would at all comfort them, every want from every side holding them;
but those who make the hand their justice are raised to a height, from which
they will also fall. And of the unjust and the bold, those who have died have
fared better than those still living until now. And more to be chosen than
both of these is he who will be like them, but has not yet come to be, because
he has not yet touched the wickedness among men. And it became clear to me
also how great an envy follows a man from his neighbors, being a sting of a
wicked spirit; and that he who has received it and as it were taken it to his
breast has nothing else but to eat through his own soul, and to saw through
and consume together with his own body an inconsolable grief, counting the
prosperity of others as that grief. And one who thinks well would choose to
fill one of the hands, with ease and meekness, rather than both with toil and
the knavery of a deceitful spirit. And there is also something else, which I
know happens contrary to what is fitting, because of a man's choice that is
not good. Who, being left alone from every side, and having neither brother
nor son, but prospering with many possessions, living with insatiability, does
not wish to give himself over to goodness even in the least. Gladly then I
would ask him, for whose sake such a one toils, fleeing headlong from doing
anything good, and torn by various desires of making money from somewhere. And
much better than this are those who have entered a partnership of life
together, from which they would harvest the best things? For of two men
rightly attached to the same affairs, even if something should fall upon one
of them, still he has no small help, the one who is with him. And greatest
also is the calamity for a man faring ill, even the loneliness of the one who
would restore him. But those who share a table have both doubled the good
fortune for themselves, and have comforted the storm of unwelcome affairs; so
that both by day they are made bright by frankness toward one another, and by
night 1000 they are made honorable by cheerfulness. But he who draws a life
without partnership leads a certain shuddering life for himself, not having
perceived that if someone should also attack men locked together, he plans
boldly and not safely, and that not even a threefold cord is naturally snapped
easily. And I prefer a poor youth who is temperate to an old king who is
foolish, to whom it has not come into mind that it is possible for one from
the prison to be set to reign, and for him to fall later justly from the
unjust domination. For it happens that those marshaled under the youth who is
also sensible are without grief, as many as are older. For those born
afterward, because they have no experience of another, cannot even praise this
one, being led both by an unreasoning judgment and by the impulse of a
contrary spirit. And as you speak in assembly, keep before your eyes both to
go through your own life rightly, and to pray for the foolish, that they may
know, having received understanding, to turn aside from the practice of the
wicked.
CHAP. V.
1 And it is good to spare the tongue, and to use a steady heart in the
eagerness about words. For one who is without reason about them must not send
forth through the voice even absurd things, or the things that have come upon
the mind; but remembering that even if we stand far from heaven, one must know
that we speak in the hearing of God, and that it is profitable to speak
without falling. And just as there follows upon various cares of soul a
fantasy of dreams of every kind, so also chatter is yoked with folly. And a
promise that comes about through a vow, let it take its end through work. And
it is proper to fools to be rejectable; but you be true, knowing that it is
much better for you not to vow, nor to promise to do something, than having
vowed, then to leave it aside. And one must by every means flee 1001 the flood
of these fine words, as God will hear. For to the one who considers these
things there is nothing more than to perceive his own works being destroyed by
God. For just as the many dreams are vain, so also the many words.


And the fear of God is saving for men, but it is rare. Wherefore one [not]
ought to wonder, looking at both the poor who are calumniated and at judges
who reason falsely. And one must turn aside from seeming to be greater than
those more powerful. For even if this should come about, still wickedness by
itself will not deliver you from the fearful things that will be for you. And
just as a possession from seizure is most harmful and most unholy, so also for
a man desirous of money there comes to be neither satiety, nor the good-will
from his neighbors, even if they should acquire ever so much silver. For this
is vain. But goodness, making those who are with it exceedingly glad, renders
them courageous, furnishing the power of seeing through each of affairs. And
it is a great thing also not to cling to such cares; the poor man at least,
even if he is a slave, and has not overfilled his own belly, partakes of a
gentle relaxation through sleep; but desire of wealth is present with
sleeplessness and toils of soul. And what could be more absurd than this, than
with much eagerness and care to treasure up and keep the wealth, guarding for
himself an occasion of countless evils? And that wealth must at some time be
lost and destroyed, whether children should come to be for the one who
acquired it, or not; and he himself, even if he should not wish it, is due to
fall into the earth and go away such as he also happened to be when he was
present into being. And being about to go away with empty hands, he increases
his own wickedness, as not considering that an end of life is laid upon him
like the beginning, and that he toils unprofitably, granting rather to some
impulse of wind than to his own eagerness, having consumed his whole life
both in most unholy desires and in unreasoning impulses, and still in griefs
and in weaknesses. And to speak in short, the days are darkness to such a man,
and the life is mourning. Yet that is good and not to be rejected; for it is a
gift of God, that a man should be able to enjoy his own labors in gladness,
having received possessions given by God and not seized. For this one is not
sick with griefs, nor is he for the most part a slave to wicked thoughts; but
he measures out his own life with well-doings, being of good cheer toward all,
and rejoicing in the gift of God.
CHAP. VI.
1 And I will set forth in the word the ill-fortune that most of all prevails
among men. When God, having supplied all 1004 the things according to their
mind, takes away from him nothing whatsoever of the things that have come into
desire, not abundance, not much glory, not the rest about which men are
fluttered; but he, prospering in all, as though held by this one God-sent
evil, the not enjoying, will store them up for his companion, having fallen
fruitless both to himself and to his neighbors. This I set down as a great
proof and a manifest refutation of surpassing wickedness; and that man, who
having been named father of very many children without harm, and having lived
a long time, was not filled in his soul with goodness for so long, not having
taken experience of death: this man I would envy neither for the many children
nor for the many days; and I prefer to him an untimely embryo fallen from a
mother's womb. For that, just as it came vainly, so it also goes away secretly
in forgetfulness, not having touched evils, nor having looked upon the sun.
This is lighter than for the wicked man, even if he should measure out his own
life by a thousand years, not to recognize goodness. And the end of both is
death. A fool is refuted above all by taking satiety of no desire. But the
temperate man is not held in these passions. And for the most part uprightness
of life leads a man to poverty. And spectacles of greedy eyes put many beside
themselves, irritating the soul, drawing it toward unprofitable eagerness
through the empty desire of being seen. The things that have now been
generated, at any rate, are already known; and it happens to be clear that
against those


above him a man is not able to array himself. Chattering, at any rate, is
turned about among men, increasing the mindlessness of those who use it.
CHAP. VII.
1 For by no means is the one who knows the things according to his mind that
will befall him through life more benefited; for let us grant this;
nevertheless even by the meddlesomeness of men he devises, so as both to busy
himself about the things that will be after the death of each person, and to
seem to know. And a good memory is gentler to a soul than oil to a body; and
an exit of life is better than a birth; and it is more to be chosen to mourn
than to revel, and to be present with those who grieve than with those who are
drunk. For it is so, that one who has taken the end of life is not cautious
about the things around him. And a prudent anger is more honorable than
laughter; for by a severe disposition of face a soul is set right. The souls
of the wise, then, are downcast and drawn in, but those of fools, being lifted
up, are poured out. And it is far more to be prayed for to receive the rebuke
of one wise man than to become a hearer of a whole 1005 chorus of paltry and
wretched men singing. For the laughter of foolish men is like the sound of
many thorns burning with a fierce fire. And this too is wretched, and indeed a
very great evil, calumny; for this plots against the souls of the wise, and
attempts to destroy the noble stand of the good. And it is fitting to praise
not the one who begins words, but the one who ceases, and to accept the
moderate character, and not the one lifted up and inflated. And one must
altogether spare wrath, and not be carried readily into anger, to which fools
are slaves. And they sin who say that a better life has been given to those
before, and they do not understand that wisdom differs very much from
abundance of money; and it is so much more manifest, as silver appears
brighter than its own shadow. For the life of man comes about not from
acquisition of perishable wealth, but from wisdom. And who will also be able,
tell me, to speak out the so great and so good providence of God? or who to
call back the things that seem to have been neglected by God justly? And I,
not acting rightly before, considered all things, both a just man remaining in
righteousness, and not departing even until death, but even being plotted
against because of it, and an impious man dying together with his own
wickedness. And being just, one must not seem to be so very much, nor overly
wise and beyond measure; lest having stumbled in something, he should sin many
times over. And do not be bold and rash, lest an untimely death seize you. And
it is a very great good to take hold of God, and being in this to sin nothing.
For to touch the undefiled with an unclean hand is abominable. But he who
yields in the fear of God escapes all the contrary things. And wisdom of help
can do more than a band of the most powerful men in a city, or it also often
pardons justly those who fail of what is due. For there is not even one who is
without stumbling. And one must not approach the words of the impious in any
way at all, lest having become an ear-witness of the words against you, as of
the chatter of a wicked servant, then being bitten in the heart, you should
later in many actions be turned even yourself to cursing in return. And I knew
all these things, having received wisdom from God, which afterward having
thrown away, I was no longer able to be the same. For wisdom fled from me to
an infinite length, and into an unmeasured depth, so that it was no longer
possible to take hold of it. So that afterward I also held wholly back even
from seeking it; and I no longer thought still of considering the
follies of the impious and their vain counsels, and a wandering life. And
being so disposed, I was carried to the things themselves, and being held by a
death-bearing desire, I knew the woman, being a certain earth, or if there is
anything else of such a kind. 1008 For her heart nets those who pass, and
having joined hand to hand only, she holds, or as if throwing bonds around she
was dragging. And you would be delivered from her only,


having God gracious and as overseer; as he who has been enslaved to sin would
not escape. And I, having sought through all women for their temperance, found
it in none. And one might know a temperate man among a thousand, but a woman
not. And I considered that above all, that men, having been made by God simple
in their souls, draw upon themselves various reasonings and endless inquiries,
and pretending to seek wisdom, they wear themselves out about little words.
CHAP. VIII.
1 And wisdom, when it is examined in a man, appears also through the face,
lighting the one who possesses it; just as shamelessness, as soon as he is
seen, convicts as worthy of hatred that one in whom it has dwelt. And one must
give heed very carefully to the words of the king, and flee an oath by every
means, especially the one that comes about toward the name of God. Yet it is
fitting to attend to an evil word; but to guard against all blasphemy toward
the Master. For it will not be possible to blame him when he brings
something, nor to speak against the decrees of the only potentate and king.
And it is better and more profitable, remaining in the holy commandments, to
stand outside the wicked words. For a wise man knows and foreknows the
judgment toward the due time, that it will be just. For all the affairs in the
life of men await the punishment from above; except that the wicked man does
not seem to know very well that, there being much providence over him, nothing
whatsoever will escape notice into the time afterward. For he does not know
the things that are coming; for no one will be able to announce any of them
according to worth; because no one will be so strong as to be able to hinder
the angel who takes away his soul, nor would any method at all be found of
setting aside somehow the season of the end; but just as for one taken in the
midst of war, things inescapable are to be seen from every side, and every
impiety of a man is destroyed together. And I am struck, whenever I look at
what and how many things men have practiced toward the hurt of their
neighbors. And I know the impious being snatched away from here beforehand and
becoming 1009 out of the way, in return for having given themselves to vanity.
For since the providence of God does not go after all swiftly because of the
great forbearance, nor does it punish at once upon the offenses, for this
reason the wicked man thinks he ought to sin still more, as one who will get
off unharmed; not understanding that even after a very long time, having done
injustice, he will not escape notice; and it is a very great good to be in awe
of God; of whom the impious man, having fallen away, will not use his own
folly for a long time. And a most evil and false opinion often feeds upon men,
concerning both the just and the unjust. For they take up the contrary things
concerning each; and one who is just has not seemed such; and again an impious
man has been thought prudent. This error I set down as hard among the things
that are most so. And I once thought that the greatest of goods was eating and
drinking; and that one most dear to God who should enjoy these through life as
much as possible; and I held such good cheer alone to be a comfort of life;
and so I gave heed to nothing else than this opinion, so as neither by night
nor by day to lead myself away from all things, as many as have been found
toward luxury for men. And I knew this much, that one mixing in those things
will by no means be able, not even having toiled much, to find the good that
truly is.
CHAP. IX.
1 For I thought then that all men had been judged worthy of the same things;
and if someone wise had taken care of righteousness, and had turned aside from
injustice, and had fled enmity, being kindly toward all, which is pleasing to
God, this one appeared to toil in vain. And an end


one seemed of the just man and of the impious, of the good and of the bad, of
the clean and of the unclean, and of the one who propitiates God and of the
one who does not. For both the unjust and the good, both the perjurer and the
one who utterly turns aside from an oath, were hoped to drive to the same end,
and a certain condemnation steals in, of all ending into like things. And I
know now that these are thoughts of fools, and errors and deceits. And they
say many things, that the one who has died has gone away completely; and that
the living is to be preferred to the dead, even if he should lie in darkness,
even if he should go through life in the manner of a dog, beside the lion that
has died. For the living know this at least, that they will die; but the dead
know not even a little. And recompenses of nothing are set forth after the
filling up of the due. Both the enmity and the friendship toward the dead have
an end. For of those, whether zeal, it has failed; or life, it has vanished.
And there is a share in nothing for the one who has once gone away from here.
Error, still singing these things, also advises such things: What are you
doing, O you, and you do not live in luxury, nor do you fill yourself with
foods of every kind, and fill yourself beyond measure with wine? Do you not
perceive that these things have been given from God toward unhindered
enjoyment? Put on a garment 1012 newly washed, and having anointed the head
with myrrh, look at this woman, and at that one, and pass through the vain
life vainly? For nothing else is left for you besides these things, not here,
nor after death. But do the things that fall in the way. For neither will
anyone exact an account of these from you; nor are the things done by men
known at all outside men. And Hades, whatever it even is, into which we are
said to go away, has no share of wisdom and of perception. These things the
vain say. But I know well that neither will those who seem swiftest accomplish
that great course, nor will the powerful and fearful, glorified among men, win
that fearful war. But neither is prudence tested in abundance of food, nor is
understanding accustomed to share with wealth. Nor do I rejoice with those who
think that all will obtain the like things. And those who consider such things
seem to be very much asleep, and not to reckon that, becoming snatched away
like fishes and birds, they will be worn away in evils, having obtained the
due rebuke suddenly. And I hold wisdom so great a thing, that I hold even a
small city inhabited by few, but besieged by the hand of a great king, to be
much and great, if it should have even one wise man as a poor citizen. For
this one would be able to save his own city both from the enemies and from the
entrenchments. And others do not know that wise man who is poor; but I even
altogether prefer the strength in wisdom to this popular power. But here
wisdom dwelling with poverty is dishonored; yet afterward it will be heard,
sounding above potentates and tyrants who reach after evils. For wisdom is
even stronger than iron; and the folly of one works danger for many, even if
he should be contemptible to many.
CHAP. X.
1 For even flies that have fallen into myrrh, and have been choked in it, make
both the look and the anointing of that pleasant ointment unseemly; and it is
in no way fitting to make mention of wisdom and of folly in the same. The wise

fool will fall into destruction through his own chatter. And having once
opened the mouth, he begins foolishly, and ceases quickly, showing through all
his own mindlessness. And it is impossible for a man to know something, and to
learn from a man the things from the beginning, or the things that are coming.
For who will be the one who declares? And a man who does not know how to go to
the good city undergoes ill-treatment, both in the eyes and in the whole face.
And I foretell evils to that city whose king is young, and whose rulers are
gluttons. And I bless the good land whose king is the son of the free; where
those who have been judged worthy to rule there will enjoy good things in due
season. But the sluggish and idle lessen a house, having become scoffers; and
misusing all things toward their own greediness of throat, led by silver, they
endure for a small price to do all things shamefully and lowly. And it is
fitting to obey a king, and rulers, or potentates, and not to be hated, nor at
all to throw out any grievous word toward them. For there is fear that this or
that, having been said in private, may somehow come into the open. For to the
only one who is both rich and a great king, swift and winged angels convey all
things, fulfilling a service at once spiritual and rational.

grant to desires, and to do the things that seem good to them, and to look at

whom I will say this much: that you are mindless, O you, not expecting the
judgment that will be from God upon all these things. And wastefulness and
wantonness are wicked, and a filthy outrage of our bodies is destructive. For
mindlessness attends upon youth; and mindlessness leads into destruction.

being an old man, and having gone through a long time of life, grew weary

good shepherd and teacher, as if all from one mouth, recounting more

unprofitable things about what is fitting, in which there is nothing more than
toiling at random. But I will need at last some such epilogue as this, that: O
men, behold, I declare to you expressly and shortly, to fear God who is both
Master of all together and overseer, and to keep also his precepts; and to be
persuaded that everyone afterward will also be judged, and that each will
receive according to worth the recompense of his own works, of good things and
