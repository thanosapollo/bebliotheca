* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Great curiosity, and a far greater memory, is needed for the study, if anyone should wish to excerpt from the most received writings of the philosophers, or of the poets, or of any teachers of secular learning and wisdom, testimonies of Christian truth, so that its rivals and persecutors may be convicted from their own instrument, both of error against themselves and of injustice against us. Some, in whom the labor of curiosity and the hold of memory have persisted from their former literature, have composed little works among us in that manner, recalling and attesting in each case the reason and origin of traditions and the arguments of opinions, by which it may be recognized that we have taken up nothing new or monstrous for which the common and public writings do not also come to our support, whether we have cast out some error or admitted some equity. But human hardness, born of unbelief, has withdrawn credit even from its own teachers, elsewhere most approved and most choice, whenever they strike against the arguments of the Christian defense. Then the poets are vain, when they mark the gods with human passions and fables; then the philosophers are harsh, when they knock at the doors of truth. Thus far he will be held wise and prudent who has spoken nearly as a Christian; yet if he has affected any prudence or wisdom, whether by spitting on ceremonies or by convicting the world, he is branded as a Christian. Therefore we shall now have nothing to do with the letters and learning of a perverse happiness, which is trusted rather in the false than in the true. Let those look to it who have pronounced of one only God. Nay, let nothing at all be related that a Christian might recognize, lest he be able to reproach them. For even what has been related, not all know, nor do those who know trust that it stands firm. So far are men from assenting to our writings, to which no one comes unless already a Christian.

I call a new witness, nay, one better known than all literature, more practiced than all doctrine, more current than any publication, greater than the whole man, that is, the whole of what a man is. Stand forth in the midst, O soul: whether you are a divine and eternal thing, according to most philosophers, so much the more you will not lie; or whether you are not divine at all, being mortal, as Epicurus alone thinks, so much the more you ought not to lie; whether you are taken from heaven or conceived from earth, whether you are put together from numbers or from atoms, whether you begin with the body or are brought in after the body, from wherever and in whatever way you make man a rational animal, most capable of sense and knowledge. But I do not call you as you are formed in the schools, exercised in libraries, fed in academies and Attic porches, belching wisdom. I appeal to you simple and rude and unpolished and unlearned, such as they have you who have you alone, you yourself entire from the crossing, from the street corner, from the weaver's shop. I have need of your ignorance, since no one believes your little skill. I demand those things which you bring with you to man, which you have learned to feel either from yourself or from whatever author of yours. You are not, so far as I know, a Christian. For a Christian is made, not born. Yet now Christians demand testimony from you, from a stranger against your own people, so that they may blush at you, because they hate and mock us for the very things that now hold you by conscience.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

We do not please when we preach God as one, by this unique name, from whom all things are and under whom the universe lies. Give your testimony, if you know it so. For we hear you also, openly and with a whole freedom not permitted to us, at home and abroad, pronouncing thus: "God grant it," and "If God will." By that voice you both signify that there is someone, and you confess all power to him to whose will you look, and at the same time you deny that the rest are gods, while you call them by their own names, Saturn, Jupiter, Mars, Minerva. For you confirm as God alone him whom you name only as God, so that when you sometimes call those others gods, you seem to have used a title that is another's, and as if on loan. The nature of the God whom we preach is not hidden from you either. "God is good," "God does good" is your voice. Plainly you add, "But man is evil," that is, by the contrary proposition, obliquely and figuratively reproaching man as evil because he has departed from the good God. Also, because among us every blessing of the goodness and kindness that belong to God is the highest sacrament of discipline and conduct, you say "God bless you" as readily as a Christian must; but when you turn the naming of God into a curse, by that same saying you confess, according to us, that all his power stands over us.

There are those who, even if they do not deny God, do not think him an inspector and umpire and judge (in which they most of all reject us, who fly to this discipline from fear of the preached judgment), honoring God in this way, while they free him from the cares of watching and the annoyances of punishment, to whom they do not even ascribe anger. For if God is angry, they say, he is corruptible and passible; and further, what suffers and what is corrupted can even admit destruction, which God does not admit. But the same people, confessing elsewhere that the soul is divine and conferred by God, fall into a testimony of the soul itself that must be turned against the former opinion. For if the soul is either divine or given by God, without doubt it knows its giver, and if it knows him, it also fears him, and at last fears him only as the one who enlarged it. Or does it not fear him whom it would rather have propitious than angry? Whence, then, the soul's natural fear toward God, if God does not know how to be angry? How is he feared who does not know how to be offended? What is feared, if not anger? Whence anger, if not from punishment? Whence punishment, if not from judgment? Whence judgment, if not from power? Whose is the highest power, if not God's alone? Hence, therefore, O soul, conscience supplies you with this, to preach at home and abroad, with no one mocking or forbidding: "God sees all things," and "I commend it to God," and "God will repay," and "God will judge between us." Whence do you have this, you who are not a Christian? And so often, even crowned with Ceres' fillet, and clothed in Saturn's scarlet, and wrapped in the linen of the goddess Isis, even in the temples themselves you implore God as judge. You stand under Aesculapius, you deck Juno in bronze, you shoe Minerva and set a helmet of dark figures on her, and you call none of the gods present to witness. In your own forum you appeal to a judge from elsewhere; in your own temples you suffer another God. O testimony of truth, which even among the demons themselves makes a witness for the Christians!

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But when we affirm that there are demons, some partisan of Chrysippus mocks, as if we did not also prove it, we who alone drive them out of bodies. That they both exist and are held in abomination, your curses answer. You call a man a demon who, by uncleanness or malice or insolence or any stain which we assign to demons, is so importunate that hatred becomes necessary. Finally, in every vexation and scorn and detestation you name Satan, whom we call the angel of malice, the craftsman of all error, the interpolator of the whole world, through whom man was deceived from the beginning so as to overstep God's command, and for that reason given over to death, and who thereafter made the whole race, infected from his seed, also the transmitter of his own condemnation. You feel, then, your destroyer, and although Christians alone know him, or whatever sect is with the Lord, you nevertheless know him, while you hate him.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Now as to what concerns a more necessary opinion of yours, and how far it reaches your own state itself, we affirm that you remain after the closing of life and await the day of judgment, and according to deserts are appointed either to torment or to refreshment, both everlasting; and that for the bearing of these you must necessarily recover your former substance and the material and memory of the same man, because you could feel nothing of evil or of good without the passible faculty of the flesh, and there would be no ground of judgment without producing the very man who deserved to suffer the judgment. That Christian opinion, though much more honorable than the Pythagorean, which does not transfer you into beasts, though fuller than the Platonic, which even restores to you the dowry of the body, though graver than the Epicurean, which defends you from destruction, is nevertheless, because of its name, assigned to vanity and stupidity and, as they say, presumption alone. But we do not blush if our presumption will be with you.

First, when you remember someone dead, you call him a poor wretch, not because he has been snatched from the good of life, but as already enrolled for punishment and judgment. Otherwise at other times you call the dead secure. You profess both the inconvenience of life and the benefit of death. Further, you call them secure whenever you withdraw to the tombs outside the gate with meats and dainties, rather feasting yourself, or return from the tombs somewhat the worse for drink. But I demand your sober judgment. You call the dead poor wretches when you speak of your own, when you are far from them. For at their banquet you could not reproach their lot as if they were present and reclining with you. You ought to flatter those on whose account you live more sumptuously. Do you then call him a poor wretch who feels nothing? What of this, that you curse him as one who feels, when you recall him with the sting of some offense? You pray that the earth lie heavy, and that torment lie upon the ash among the dead below. Equally, on the good side, to him to whom you owe thanks, you pray refreshment upon his bones and ashes, and you wish that he rest well among the dead below. If there is no suffering for you after death, if no continuance of sense, if finally you yourself are nothing where you have left the body, why do you lie against yourself, as if you could suffer something further? Nay, why do you fear death at all? Nothing is to be feared by you after death, since nothing is to be experienced after death. For even if it can be said that death is feared not as threatening anything beyond, but as cutting off the advantage of life, yet when the far more numerous inconveniences of life fall away together, it dissolves the fear by the gain of the heavier part, and the loss of goods is no longer to be feared, which is balanced by another good, that is, peace from inconveniences. That is not to be feared which frees us from everything to be feared. If you fear to depart from life because you know it as best, certainly you ought not to fear death, which you do not know as evil. But when you fear, you know it as evil. You would not know it as evil, because you would not fear it, if you did not know that there is something after death which makes it evil, so that you fear.

Let us now omit the natural fear of death. Let no one fear what he cannot escape. I meet you on the other side, of a gladder hope after death. For a desire of fame after death is inborn in almost all. It would be long to unroll the Curtii and the Reguli and the Greek men, whose praises of a death despised for posthumous fame are numberless. Who does not today so study to keep his name by a memory cultivated after death, whether by works of literature, or by simple praise of character, or by the very ambition of tombs? Whence does the soul today affect something that it would want after death, and so greatly prepare what it will use after dying? It would care nothing at all about what comes after, if it knew nothing about what comes after. But perhaps you are more sure of sense after your departure than of a resurrection at some time, of which we are marked as presumptuous. Yet this too is preached by the soul. For if someone inquires after one long since dead as after a living man, it readily occurs to say, "He has already gone, and he ought to return."

These testimonies of the soul, the more common they are, the more simple; the more simple, the more common; the more common, the more shared; the more shared, the more natural; the more natural, the more divine. I do not think they can seem frivolous and cold to anyone, if he considers the majesty of nature, from which the soul's authority is rated. As much as you grant the teacher, so much you will award the pupil. Nature is the teacher, the soul the pupil. Whatever the one has taught or the other thoroughly learned has been handed down from God, the teacher, of course, of the teacher herself. What the soul can presume of its principal founder, it is in you to estimate from that which is in you. Feel her who causes you to feel. Consider her a prophet in presentiments, an augur in omens, a seer in events. Is it a wonder if, given by God, she knows how to divine for man? As much a wonder if she knows him by whom she was given? Even when circumvented by the adversary she remembers her author and his goodness and his decree and her own end and the adversary himself. So is it a wonder if, given by God, she sings the same things that God has given his own to know?

But he who has not thought such outbursts of the soul to be the teaching of a congenital nature and of an inborn conscience, its silent deposits, will say rather that, once opinions were scattered among the crowd, the use of published writings has already become, as if a confirmed vice, a habit of talking in that way. Certainly the soul is prior to the letter, and speech prior to the book, and sense prior to the pen, and man himself prior to the philosopher and the poet. Are we then to believe that before literature and its publication men lived mute of such pronouncements? Did no one speak of God and his goodness, no one of death, no one of the underworld? Speech was a beggar, I suppose; nay, it could not even exist, since even then those things were lacking without which even today it cannot be happier and richer and wiser, if those things which today are so easy, so constant, so near, born as it were on the very lips, were not there formerly, before letters had sprouted in the world, before Mercury, I suppose, had been born. And whence, I ask, did it befall the letters themselves to know and to sow into the use of speech what no mind had ever conceived, or tongue put forth, or ear received? But since the divine scriptures which are with us, or with the Jews, upon whose wild olive we have been grafted, precede the secular writings by much, or by only some modest age, as we have shown in its place for the demonstration of their credit; even if the soul has taken these utterances from writings, it will have to be believed that it was from ours, not from yours, because earlier things are stronger for instructing the soul than later, which themselves also waited to be instructed by the earlier; and even if we grant that it was instructed from yours, still the tradition belongs to the principal origin, and whatever it has befallen you to have taken from ours and handed on is altogether ours. That being so, it does not much matter whether the soul's conscience was formed by God, or by the writings of God. What then do you want, O man: that these things have gone out from the human opinions of your writings into the callousness of common use?

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Believe, then, your own, and so much the more believe the divine from our records, but equally believe nature from the soul's own judgment. Choose which of these you would observe as the more faithful sister of truth. If you doubt your writings, neither God nor nature lies. That you may believe both nature and God, believe the soul; so it will come about that you believe yourself also. She certainly is the one whom you value as much as she values you: whose you are entirely, who is everything to you, without whom you can neither live nor die, on whose account you neglect God. For when you fear to become a Christian, meet her. Why does she worship another, and name God? Why, when she marks spirits that must be cursed, does she pronounce them demons? Why does she call heaven to witness, and curse toward the earth? Why does she serve in one place, and in another invoke a vindicator? What does she judge of the dead? What words of Christians does she have, whom she does not even wish heard or seen? Why has she either given us those words, or received them from us? Why has she either taught, or learned? Hold suspect an agreement of preaching in so great a disagreement of conduct. You are vain if you will charge this to this tongue alone, or to the Greek, which are held neighboring to each other, so as to deny the universality of nature. The soul does not fall from heaven for Latins or Argives alone. Of all nations man is one name, one soul, a varied voice, one spirit, a varied sound, each nation its own speech, but the matter of speech common. God is everywhere and the goodness of God everywhere, the demon everywhere and the cursing of the demon everywhere, the invocation of divine judgment everywhere, death everywhere and the conscience of death everywhere, and testimony everywhere. Every soul proclaims by its own right what we are not allowed even to mutter. Rightly, then, every soul is both defendant and witness, defendant of error so far as it is also witness of truth, and it will stand before the halls of God on the day of judgment having nothing to say. You used to preach God, and you did not seek him; you used to abominate demons, and you worshipped them; you used to appeal to the judgment of God, and you did not believe that it exists; you used to presume the punishments below, and you did not take care against them; you used to know the flavor of the Christian name, and you used to persecute the Christian.
