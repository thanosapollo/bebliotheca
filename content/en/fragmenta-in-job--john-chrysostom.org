PREFACE That the book is useful to everyone, in every form of lives and
circumstances, to man and woman, elder and young, private person and ruler,
rich and poor, to the one flourishing in the things on the right, and to the
one pressed by the things on the left, the proof is at hand to take. This
wonderful and great man, celebrated everywhere in the inhabited world, the
athlete and crown-bearer of piety, who went through all the contests, who set
up countless trophies against the devil, this man became rich and poor, both
glorious and contemptible, both many-childed and childless, in royal courts
and on a dunghill, in a splendid robe, and after that in a fountain of worms;
he enjoyed countless attendance, and after these things he endured countless
insults, servants rising up, friends reproaching, a wife plotting. All things
flowed away from him as from fountains [perhaps: flowed upon him], abundance
of money, greatness of power, conspicuousness of glory, security of peace,
honor, and attendance, and health of body, and good order of children. Nothing
was painful; wealth was present to him with security, and prosperity that did
not fall; and very reasonably; for God walled him round from every side; but
later all those things flew away, and countless storms settled =64.508= upon
that house, and all successive and continuous, and all with excess; for all
that was his was snatched away at once, and the children were buried by an
untimely and violent death, slaughtered at the table and the banquet, not with
a knife and a sword, but by an evil spirit that bound the house. And a wife
after these things was armed, and brought the engines against the righteous
man; and friends and servants, some spat in his face, others trampled and
leaped upon him; and he was cast out of every house, and his dwelling was the
dunghill, and he teemed with fountains of worms from every side, and the
adamant was flowing with blood and ichor, and taking a potsherd he poured out
the ichors, becoming his own executioner. And pains successive and
unendurable, and night harder than day, and day more burdensome than night,
and everything cliffs and reefs; and the one consoling, no one, but those
trampling upon him, countless; yet in so great a storm, and waves so
unspeakable, he stood nobly and unturned. The cause was that he reasoned well
the nature of things, and knowing their change, he foresaw the future, and
doing well, he was not driven to despair by despondency; for this reason also
in prosperity he was moderate. And not only of endurance and patience, but
also of the rest of virtue, this much-suffering man is set forth as a pattern
to those who attend; of courage and steadfastness, in trials; of not
insulting, but being meek, and moderate, and sociable with cheerfulness, in
wealth and power; of self-control, in pleasures; and of justice, in judgments
and contracts; and upon all, of concord, and of the head of the virtues, love;
for he accomplished every virtue with the fitting exactness. Taking this image
that is sketched, fix it in your conscience; and if you are in despondency,
flee to it; and if in wealth, take the medicine from here, so as neither to be
drowned by poverty nor puffed up by wealth; and if you lose children, you have
consolation from here; for you will find here the excess both of disasters and
of endurance; and if you fall into disease, think of the fountains of worms,
and you will bear all things gently; and if a friend plots, again bring the


saint into the midst, and you will prevail over the passion; and if chance
people abuse you, think of what he suffered from the servants, and you will
receive much healing; and if someone wraps you in an evil opinion, consider
what they said about this man, that he has not yet paid a penalty worthy of
his sins, and how they reproached him, and you will prevail over this passion.
For there is no disaster among men which this man did not endure, harder than
any adamant, bearing hunger, and poverty, and disease, and loss of children,
and the sudden damage of so much money; and after that plotted against by a
wife, insulted by friends, plotted against by servants, through all things he
was shown to be harder than any rock =64.509=; and these things, before the
law and the grace. For we shall not have a slight defense, when we who after
law and grace have enjoyed so great a gift bear less than this man, who at the
beginning and prefaces of the life of men displayed so great a philosophy.

CHAPTER 1 "There was a certain man in the land of Ausitis, whose name was
Job." What does the preface mean, and for what reason did the writer begin
thus? He was about to narrate a virtuous life, and one that far outstrips our
condition; so that no one, looking up to the excess of the contests and of the
endurance, might suppose it to be some foreign nature, and not human, he shows
the nature in advance, that you may wonder at the purpose, that he was a man,
by nature one of the many, but by virtue higher than the many. He spoke the
common of nature, that he might show the peculiar of choice, that sharing the
same nature with all, he lived beyond nature, and greater than according to
man. And he proclaims him also from the country, that from here too you may
wonder, what a rose blossomed from a thorn. See the first encomium, that the
land of Ausitis brought forth such a shoot. For that in Arabia someone should
be so approved, where all were corrupted, where there was no pattern of good
order, this was wonderful. "And that man was true, blameless, just,
god-fearing, abstaining from every evil deed." The outside wise men, defining
man, say: Man is a rational mortal animal; but the divine Scripture defines
him who has kept the according to the image, and is known from virtue, to be
man, and the one who has confused and corrupted the godlike types and
characters it does not even wish to call a man; for if someone has the
character of nature, but insults it by the vice of choice, he hears from the
Prophet: Man being in honor did not understand, he was compared to the
senseless cattle, and became like them. Here then Scripture, using its own
custom, and showing that nothing so characterizes the man as the blameless,
the just, the god-fearing, from here also declares Job known, and says that he
was a true man. For the base are false men, like those painted. These things
too the Ecclesiast, summing up at the end all the commandments into the fear
of God, The end, he says, of the word, hear all: fear God, and keep his
commandments, because =64.512= this is every man. And another prophesying in
Jerusalem, since he saw no one who heard, I came, he says, and there was no
man; I called, and there was none who obeyed. And we too according to common
custom, whenever we send a servant or a son to some action, are accustomed to
say to him, as a man take hold of the action, that is, keep the human


condition, as one of sense, as one having reason, so think, so act, from the
working show that you are truly a man. For not from the shape, but from the
working, what each is is known; and if not, he is called homonymously what he
is called. And that man alone is a true man who preserves the image, who does
not make the God-given beauty vanish, such as Job, true, not counterfeit,
showing by works the virtue of the man, not pretending by shape, or acting it
theatrically. And what God formerly required of Abraham saying: But you, be
well-pleasing before me, and become blameless; this now Scripture also
witnesses to Job; for having said that he was true, it added that he was also
blameless. And test the word for me, that you may know the great exactness of
the righteous man; why did it not say unaccused, but blameless? because
accusation is set upon the greatest sins, but blame upon small and least
things; not only then, he says, did he abstain from the heaviest evils, but he
did not even endure to be caught in the lightest, which is blame rather than
accusation, and brings only condemnation, not punishment. Aquila indeed, and
Symmachus, i.e. blameless, the one rendered simple, the other unblemished; the
one hinting at the unmalicious and unfeigned of the righteous man, the other
at what had been trained toward exactness into virtue. For unblemished is the
one lacking none of the goods, the one conducting his life from every vice
without stumbling; then after the blameless, it adds that he is also just, and
justice is a gathering and union of all the fair and good things. For if
something of the things seeming to be praiseworthy should come to be in
someone by itself apart from the rest of virtue, this is incomplete and
useless, rather even often found contrary and blameworthy. For example someone
is intelligent and understanding; if he should not counsel just things, he is
a knave and ill-natured, this man is not prudent. For concerning such it has
been said, They are wise to do evil, but to do well they have not known; again
another is courageous toward contests and dangers; if he should not wish to
act justly, he is savage and violent, as those who say: And let our strength
be a law of justice; for the weak is proved useless. Of the pleasures about
the body [if] someone masters them, yet he would not be self-controlled, if a
lover of money, a lover of wealth, a prodigal, not liberal, unsparing of money
toward desire. In general, every portion of justice, cut off from the rest as
from a body, is nothing. But the harmony and assembly of all the admirable
things would reasonably be thought one =64.513= justice; and if some of the
wonderful things should be absent, it limps in this and is mutilated, and the
name of justice is false. For the just man first is of necessity wise; for how
would he do the things that ought, and deal fittingly with all, not
understanding the rank and power of each? Then being self-controlled, for
incontinence is injustice; god-loving, For the pious, he says, counseled just
things, gentle, philanthropic, beneficent; and in a word the agreement of all
the things to be done according to right reason completes the just man; and
this Ezekiel taught us exactly, through whom God says, But the man who shall
be just, the one doing justice. Then it describes the just man, composing the
complete and whole virtue from the parts; and first it attaches piety to him,
saying: From the mountains he shall not eat; for on the mountains they
performed the idolatries. Then it says self-control must be present to him;
And the wife, it says, of the neighbor he shall not defile, and to a woman
being in her uncleanness he shall not approach. Then it also writes upon him
the meek and the equitable, And a man, it says, he shall not oppress; toward
this it declares him to act as his own in saying: And he shall not seize a
seizure; and it shapes him merciful and sociable saying: He shall give his
bread to the hungry, and shall clothe the naked, he shall not give his silver
upon interest, and


increase he shall not take; and it declares him easy in contracts and
peaceful, so as to be able also to arbitrate best for those in dispute; For
also from injustice, it says, he shall turn his hand and shall make a just
judgment between a man and between his neighbor. For this is the exceptional
and most distinguished, composed with all the other achievements of justice,
the civic and judicial habit, bringing what is suitable to each, wherefore it
is also named from judgment. And having gone through all the things to be
done, and the things not, it fitted the common rule and type to all, looking
to which one will miss in nothing. In my ordinances, it says, he has walked,
my justifications he has kept to do them. And all these things bound together
and mixed, and held as one many-membered and all-harmonious body, the just man
becomes. And having completed him through so many things, and as it were
composing for him one definition and circumscription from so many, it shut all
into one name, and says: This just man shall live in life. Since then we have
heard what things characterize the just man, come let us consider also the
things concerning Job beside these. The just man is pious, and what of Job?
did he not propitiate God with sacrifices each day? self-controlled, this man
too, A covenant, he says, =64.516= I set for my eyes, not to consider a
virgin; meek and equitable, and what milder than this man? of whom even the
servants from the excess of kindness so hung, as almost even to desire to
swallow him; for they said: Who would give us to be filled with his flesh?
Merciful and sociable, and who here is a match for Job? Was not the door
opened to everyone who came, and no one went out with empty bosom; the just
man will arbitrate justly for those in dispute, here too Job may boast, and
say: A judgment which I did not know I tracked out, and I crushed the grinders
of teeth; the just man will wrong no one, but Job even curses himself, If I
raised a hand against an orphan, then let my shoulder fall from the
collarbone. Since then Job exacted every portion of virtue with excess, for
this reason he is named both blameless and just. Thus Job too was just, not
having a part of virtue in himself, but every human virtue whole and
universal, not abstaining from this evil, and clinging to another; thus we too
call this a just balance, the one equal everywhere, not if it can weigh gold
equally, but lead otherwise; but the one providing equality in all materials,
and again a measure equal everywhere. Thus Job too was just, equal everywhere;
for he did not keep this equality in money only, but also in all the other
things, nowhere overstepping the measure; for one could not say that in money
he loved equal law, but in converse with neighbors he overstepped the measure,
being somehow living and contemptuous, but this too he fled from much
abundance saying: And if I also made light of the judgment of a man-servant or
a maid-servant, when they contended with me, or was I not as they became? This
too then is a very great injustice, to be a braggart and proud; for just as we
call a greedy man the one wishing to take the things of others, and not
content with his own, so we also call boastful, whenever he demands more than
what is owed a man, from the neighbor, whenever someone sets himself in all
honor, and wishes to dishonor another, and this comes from nowhere else than
from injustice; for that this is injustice, God made one substance of all, he
gave all things common and equal toward the neighbor; how then do you cast him
out and deprive him of the honor which God gave; not letting him be a partner,
but making the whole yours, not in money, but in glory making him poor? God
granted one substance to both and the presidency of the substance; for the,
Let us make man, is common of the whole race; how then do you


cast him out of the paternal substance, bringing him down to extreme
cheapness, and appropriating the common? But the blessed Job was not such,
wherefore he also said: I did not =64.517= make light of the judgment of a
man-servant or a maid-servant; for this reason the writer also, calling him
just, presents him not as having the partial justice, that in contracts and
judgments, but the universal virtue, according to that one of whom it is
written, For the just man a law is not laid down. And it adds that he was also
god-fearing, that it may show that he was perfect both in the things toward
men, and in the things toward God; for justice is toward men, but god-fearing
toward God. See, the virtues follow one another, and support one another; for
a good life makes one know God, and the knowledge of God becomes a guard of
the life. Whence Job too, since he was just, for this reason was also
god-fearing; and since he always had God before his eyes, for this reason he
also pursued the just justly, for the good itself, and not for something else;
and it is not possible to become just without first becoming blameless. For
these reasons the true man leads, the one not wearing a fiction and a stage of
piety, but pursuing virtue in truth and in deed, and being formed according to
it; and there follows this one, the blameless; for the genuine worker of
virtue is guarded even from the things seeming small; and that one again is
followed by the one named just paronymously from the perfect justice, the one
completed according to every kind of virtue; then the god-fearing after these
enters more splendidly, distinguished at once by good works and right
doctrines; to these is added the, abstaining from every evil deed, which
itself also leaves no excess; for it has not been said that he abstained from
this base thing, but not from that, or that he mastered many, but was mastered
by a few, but he wished to touch no wickedness. Yet abstinence from evils
alone is not enough, but there must also be present the working of the goods;
for even the idleness of virtue knows how to procure punishment, not only the
working of wickedness. Since those too, who did not feed the hungry, because
they did not work almsgiving, for this reason are handed over to the immortal
punishment. Whence we learn that abstaining from evils is not a beginning of
salvation for us, if there is not also present the acquisition of the goods,
and the working of virtue. But you, see for me how in a little the law of the
divine Spirit gathered the praise of the man, calling Job true according to
the creation, as it were registering this as fatherland and ancestors,
blameless according to the action, just according to good order, and the
sharing of the things that were his, and still according to bringing out right
votes upon those being judged, and the validity of all, god-fearing according
to the faith, and the hatred toward the idols, abstaining from every evil deed
according to the hottest enmity of the wicked. And to speak in a word,
nothing, he says, =64.520= worthy of blame did he ever do, but he also kept
the just things toward men, and pursued the things about worship purely, doing
all things according to God, and genuinely being pious toward the Divine. "And
there were born to him seven sons, and three daughters." First it spoke the
virtue, and then the things given by God. For of old these things came from
virtue, I mean good children, and many children; For there shall not be in
you, it says, one barren, nor a sterile woman upon your land. But Abraham was
barren for this reason, that you may learn that these are not truly prizes of
virtue, but other things; and for this reason he promised these,
condescending. And we gather that marriage is no hindrance toward virtue; for
otherwise it would have become a hindrance to Job too. But this bond did not
prevent the righteous man from running straight, so that some also pretext
this in vain. "And his cattle were seven thousand sheep, three thousand
camels, five hundred yoke of oxen, five hundred grazing she-asses, and service
very much, and there were great works for him upon the earth." See then wealth


having the more rustic; for it did not list loans and interests, nor
gold-roofed houses; whence nothing was impossible, so as to be able also to do
good; but sheep, and oxen, and the things from seasons and farming, from which
he aided those in need. For as I said, not vaunting from precious matter, but
content with the things measured to need, this king Scripture presents to us.
It does not mention horses, and chariots gold-inlaid, or treasures of silver
and gold, and precious stones, and other things that inflame the wealth-loving
disease, but the things that least go beyond need. For having children, he
remained unwounded by the money, setting as plenty and abundance the want of
desire, and as ambition, not being enslaved to the passions. And since it has
been obelized, the, and service very much, one must know that as many things
as have been obelized do not lie in the Hebrew, but were set by the Seventy
only toward clarity. To bring a spiritual contemplation upon the listing of
the animals in different kind, at any rate, is at once dream-interpreting and
superfluous. "And that man was well-born, of those from the risings of the
sun." Symmachus however rendered, greatest of all the eastern men. Of all, he
says, he was more splendid and more conspicuous, and he had famous and
splendid ancestors to number, but none of these lifted him into pride. "And
his sons going together to one another, =64.521= made a drinking each day,
taking along also their three sisters, to eat and drink with them." And having
virtue in accord, they were also without faction in the sensible things, going
to one another, and not far, nor making mixings toward others; and they also
made the sisters witnesses of the self-controlled; for they did not call some
foreign women to the friendship-cups and the dinners, but they took the
sisters along, which is a clear sign of self-control and seemliness. But their
concord and being together the devil found for the sudden plot against them,
to throw all down at once in the house of the elder brother. "And when the
days of the drinking were finished, Job sent and purified them, rising early."
You see a purification not Mosaic, but apostolic, wiping their mind, not the
body, with the prayers. And some say that the priests of old too were, like
Melchizedek, unordained; this then the, he sent, makes clear. "And it came to
pass as this day, and behold the angels of God came to stand before the Lord,
and the devil came with them." God is invisible to all generated creation,
without quantity, without magnitude, present everywhere, and standing over
all, encompassing all things, and there is no place outside his lordship, he
is present to each, as is fitting; to the holy angels, as holy, sending
unspeakable voices of commands, but to the wicked, as wicked, letting go
trial-permissions of men; for the devil can do nothing, except as many things
as are permitted by God. And he does not stand into the places above heaven,
far be it; for from there he was rolled out; For how, it says, did Lucifer
fall from heaven, who rose in the morning? And he is said to stand with the
angels, as himself too being in the places of the lordship of God, and waiting
to be commanded, or permitted, to make the trials against men; not fulfilling
some liturgy to God in this, but since for the turning aside and the vice he
was brought down to this, and he rages against us. He is permitted by the one
who administers our things well, to bring upon us the trials toward what we
can bear; these very things then Scripture having shaped, for our
understanding brought the word forward according to a figure. And the, It came
to pass as this day, i.e., there came some day, on which the theater was
opened, and the athlete went down


to the wrestling. And since the holy angels care for us, and tend us; but the
devil acts against our salvation, and about the same thing, that is the man,
they are borne against one another; they are said to stand together, God
overseeing the =64.524= things done; and we conquer, or are defeated, not from
[the] power of those acting against, or the inability of those fighting with
us, but from our choice. For both urging toward themselves, the holy angels
toward the good things, but the accursed demons toward the base, our choice
honored with free will proceeds to whichever part it wishes. For neither does
God force, having honored us with free will, nor has the devil acquired some
compelling power against us, but according to choice we either succeed, or
sin, inclining to angels, or to demons. "And the Lord said to the devil, From
where have you come?" Why then does the devil also stand with the angelic
liturgy, and in the midst of the holy spirits does the unclean speak freely?
Neither did the angels stand by God bodily, nor Satan; but every ministration
is called a standing-by; as a creature of God then, the devil too is commanded
to do the things that belong; for even if he threw off the reins in purpose,
yet he lies under the yoke of slavery, and is commanded as an executioner to
do the things ordered. And just as the rulers among us, whenever they call
someone with honor, send the honorable, but whenever they drag with dishonor,
they send some savage soldiers; so also God, to the holy sends ministering
angels for salvation, but whenever he wishes to punish, he permits the
evil-doing powers. Whence Paul also hands over the one who sinned in Corinth
to Satan for destruction of the flesh, and others again, that they may not
blaspheme. "And answering the devil said to the Lord: Having gone round the
earth and walked about the under-heaven, I am present." By bringing the word
forward according to question and answer, Scripture more drew the truth, and
cuts off every pretext of the shameless. For the things it says the devil said
to God, he did not say, but he thought; for he did not have a share of this
freedom of speech. And from saying that he goes round everywhere, first he
becomes accuser of himself, as no longer having a firm stand, but being
carried unstably everywhere, having taken up the fight against all men. Then
we learn that the inhabited world is filled with both demons and angels, and
that both are under the authority of God, and that angels come to God
receiving a command; but the devil will be able to do nothing of the things
according to his pleasure, unless he take permission from above. For even if
he threw off the reins, and is outside serving God, yet the fear lies as a
bit, and he is not permitted to use his own authority. But see, those come as
servants, reporting the =64.525= things done; but this one has nothing to say.
Heaven then is untrodden by this wicked demon, and he is not present
everywhere at the same time, but from a circuit And the under-heaven, he says,
that he may hint also at the desert, and if anything lies under heaven, and
the aerial place; and he wanders also the uninhabited, perhaps by some economy
of God, so that he may not always annoy our race. "And the Lord said to him,
Have you set your mind against my servant Job?" From this we learn that God
wished Job to be tried, as someone having a noble athlete; for this reason he
asks the devil, that he may give him a starting-point of the wrestling, but he
did not wish himself to provide the starting-point, lest he seem to be some
ambitious one. See how little by little the manner of the dialogue is exposed;
for it no longer shows him making an announcement of words, but that Have you
set your mind? which as a


voice the history gave forth. God then permitted the devil to know even
without a voice that the wicked counsel and deed had not escaped him, and as
it were speaks to him, From where have you come? not wishing to learn the
haunt of the wicked (for how, he who has the knowledge of all things standing
by?), but he is accustomed for the most part in the figure of a question to
characterize the things said, just as also in the Gospels the Lord brings the
inquiries to those about Cleopas, What are these words which you toss against
one another? And who would dare to say that the one who formed our hearts
alone has need of the question? Such also is the, How many loaves do you have?
and elsewhere confirming his resurrection, he said, Have you anything eatable
here? and concerning Lazarus he inquired, Where have you laid him? he who
anticipated the hearing, and foretold his end. In the same manner then here
too, saying to the devil, From where have you come? and, Have you set your
mind on my servant? he does not put forward words of ignorance, nor does he
wish to be taught the diet of the hostile one, but he shows that one himself
accuser of his own savagery. For vice is accustomed to wound itself. "A man
blameless, true, god-fearing, abstaining from every evil deed." What harm of
men despising and of the whole inhabited world, when the master of the angels
praises and proclaims? Just as then if he does not bless, even if all who
dwell earth and sea praise, there is no profit. This then let us aim at
everywhere, how he himself may proclaim us; and if this is, we are higher than
all, even if in poverty, and in disease, even if we are in the sensible
=64.528= evils. Since the blessed Job too sitting on a dunghill, and
surrounded by the ichor of the wounds, and teeming with countless fountains of
worms, and suffering those incurable things, spat upon by servants, by
friends, by enemies, plotted against by a wife, brought down into poverty,
into hunger, into that incurable sickness, was more blessed than all. How?
Because God blessed him, saying, A man just, blameless, true, god-fearing. For
how was he not exceeding all those upon earth, the God of all witnessing to
him, in saying, blameless, a true man, having a certain incomparable virtue,
both keeping the just things toward men blamelessly, and pursuing the piety
toward God exactly? And that the devil, looking at the things concerning him,
possessed no small wonder about him. Why then does he change the order of the
encomia here? Perhaps he does this indifferently, and perhaps also according
to the nature of the things and the harmony he arranges the praise. For first
every man is true, according to the account of the creation; then thus
blameless, according to the manner of the way of life; but here, because of
the fight toward the devil, he sets the blameless before the true. For virtue
knows how to war against vice, and the just of the manner becomes a sharpest
arrow to the envious one. Then thus he also joins the true to the blameless,
that he may show the nature also agreeing with the manner, and choice adorning
the well-born of nature. "And you have made his cattle many upon the earth."
You see the wealth given by God, you see that it was not from injustice; For
you, he says, fenced round, you blessed, you made his things many. How much
must Job have toiled, so as to persuade men that the wealth was not from
injustice? Behold the devil witnessed this to him, even if he does not know.
"Surely he will bless you to your face." For this is the, to the face, openly,
shamelessly, not shrinking. "Then the Lord said to the devil: Behold all
things as many as he has I give into your hand, but him do not touch." For
thus, he says, I am confident in my athlete, that you said, Send your hand;
but I say that Into your hand I give all things as many as are


his; in your hand, the filthy, the insatiable; what do you say? what need
after your witness of another torture? That the devil may be muzzled, he says,
that the righteous man may appear more splendid, that for those after these

readily, as even from treasures throwing out, he shows them whenever he

"Wherefore I made light of myself, and I melted; and I thought myself earth
and ash." These things are a defense for all the former; for not having been
released from the trial does he say these things, but still being in the
terrible things he sang a palinode. Nothing, he says, I set myself, I make a
defense for the former, and of these I was unworthy. What then God? Since he
condemned himself, then he justified him. And consider the holy men, how when
they are thought worthy of greater things, they acquire more humility; so also
Abraham, when he saw the Lord, said: And I am earth and ash. "And now take
seven calves, and seven rams, and go toward my servant Job, and he will make

unremembering of evil, he makes them become witnesses of the virtue of the
man; and he shows the greatness of the sin, also through the excess according
to the offering; for =64.656= he would not have needed so many victims, the
sins owing to be loosed not being great. And he hints, that not even a

Lord gave double as many as were before Job into a doubling." God let the
devil show all his power; and when he emptied all the arrows, and no manner of

of Chobor], Zophar the king of the Minaioi." And each of those who read, as
looking into some archetypal image this noble athlete, let him imitate the
courage, let him be zealous for the endurance, that having walked the same
road, and having arrayed nobly toward all the engines of the devil, he may be
able to obtain the goods promised to those who love God; and to become the
passions of this blessed one, medicines of our evils; and the harsh swell of

undergone the terrible things of the inhabited world, let us be nobly disposed

stretching the hands from every side, and receiving and recovering the
children who have feared, so toward this book let us always flee; and if most

with whom to the Father the glory, together with the Holy Spirit, now and
always, and into the ages of the ages. Amen.
