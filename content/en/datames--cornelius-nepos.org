* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Datames, son of Camisares, a Carian by nation, born of Scythissa his
mother, was first among the soldiers of Artaxerxes who guarded the
palace. His father Camisares, because he had been found strong of hand,
vigorous in war, and faithful to the king in many places, held as his
province that part of Cilicia next to Cappadocia which the Leucosyri
inhabit. Datames, while first performing military service, showed what
manner of man he was in the war the king waged against the Cadusii. For
here his work was of great moment. And so it came about, when Camisares
had fallen in that war, that his father's province was given over to
him.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
He showed himself of equal virtue afterward, when Autophrodates, by the
king's command, was pursuing with war those who had defected. For by
this man's work the enemy, after they had already entered the camp, were
routed, many thousands of the king's men having been killed, and the
rest of the king's army was saved: from which affair he began to be set
over greater things. There was at that time Thuys, dynast of
Paphlagonia, of an ancient line, sprung from that Pylaemenes whom Homer
says was slain by Patroclus in the Trojan war. He was not obedient to
the king. For that reason the king resolved to pursue him with war, and
set Datames over the matter, a kinsman of the Paphlagonian: for they
were born of brother and sister. For that reason Datames first wished to
try to bring his kinsman back to duty without arms. When he had come to
him without a guard, because he feared no ambush from a friend, he
nearly perished: for Thuys wished to kill him in secret. Datames' mother
was with him, the Paphlagonian's aunt. She learned what was being done
and warned her son. He escaped the danger by flight and declared war on
Thuys. In that war, although he had been deserted by Ariobarzanes,
prefect of Lydia and Ionia and all Phrygia, he pressed on none the less
vigorously and took Thuys alive, with his wife and children.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
He took care that the report of this deed should not reach the king
before he himself did. And so, no one knowing of it, he came to where
the king was, and on the next day he clothed Thuys, a man of very great
body and terrible face, because he was dark, with long hair and a beard
grown long, in the finest garment which the king's satraps were
accustomed to wear, and adorned him also with a necklace and golden
bracelets and the rest of the royal array; he himself, wrapped in a
coarse double cloak and a shaggy tunic, wearing a hunting helmet on his
head, a club in his right hand and a leash in his left, so drove Thuys
bound before him, as if he were leading a captured wild beast. When all
looked at this because of the novelty of the dress and the unknown
form, and a great crowd gathered for that reason, there was someone who
recognized Thuys and reported it to the king. At first he did not
believe it, and so he sent Pharnabazus to inquire. When he learned from
him what had been done, he at once ordered him to be admitted, greatly
delighted both with the deed and with the array, above all that a noble
king had come into his power unlooked for. And so, having rewarded
Datames magnificently, he sent him to the army which was then being
gathered under Pharnabazus and Tithraustes for the Egyptian war, and
ordered him to be of equal command with them. But after the king
recalled Pharnabazus, the supreme command was handed over to him.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
When he was preparing an army with the greatest zeal and making ready to
set out for Egypt, letters were suddenly sent to him from the king, that
he should attack Aspis, who held Cataonia: which people lies above
Cilicia, bordering on Cappadocia. For Aspis, inhabiting a wooded region
fortified with strongholds, not only did not obey the king's command,
but also harassed the neighboring regions and seized what was being
carried to the king. Although Datames was far from those regions and was
being drawn away from a greater matter, yet he thought the king's will
must be complied with. And so with a few men, but brave ones, he boarded
a ship, judging, as it turned out, that he would more easily crush the
man unawares with a small band than when prepared, even with however
great an army. Carried by this ship to Cilicia, having gone ashore from
there and making the journey day and night, he crossed the Taurus and
came to the place he had aimed at. He asks in what places Aspis is: he
learns that he is not far off and had then gone hunting. While he is
watching him, the reason of his coming is discovered. Aspis prepares the
Pisidians, with those he had with him, to resist. When Datames heard
this, he takes arms and orders his men to follow; he himself, his horse
spurred on, rides at the enemy. Aspis, seeing him from afar bearing down
upon him, takes fright and, deterred from the attempt to resist,
surrenders himself. Datames hands this man bound over to Mithridates to
be led to the king.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
While these things are being done, Artaxerxes, remembering from how
great a war to how small a matter he had sent the chief of his generals,
reproaches himself and sent a messenger to the army at Ace, because he
thought Datames had not yet set out, who should say that he was not to
leave the army. This man, before he arrived where he had been sent, met
on the way those who were leading Aspis. By which swiftness, although
Datames had obtained great goodwill of the king, he received no less
envy of the courtiers, because they saw that he alone was valued more
than all of them. Whereby they all agreed to crush him. These things
Pandantes, keeper of the royal treasury, a friend of Datames, writes out
and sends to him, in which he shows him that he will be in great danger
if anything adverse should happen in Egypt while he was in command. For
that it is the royal custom to attribute adverse chances to men, and
favorable ones to their own fortune: and so they are easily driven to
the destruction of those under whose leadership affairs badly conducted
are reported. That he will be in the greater danger because he has as
his bitterest enemies those whom the king most obeys. When he had
learned of such letters, having already come to the army at Ace, because
he was not unaware that these things were truly written, he resolved to
revolt from the king. Yet he did nothing that was unworthy of his good
faith. For he set Mandrocles of Magnesia over the army; he himself with
his own men withdraws into Cappadocia and occupies Paphlagonia adjoining
it, concealing of what mind he was toward the king. Secretly he makes
friendship with Ariobarzanes, gathers a force, and hands over fortified
cities to his own men to be guarded.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
But these things, because of the winter season, were proceeding less
prosperously. He hears that certain Pisidians are preparing forces
against him. He sends his son Arsidaeus there with an army; the young
man falls in battle. The father sets out there with a force not so
great, concealing how great a wound he had received, because he wished
to reach the enemy before the report of the badly conducted affair
reached his own men, lest, the death of his son being known, the spirits
of the soldiers be weakened. He arrives where he had aimed, and pitches
camp in such places that he could neither be surrounded by the multitude
of the adversaries nor be hindered from having his force ready for
fighting. There was with him Mithrobarzanes, his father-in-law, prefect
of the horse. That man, despairing of his son-in-law's affairs, deserts
to the enemy. When Datames heard this, he perceived that if it had gone
out among the crowd that he had been abandoned by a man so closely bound
to him, it would come about that the rest would follow the same counsel;
and so he puts it out to the common soldiers that Mithrobarzanes had
gone by his own order as a pretended deserter, so that, more easily
received, he might kill the enemy: wherefore it was not right that he be
left, and all should follow at once. If they did this with vigorous
spirit, it would come about that the adversaries could not resist, when
they were being cut down both within the rampart and outside. This plan
being approved, he leads out the army and pursues Mithrobarzanes, who
had only just reached the enemy, when Datames orders the standards to be
advanced. The Pisidians, disturbed by the new affair, are led to the
opinion that the deserters had done it in bad faith and by arrangement,
so that, once received, they might be a greater calamity. First they
attack them. Those men, since they did not know what was being done or
why it was happening, were forced to fight with those to whom they had
gone over, and to stand with those they had left: and since neither side
spared them, they were quickly cut to pieces. Datames attacks the
remaining Pisidians as they resist: at the first onset he drives them,
pursues the fleeing, kills many, and takes the enemy's camp. By such a
plan at one time he both struck down the traitors and routed the enemy,
and turned to his own safety what had been devised for his destruction.
Than which we read nowhere a thought of any commander more acute, nor a
deed more swift.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:
Yet from this man Sisinas, his eldest son, defected and went over to the
king and reported his father's defection. Artaxerxes, moved by this
news, because he understood that he had business with a brave and
vigorous man, who had been accustomed both to think before he attempted
and, when he had thought, to dare to act, sends Autophrodates into
Cappadocia. Datames strove to occupy beforehand the pass in which the
Cilician Gates are situated, so that this man might not be able to
enter. But he could not gather forces so suddenly. Driven from that
attempt, with the band he had gathered he chose such a place that he
might neither be surrounded by the enemy nor might the adversary pass by
without being pressed in places of double danger, and, if he wished to
fight, the multitude of the enemy could not much harm his fewness.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:
Although Autophrodates saw these things, yet he preferred at once to
engage rather than to withdraw with so great forces or to sit so long in
one place. He had twenty thousand barbarian horsemen, a hundred thousand
foot whom they call Cardaces, and three thousand slingers of the same
kind, besides eight thousand Cappadocians, ten thousand Armenians, five
thousand Paphlagonians, ten thousand Phrygians, five thousand Lydians,
about three thousand Aspendians and Pisidians, two thousand Cilicians,
as many Captiani, three thousand hired from Greece, and a very great
number of light-armed. Against these forces all Datames' hope rested in
himself and in the nature of the place (for he did not have a twentieth
part of this man's soldiers). Relying on which he engaged and cut down
many thousands of the adversaries, when of his own army not more than a
thousand men had fallen. For which cause on the next day he set up a
trophy in the place where they had fought the day before. When from here
he had moved camp and, always inferior in forces, came off superior in
all battles, because he never joined battle except when he had shut the
adversaries in the narrows of places, which often happened to one
skilled in the regions and thinking cunningly, Autophrodates, when he
saw the war being prolonged with greater calamity to the king than to
the adversaries, reminding him of former peace and friendship, urged him
to return into favor with the king. Although he thought that it would
not be trustworthy, yet he accepted the condition and said that he would
send envoys to Artaxerxes. Thus the war which the king had undertaken
against Datames was quieted. Autophrodates withdrew into Phrygia.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:
But the king, because he had conceived implacable hatred against
Datames, after he perceived that he could not be crushed by war, strove
to kill him by ambushes: most of which that man avoided. As when it had
been reported to him that certain men who were in the number of his
friends were laying an ambush for him (about whom, because enemies had
reported it, he thought it must neither be believed nor neglected), he
wished to try whether a true or a false thing had been reported to him.
And so he set out on that journey in which they had said the ambush
would be. But he chose a man most like himself in body and stature and
gave him his own clothing and ordered him to go in that place where he
himself was accustomed; he himself, however, in military array and dress
began to make the journey among his bodyguards. But the ambushers, after
the column reached the place they had occupied, deceived by the rank and
the clothing, make an attack on him who had been substituted. But
Datames had told those with whom he was making the journey to be ready
to do what they had seen him do. He himself, when he noticed the
ambushers rushing together, cast weapons at them. When all had done this
same thing, before they reached him whom they wished to attack, they
fell pierced.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:
Yet this man, so cunning, was at last caught by the treachery of
Mithridates, son of Ariobarzanes. For that man promised the king that he
would kill him, if the king would permit that he might do with impunity
whatever he wished, and had given his right hand concerning that matter
according to the Persian custom. When he received this sent from the
king, he prepares forces and, while absent, makes friendship with
Datames, harasses the king's provinces, storms strongholds, takes great
plunder, of which he distributes part to his own men, part he sends to
Datames; in like manner he hands over to him a good many strongholds.
By doing these things for a long time he persuaded the man that he had
undertaken endless war against the king, while none the more, lest he
offer him any suspicion of ambush, did he seek a conference with him or
strive to come into his sight. Thus while absent he carried on
friendship, so that they seemed to be bound not by mutual benefits but
by the common hatred they had conceived toward the king.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:
When he thought he had sufficiently confirmed this, he informs Datames
that it is time for greater things to be prepared and war to be
undertaken with the king himself, and concerning that matter, if it
seemed good to him, he should come to a conference in whatever place he
wished. The matter being approved, a time for conferring is taken and a
place at which they should meet. Here Mithridates, with one man in whom
he had most trust, came some days before and buried swords separately in
several places and carefully marked those places. On the day of the
conference itself, however, both send men to explore the place and
search their persons; then they themselves met. When they had been in
conference here for some while and had gone away in different directions
and Datames was already far off, Mithridates, before he reached his own
men, lest he breed any suspicion, returns to the same place and there,
where the weapon had been buried, sat down, as if from weariness he
wished to rest, and called Datames back, pretending he had forgotten
something in the conference. Meanwhile he brought out the weapon that
was hidden and, bared from the sheath, covered it with his garment, and
said to Datames as he came that as he was leaving he had noticed a
certain place, which was in view, to be suitable for pitching camp.
When he pointed to it with his finger and the other looked back, he
pierced him from behind with the steel and killed him before anyone
could come to help. Thus that man, who had taken many by counsel, no one
by treachery, was caught by a pretended friendship.
