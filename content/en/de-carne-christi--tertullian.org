* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Those who so strive to disturb the faith of the resurrection, which had
remained without controversy before these kinsmen of the Sadducees, that they
deny that this hope even belongs to the flesh, with good reason also tear the
flesh of Christ with questions, as if it were either none at all or somehow
other than human, lest, if it be established that it was human, it be
prejudged against them that that flesh must in every way rise which has risen
in Christ. Therefore from the same place from which they destroy the hopes of
the flesh, from there they will have to be built up for us. Let us examine the
bodily substance of the Lord; for about the spiritual it is certain. The flesh
is in question. Its truth and quality are under review: whether it was, and
whence, and of what kind it was. The report of it will give a law to our
resurrection. Marcion, in order to deny the flesh of Christ, denied also the
nativity, or, in order to deny the nativity, denied the flesh as well, namely
lest nativity and flesh should answer testimony to one another, because there
is neither nativity without flesh nor flesh without nativity; as if by the
same heretical licence he himself could not either, having admitted the flesh,
deny the nativity, as Apelles his disciple and afterward deserter, or, having
confessed both flesh and nativity, interpret them otherwise, as his
fellow-disciple and fellow-deserter Valentinus. But even he who introduced a
putative flesh of Christ could equally have feigned the nativity also as a
phantasm, so that the conception and the pregnancy and the birth of the virgin
and thereafter the course of the infant himself should be held τῷ δοκεῖν. They
would have deceived the same eyes and the same senses that the opinion of the
flesh eluded.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Plainly the nativity is announced by Gabriel. What has he to do with the angel
of the Creator? And a conception is brought into the virgin's womb. What has
he to do with Isaiah the prophet of the Creator? He hates delays, who was
suddenly bringing Christ down from heaven. Take away from here, he says, the
always troublesome censuses of Caesar and the narrow lodgings and the filthy
rags and the hard mangers. Let the angelic multitude look to it, honoring
their Lord by night. Let the shepherds rather keep their flocks; and let the
magi not weary themselves from afar; I make them a gift of their gold. Let
Herod also be better, lest Jeremiah boast. But let the infant not even be
circumcised, lest he feel pain, nor be carried to the temple, lest he burden
his parents with the cost of the offering, nor be delivered into the hands of
Simeon, lest the old man who is about to die be saddened from that hour. Let
that old woman also be silent, lest she bewitch the boy. With these counsels,
I suppose, you dared, Marcion, to blot out so many original documents of
Christ, lest his flesh be proved. From what source, I ask you? Exhibit your
authority. If you are a prophet, foretell something; if an apostle, preach in
public; if apostolic, think with the apostles; if you are only a Christian,
believe what has been handed down; if you are none of these, I would justly
say, die. For you are also dead, who are not a Christian, by not believing
what, when believed, makes Christians, and you are so much the more dead as
you are the more not a Christian, who, when you had been one, fell away, by
rescinding what you formerly believed, as you yourself also confess in a
certain letter, and your people do not deny, and ours prove. Therefore,
rescinding what you believed, now no longer believing, you have rescinded it;
yet not because you have ceased to believe have you rightly rescinded it; on
the contrary, by rescinding what you believed you prove that before you
rescinded it it was otherwise. What you believed otherwise, that had been so
handed down. Further, what had been handed down, that was true, as handed down
by those whose it was to hand it down. Therefore, rescinding what had been
handed down, you rescinded what was true. You have done it by no right. But we
have already used prescriptions of this kind more fully against all heresies
elsewhere. After those we now treat the matter from abundance, asking for the
reason why you have not thought that Christ was born.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

It is necessary, insofar as you think this was permitted to your own judgment,
that you have reckoned nativity either impossible or unfitting for God. But
nothing is impossible to God except what he does not will. Whether therefore
he willed to be born (because if he willed, he both could and was born) let us
consider. I run to a short cut. For if God had not willed himself to be born,
for whatever cause, he would not have presented himself as seeming to be a
man. For who, seeing him a man, would deny that he was born? Thus what he had
not willed to be, he would not have willed to seem at all. Of every
displeasing thing even the opinion is rejected, because it makes no difference
whether a thing is or is not, if, when it is not, it is presumed to be.
Plainly it does matter, that he should not suffer as false what truly is not.
But it was enough for him, you say, his own conscience. Let men look to it if
they thought him born, because they saw a man. How much more worthily then,
and how much more consistently, would he have borne a human estimation if
truly born, seeing that he was going to undergo the same estimation even if
not born, with injury to his own conscience, which you count as a ground of
confidence, that, not born, he should against his own conscience bear to be
thought born? Teach what was of such worth that Christ, knowing what he was,
should exhibit himself as that which he was not. You cannot say, Lest if he
had been born and had put on a man, he should have ceased to be God, losing
what he was while he becomes what he was not. For there is no danger to God of
his own standing. But for this reason, you say, I deny that God was truly
turned into a man, so that he was both born and embodied in flesh, because he
who is without end must also of necessity be inconvertible. For to be turned
into something else is an end of the former thing. Conversion therefore does
not belong to him to whom an end does not belong. Plainly the nature of
convertible things is under this law, that they should not remain in that
which is converted in them, and so by not remaining should perish, while by
converting they lose what they were. But nothing is equal to God; his nature
stands apart from the condition of all things. If therefore those things which
stand apart from God, from which God stands apart, when they are converted
lose what they were, where will the diversity of divinity from other things
be, unless the contrary should hold, that is, that God can both be converted
into all things and persevere as he is? Otherwise he will be equal to those
things which, when converted, lose what they were, to whom he is certainly not
equal in all things, and so not in the issue of conversion. You have sometimes
read, and believed, that angels of the Creator were converted into human
shape, and that they wore so much truth of body that Abraham even washed their
feet, and Lot was snatched from the Sodomites by their hands, and an angel
also, having wrestled with a man, desired to be dismissed with the whole
weight of the body, so much was he being held. What was permitted to angels
lower than God, converted into human corpulence, so that they nevertheless
remained angels, will you take this away from the more powerful God, as if
Christ, having truly put on a man, could not have persevered as God? Or did
those angels too appear as phantasms of flesh? But you will not dare to say
this. For if with you the angels of the Creator are such as Christ also is,
Christ will belong to that God whose angels are such as Christ also is. If you
had not on purpose rejected some scriptures that resist your opinion and
corrupted others, the Gospel of John would have confounded you in this kind,
preaching that the Spirit, having slipped down in the body of a dove, settled
upon the Lord. Which Spirit, since this is what he was, was as truly also a
dove as he was also Spirit, and had not destroyed his own substance by the
assumed foreign substance. But you ask where the body of the dove is, when the
Spirit has been taken back into heaven, and likewise that of the angels. It
was withdrawn by the same reason by which it had also been put forth. If you
had seen when it was being brought forth from nothing, you would have known
also when it was being withdrawn into nothing. If the beginning was not
visible, neither was the end. Yet there was solidity of body, at whatever
moment the body was seen. That which is written cannot not have been.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Therefore if you do not reject embodiment as either impossible or dangerous to
God, it remains that you cast it off and accuse it as if unworthy. Beginning
indeed from the nativity itself, plead, come, already the filth of the genital
elements in the womb, the foul clots of humor and blood, of the flesh to be
fed from the same mire through nine months. Describe the womb growing insolent
from day to day, heavy, anxious, not safe even for sleep, uncertain with the
cravings of disgust and of appetite. Inveigh already also against the very
modesty of the woman in labor, which is to be honored either for the danger or
held religious for nature. You shudder, of course, also at the infant poured
out with its own impediments, and of course you disdain him even when washed,
that he is bound with rags, that he is shaped with ointments, that he is
amused with endearments. This veneration of nature, Marcion, you spit out, and
how were you born? You hate the human being who is being born, and how do you
love anyone? You indeed plainly have not loved yourself, when you withdrew
from the church and the faith of Christ. But look to it, if you displease
yourself, or if you were born otherwise. Certainly Christ loved that human
being clotted in uncleannesses in the womb, that one brought forth through the
shameful parts, that one nourished through mockeries. For him he came down,
for him he preached, for him he cast himself down with every humility even to
death, and the death of the cross; he loved, of course, the one whom he
redeemed at a great price. If Christ is the Creator's, he has loved his own by
right; if he is from another god, he has loved him still more, since he
redeemed a stranger's. He therefore loved, together with the human being, also
his nativity, also his flesh. Nothing can be loved without that through which
it is what it is. Either take away nativity, and exhibit a human being, or
remove the flesh, and present the one whom God redeemed, if these things are
the human being whom God redeemed. Do you make these things things to be
blushed at for him whom he redeemed, and unworthy, which, unless he had loved
them, he would not have redeemed? He reforms nativity from death by a heavenly
regeneration, he restores the flesh from every vexation, he cleanses the
leprous, he relights the blind, he makes whole the paralytic, he expiates the
demoniac, he raises the dead, and shall we blush at that flesh? If he had
truly willed to come forth from a she-wolf or a sow or a cow and, clothed in
the body of a wild beast or of cattle, were preaching the kingdom of heaven,
your censure, I suppose, would prescribe to him, This is shameful for God, and
this is unworthy of the Son of God, and foolish. For this reason, because it
would so believe; if indeed it is foolish if we judge God from our own sense.
But look around, Marcion, if at any rate you have not deleted it: God has
chosen the foolish things of the world, that he may confound wisdom. What then
are these foolish things? The conversion of human beings to the worship of the
true God, the rejection of error, the discipline of justice, of chastity, of
mercy, of patience, of all innocence? These indeed are not foolish. Seek
therefore of what things he has spoken; and if you have presumed to have found
them, will anything be so foolish as to believe in a God born, and indeed from
a virgin, and indeed fleshly, who has been rolled through those insults of
nature? Let someone say these things are not foolish, and let there be other
things which God has chosen in rivalry of the wisdom of the world. And yet
with that wisdom it is more easily believed that Jupiter became a bull or a
swan than that Christ was truly a man, according to Marcion.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

There are plainly also other things equally foolish, which belong to the
insults and passions of God. Or let them call God crucified prudence. Take
this away also, Marcion, nay rather this first. For what is more unworthy of
God, what more to be blushed at, to be born or to die? to wear flesh or a
cross? to be circumcised or to be fastened up? to be brought up or to be
buried? to be laid in a manger or to be hidden in a tomb? You will be wiser if
you do not believe even these things. But you will not be wise unless you have
been a fool in the world, by believing the foolish things of God. Or is it for
this reason that you have not cut the passions away from Christ, because as a
phantasm he was vacant of the sense of them? We have said before that he could
equally have undergone even the empty mockeries of an imaginary nativity and
infancy. But now here answer, slayer of the truth: was God not truly
crucified? was he not truly dead, so as to have been truly crucified? was he
not truly raised, so as to have been, of course, truly dead? Falsely then Paul
determined to know among us only Jesus crucified, falsely he thrust in that he
was buried, falsely he pressed that he was raised. False then is our faith
also, and the whole of what we hope from Christ will be a phantasm. Most
wicked of men, who excuse the slayers of God. For Christ suffered nothing from
them, if he suffered nothing truly. Spare the one hope of the whole world, you
who destroy the necessary disgrace of the faith. Whatever is unworthy of God
is expedient for me. I am saved if I am not confounded concerning my Lord. He
who shall have been ashamed of me, he says, I also will be ashamed of him.
Otherwise I do not find materials of confusion which, through contempt of
blush, may prove me well shameless and happily foolish. The Son of God was
crucified; it is not shameful, because it is to be ashamed of. And the Son of
God died; it is altogether credible, because it is unfitting. And buried he
rose; it is certain, because it is impossible. For how will they be true in
him, if he himself was not true, if he did not truly have in himself that
which could be fastened, that which could die, that which could be buried and
raised? this flesh, of course, suffused with blood, built of bones, woven with
sinews, entwined with veins, which knows how to be born and to die, human
without doubt, as born of a human being. And therefore this will be mortal in
Christ, because Christ is a man and the Son of man. Or why is Christ a man and
the Son of man, if he is nothing of man and nothing from man? unless either
man is something other than flesh, or the flesh of man is from somewhere else
than from man, or Mary is something other than a human being, or a human being
is Marcion's god. Otherwise Christ would not be called a man without flesh,
nor the Son of man without some human parent, just as he would not be God
without the Spirit of God, nor the Son of God without God the Father. Thus the
register of both substances exhibited a man and God, born from this side, not
born from that, fleshly from this side, spiritual from that, weak from this
side, exceedingly strong from that, dying from this side, living from that.
Which property of the conditions, divine and human, has been equally scored
off by the truth of each nature, by the same faith of both spirit and flesh.
The powers of the Spirit of God proved God, the passions proved the flesh of a
man. If the powers were not without spirit, equally the passions also were not
without flesh. If the flesh with the passions is feigned, then the spirit also
with the powers is false. Why do you halve Christ by a lie? He was wholly
truth. He preferred, believe it, to be born rather than to lie in any part,
and that against himself, so as to wear a flesh hard without bones, solid
without muscles, bloody without blood, clothed without a tunic, hungry without
hunger, eating without teeth, speaking without a tongue, so that his speech
should have been a phantasm to the ears through an image of a voice. He was
therefore a phantasm even after the resurrection, when he offers his hands and
feet to the disciples to be inspected, saying, See that it is I, because a
spirit does not have bones, as you see me having; without doubt hands and feet
and bones, which a spirit does not have, but flesh. How do you interpret this
voice, Marcion, who from a God most good and simple and only good bring in
Jesus? See, he deceives and tricks and circumvents the eyes of all, the senses
of all, the approaches and contacts of all. Therefore you ought already not to
have brought Christ down from heaven, but from some juggler's troop, nor a God
beside man, but a magician man, nor the high priest of salvation, but the
craftsman of a spectacle, nor the raiser of the dead, but the caller-away of
the living. Except that even if he was a magician, he was born.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

But certain pupils of that Pontic man, forced to be wiser than their master,
grant to Christ the truth of flesh, yet without prejudice to a nativity still
to be refused. Let him have had flesh, they say, provided it was altogether
not born. We have come therefore, as the saying is, from the lime-kiln into
the charcoal-pit, from Marcion to Apelles, who, after he had slipped from the
discipline of Marcion into a woman by the flesh, and was thereafter overthrown
in spirit upon the virgin Philumene, took up from that source the preaching of
a solid body of Christ, but without nativity. And to that angel of Philumene
the apostle will answer with the same voice with which he was already then
foretelling that very man, saying, Even if an angel from heaven should
evangelize you otherwise than we have evangelized, let him be anathema. But
against those things which they argue besides, we will resist. They confess
that Christ truly had a body. From what matter, if not from that quality in
which he was seen? From where a body, if the body is not flesh? From where
flesh, if not born? Because those things that are going to be born would have
it to be born. From the stars, they say, and from the substances of the higher
world he borrowed flesh. And they of course put forward that a body without
nativity is not to be wondered at, since even among us it was permitted to
angels to have proceeded into flesh with no work of a womb. We acknowledge
indeed that it is so related, but yet what sort of thing is it that the faith
of another rule should borrow an instrument for its argumentations from that
faith which it attacks? What has he to do with Moses, who has rejected the God
of Moses? If it is another god, let his things be otherwise. But let all
heretics use the scriptures of him whose world also they use. This too will be
a testimony of judgment against them, that they furnish their blasphemies from
his own examples. It is easy for the truth, even when it prescribes nothing of
this kind against them, to prevail. Therefore those who put forward the flesh
of Christ after the example of the angels, saying it was not born, though it
was flesh, I would like them to compare also the causes, both of Christ and of
the angels, for which they proceeded into flesh. No angel ever came down for
this, that he should be crucified, that he should experience death, that he
should be raised from death. If there was never a cause of this kind for the
embodiment of angels, you have the cause why they received flesh without being
born. They had not come to die, therefore not to be born either. But Christ,
sent to die, had of necessity to be born also, that he might be able to die;
for nothing is accustomed to die except what is born. There is a mutual debt
of nativity with mortality. The form of dying is the cause of being born. If
Christ died on account of that which dies, but that dies which is also born,
it was consequent, nay rather preceding, that he should equally be born on
account of that which is born, because he had to die on account of that very
thing which, because it is born, dies. It did not belong to him not to be
born, for whom it belonged to die. Yet then also among those angels the Lord
himself appeared to Abraham without nativity, with flesh of course, on account
of the same diversity of cause. But you do not receive this, not receiving
Christ, who already then was learning to address and to free and to judge the
human race in the habit of a flesh not yet born, because not yet going to die,
unless first both his nativity and his mortality should be announced.
Therefore let them prove that those angels conceived flesh from the stars. If
they do not prove it, because it is not even written, neither will the flesh
of Christ be from there, to whom they accommodate the example of the angels.
It is established that the angels did not carry flesh of their own, as being
by nature of a spiritual substance, even if of some body, yet of their own
kind, but transfigurable into human flesh for a time, able to be seen and to
meet with human beings. Therefore, since it has not been related whence they
took flesh, it is left to our understanding not to doubt that this is proper
to angelic power, to take a body for themselves from no matter. How much more,
you say, from some matter? That is certain. But nothing is established about
this, because scripture does not exhibit it. For the rest, those who are able
to make themselves what by nature they are not, why should they not be able to
make it from no substance? If they become what they are not, why should they
not become it from that which is not? But that which is not, when it is made,
is from nothing. For this reason it is neither asked nor shown what was
afterward done with their bodies. What was from nothing became nothing. They
are able to convert nothing itself into flesh, who were able to convert
themselves into flesh. It is more to change a nature than to make matter. But
even if it was necessary that the angels should have taken flesh from matter,
it is more credible of course from earthly matter than from any kind of
heavenly substances, since it existed of so earthly a quality that it was fed
with earthly foods. Grant that it be now also starry, fed in the same way with
earthly foods when it was not earthly, as the earthly was fed with heavenly
foods when it was not heavenly (for we read that manna was for food to the
people: Bread, he says, of angels man ate), yet the once separated condition
of the Lord's flesh is not broken by reason of another arrangement. As one who
was truly going to be a man even to death, he had to put on that flesh whose
death it is. Further, nativity precedes that flesh whose death it is.

But whenever there is a contest about nativity, all who reject it as
prejudging the truth of flesh in Christ wish to deny that God himself was
born, because he said, Who is my mother, and who are my brothers? Let Apelles
therefore also hear what has already been answered by us to Marcion in that
little book in which we challenged his gospel, namely that the subject-matter
of that pronouncement must be considered. First indeed no one would ever have
announced to him that his mother and brothers were standing outside who was
not sure both that he had a mother and brothers and that they were those whom
he was then announcing, either known before or found out then and there;
although heresies have for this reason taken these things out of the gospel,
because even those who were wondering at his teaching said that they knew very
well that his father was believed to be Joseph the carpenter, and his mother
Mary, and his brothers and sisters. But they had announced to him a mother and
brothers, whom he did not have, for the sake of tempting him. This indeed
scripture does not say, which is not otherwise silent when something has been
done toward him for the sake of temptation. Behold, it says, a doctor of the
law rose up, tempting him. And elsewhere, And the Pharisees came to him,
tempting him. Nothing forbade it to be signified here also that it was done
for the sake of tempting. I do not receive what you bring in from yourself
outside scripture. Next, a subject-matter of temptation ought to underlie.
What did they think was to be tempted in him? whether, of course, he had been
born or not? For if his answer denied this, this is what the announcer of the
tempter aimed at. But no temptation, stretching toward knowledge of that about
which it tempts by doubting, proceeds so suddenly that a question does not
first go before which, bringing in doubt, forces the temptation. Further, if
it was nowhere rolled about concerning the nativity of Christ, why do you
argue that they wished by temptation to inquire what they never brought
forward into question? To this we add also, if he were to be tempted about
nativity, he would not at any rate be tempted in this way, by the announcement
of those persons who could have been absent even if Christ had been born. We
are all born, and yet we do not all have either brothers or a mother. He can
still have a father rather than a mother, and uncles rather than brothers. So
little does a temptation of nativity fit, which it was permitted to stand even
without the naming of a mother or of brothers. It is easier, plainly, that
those who were sure that he had both a mother and brothers should have tempted
his divinity rather than his nativity, whether, acting inside, he knew what
was outside, attacked by a lie of the announced presence of those who were not
in presence. Except that in this way the ingenuity of the temptation would
have been empty. For it could happen that those whom they would announce as
standing outside, he would know to be absent, already by the necessity of
sickness or of business or of a known journey. No one tempts in that way in
which he knows he can carry back the blush of the temptation. No fitting
subject-matter of temptation therefore being present, the simplicity of the
announcement is set free, that his mother and brothers had truly come upon
him. But let Apelles also learn what was the reason of the answer denying
mother and brothers for the present. The brothers of the Lord had not believed
in him, as is contained in the gospel published before Marcion. The mother
likewise is not shown to have clung to him, when Martha and other Marys are
frequented in his company. In this place, finally, their unbelief appears:
when Jesus was teaching the way of life, when he was preaching the kingdom of
God, when he was working at the healing of weaknesses and faults, while
strangers were fixed upon him, those so near were away. Finally they come up
and remain outside, and they are not within, not reckoning, of course, what
was being done inside, nor do they even wait, as if they were bringing
something necessary to that which he was then especially doing, but they
interrupt still more and wish him called back from so great a work. I ask you,
Apelles, or you, Marcion, if by chance while playing at a board or contending
about actors or charioteers you were called away by such a message, would you
not have said, Who is my mother, or who my brothers? Christ, preaching God and
proving him, fulfilling the law and the prophets, scattering the darkness of
so great an age behind, did he unworthily use this saying to strike the
unbelief of those standing outside, or to shake off the importunity of those
calling him back from the work? For the rest, for denying nativity there would
have been another place and time and order of speech for him, not that which
could be pronounced even by one who had both a mother and brothers. When
indignation denies parents, it does not deny, but rebukes. Finally he made
others preferable, and, showing the merit of the preference, namely the
hearing of the word, he demonstrates on what condition he denied mother and
brothers. For by the same by which he adopted for himself others who were
clinging to him, by that he denied those who were standing away from him.
Christ is also accustomed to fulfill what he teaches others. What sort of
thing would it have been, then, if, teaching not to make so much of mother or
father or brothers as of the word of God, he himself, the word of God, when
mother and brotherhood were announced, should have deserted it? He therefore
denied parents in the way he taught they were to be denied for the work of
God. But otherwise also there is a figure of the synagogue in the mother put
aside and of the Jews in the unbelieving brothers. Israel was outside in them,
but the new disciples inside, hearing and believing, clinging to Christ,
outlined the church, which, having refused the carnal kind, he named a
preferable mother and a worthier brotherhood. In the same sense, finally, he
also answered that exclamation, not denying the womb and the breasts of a
mother, but designating as happier those who hear the word of God.

To these chapters alone, with which Marcion and Apelles seem to themselves
especially furnished, interpreted according to the truth of the intact and
uncorrupted gospel, it ought to have been enough for the proof of human flesh
in Christ through the defense of nativity. But since these Apelleians also
especially put forward the ignominy of the flesh, which they will have to have
been built for souls solicited by that fiery president of evil, and therefore
unworthy of Christ, and therefore a substance from the stars to have belonged
to him, I ought to strike them back from their own equipment. They name a
certain illustrious angel who established this world and, when it was
established, admitted repentance. And this we have treated in its own place.
For we have a little book to them also, whether he who had the spirit and will
and power of Christ for those works did anything worthy of repentance, since
they interpret the angel also from the figure of the wandering sheep. The
world, therefore, the repentance of its institutor being witness, will be a
sin, if indeed every repentance is a confession of a fault, because it has no
place except in a fault. If the world is a fault, as body and members the
fault will equally be also heaven and the heavenly things and whatever has
been conceived and brought forth from there. And a bad tree must of necessity
put forth bad fruits. The flesh of Christ therefore, built from heavenly
things, consists of the elements of sin, sinful from a sinful register, and
will already be a part of that substance, that is of ours, which they disdain
to bring upon Christ as sinful. Thus if there is no difference of ignominy,
let those to whom ours is displeasing either think out some matter of a purer
mark for Christ, or acknowledge the same, than which even a heavenly one could
not be better. We read plainly, The first man from the clay of the earth, the
second man from heaven. Yet it does not look to a difference of matter, but
only opposes to the earthly substance of the flesh of the first man formerly,
that is of Adam, the heavenly substance from the spirit of the second man,
that is of Christ. And so far does he refer the heavenly man to the spirit,
not to the flesh, that those whom he compares to him are established as made
heavenly in this earthly flesh, by the spirit of course. But if Christ is
heavenly according to the flesh also, those who are not heavenly according to
the flesh would not be compared to him. If therefore those who are made
heavenly, such as Christ also is, wear an earthly substance of flesh, from
this also it is confirmed that Christ himself also was heavenly in earthly
flesh, as those are who are made equal to him.

We put forward still that nothing which has been taken from another, so as to
be other than that from which it has been taken, is so other that it does not
suggest whence it has been taken. Every matter is not without the testimonies
of its origin, even if it is changed into a new property. This body of ours
itself, certainly, which, formed from clay, truth has handed on even to the
fables of the nations, confesses the origin of each element, earth by the
flesh, water by the blood. For although the appearance of the quality is
other, this is what it is that one thing is made from another; for the rest
what is blood but a red humor? what is flesh but earth in its own figure?
Consider the several qualities, the muscles as clods, the bones as rocks, even
around the nipples certain pebbles. Look at the tenacious connections of the
sinews as the offshoots of roots, and the branching runnings of the veins as
the windings of streams, and the down as mosses, and the hair as turf, and the
very treasures of the marrows in the hidden place as the mines of the flesh.
All these signs of earthly origin were also in Christ and are, which hid him
as the Son of God, not otherwise thought only a man than as existing in a
human substance of body. Or bring out something in him heavenly, begged from
the Bears and the Pleiades and the Hyades. For the things we have enumerated
are so much testimonies of earthly flesh that they are of ours also. But I
catch nothing new, nothing foreign. Finally it was only by words and deeds, by
teaching and by power alone, that they were astonished at Christ as a man. But
a novelty of flesh in him would also have been noted, held as a miracle. But
the unmarvelous condition itself of earthly flesh was what made the rest of
his things to be marveled at, when they said, Whence to this man this teaching
and these signs? This was the voice even of those looking down on his form. So
little was it a body of human handsomeness, still less of heavenly brightness.
Even if the prophets also are silent among us about his ignoble aspect, the
passions themselves and the insults themselves speak. The passions indeed
proved human flesh, but the insults proved it unhandsome. Or would anyone have
dared to graze a new body with the merest nail, to contaminate a face with
spittings, unless it deserved it? Why do you call heavenly a flesh which you
have no means of understanding as heavenly? Why do you deny earthly a flesh
which you have the means of recognizing as earthly? He hungered under the
devil, he thirsted under the Samaritan woman, he wept over Lazarus, he
trembles at death (For the flesh, he says, is weak), he pours blood at last.
These, I suppose, are heavenly signs. But how, I say, could it be despised and
suffer, as I have also said, if something from heavenly high birth had shone
in that flesh? From this therefore we convict that there was nothing in it
from the heavens, for this reason, that it might be able to be despised and to
suffer.

I turn to others equally wise in their own eyes, who affirm the flesh of
Christ to be animal, because the soul was made flesh, therefore the flesh also
is soul, and as the flesh is animal, so the soul also is fleshly. And here
therefore I seek the causes. If, in order to make the soul safe, Christ took
up a soul in himself, because it would not be safe except through him, while
in him, I do not see why he made it flesh by putting on an animal flesh, as if
he could not otherwise make the soul safe unless it had been made flesh. For
when he presents our souls as safe, not only not fleshly, but even disjoined
from flesh, how much more could he bring back into salvation that one which he
himself took up, even if not fleshly? Likewise, since they presume that Christ
proceeded for the sake of freeing not the flesh but our soul alone, first how
absurd it is that, about to free the soul alone, he should have made it that
kind of body which he was not going to free. Next, if he had taken up the
freeing of our souls through that one which he wore, he ought to have worn
that one also which he wore as ours, that is of our form, of whatever form our
soul is in secret, yet not of a fleshly form. For the rest he has not freed
our soul, if he had a fleshly one; for ours is not fleshly. Further, if he has
not freed ours, because he freed a fleshly one, it is nothing to us, because
he has not freed ours. But neither was that one to be freed which was not
ours, as being, of course, fleshly. For it was not in danger, if it was not
ours, that is not fleshly. But it is established that that one was freed.
Therefore it was not fleshly, and it was ours, if it was that which was to be
freed, since it was in danger. Already therefore if the soul was not fleshly
in Christ, neither can the flesh have been animal.

But we meet another argumentation of theirs, demanding why, by undergoing an
animal flesh, Christ should seem to have had a fleshly soul. For God, they
say, longed to exhibit the soul visible to human beings, by making it a body,
which had formerly existed invisible, seeing by nature nothing, not even
itself, because of the hindrance of this flesh, so that it might even be
disputed whether the soul was born or not. And so the soul was made a body in
Christ, that we might see it both being born and dying, and, what is more,
rising. And this too, what sort of thing was it, that the soul should be
demonstrated to itself or to us through flesh, which could not be recognized
through flesh, so that it should be so shown, while it becomes that in which
it was hidden, that is flesh? It received darkness, forsooth, that it might be
able to shine. Finally let us first treat for this, whether the soul had to be
shown in this way, then whether they allege it as formerly invisible in the
whole, whether as incorporeal, or as even having some kind of body of its own.
And yet when they call it invisible, they constitute it corporeal, as having
that which may be invisible. For how can that which has nothing invisible be
called invisible? But it cannot even be, unless it has that through which it
is. And when it is, it must of necessity have something through which it is.
If it has something through which it is, this will be its body. Everything
that is, is a body of its own kind. Nothing is incorporeal except what is not.
The soul therefore having an invisible body, he who had taken up making it
visible would of course more worthily have made visible that of it which was
held invisible, because here too neither a lie nor weakness belongs to God; a
lie, if he demonstrated the soul as other than what it was, weakness, if he
could not demonstrate that which it was. No one wishing to show a man puts a
helmet or a mask upon him. But this was done to the soul, if, converted into
flesh, it put on a foreign surface. But even if the soul is reckoned
incorporeal, so that by some hidden force of reason the soul indeed is, yet is
not a body, whatever the soul is, it was equally not impossible to God, and it
belonged more congruently to his purpose, to demonstrate it by some new
appearance of body than by this common to all, already of another's notice,
lest without cause he should have longed to make the soul visible from
invisible, opportune of course for these questions, through the defense of
human flesh upon it. But Christ could not among human beings be seen except as
a man. Give back therefore to Christ his own faith, that he who willed to walk
as a man has shown a soul also of human condition, not making it fleshly, but
clothing it with flesh.

Let the soul now have been shown through flesh, if it shall have been

and to us. Although in this the distinction is vain, as if we were apart from

without sense, nothing sensual without soul. And that I may speak more
pressingly, sense is the soul of the soul. Therefore when the soul furnishes
sensing to all, and itself senses the senses of all also, still more the
qualities, to whom is it likely that it itself was not allotted the sense of
itself from the beginning? Whence for it to know what is sometimes necessary
to itself, from the necessity of natural things, if it does not know its own
quality, to which something is necessary? This indeed is to be recognized in
every soul, knowledge of itself I mean, without which knowledge of itself no
soul could have administered itself. I think however that it belongs more to
the human being, the only rational animal, to have been allotted also a soul
which makes him a rational animal, itself in the first place rational.
Further, how is that rational which makes the human being a rational animal,
if it itself does not know its own reason, being ignorant of itself? But so
far is it from being ignorant, that it knows its author and judge and
standing. Knowing nothing yet of God, it names God. Admitting nothing yet of
his judgment, it says that it commends itself to God. Hearing nothing more
than that there is no hope after death, it imprecates both well and ill upon
each who has died. These things a little book which we have written, On the
Testimony of the Soul, follows out more fully. Otherwise, if the soul was
ignorant of itself from the beginning, it ought to have known nothing from
Christ except of what kind it was. But now it has not learned its own likeness
from Christ, but salvation. For this reason the Son of God came down and
underwent a soul, not that the soul itself might know itself in Christ, but
that it might know Christ in itself. For it is not by being ignorant of itself
that it is in danger concerning salvation, but of the word of God. Life, he
says, has been manifested, not the soul. And I have come, he says, to make the
soul safe; he did not say to show it. We were ignorant, I suppose, that the
soul, though invisible, is born and dies, unless it were exhibited bodily. We
were ignorant, plainly, that it would rise with the flesh. This will be what
Christ manifested. But this too not otherwise in himself than in some Lazarus,
whose flesh was not animal, and so neither was the soul fleshly. What then
further has become known to us about the formerly unknown arrangement of the
soul? What of it was invisible that would desire visibility through flesh?

The soul was made flesh that the soul might be shown. Was the flesh therefore
also made soul, that the flesh might be manifested? If the flesh is soul, it
is already not soul, but flesh. If the soul is flesh, it is already not flesh,
but soul. Where then is the flesh, and where is the soul, each having been
made the other. Nay, if they are neither, while each becomes the other, it is
certainly most perverse that, naming flesh, we should understand soul, and,
signifying soul, interpret flesh. All things will be in danger of being taken
otherwise than they are, and of losing what they are, while they are taken
otherwise, if they are named otherwise than they are. The faith of names is
the safety of properties. Even when qualities are changed, they receive the
possessions of vocabularies. For example, clay, when baked, undertakes the
vocabulary of a potsherd. Nor does it communicate with the vocabulary of the
former kind, because not with the kind itself. In the same way the soul of
Christ also, made flesh, cannot not be that which it has been made, and not be
that which it was, having been made something else of course. And since we
have applied a neighboring example, we will use it more fully. For certainly a
potsherd from clay is one body and one vocabulary of one body of course. Nor
can it be called both potsherd and clay; because what it was, it is not, and
what it is not also does not cling. And the soul does not cling. Therefore the
soul also, made flesh, is a uniform solidity and a singularity entire and an
undivided substance. But in Christ we find soul and flesh put forth in simple
and bare vocabularies, that is soul as soul and flesh as flesh, nowhere soul
as flesh or flesh as soul, when they ought so to have been named, if they had
so been, but each substance even pronounced divided for itself by himself, of
course for the distinction of two qualities, apart the soul and apart the
flesh. Why is it anxious, he says, my soul, even to death? and, The bread
which I shall give for the salvation of the world is my flesh. Further, if the
soul had been flesh, there would have been one thing in Christ, a fleshly soul
or an animal flesh. But when he divides the kinds, flesh and soul, he shows
two. If two, then not one; if not one, then neither is the soul fleshly nor
the flesh animal. For soul-flesh, or flesh-soul, is one. Unless he was also
wearing apart another soul besides that which was flesh, and carrying about
another flesh besides that which was soul. But if there is one flesh and one
soul, that one sad even to death and that one bread for the salvation of the
world, the number of two substances, differing in their own kind, is safe,
excluding the single species of a fleshly soul.

therefore that the nativity of the Lord is denied from intercourse, which is
what the will of a man and of the flesh tastes of, not from participation of
the womb. And why at any rate did he so exaggeratingly press that he was not
born from blood nor from the will of the flesh or of a man, unless because
that was the flesh which no one would doubt was born from intercourse?
Further, denying from intercourse he did not deny from flesh, nay he confirmed
from flesh, because he did not likewise deny from flesh as he denied from
intercourse. I ask you, if the Spirit of God did not come down into the womb
as going to participate in flesh from the womb, why did he come down into the
womb? For he could have been made spiritual flesh outside it; much more simply
than it would be made inside the womb, it would be made outside the womb.
Without cause he brought himself into that from which he brought nothing out.
But he did not come down into the womb without cause. Therefore he received
from it, because if he did not receive from it, he came down into it without
cause, especially as going to be flesh of that quality which was not of the
womb, that is spiritual.

But what sort of twist of yours is it, that you seek to take away the very
syllable FROM, written in the office of a preposition, and rather to use
another, which in this kind is not found in the holy scriptures? You say he
was born through the virgin, not from the virgin, and in the womb, not from
the womb, because the angel also in dreams to Joseph: For that which has been
born in her, he says, is from the Holy Spirit; he did not say from her. Yet
surely, even if he had said from her, he had said in her; for that was in her
which was from her. Equally therefore also when he says in her it sounds with
from her, because that was from her which was in her. But it is well that the
same Matthew, running down the origin of the Lord from Abraham even to Mary,
says, Jacob generated Joseph, the husband of Mary, from whom Christ is born.
But Paul also imposes silence on these grammarians. God sent, he says, his
Son, made from a woman. Did he say through a woman, or in a woman? This indeed
more pressingly, that he says made rather than born; for he would have
enounced born more simply. But by saying made he also sealed, And the Word was
made flesh, and asserted the truth of flesh made from the virgin. For us also
to this kind the psalms will be patrons, not indeed of the apostate and
heretical and Platonic Valentinus, but of the most holy and most received
prophet David. He sings among us of Christ, through whom Christ himself sang
himself. Receive Christ and hear the Lord speaking with God the Father:
Because you are he who tore me away from the womb of my mother. Behold one.
And my hope from the breasts of my mother, upon you I was cast from the womb.
Behold another. And from the womb of my mother you are my God. Behold another.
Now let us fight to the senses themselves. You tore me away, he says, from the
womb. What is torn away, unless what clings, what is fixed, bound to that from
which it is torn away that it may be taken off? If he did not cling to the
womb, how was he torn away? if he who was torn away clung, how would he have
clung, unless while he is from the womb, through that umbilical cord, as an
offshoot of his own little bag, bound to the origin of the womb? Even when
something foreign is glued to something foreign, it is so concarned and
inviscerated with that to which it is glued that, when it is torn away, it
snatches with itself something from the body from which it is torn away, as a
kind of sequel of a broken unity and of the offshoot of a mutual joining. For
the rest, what breasts of his mother does he name? without doubt those which
he drank. Let midwives, physicians, and naturalists answer about the nature of
the breasts, whether they are accustomed to flow otherwise, without the
genital passion of the womb, the veins hanging from there, decocting that
bilge of the lower blood into the breast, and by the very translation into a
gladder matter of milk. From there indeed it comes about that in the time of
the breasts the months of bloods are vacant. But if the Word was made flesh
from itself, not from communication of the womb, the womb having worked
nothing, having performed nothing, having suffered nothing, how did it pour
its own fountain over into the breasts, which it does not change unless by
having? But it could not have blood for supplying milk, if it did not have the
causes of that blood itself, namely the tearing-away of its own flesh. What
there was of novelty in Christ of being born from a virgin is open, namely
only this, that from a virgin, according to the reason which we have set out,
so that our regeneration might be virgin, spiritually sanctified from all
defilements through Christ, himself a virgin even carnally, as from the flesh
of a virgin.

If therefore they contend that this belonged to the novelty, that, as not from
the seed of a man, so neither from the flesh of the virgin should the Word of
God become flesh, why should this not be the whole novelty, that flesh not
born from seed should have proceeded by flesh? I will still come closer to the
engagement. Behold, he says, a virgin shall conceive in the womb. What then?
the Word of God of course, not the seed of a man, certainly that she might
bear a son. For she shall also bear, he says, a son. Therefore as it was hers
to have conceived, so that which she bore is hers, although that which she
conceived was not hers. On the contrary, if the Word was made flesh from
itself, already it conceived and bore itself, and the prophecy is vacant. For
the virgin neither conceived nor bore, if not whatever she bore from the Word
conceived is her flesh. Will this voice of the prophet alone be voided? or
also that of the angel announcing the conception and birth of the virgin? or
also every scripture already, whichever pronounces a mother of Christ? For how
a mother, unless because it was in her womb? But he received nothing from her
womb that would make her a mother, in whose womb he was. Foreign flesh does
not owe this name. None but a daughter of the womb, flesh, calls it a mother's
womb. Further, she is not a daughter of the womb if she has been born to
herself. Elizabeth therefore also will be silent, carrying a prophet, an
infant already conscious of his Lord, and besides filled with the Holy Spirit.
For without cause she says, And whence to me that the mother of my Lord should
come to me? If Mary was carrying in the womb not a son but a guest, Jesus, how
does she say, Blessed is the fruit of your womb? Who is this fruit of the
womb, who did not sprout from the womb, who did not put a root in the womb,
who is not of her whose the womb is, and who is of course the fruit of the
womb, Christ? Or because he himself is the flower from the rod that went forth
from the root of Jesse, but the root of Jesse is the kind of David, the rod
from the root Mary from David, the flower from the rod the son of Mary, who is
called Jesus Christ, will he himself also be the fruit? For the flower is
fruit, because through the flower and from the flower every fruit is trained
into fruit. What then? They deny also to the fruit its flower and to the
flower its rod and to the rod its root, so that the root may not claim for
itself through the rod the property of that which is from the rod, of the
flower and the fruit; if indeed every grade of kind is reckoned from the last
to the principal, so that they may know already now that the flesh of Christ
clings not only to Mary, but also to David through Mary and to Jesse through
David. Therefore God swears to him that this fruit from the loins of David,
that is from the posterity of his flesh, will sit on his own throne. If from
the loins of David, how much more from the loins of Mary, on whose account he
was in the loins of David?

Let them therefore blot out also the testimonies of the demons proclaiming
Jesus the Son of David, but they will not be able to blot out the testimonies
of the apostles, if those of the demons are unworthy. Matthew himself first,
the most faithful commentator of the gospel, as a companion of the Lord, for
no other cause than that he might make us possessors of the carnal origin of
Christ, so began: The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of
David, the son of Abraham. With the kind flowing from these fountains of
origin, when the order is led down step by step to the nativity of Christ,
what else than the flesh itself of Abraham and of David, making through each
an offshoot of itself even to the virgin, is described, bringing in Christ,
nay rather Christ himself is led forth from the virgin? But Paul also, as
being both disciple and teacher and witness of the same gospel, as apostle of
that same Christ himself, confirms that Christ is from the seed of David
according to the flesh, his of course. Therefore the flesh of Christ is from
the seed of David. If according to the flesh of Mary it is from the seed of
David, therefore it is from the flesh of Mary, while it is from the seed of
David. Whichever way you twist the saying, either that which is from the seed
of David is from the flesh of Mary, or that which is from the flesh of Mary is
from the seed of David. The same apostle dissolves this whole controversy,
defining him to be himself the seed of Abraham. Since Abraham, much more of
course David, as of a more recent one. For reweaving the promise of the
blessing of the nations in the name of Abraham, And in your seed shall all
nations be blessed, He did not say, he says, seeds, as of many, but seed, of
one, which is Christ. We who read and believe these things, how ought we and
how can we recognize in Christ the quality of flesh? of course no other than
Abraham's, if indeed Christ is the seed of Abraham; nor other than Jesse's, if
indeed from the root of Jesse the flower is Christ; nor other than David's, if
indeed the fruit from the loins of David is Christ; nor other than from Mary,
if indeed from the womb of Mary is Christ; and still higher, nor other than
Adam's, if indeed the second Adam is Christ. It is consequent therefore that
they either contend that those men had spiritual flesh, so that the same
condition of substance may be led down into Christ, or concede that the flesh
of Christ was not spiritual, which is not registered from a spiritual stock.

But we acknowledge that the prophetic voice of Simeon is fulfilled, pronounced
over the still recent infant Lord: Behold this one is set for the ruin and
raising of many in Israel, and for a sign, which shall be spoken against. For
the sign of the nativity of Christ according to Isaiah: Therefore the Lord
himself will give you a sign: Behold a virgin shall conceive in the womb, and
shall bear a son. We acknowledge therefore a contradictable sign, the
conception and birth of the virgin Mary, about which these Academics: She bore
and did not bear, a virgin and not a virgin; as if not, even if it had so to
be said, it would more fitly be said by us. For she bore, who from her own
flesh, and she did not bear, who not from the seed of a man, and a virgin, as
far as from a man, not a virgin, as far as from birth: yet not so that
therefore she did not bear, and therefore a virgin who is not a virgin,
because not a mother from her own inward parts. But with us nothing is
doubtful, nor twisted into a two-headed defense. Light is light, and Darkness
darkness, and Yes yes, and No no; what is more, this is from evil. She bore
who bore; and if a virgin conceived, in her birth she wedded. For she wedded
by the very law of the opened body, in which it made no difference concerning
the force of the male, admitted or sent forth; the same thing of the sex
unsealed it. This finally is the womb on account of which it is written also
of others: Every male opening the womb shall be called holy to the Lord. Who
truly holy than the Son of God? Who properly opened the womb than he who
opened what was shut? For the rest, nuptials open it for all. And so it was
the more opened, because it was the more shut. Of course she is more to be
called not a virgin than a virgin, by a kind of leap a mother before she was a
bride. And what further is to be treated about this? Since by this reason the
apostle pronounced the Son of God brought forth not from a virgin but from a
woman, he acknowledged the nuptial passion of the opened womb. We read indeed
in Ezekiel about that cow which bore and did not bear. But see lest the Holy
Spirit, already now foreseeing you, marked you with this voice, as going to
dispute over the womb of Mary. For the rest he would not have pronounced
against that simplicity of his own doubtfully, Isaiah saying, She shall
conceive and bear.

For what Isaiah hurls in the taunt of the heretics themselves, and first, Woe
to those who make sweet bitter and darkness light, he marks of course those
who do not even keep these vocabularies in the light of their properties, so
that the soul should not be other than what it is called, and the flesh not
other than what is seen, and God not other than he who is preached. Therefore,
looking forward also to Marcion, I am, he says, God, and there is no other
besides me. And when otherwise he says the same thing in the same way, Before
me God was not, he strikes I know not what genealogies of Aeons of the
Valentinians. And, Not from blood nor from the will of flesh and of a man, but
he was born from God, he answers Ebion. Equally, Even if an angel from heaven
should evangelize you otherwise than we, let him be anathema, he directs at
the working, the child of the Apelleian virgin Philumene. Certainly he who
denies that Christ has come in the flesh, this one is antichrist. Pronouncing
flesh naked and absolute and by the simple name of its own nature, he strikes
all its disputers. Just as also, defining Christ himself also as one, he
shakes the arguers of a many-formed Christ, who make one Christ, another
Jesus, another slipped from the midst of the crowds, another detained, another
bright in the retirement of the mountain in the circuit of a cloud under three
witnesses, another passible to the rest, ignoble, another great-souled,
another truly trembling, last of all another who suffered, another raised,
through which they assert their own resurrection also in another flesh. But it
is well that the same will come from the heavens who suffered, the same will
appear to all who was raised. And they shall see and recognize who pierced
him, of course that very flesh against which they raged, without which he
himself will be able neither to be nor to be recognized: so that those also
may blush who affirm that flesh sits in the heavens empty of sense as a sheath
with Christ taken out, or who make flesh and soul the same, or only soul, but
flesh no longer.

But thus far about the present matter. For I think the proof of flesh in
Christ, both born from a virgin and human, has already been sufficiently
furnished. Which even discussed alone could have sufficed, apart from the
engagement of the several opinions from the other side, which we have
challenged from abundance, both by their argumentations and by the scriptures
which they use, so that by that which we have proved, what the flesh of Christ

make a close from a common preface, it will have here a pre-construction, now
that it is manifest of what kind that was which rose in Christ.
