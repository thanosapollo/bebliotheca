HOMILY ON EUTROPIUS THE EUNUCH, PATRICIAN AND CONSUL.
=52.391=
1. Always, and now most of all, it is timely to say: Vanity of vanities, and
all is vanity. Where now is the brilliant array of the consulship? Where the
bright lamps? Where the applause, and the dances, and the banquets, and the
festivals? Where the crowns and the hangings? Where the city's uproar, and the
acclamations in the hippodromes, and the flatteries of the spectators?

All those things are gone; and a wind blowing all at once has thrown down the
leaves, and shown us the tree bare, and shaken from the very root besides; for
such was the blast of the wind that it even threatened to tear it up by the
roots, and to shake even these sinews of the tree. Where now are the
feigned friends? Where the drinking-bouts and the dinners? Where the swarm of
parasites, and the unmixed wine poured all day long, and the varied arts of
the cooks, and the attendants of power, who do and say everything to please?
Night was all that, and a dream, and when day came it vanished; they were
spring flowers, and when spring passed all withered; it was a shadow, and it
ran past; it was fruit, and it dissolved; they were bubbles, and they burst;
it was a spider's web, and it was torn apart. Therefore we chant this
spiritual saying continually, repeating: Vanity of vanities, and all is
vanity. For this saying ought to be written continually on walls, and on
garments, and in the marketplace, and in the house, and on roads, and on
doors, and at entries, and above all in each man's conscience, and to be
studied without cease. Since the deceit of affairs, and the masks, and the
play-acting, seem to most people to be truth, each man ought every day, at
dinner, and at breakfast, and in gatherings, to say this to his neighbor, and
to hear it from his neighbor, that Vanity of vanities, all things =52.392= are
vanity.

Did I not tell you continually that wealth is a runaway? But you would not
bear with us. Did I not say that it is an ungrateful servant? But you would
not be persuaded. See, experience has shown from the facts that it is not only
a runaway, nor only ungrateful, but a murderer too; for this has made you
tremble now and be afraid. Did I not tell you, when you continually rebuked me
for speaking the truth, that I love you more than those who flatter? that I
who reprove care more than those who gratify? Did I not add to these words
that the wounds of friends are more trustworthy than the willing kisses of
enemies?
If you had borne my wounds, those kisses of theirs would not have begotten
this death for you; for my wounds work health, but their kisses prepared an
incurable disease. Where now are the cupbearers? Where those who strutted in
the marketplace, and spoke countless praises before all? They have run away,
they have denied the friendship, they procure safety for themselves through
your distress. But we are not so; even when you were displeased we do not leap
away, and now that you have fallen we wrap you and tend you.

And the Church which you warred against has spread her bosom and received you;
but the theatres you cultivated, over which you often were angry with us, have
betrayed and destroyed you. Yet we did not cease always saying: Why do you do
these things? You drive the Church into a frenzy, and you carry yourself over
the cliffs; and you ran past it all. And the hippodromes, having spent your
wealth, sharpened the sword; but the Church, which had tasted your untimely
wrath, everywhere


runs, wishing to snatch you from the nets. =52.393= 2. And I say these things
now, not trampling on the one who lies fallen, but wishing to make those who
stand more secure; not tearing open the wounds of the injured, but keeping
those not yet wounded in safe health; not drowning the one tossed by the
waves, but instructing those who sail with a fair wind, so that they may not
go under. And how might this come about? If we take to heart the changes of
human affairs. For if this man had feared a change, he would not have suffered
a change; but since he became no better either from himself or from others,
you at least who flourish in wealth, gain from this man's misfortune; for
nothing among human affairs is weaker. Therefore whatever name one may give to
their worthlessness, he will say less than the truth; whether he name them
smoke, or grass, or a dream, or spring flowers, or anything at all; so
perishable are they, and more worthless than things that are nothing; and that
together with their nothingness they also have much that is precipitous, is
clear from this.

For who became higher than this man? Did he not surpass the whole inhabited
world in wealth? Did he not rise to the very peaks of dignities? Did not all
tremble at him, and fear him? But see, he has become more wretched than
prisoners, and more pitiable than servants, and more destitute than beggars
wasting with hunger, seeing every day sharpened swords, and the pit, and
executioners, and the leading away to death; and he does not even know whether
he ever was in that pleasure, nor does he perceive the very ray of it; but at
high noon, as if in thickest night, this man, hemmed in, is blinded in his
eyes. Rather, however much we may contend, we shall not be able by speech
to set forth the suffering which he is likely undergoing, expecting to be
killed every hour. But what need is there of words from us, when he himself
has sketched these things for us clearly as in a picture? For yesterday, when
they came upon him from the imperial courts wishing to drag him away by force,
and he ran to the holy vessels, his face was, and is even now, in no better
state than one already dead; a chattering of the teeth, and a clatter and
trembling of the whole body, and a voice broken off, and a tongue dissolving,
and such an appearance as a soul of stone is likely to have.

3. And I say these things, not reviling, nor trampling on his misfortune, but
wishing to soften your mind, and to draw it to pity, and to persuade you to be
content with the punishment that has already come. For since there are many
among us so inhuman as even to accuse us likewise, because we received him at
the bema; wishing to soften their lack of affection by the narratives, I make
a show of his sufferings. For why are you angry, tell me, beloved? Because, he
says, he who warred against the Church continually has taken refuge in it. For
this very reason one ought most of all to glorify God, that He allowed him to
be brought into such necessity, so as to learn both the power of the Church
=52.394= and her loving-kindness; the power, from the fact that he suffered so
great a change from his wars against her; the loving-kindness, from the fact
that, having been warred against, she now holds out the shield, and has
received him under her own wings, and has set him in all safety, bearing no
grudge for any of the former things, but spreading her bosom to him with much
affection. For this is brighter than any trophy, this is a conspicuous
victory, this puts Greeks to shame, this also confounds Jews, this shows her
face bright: that having taken the enemy captive, she spares him, and when all
have overlooked him in his desolation, she alone, like an affectionate mother,
hid him under her hangings, and stood against imperial wrath, against the
people's rage, and against unbearable hatred; this is an ornament to the
altar.


What sort of ornament, he says, is it that the accursed and greedy and
rapacious man should touch the altar? Do not say these things; since the
harlot also touched the feet of Christ, she who was exceedingly accursed and
unclean; and what happened was not a charge against Jesus, but a wonder and a
great hymn; for the unclean woman did not harm the clean one, but the clean
and unblemished One through the touch made the accursed harlot clean. Do not
then bear a grudge, O man. We are servants of that One who was crucified and
said: Forgive them, for they do not know what they do. But he walled off, he
says, the refuge here with various writings and laws. But see, through the
deeds he has learned what he did, and he himself first dissolved the law, by
what he has done, and he has become a spectacle for the inhabited world, and
being silent from here he sends forth a voice advising all, Do not do such
things, lest you suffer such things.

He has appeared as a teacher through the misfortune, and the altar sends forth
a great radiance, appearing now especially fearful also from this, that it has
the lion bound; since even for an imperial image it would be a great ornament,
not only when the emperor sits on the throne clothed in purple and wearing a
diadem, but also when under the imperial foot barbarians with their hands
bound behind them bow their heads, lying down. And that he has not used mere
persuasiveness of words, you are witnesses of the eagerness and the concourse.
For the spectacle is brilliant for us today, and the gathering is bright, and
as great a people as I have seen assembled at the holy Pascha, so great do I
see here now also; and thus being silent he has called all, sending forth
through the facts a voice more brilliant than a trumpet. And virgins emptying
chambers, and women the women's quarters, and men the marketplace, you have
all run together here, that you may see human nature exposed, and the
perishable nature of the affairs of life stripped bare, and the harlot's face
which yesterday and the day before shone brightly (for such is prosperity from
extortions, appearing more shameful than any wrinkled old woman), having wiped
off, as with a sponge, by the change, the paints and the overlays.

4. For such is the force of this misfortune: it has made the bright and
conspicuous man appear now cheaper than all. And if a rich man comes in, he
gains greatly; for seeing him who shook the whole inhabited world brought down
from so great a height, and contracted =52.395=, and become more cowardly than
a hare and a frog, and without bonds nailed to this pillar, and bound round by
fear instead of a chain, and afraid, and trembling, he allays the
inflammation, he takes down the swelling, and having philosophized what one
ought to philosophize about human things, he goes away thus, learning through
the facts what the Scriptures say through words: that All flesh is grass, and
all the glory of man as the flower of grass; and the grass withered, and the
flower fell; as, They shall quickly dry up like grass, and like green herbs
they shall quickly fall; that His days are as smoke, and as many such things.
Again the poor man coming in, and looking toward this sight, does not hold
himself cheap, nor is he pained because of poverty; but he even thanks
poverty, that it has become for him an inviolable place and a harbor without
waves, and a secure wall; and seeing these things he would often choose to
remain where he is, rather than, having taken the goods of all for a short
time, afterward even to be in danger for his own blood. Do you see that no
small gain has come both to rich and to poor, and to lowly and to high, and to
slaves and to free, from this man's taking refuge here? Do you see how each
goes away having taken medicines from here, healed from this sight alone?

Have I then softened your passion, and cast out anger? Have I quenched the
inhumanity? Have I brought you to sympathy? I think so very much, and the
faces show it, and the fountains of tears.


Since then the rock has become for you deep soil, and a rich land, come then,
having also sprouted the fruit of almsgiving, and having shown the ear of
grain flourishing with sympathy, let us fall before the emperor, or rather let
us entreat the loving God to soften the emperor's wrath, and to make his heart
tender, so as to give us the gift entire. For already from that day on which
this man took refuge here, the change has been no small one; for when the
emperor learned that he had run down to this inviolable place, the army being
present, and being provoked over the wrongs done to him, and demanding him for
slaughter, he stretched out a long speech, allaying the soldiers' wrath,
asking that not only the sins, but also if any success had come about for him,
this too should be reckoned, and for the one sort acknowledging gratitude, and
for those that were otherwise forgiving as toward a man. And when they pressed
again for vengeance for the insulted emperor, shouting, leaping, speaking of
death, and shaking the spears, then sending forth fountains of tears from his
most gentle eyes, and reminding them of the holy table, to which he had fled,
he thus stopped the anger.

5. But let us too add what is from ourselves. For of what pardon would you be
worthy, if the emperor who was insulted bears no grudge, and you who have
suffered nothing of the kind have shown so great anger? And how, when this
spectacle is dismissed, will you touch the mysteries, and say that prayer
through which we are commanded to say: Forgive us as we also forgive our
debtors, while you demand justice of your debtor? He did great injustices and
insulted? We too will not contradict. But it is not a time of a lawcourt now,
but of mercy; not of accounting, but of loving-kindness; not of examination,
but of pardon; not of a vote and a trial, but of pity and grace. Let no one
then be inflamed, nor be displeased, but rather let us beseech the loving God
to give him a term of life, and to snatch him from the threatened slaughter,
so that he may strip off the offenses, and let us go together to the loving
emperor, on behalf of the Church, on behalf of the altar, asking that one man
be granted as a gift to the holy table. If we do this, the emperor himself
will also accept it, and God before the emperor will praise it, and will give
us a great recompense for the loving-kindness. For just as He turns away from
and hates the savage and inhuman man, so He receives and loves the merciful
and loving; and if such a one is righteous, He weaves brighter crowns for him;
if a sinner, He runs past the sins, giving him this as a recompense for
sympathy toward the fellow-servant.

For I desire mercy, He says, and not sacrifice; and everywhere in the
Scriptures you see Him always seeking this, and saying that this is a
remission of sins. Thus then we too shall make Him gracious, thus we shall
dissolve our own offenses, thus we shall adorn the Church, thus also the
loving emperor will praise us, as I said before, and the whole people will
applaud, and the ends of the inhabited world will wonder at the city's
loving-kindness and gentleness, and those everywhere on the earth, having
learned the things that happened, will proclaim us.

In order then that we may enjoy such great goods, let us fall down, let us
entreat, let us beseech, let us snatch from the danger the captive, the
fugitive, the suppliant, so that we ourselves too may attain the goods to
come, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be
the glory and the power, now and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
