All that was fitting to the birthday gods and the ancestral gods, and before
these and after them to the Savior and guide of life, and whatever one might
name, has been duly prayed, beginning from the hearth. And with those things
by which one might honor this day, giving precedence at least to the custom
that has prevailed in these matters, it is now being fulfilled as far as
possible, all contributing every eagerness. So that it is no small proof, from
those present, of how far in gladness the city's form has advanced, not
privately only but also publicly, as if now especially it has taken a sudden
perception of how far it differs from the rest in prosperity, inasmuch as,
besides all the just claims by which it was judged to hold first place, it has
this man's birth to add. For especially each of those others too, I mean those
of which there is even account and a great name, do not refrain from making
him a citizen and calling him by this title, where they even claim him
outright as a patron, like certain mothers disputing over a child, such as one
may see this one to be, granting the being to the mother who obtained it by
nature, yet not depriving themselves of being so called. And the more this
city sees those others forcing, in their judgment, to outstrip nature, the
more it doubles its affection. So that this strife alone may most of all be
called good, according to Hesiod, and this city wins it without dispute, the
one at which the sacrifices now take place and the speeches; since this city
also gives you the speeches, O dearest of children, not these only, but also
those of each day. As for me, the god, having saved me from the last
extremities, has given me to the city; and whatever you receive from me, you
have from her who received me and now gives; so that it is possible for you
both to call the city mother and to claim that upon the

same you have been genuinely reared. And this is no small thing, I think,
toward the account of what is noble, both for you and for her: for you, in
that you did not dishonor your fatherland as if it would not suffice for a
noble soul toward the practice of virtue and education, and choose to tend a
foreign hearth; and for her it is possible, I suppose, to take pride no less
than anyone, that she was fortunate not only to bear you, but also, when you
were already advancing in age, kept you with herself, having been judged to
win even in the zeal for speeches and wisdom, together with the other goods
she has, along with those or even before those. And so, of the prizes for
these things, some are near to being obtained, with good hopes, and some have
already been reaped from your family; since this too is no small thing, I
think, between you for one another, that it is not you alone who were born
upon this city, I mean, while having ancestors to count from elsewhere, but
that a succession beginning from of old, from a point whence one could not
even have found it because of the length of time, if the splendor had not been
set forth, has advanced as far as you, and through you awaits the age that
follows, full. For from Quadratus, and it is clear, I think, even when one has
uttered the name, to all alike, it is possible, going down by number, to see
the whole family as far as this one, a pure and genuine offshoot of this
earth, a poet would say, and one that flowers upon itself and will bloom still
more, as if always beginning and never ceasing, so long as the earth that
nourishes this fruit remains immortal. And this too comes upon me to consider
as an advantage of the family, for it has just now entered my mind: to be able
to say that the founders were genuine citizens is perhaps a good common even
with others; but to have been born from Quadratus, apart from the other things
that belonged to him, turns the advantage around against the rest. For to
those others it is possible to take pride in this very thing, that they are
citizens, and this is their greatest point, as indeed it is; but he who was
called by the god as one who would take up the city, worn out by

time, accomplished this very thing, so that henceforth one may say that the
other families belong to the city, but the city belongs to this family, and
yet this is not my speech, but the city itself agrees and cries it out in the
council-chambers, in the theaters, in the assemblies, in whatever part you
might even say, since everything has been adorned by him.

This being so, all that his descendants and children and children's children,
the poetic phrase, having chosen the citizenship of the Romans, passed through
splendidly as splendid men, I think I would do rightly in passing over. If
they were small, one would always have mentioned whatever came to hand,
dignifying the speech; but since they are great, or rather have no room for
exaggeration, so that it is a labor, having mentioned a part, to go through
them worthily except with some splendid fortune of the speech, why would one,
abandoning what is at hand, which is not itself small, if it is not even
greater than those ancient things, and which still needs a prayer, take a
further risk about the whole in addition to what lies before us? It would be
best, leaving aside the intervening ancestors of this boy who is best in all
things, to mention briefly those who are in a way before our eyes, and of
these only so much as bears on the present speech. And the boy's own name
suggests it, so that from home to home it gives the beginning of family and of
speech alike. Noble, then, and fine and good, and having pursued every virtue
that the nature of men has thought worthy of these names, and then having
become father of this boy, Fronto, concerning whom what should we say, when
the hearers will anticipate whatever one might say, inasmuch as, I think, the
man has not long since departed to the fairer and more god-beloved portion,
except if one, in Homeric fashion, should make the whole clear with a brief
address, saying /You are of good blood, dear child./ But his father's father,
and this boy's namesake, that famous Apellas, for this beginning, I think, a
little earlier

the speech was offering, what sort of man was he for us? Of the other things
that are sung I would bring none into the midst, not courage, not greatness of
soul, not of how many nations he was governor, nor how many honors he reaped
from emperors, none of these; for these things one must pray someday to say
about this one who is now with us, and to have good hopes that he will stand
better not only than his father, this one at least, but also than his
grandfather, and I will add that he will be established better than the
remaining ancestors, as it seems good both to the other gods and to Paean. But
what I already see as common to this one and to that one, just as the name is,
of this I think it timely for me to make mention now, and even as I begin the
speech I am finishing what lies ahead, and I cannot pass it by for excess of
pleasure. But, O Lord Asclepius, a little earlier I was calling you in the
prayers; be present in everything, and most of all in what is now being said.
O Lord, this too, then, was your work and a device of your grace. For formerly
you made that Apellas so very much your friend that you neglected him neither
when he was resting and sleeping, nor stood away when he was awake and
toiling, but day and night did the same thing, in every way, as they say, to
save the man from the midst of fire; and whom would this one not snatch even
from blazing fire if he wished, when he would even save by fire, if he should
wish this? But now, as it seems, you are rearing for yourself another servant
in his place, so that he inherits at once the grandfather's name and the honor
from you. And yet I think you would contend with your father, and I with
Homer, in this: for the plant that sprouts by the altars is for you altogether
better than that of the Delian, and it is possible for me to say that it is
your plant, and not to liken it to another, as that one did. And how is the
stem not of the Savior himself, and that in the undefiled gardens of the
Graces, from which it came forth, being reared, from the blessed womb; and it
is possible to compare it not only with the roots of the family,

which have already been spoken of, but also with what blossoms upon it at this
very age, and quite clearly, dividing it thus from the things that belong to
the soul and from those that belong to the body. The gentleness of the soul,
at least, outruns the prime of age, hastening toward old age, yet the
gentleness does not resemble a worn-out one, such as the necessities of life
have worked into many, but it is young together with him, blended with a
fitting pride. And let one observe it from the cast of the eyes, if anyone has
still to learn in fact what is said; for they neither stand up staring, nor
resemble those brought down by some sleep through lowliness, but a quiet,
gentle dignity raises the look. Grace, then, the gift set forth for children
from the gods as if from some agreement, is not, I suppose, naked according to
the painters, but having clothed itself in much modesty, it has been melted
into all the parts. Therefore neither has another smiled with so great an
Aphrodite, nor yet has he stopped with a blush the laugh already being moved.
Nor, then, has the spirited part left him, by which one might judge the nature
of males, nor yet has it been provoked and driven out into a wild and
beastlike one; and he himself has furnished proofs of both these things, now
being evident in his face as to the motion of the soul, now gently persuading
it to be still and setting a calm. And persuasion in earnest and in play, who
would raise from these lips of his, not only when he has spoken, but even when
he is silent? For neither would anyone by speaking win them over as this one
does, having shown beforehand by silence what he wishes, nor would Nestor
still seem, old man though he was, to speak things like honey, when examined
beside this one. In short, he never is in earnest without brightness, but
everything, even if it is hard, cannot be done without a festival, and what is
in its turn pleasant for him has not been released from earnestness. And if

Plato's argument rightly urges us, and one must think it is so, that in their
play they should learn in advance of the lessons as many as it is necessary to
have learned beforehand, as a carpenter to measure or to take a level, and a
military man to ride while playing, or doing some other such thing, and to try
through the games to turn the pleasures and desires of the children toward
that place where, having arrived, they must have their end, if this must be
said to be well for Plato, and I somehow happened to recall his words, neither
taking away anything nor adding, who from the rest of the company of children
would be a match for this one for us, for whom contests of horses and
trainings of dogs have been let go, so far is he from being sick with any of
the worse things; rather he lives by these things in which both his present
life consists and the rest is going to consist: I mean the contests of boys in
the palaestra and contests of speeches; and already going forward he
prophesies the future by his play, quite simply training his own fortune, and
already he sits as a judge, and nature has certainly not been ignorant of the
road through play to the true fortune. And no one would blame this one as
having judged unjustly, as they say the teacher of justice blamed Cyrus, since
he is even wiser than Cyrus in the judicial art, having learned it from no
one. Such a thing is a pure nature, molded from undefiled wax according to
Plato, having the molding midway between the wet and soft and the hard and
stony. And what would you say when he refers it to a god, leader of the whole,
and a god to whom it was given from the ancestors themselves to have care, a
fitting good successor of good fathers, descendant and child and all that it
is possible to call him. O those thrice and many times blessed, who, reaping
in you the only immortality granted to the race of men, and along with the
memory of themselves leaving you as an inheritance more magnificent than the
treasures under the earth and about the earth their own glory, upon which the
god himself, having taken his stand, having asked him for himself,

as much as he himself wished and as the former rank of the family demanded,
now sets you safely. For what a day, what a day will shortly succeed this
blessed and god-pleasing day, when two beginnings of one year come together
into the same; for both the present beginning of your birth, bringing the
greatest good, the year from beginning to end, and the other beginning that
will at once succeed this one, and these of one and the same year, has been
established, rendering to you the ancestral, hereditary return for nurture,
with good fortune. And it seems to me that the god, taking in advance the
honor customary upon children, has rendered to you, from your father, as has
often been said, and from those coming down from above on the father's side to
you, the honor upon the contests of the sacred Asclepieia, so that he has at
once fulfilled all that was lawful and, as you pass henceforth from boys to
men, as the imperial law indeed arranges, you have already been given over to
the honors from the emperor toward you, which he assigns to those of such an
age already becoming men. In every way Apollo is not far from prophecy for us,
inasmuch as those hopes are good, and good are those that will now at once
come before our eyes. I, at least, already seem to myself to perceive a
certain lightness, as Diomedes has been made by Homer through Athena both
light in his limbs at once and clear in his sight, and I cannot hold myself
for gladness. For beautiful, and perhaps fitting to adorn, are also the years
of your birth already past, to show all that belongs to you, but this one now
present seems to me far more beautiful than those, destined to be defeated
only by those after it, and in the rest already defeating those that have gone
by; in which we shall look upon you, household and kinsmen and teachers and
agemates and every friendly kindred, as you present a sweeter sight from the
sacred purple of the Savior, and as you make the season of your age fitting to
the season of the year, quite simply like an autumn star

like it, and sending forth the radiance according to that one; and this one a
fitting president of the games for him who chose him. These things are yours,
O Lord, for so it is better to lead the speech back to a beginning, having
prayed again, these things are yours. Come then, taking the tiller for us
yourself, as indeed you already hold it, keep the house safe to the end,
giving from the great emperor to this one the honors which this one's
ancestors reaped from that one's ancestors, and giving to the emperor himself
and to the emperor's family to administer all time, as he was allotted from
Zeus, and having granted this one to pass the destined time in prosperity,
preserve through children from children the succession of the family both for
the city and for yourself.
