LETTER XXVI.
To Evagrius the monk, concerning the Godhead.
I both greatly marvel and am exceedingly astonished at your sobriety, how you
become the cause of such contemplations and of inquiries so great, by precise
questions setting us under the necessity of speaking and under the struggle of
demonstration, bringing upon us questions that are necessary and useful to us.
Clearly, then, there is every necessity that we make our answers after the
questions, and that they be effective. And now, then, the question put forward
by you was of this kind and concerning this: in what manner the nature of
Father and of Son and of Holy Spirit would be (which one would more rightly
call essence rather than nature), whether some simple thing or composite. For
if it is simple, how will it admit the number three of those aforementioned?
For the simple is uniform and without number. But what falls under numbers
must of necessity be divided, even if it is not subjected to numbers; and what
is divided is passible, for division is a passion. If, then, the nature of the
Superior is simple, the positing of the names is superfluous; but if the
positing of the names is true, and one must obey the names, the uniform and
simple at once goes out of the way. What, then, would the nature of the matter
be?
These things you said to us. Of which the account of the truth will set forth
the demonstrations precisely, not irrationally putting forward, by want of
demonstration, a phantasm of unproven faith, nor trying to cover the
unsoundness of its own persuasion with testimonies of ancient myths, but
setting forth into the open the confirmation of the contemplation by the
understanding of precise inquiry and by the rightness of reasonings. Come
then, henceforth let the discourse hold festival with us, and let it declare
how one must conceive the Divine, whether simple or triple-plaited. For so the
triad of names constrains us both to speak and to believe, and some, making
use of these, =46.1104= have constructed unsound and altogether stale
doctrines, holding that the essence together with the appellation of the names
undergoes the passion of division. But those, as you yourself say, must be
left aside, who advocate unsoundly the dogma of their assumption; and let us
turn our own mind toward the right grasp of knowledge. What God is, then, we
shall first set down; and then in this way we shall come precisely to the
demonstrations. It is altogether a simple and undivided essence, having
obtained from nature the simple and the incorporeal. But perhaps the argument
from the division of the names opposes me, stripping the Superior of its
uniformity by the number three. Is it then necessary, because of the uniform,
that we flee the confession of Father and Son and Holy Spirit? God forbid! For
the positing of the names will not harm the undivided unity of the Superior.
For intelligible things are both myriad-named (since they are called by a
multitude of names among each nation) and yet outside every appellation, since
there is no proper name of the intelligible and incorporeal. For how could
things that do not even fall under our sight, nor can be caught at all by
human senses, be properly named? And let the soul, the smallest portion of the
intelligibles, be taken by us toward an exact understanding of the All. For
soul is addressed by a feminine name, yet it stands apart from every womanish
nature, being in essence neither male nor female. And likewise the word that
is brought to birth from it has a masculine name, yet it too, as we say, is
outside all male-and-female corporeality.
And if the last of the intelligibles, soul and word, do not possess a proper
name, how shall we say that the first intelligibles, those beyond all the
intelligibles, have been called by proper names? But the naming is useful,
leading us of necessity into a conception of the intelligibles. Some, however,
holding that the essence is coarsely co-divided together with the
appellations, =46.1105= form in their own thoughts opinions altogether
unworthy of the divine things. And it is consistent that we who are knowers of
the truth know that the divine and undivided essence of the Superior is
indivisible and uniform; yet toward the usefulness of the salvation of our
souls it both seems to be partitioned by the namings and to undergo the
necessity of division, as we have said. For just as the soul, being something
intelligible, brings forth a multitude of infinite thoughts, yet is neither
divided by the passion of a thought, nor does the soul ever grow poor of the
multitude of thoughts because of those that have already come, but rather
becomes richer, not poorer; and just as this uttered word, common to us all,
is undivided from the soul that offered it, and is no less established in the
souls of the hearers at the same time, both not being separated from that one
and being found in these, working union rather than division of them and of
our souls: so conceive also the Son as never having been separated from the
Father, and again the Holy Spirit of this one, likewise as the thought in the
mind.
For as it is not possible to conceive any division and cutting between mind
and thought and soul, so neither can a cutting or division ever be conceived
in the midst of the Holy Spirit and the Savior and the Father; because the
nature of the intelligible and divine things, as we have said, is indivisible.
Or again, just as it is likewise not possible to find a division in the midst
of the orb and the ray, because of the impassible and incorporeal, the simple
and the undivided, but the ray is joined to the orb, and conversely the orb,
like some eye, pours the rays upon the whole like a river, working for us as
it were certain floods of light, and at once making a sea of the ordered
world: so also certain rays, as it were, of the Father have been sent upon us,
both the radiant Jesus and the Holy Spirit. For just as the rays of light,
having by nature an undivided relation toward one another, are neither
separated from the light nor cut off from one another, and send the grace of
the light even as far as us: in the same manner also our Savior and the Holy
Spirit, the twin ray of the Father, both minister the light of truth even as
far as us, and are united to the Father. And just as from some spring of
waters that sends forth nectareous water unstintingly, it happens that a very
abundant stream, an uncheckable current, is divided in its flow into two
rivers, having from the beginning one flow from one eye =46.1108= of the
spring; and when the rivers have been formed two-streamed in their
appearances, yet nothing has been harmed in the essence from the division (for
by the position of the rivers the current is divided, but it possesses one and
the same quality of the liquid; for even if each of the aforementioned rivers
seems to be terminated far off somewhere, and to stand far from the spring,
still it has its beginning united continuously to the mother-spring):
similarly, then, also the God of all good things, the president of truth, and
Father of the Savior, the first cause of life, and the plant of immortality,
the spring of everlasting life, having sent toward us a two-streamed
intelligible grace of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, has himself suffered
nothing, harmed in his essence (for he underwent no diminution through their
arrival to us), and they have come even as far as us, and are no less
inseparable from the Father. For undivided, as we said from the beginning, is
the nature of the superior ones. Very many things, then, O most honorable one,
it was possible to find, even more than what has been said, toward a clear
demonstration of the most necessary union of Father and Son and Holy Spirit,
in what manner one must conceive it to be; but since it is easier for you and
those like you to learn the greater part from a few things, for this reason I
thought it right to halt the discourse concerning this contemplation here.
=46.1109=
Why, leaving off believing, do we turn the faith into a technical art, and
fulfill the desire of the common enemy? For the devil, the common destroyer of
humanity, having calculated that when calm reigns among us the things of piety
flourish, and when faith prevails, good life prevails, and we become friends
of the Creator, lest we come upon that from which he himself fell, see what he
has done, and how craftily he has rhapsodized the war against us. The
contriver of evils, wise in deceits, varied in plots, and rich in evil
devices, prepares certain men under a pretext of piety to campaign against the
faith, and through them has thrown upon all the pious an untimely
contentiousness and a soul-destroying war, and has set the members of the
Church campaigning against one another.
Let us then turn this very cleverness against his own head, and let us change
his gladness =46.1112= into pain and inconsolable mourning. Let us mourn the
peace which he hates. Let us abandon the wrangling and the weighing of the
words of the dogma. Let us cease from wanting to be teachers of the teachers.
Let us hate word-fighting unto the ruin of those who hear. Let us believe as
our fathers have handed down. We are not wiser than the fathers; we are not
more precise than the teachers. God has called us in peace, not in battle. As
we were called, so let us remain at the mystical table, at which we partake of
the heavenly things. Let us not become at the same time table-companions and
plotters against one another; not communicants here, and plotters outside. The
Lord was called firstborn from the Virgin, not because he summoned some second
of like kind, but because he is firstborn incomparably in relation to the sons
of men. For neither because the Apostle said concerning the Lord's
beginningless generation from the Father, Who is the firstborn of all
creation, did he thereby also write a brother of the Lord. Nor again did the
same, having called him firstborn from the dead, thereby introduce a
blood-kinsman of the Lord who similarly trampled Hades. But the Lord is
firstborn from the Father and from the Virgin and from the dead, that through
all things the unique dignity of the sonship may be known. For the Master's
birth is incomparable; for where the mother is a virgin, how is a birth to be
interpreted?
