Pref.1 This was the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian, the month
Xanthicus, which would be called April according to the Romans, in which, the
feast of the saving passion coming on, Flavianus was governing the nation of
the Palestinians, and letters were spread suddenly everywhere, commanding that
the churches be brought to the ground, and that the scriptures become unseen
by fire, and proclaiming that those who had obtained honor be dishonored, and
that those in households, if they persisted in the purpose of Christianity, be
deprived of freedom. Pref.2 And such was the force of the first writing
against us; and not long after, other letters having arrived, it was commanded
that all the presidents of the churches everywhere first be given over to
bonds, then later be forced by every device to sacrifice. 1.1 First therefore
of the martyrs in Palestine, Procopius, before he had experience of prison,
immediately from the first entrance, standing at the gubernatorial courts and
being commanded to sacrifice to the so-called gods, said that he knew one
only, to whom it was fitting to sacrifice as he himself wishes; and when he
was ordered to pour a libation even to the four emperors, having uttered a
word of those not pleasing to them, he is at once beheaded, having spoken that
poetic saying "it is not good that there be many lords; let there be one lord,
one king." 1.2 On the seventh of the month Daisius, which would be said among
the Romans the seventh before the Ides of June, on the fourth day of the week,
this first sign was accomplished at Caesarea of Palestine. 1.3 And after this
in the same city very many rulers of the local churches, having contended
eagerly with terrible outrages, displayed to those looking on a history of
great contests, but others, their soul having gone numb beforehand from
cowardice, failed so readily from the first assault, and of the rest each
passed through various kinds of tortures, now with countless scourges, now
with rackings and scrapings of the sides and intolerable bonds, by which it
happened that for some even the hands were paralyzed, 1.4 yet nevertheless
they bore the end that resulted according to the unspeakable judgments of God.
For one, others holding his two hands and bringing him to the altar and
throwing the polluted and accursed sacrifice against his right hand, was
released as if he had sacrificed; and another, not having touched at all, but
others having said that he had sacrificed, went away in silence; another being
carried half-dead was thrown as if already a corpse and was even released from
the bonds, being counted among those who had sacrificed; and another, crying
out and testifying that he was not complying, being struck on the mouth and
silenced by the many hands of those appointed for this, was thrust out with
violence, even if he had not sacrificed; 1.5 thus from every side it was of
great importance to them to seem to have accomplished it. Out of so many then
only Alphaeus and Zacchaeus were deemed worthy of the crown of the holy
martyrs; who after scourges and scrapings and harsh bonds and the pains after
these and other various examinations, having had their feet stretched a night
and a day under four points of the penal wood, on the seventeenth of the month
Dius, this among the Romans being the fifteenth before the Kalends of
December, having confessed only one God and only Christ as king, Jesus, as if
having uttered something blasphemous, had their heads cut off likewise as the
former martyr. 2.1 Worthy of memory also are the things accomplished
concerning Romanus at Antioch on the same day. For this man being a
Palestinian, a deacon and exorcist of the parish in Caesarea, having happened
to be there at the demolition of the churches, seeing many men together with
women and children approaching the idols in heaps and sacrificing

having seen it, having deemed the sight unbearable, with zeal of godliness he
goes to them and rebukes them crying out with a great voice; 2.2 and he
himself having been seized on account of the daring has been shown a most
noble martyr of the truth, if ever anyone was. For when the judge had
pronounced against him the death through fire, with a bright face and a most
eager disposition he received the sentence gladly and is led away; then he is
bound to the stake, and the material having been heaped upon him and those
about to kindle the pyre waiting for the emperor's additional decision as he
was present, "Where is my fire?" he cried; 2.3 and saying these things he is
recalled to the emperor, to be subjected to a newer punishment of the tongue;
having endured the cutting of which most manfully, he showed by all deeds that
a divine power stands by those who endure anything whatever of hardship for
piety, lightening the pains and strengthening the eagerness. Learning then of
the novelty of the punishment and not being dismayed, the noble man gladly put
forth his tongue, most eagerly offering it ready to those cutting it off; 2.4
after which punishment having been thrown into bonds and having suffered a
very long time there, at last when the imperial vicennalia arrived, freedom of
all everywhere in the prisons having been proclaimed according to a customary
gift, he alone, both feet stretched under five points, lying on the wood
itself, a noose having been thrown about him, was adorned with martyrdom as he
had also longed. 2.5 But this man, even if beyond the borders, yet being a
Palestinian, would be worthy to be numbered among the Palestinian martyrs.
These things were accomplished in this manner in the first year, the
persecution having been raised against the presidents of the church alone; 3.1
And when the second year had intervened and the war against us had been
intensified more vehemently, Urbanus then governing the province, imperial
letters having first arrived, in which by a general command all throughout the
cities were ordered to sacrifice and pour libations to the idols, Timothy in
Gaza, a city of Palestine, having endured countless tortures, after all of
them having been given over to a slow and gentle fire, having furnished a most
genuine proof of the genuineness of piety toward the divine through endurance
toward all things, carried off the crown of the sacred victors, the athletes
of godliness. And together with him Agapius and our Thecla, having displayed a
most noble stand, were condemned to the beasts for food. 3.2 Who that saw the
things after these did not wonder, or having learned by hearing was not
struck? For when the nations were holding a public feast and the customary
spectacles, along with the other things they were eager about there was much
talk that those just condemned to the beasts would also exhibit the contest.
3.3 As the rumor then was growing and increasing among all, six young men in
number, of whom one was Pontic by race, named Timolaus, and one from Tripolis
of Phoenicia was called Dionysius, and another of them a subdeacon of the
parish in Diospolis, and Romulus was the name for this one too, and besides
these two Egyptians, Paesis and Alexander, and another of the same name as
this one, Alexander of those from Gaza, binding their own hands first so as to
signify their extreme eagerness for martyrdom, run up to Urbanus as he was
about to go up to the hunt, confessing themselves Christians and showing
through their array against all terrible things that those who boast piety
toward the God of all have not cowered even at the attacks of the beasts. 3.4
Having immediately put both the ruler himself and those about him into no
ordinary astonishment, they are shut in prison, and after not many days two
others having been enrolled with them, one who even before them had already
contended in terrible and various tortures in other confessions, and Agapius
was his name too, and one who was serving them in their bodily

needs, and the name of this one too was Dionysius, all of them, having become
eight in number, on one day have their heads cut off again at Caesarea itself,
on the twenty-fourth day of the month Dystrus, which itself also happened to
be the ninth before the Kalends of April. 3.5 At this time a change of the
rulers, of the very highest of all and of the second after him, takes place
into private station, and public affairs begin to be sick for them; 3.6 and a
little later the empire of the Romans having split against itself, a truceless
war is raised among them, and the things of the split and of the tumults upon
it did not take settlement before peace concerning us was administered
throughout the whole inhabited world under the Roman empire. 3.7 For at the
same time this rose upon all like a light as if from a gloomy and most dark
night, and again the common things of the Roman hegemony were once more stable
and friendly and peaceful, recovering the goodwill toward one another from
their ancestors. But of these things we shall render the account more
completely at the fitting time, and now let us go on to the sequence of what
follows. 4.1 Maximinus Caesar, having come to the rule from the spot, as if
displaying to all the tokens of his inborn hatred of God and impiety, stripped
for the persecution against us more nobly than those before. 4.2 When no small
confusion was hanging over all, and some were being scattered in one place and
some in another and making it their care to flee the terrible thing, and a
harsh movement was occupying the whole, what word would suffice us for the
worthy narration of the divine love and the boldness of the confession toward
God of the blessed and truly innocent lamb, the martyr, I mean Apphianus, who
before the gates was set forth as a wondrous example of piety toward the only
God for all in Caesarea to behold? 4.3 He had not yet this twentieth year of
the age of the body. First then for the sake of Greek education (for he
happened to be of those very much overflowing in wealth according to the
world) having spent the greater time at Berytus, it is paradoxical even to say
how in such a city he became above the desires of youth and, corrupted in
character neither by the prime of the body nor by the companionship of the
young, he embraced temperance, conducting himself orderly and solemnly and
piously according to the word that belongs to Christianity and training his
own life; 4.4 and if we must, having made mention also of his fatherland,
adorn this too through the noble athlete of godliness who was brought forth
from it, we shall reasonably do this also. 4.5 If anyone then knows Gaga, a
city of Lycia not without note, from there the youth originated, and after the
return from the education at Berytus, his father carrying off the first places
of the fatherland, not being able to bear the association together with his
father and those belonging to the family, because living did not even seem to
them to be living according to the laws of godliness, but as if possessed by a
divine spirit and according to some natural, or rather inspired and true
philosophy, having thought more highly than the customary glory of life and
having spat upon the pleasures of bodies, having fled his own people secretly
and having turned to none of the daily expenses, by hope and faith toward God
he was led, being guided by the divine Spirit to the city of the Caesareans,
where the crown of the martyrdom of godliness had been prepared for him. 4.6
And having come to be together with us ourselves and having collected as much
as possible a perfect habit in the divine words and having prepared himself
most eagerly with fitting exercises, what end he has displayed, who again that
saw was not struck, 4.7 and who again having received it by hearing would not
justly wonder at the courage, the boldness, the stand, and before these the
daring and the undertaking itself, furnishing the tokens of a zeal of
godliness and of a spirit truly above man?

4.8 For when a second uprising against us had come about under Maximinus in
the third year of the persecution against us, and letters of the tyrant having
first gone about, that all simply in a body with care and zeal of the rulers
in the cities should sacrifice, and heralds throughout the whole city of the
Caesareans calling out men together with women and children to the houses of
the idols by gubernatorial command, and besides this chiliarchs calling each
by name from a register, and an unspeakable swell of evils being confused from
every side, the one declared, fearlessly, no one being privy to him of what
was about to be done, and having stolen away both us who were with him at home
and still the whole military company about the governor, approaches Urbanus as
he was pouring a libation, and taking hold of his right hand undismayed, he
immediately stopped him from sacrificing, and very much in the manner of
counsel with a certain divine bearing he exhorted him to cease from the error;
for it was not well, having left the one and only true God, to sacrifice to
idols and demons. 4.9 And this, as it seems, was undertaken by the youth, the
inspired power that was leading him on to this all but crying out through what
was happening that Christians, at least those who are truly such, fall so far
short of changing from the piety toward the God of all of which they were once
deemed worthy, that they not only stand above the threats and the punishments
upon them, but speak still more freely and with a noble and unshaken tongue
talk with free speech and, if it is possible, even exhort those persecuting
them, having changed from ignorance, to recognize the only God who is. =4.10=
Upon these things the one about whom the account is, immediately, as was
likely for him as upon such a daring, having been torn apart like wild beasts
by those about the governor and having endured most manfully countless blows
over the whole body, for the time is immediately given over to prison, =4.11=
where having been stretched a night and a day by both feet upon the torturing
wood, on the following day he is brought to the judge; then as he was forcing
him to sacrifice, he displays every endurance toward pains and dreadful
sufferings, having had his sides scraped not once nor twice but even more
often to the bones and the inward parts themselves, and having received so
many blows upon face and neck that, his face having swollen, he was no longer
recognized even by those who knew him well and accurately of old. =4.12= But
since he did not yield even toward such things, covering his two feet with
linens soaked in oil, the torturers kindled fire beneath by command; at which
what sort of sufferings the blessed one bore I think surpasses every word; at
any rate the fire came upon him melting the flesh and the bones, so that like
wax the moisture of the body being melted was poured and dripped. =4.13= But
not even yielding at these things, his opponents having already been defeated
and all but having given up toward his endurance above man, he is shut in
bonds again, and being brought to the judge again on the third day and having
confessed the same purpose, although he was already half-dead, he is given
over under the sea. =4.14= And the things that happened immediately after
these, being spoken, it is not unlikely will be disbelieved by those who have
not taken them in by sight, but us, although we know this accurately, speech
does not choose not to hand over the truth from every side to the account of
the history, since witnesses of what happened are, to speak simply, all who
inhabit Caesarea; at any rate no age was left out of this paradoxical
spectacle. =4.15= Immediately then as they seemed especially to throw that
truly sacred and thrice-blessed one into the middle of the open sea in
boundless depths, a sudden commotion not ordinary and a boiling spreads
through the sea itself and all the surrounding air, so that both the land and
the whole city were shaken by what happened, and together with this
paradoxical and sudden earthquake the

corpse of the divine martyr the sea, as if not able to bear him, casts up
before the gates of the city. And such were the things concerning the divine
Apphianus, perfected on the second of the month Xanthicus, which would be the
fourth before the Nones of April, on the day of Preparation; 5.1 and about the
same time on the very days at the city of the Tyrians a youth, Ulpianus by
name, after terrible outrages himself and most harsh scourges, having been
wrapped in a raw oxhide together with a dog and an asp, the venomous serpent,
is given over to the sea; wherefore he too seems to me reasonably to be
remembered by us in the martyrdoms of Apphianus. 5.2 And having endured things
akin to Apphianus a little later in time, the one who was not only his brother
according to God, and also by body of the same father, Aedesius, after
countless confessions and long-lasting evils of bonds and gubernatorial
sentences, in which he was given to the mines in Palestine, and after the
philosophic conduct in the form of a cloak in all these (for he indeed
possessed more than the education of his brother, and started from philosophic
studies), ending then at the city of the Alexandrians, 5.3 having seen the
judge there judging Christians and insulting beyond what was fitting, and now
variously abusing solemn men, now giving over to brothel-keepers women of the
highest temperance and self-virgin ascetics for shameful outrages, having
undertaken the same as his brother, because the things happening seemed to him
unbearable, he approaches with a bold bearing, and having wrapped the judge in
shame and dishonor by words and deeds, and upon these having endured most
steadfastly manifold outrages of tortures, he carried off the brotherly end,
having been given over to the sea. But the things concerning this man, as I
said, happened in this manner a little later in time; 6.1 In the fourth year
then of the persecution against us, twelve days before the Kalends of
December, which would be the twentieth of the month Dius, on the day before
the Sabbath, at the same Caesarea, something truly worthy of writing was
accomplished, the tyrant Maximinus himself being present and providing
spectacles to the crowds on the so-called birthday of his, such a thing. 6.2
It having been a custom formerly under emperors, if also at other times, that
the ambitious spectacles furnish more delights to the spectators of new and
strange spectacles that also change the customary ones, animals sometimes
being brought in from India or Ethiopia or even from elsewhere, or even men by
certain skilled bodily exercises displaying paradoxical entertainments to
those seeing, altogether then too, as of an emperor providing the spectacles,
something more and paradoxical had to belong to the ambitious displays. 6.3
What then was this? A martyr of our doctrine was brought into the midst,
contending for the only and true piety; this was Agapius, the second who a
little before was shown as given with Thecla to the beasts for food; who also
at other times having been led in procession in the stadium from the prison a
third time and many times together with criminals, the judge always at times
after the threats deferring him to other contests either by pity or by hope
that he would change from his purpose, then with the emperor present he was
led, as if having been kept on purpose for that time, so that that saving word
also, which he had foretold to the disciples by divine knowledge, that they
would be brought even before kings for the sake of the witness to him, might
be fulfilled even upon him. 6.4 He is then brought into the midst of the
stadium together with a certain criminal, and they said this one was involved
in the murder of his master. 6.5 Then the murderer of his master, having been
thrown to the beasts, was deemed worthy of pity and humanity all but according
to that Barabbas himself at the time of the Saviour, and the whole theater
rang with shouts and acclamations at this, as if the blood-guilty one had been
saved humanely by the emperor and deemed worthy of honor and freedom; 6.6 but
the

athlete of godliness is first called up by the tyrant, then having been asked
for a denial of his purpose upon a promise of freedom, he testifies with a
great voice that not for the sake of a base cause, but of piety toward the
creator of all, he will eagerly and with pleasure nobly withstand whatever
might be brought upon him, 6.7 and having said this, he adds the deed together
with the word, having run straight to meet a bear that had been released
against him and having given himself to it most gladly for food; after which
he is taken up still breathing into the prison, and having lived one day
there, on the next he is sunk in the middle of the open sea, stones having
been fastened to his feet. Such also was the martyrdom of Agapius. 7.1 And
already the persecution having been extended even into the fifth year, on the
second of the month Xanthicus, which is the fourth before the Nones of April,
on the very Lord's day of the resurrection of our Saviour, again at Caesarea
Theodosia, a virgin of those from Tyre, a faithful and most solemn maiden of
not even a full eighteen years, approaches certain prisoners who themselves
also were confessing the kingdom of Christ and sitting before the court, at
once greeting them kindly and, as was likely, exhorting them to remember her
toward the Lord when they came to be. 7.2 And having done this, soldiers
seizing her as if she had done some unholy and impious thing bring her to the
governor, and immediately this one, as being some madman and most beastlike in
anger, having outraged her with terrible and most dreadful tortures upon sides
and breasts even to the bones themselves, still breathing, yet standing toward
all things with a rejoicing and bright face, he commands to be thrown into the
waves of the sea. Then passing from her to the remaining confessors, he gives
all over to the copper mines at Phaeno of Palestine. 7.3 Upon these, on the
fifth of the month Dius, and according to the Romans on the Nones of November,
in the same city those about Silvanus, who was still then a presbyter who had
confessed, who not long later happened both to be honored with the episcopate
and to be perfected by martyrdom, having displayed a most noble stand for
piety, the same one judges to the labors of the same copper mine, having first
commanded that the tendons of their feet be disabled by cauteries; 7.4 and
together with the sentence against these a man who had been distinguished in
countless other confessions, this was Domninus, who through excess of freedom
was most known to all in Palestine, he gives over to the punishment through
fire; after whom the same judge, being some terrible inventor of evil and a
novelty-maker of attempts against the teaching of Christ, was devising
punishments never even heard of against the godly, and three he condemns to
single combat for boxing, and Auxentius, a solemn and sacred old man, he gives
over as food to beasts, and others again, bearing the age of complete men,
having cut them into eunuchs he condemns to the same mines, and others again
after harsh tortures he shuts in prison; among whom was also Pamphilus, to me
at least the most longed-for of all companions, a man most renowned of the
martyrs among us for every virtue. 7.5 Urbanus having first taken a trial of
this man in rhetorical words and philosophic studies, then later having forced
him to sacrifice, when he saw him refusing and not putting the threats in
account at all, at the very last having grown savage, he commands him to be
outraged with more vehement tortures; 7.6 and having all but been filled with
those through persistent and contentious scrapers upon his sides, the most
beastlike one, yet having poured shame upon himself in the presence of all,
enrolls him also among the confessors in the prison. 7.7 And of what sort of
requital he will obtain from the divine justice for the cruelty against the
saints, having so insulted against the martyrs of Christ, it is easy to
discern from the preludes here, through which immediately and not long after
for the things against Pamphilus

having been dared the divine justice visited him still holding the
governorship, having so suddenly stripped him who yesterday was judging
somewhere above on a high tribunal and was escorted by the military company
and was ruling the whole nation of the Palestinians and had been established
as a table-companion and as a most dear messmate with the tyrant himself,
through one night, and having set him deserted of so many dignities, and
having poured dishonor and shame upon those who formerly gazed at him as
ruler, and having displayed him a coward and unmanly and sending forth
womanish voices and supplications to the whole nation which he also ruled, and
having set Maximinus himself, in whom formerly he was exulting and swaggering
as if he especially loved him because of the things done by him against us, as
a harsh and most cruel judge upon him at Caesarea itself, so as even to bring
out the vote of death against him after much shame at the enormities of which
he was convicted. 7.8 But let this be said by us as a by-work of the way; and
there would be a fitting time at which the account at leisure will include the
ends of the impious who especially campaigned against us, of Maximinus himself
and of those about him, and the catastrophes of their lives. 8.1 And into the
sixth year too the winter against us having blown persistently, the mine in
the Thebaid called by the name of the porphyry stone which it generates had
before this a very great multitude of the confessors of godliness; from whom
men a hundred in number lacking three, together with women and quite infants,
are sent to the governor of Palestine; whom at the ** of the Jews having
confessed the God of all and the Christ, Firmilianus, the governor sent here
as successor to Urbanus, commands as if from an imperial nod that the tendons
of their left feet be cut off with the sinews by cauteries, and again that the
right eyes first be knocked out with swords together with the lids and pupils,
then be wholly disabled to the roots themselves by fire through cauteries; and
then he gives them over to the mines in the province to be worn out for the
sake of toil and suffering. 8.2 Not only these who suffered such things was it
enough to take in with the eyes, but also those of the Palestinians who a
little before were shown as having been condemned to single combats for
boxing, since they endured neither the foods from the imperial treasury nor
the exercises suitable for boxing, 8.3 already having stood not only before
procurators nor governors, but even before Maximinus himself for this very
reason, they displayed a most noble stand of confession through endurance of
hunger and patience of scourges, having suffered the like to those declared,
with other confessors added to them at Caesarea itself; 8.4 at whose heels
others having been caught at the assembling for the divine readings at the
city of the Gazaeans, some endured the same sufferings as these upon the feet
and the eyes, and some even greater through the most fearful tortures they
experienced upon the sides; 8.5 from whom one, a woman in body but male in
reasoning, not bearing a threat of fornication, having spoken a word against
the tyrant as having entrusted the rule to such cruel judges, is first
scourged, then being raised upon the wood she is outraged in the sides. 8.6
And as those appointed for this were applying the tortures persistently and
vehemently by command of the judge, beyond those fighters among the Greeks
celebrated for freedom, another, not bearing the pitiless and cruel and
inhuman of the things being done, similarly to the former having taken up the
labor of virginity, very cheap in body to appearance and contemptible in look,
but otherwise stout in soul and having taken to her breast a reasoning greater
than the body, "And until when," from the midst of the crowd, "do you so
cruelly torture my sister?" she cried out to the judge. And he more bitterly

having been stirred, immediately commands the woman to be seized; 8.7 then she
is dragged into the midst, and having inscribed upon herself the revered name
of the Saviour, first she was being persuaded by words to sacrifice, and as
she was not obeying, she was being dragged by force to the altar. And she,
doing things akin to herself and holding to the eagerness of the former, with
a still and bold foot she plants a kick on the altar, and overturns the things
upon it together with the pyre laid on it; 8.8 at which the judge, having
sharpened his anger like a wild beast, first lays upon her so many outrages
upon the sides as upon none of those ever, all but longing even to be filled
with her raw flesh; and when the things of the madness were already taking
satiety for him, having yoked both, this one herself together with the one
addressed as sister by her, he votes the death through fire upon them. Of
these the former was said to be of the country of the Gazaeans, and the other
one must know originated from that of the Caesareans, the one known to many,
Valentina by name. 8.9 And how would I go through according to worth the
martyrdom after this, of which the thrice-blessed Paul was deemed worthy,
having been judged at the same hour as these under one sentence to death, and
at the very perfection having entreated the one about to cut him off, as much
as not yet, to grant him a little of an hour; =8.10= having obtained which,
with a bright and loud voice he first was administering through prayers to God
the reconciliations on behalf of his fellow-nationals, beseeching that freedom
be granted them as quickly as possible, then he asked on behalf of the
bringing of the Jews to God through Christ, then next he was coming down in
the word praying the same also for Samaritans, and he was calling those of the
nations who were in error and ignorance of God to come to knowledge of him and
to take up the truly piety, not leaving even those then standing about in
mixed crowd uncared for; =8.11= after all whom, O the much and unspeakable
forbearance, even on behalf of the judge who had sentenced death upon him and
of the rulers over all and still even of the one as much as not yet about to
cut off his head, within hearing of that one himself and of all present, he
was asking the God of all, exhorting that the sin against him in no way come
to be in number for them. =8.12= Having prayed these and such things with a
great voice and all but having drawn all to pity and tears, as if being slain
unjustly, having arranged himself as the law and having given his neck bare to
the cutting of the sword, he was adorned with divine martyrdom, on the
twenty-fifth of the month Panemus, which would be said the eighth before the
Kalends of August. And the things concerning these had such an end; =8.13= and
no long time having run by, again from the land of the Egyptians the wondrous
athletes of the confession toward Christ, a hundred and thirty, having
undergone by command of Maximinus the same disasters as those formerly in
Egypt itself upon the eyes and the feet, are sent to the mines in Palestine
that have been declared, and some to those condemned in Cilicia. 9.1 Upon such
deeds of the magnificent martyrs of Christ, the conflagration of the
persecution having slackened and as if being quenched by their sacred blood,
and relaxation and freedom already having been granted to those being worn out
in the Thebaid at the mines there for Christ, and we being about a little to
breathe a pure air, I know not how from some stirring again from the beginning
the one who had obtained the authority to persecute was kindled against
Christians. 9.2 Suddenly then again letters of Maximinus against us go about
everywhere, and the governors in the province and besides the one appointed to
rule the camps were urging by proclamations and letters and public ordinances
the curators in all cities together with generals and tabularii to bring the
imperial command to fulfillment, ordering that with all haste they should
rebuild the fallen things of the idol-temples, and that all in a body, men
together with women and

their care to sacrifice and pour libations and to taste accurately of their
accursed sacrifices, and that the wares in the market be polluted with the
libations from the sacrifices, and that watchers be stationed before the
baths, so that they might pollute those cleansing themselves in these with the
all-defiled sacrifices. 9.3 These things then being so accomplished, and of
our people again being held by the greatest care, as was likely, and the
unbelieving nations blaming the heaviness of the things happening and as if
already the excess of the enormity (for these things appeared even to them to
be cloying and burdensome), and a very great winter hanging over all
everywhere, conversely the divine power of our Saviour was breathing such
courage into its athletes that, no one even drawing or dragging, they trampled
the threat of such things. 9.4 Three then of the faithful having arrayed
themselves together rush upon the ruler as he was sacrificing to idols, crying
out to him to cease from the error; for there is no other God except the maker
and creator of all. And being asked then who they were, they confessed
themselves Christians boldly; 9.5 at which Firmilianus, having been stirred
more sharply, not even having outraged them with tortures, gives them over to
capital punishment. Of these the elder was named Antoninus, and the other was
called Zebinas, originating from that of the Eleutheropolitans, and Germanus
was the name for the third also. On the thirteenth of the month Dius, the Ides
of November, the things concerning these also were done. 9.6 And there becomes
a fellow-traveler to them on the same day Ennathas, a woman of those from
Scythopolis, herself also adorned with the crown of virginity, not having done
the same as they, 9.7 but having been dragged by force and brought to the
judge after scourges and terrible outrages, which one of the chiliarchs
stationed in the neighborhood dared to bring upon her not even with the
judgment of the greater authority, Maxys by name, a man worse than the name,
polluted in other things, but exceedingly strong in character and in the whole
manner some terrible one truly and slandered among all who knew him. This man
at least, having stripped the blessed woman of all clothing, so as to be
covered only from the loin and to the feet, and to have the rest of the body
naked, and having led her around the whole city of the Caesareans in a circle,
makes it of much account that she be beaten with thongs, being dragged through
all the markets. 9.8 And after such things, having displayed a most bold stand
even upon the gubernatorial tribunals themselves, the judge gives her over
alive to fire; who also having stretched the rage against the godly toward the
inhuman, went beyond the laws of nature, not even respecting to grudge burial
to the lifeless bodies of the sacred men. 9.9 By night then carefully and by
day he commands the corpses to be guarded in the open air for food to beasts,
and it was possible to see for more days no small number of men serving this
beastlike and barbarian counsel; but some from a distance, as some thing
worthy of zeal, were watching so that the corpses might not be stolen, and
wild beasts and dogs and the flesh-eating of birds were tearing the human
limbs here and there, and =9.10= the whole city in a circle at least was being
strewn with human inward parts and bones, so that nothing more terrible ever,
nor more shuddering, appeared even to those who formerly were hostile toward
us, lamenting not so much the disaster toward those upon whom these things
were done, as at the outrage of themselves and of the common nature of all.
=9.11= For there was set forth nearest the gates a spectacle greater than
every word and tragic hearing, the human fleshes being eaten not in one place,
but being thrown about in every place; at any rate some have said they saw
whole limbs and fleshes and certain parts of inward parts even within the
gates; =9.12= upon which things being accomplished for very many days such a
paradoxical thing happens. It was clear weather and bright air and a most calm
condition of the surrounding; then suddenly of the

to all that the earth wept by an unspeakable word, not bearing the unholy work
of the things then done, and that to the refutation of unfeeling and
unsympathetic nature of men stones and the lifeless matter lamented over what
had happened. =9.13= Nonsense perhaps and a myth I well know the saying will
seem to be to those after us, but not to those for whom the time confirmed the
truth. 10.1 And of the following month Apellaeus on the fourteenth, it would
be said the nineteenth before the Kalends of January, again some of them from
Egypt happened to be seized by those searching those passing at the gates (and
these had set out for the sake of care of the confessors in Cilicia), some
accepted the same vote as those whom they happened to be about to serve,
having been disabled in eyes and feet, and three of them at Ascalon, where
they were also held, having furnished a wondrous presence of courage, carried
off a different end of martyrdom, one of them having been given over to fire,
Ares by name, and the others having had their heads cut off; Promus and Elias
was the name for these. 10.2 And on the eleventh day of the month Audynaeus,
and it would be the third before the Ides of January, at the same Caesarea
Peter an ascetic, the one also Apselamus, from the village Anea of the borders
of Eleutheropolis, through fire, like gold most unmixed, of the faith toward
the Christ of God by noble reasoning has given the proof, having overlooked
the judge and those about him entreating countless times that he would pity
himself and spare his own youth and prime, and having preferred before all
things and life itself the hope toward the God of all. 10.3 And with this one
a certain Asclepius seeming to be a bishop of the error according to Marcion,
by zeal, as he thought, of piety, but not at all of that according to
knowledge, yet nevertheless by one and the same pyre has gone out of life. But
these things thus; 11.1 Time then calls to recount the great and renowned
theater of those perfected about the name most longed-for by me, of Pamphilus,
and they were twelve in all, deemed worthy of some prophetic or even apostolic
gift and number; 11.2 of whom the chief and the only one adorned with the
honor of the presbyterate at Caesarea was Pamphilus, a man who even throughout
his whole life was distinguished in every virtue, by renunciation and contempt
of life, by sharing of substance toward the needy, by little-care of worldly
hopes, by philosophic conduct and exercise; and especially beyond all among us
he was distinguished by the most genuine zeal about the divine oracles and by
unwearied industry about what he proposed and by the benefit toward those
belonging and all who approached him; 11.3 of whose remaining accomplishments
of virtue, being of a longer narration, we have already formerly handed over
in three memoirs on a writing of the subject of his life of its own. But
having sent those who are ambitious to know these things too to those, let us
now hold to the sequence according to the martyrs. 11.4 Second after Pamphilus
there was coming to the contest, honored with a sacred-looking grayness,
Valens, a deacon of those from Aelia, a most solemn old man even by look, if
ever anyone else knowing of the divine scriptures; so many memories of them at
least he had taken to his breast that he did not lack the reading from letters
of whatever scripture he might take, of such expositions as he recited from
memory. 11.5 Third the most fervent and boiling in the spirit from the city of
the Jamnites was recognized among them, Paul, who before the martyrdom through
endurance of cauteries had contended the contest of the confession. For these
who had worn away a time of two whole years in the prison the occasion of the
martyrdom becomes an arrival again of Egyptian brothers of those also
perfected with them. 11.6 those

in Cilicia these having escorted the confessors as far as the mines there,
were returning to their own. Likewise then they themselves also at the very
entries of the gates at Caesarea, who they were and from where arriving,
having been asked by the guards (and these were some barbarian in manner) and
having hidden nothing of the truth, as criminals taken in the act, they were
being held; and they were five in number; 11.7 who also having been brought to
the tyrant and having spoken freely upon this one, are immediately shut in
prison; and on the next day, the sixteenth day of the month Peritius,
according to the Romans of March the fourteenth before the Kalends, by command
they bring these themselves together with those declared about Pamphilus to
the judge; 11.8 who also first takes a trial of the unconquerable stand of the
Egyptians with all kinds of tortures and with inventions of strange and
various machines. Having trained the spokesman of all in these contests, he
first asked who he was, then having heard instead of the proper name some
prophetic name (and this was also happening toward all, instead of the
idolatrous names that had been named upon them from the fathers, if it
happened, having transferred to themselves the appellations. You would hear at
any rate of them inscribing themselves Elijah and Jeremiah and Isaiah and
Samuel and Daniel and showing the Jew in secret and genuine and purely Israel
of God not only by deeds but by voices properly brought forth) having heard
then some such name from the martyr, Firmilianus, yet not having attended to
the force of the saying, asked second what his fatherland might be; 11.9 and
he sends forth a second voice in accord with the former, saying that Jerusalem
was his own fatherland, thinking that one indeed about which it has been said
by Paul but the Jerusalem above is free, which is our mother and you have come
to Mount Zion and the city of the living God, heavenly Jerusalem. =11.10= And
he was thinking this; but the other, having thrown his thought upon earth and
on the ground, was inquiring accurately what this was and where on earth it
lay, then he also applied tortures, so that he would confess the truth. And
he, being racked with the two hands behind and being broken in the two feet by
certain strange engines, was affirming to speak the truth. =11.11= Then again
as he asked many times who he was and where lay the city which he names, he
said this was a fatherland of the godly alone; for no others or these alone
have a share of it, and it lies toward the east itself and toward the rising
sun. =11.12= And he again through these was philosophizing according to his
own mind, making no turning toward those around who were outraging him with
tortures, and as if fleshless and bodiless not even seeming to perceive the
sufferings; but the other being at a loss was writhing, thinking that
Christians had altogether established a city hostile and at war with Romans,
and he was much in searching this out and examining the declared country
toward the east. =11.13= And when having scraped the youth still more with
scourges and having punished him with all kinds of tortures he recognized the
stand of the things formerly said by him as unchanged, he brings out the
capital vote of death against him. Such dramaturgy then the things concerning
this one had obtained; and having trained the rest too in the similar contests
he dispatches them in the like manner. =11.14= Then having grown weary and
having discerned that he was punishing the men in vain, having taken satiety
of desire, he passes to those about Pamphilus, and having been taught that
already even formerly through tortures they displayed an unchangeable
eagerness for the faith, having asked if then even now they would still obey,
and having received from each one only the last voice of their confession
according to the martyrdom, he brings upon them the same punishment as the
former. =11.15= These having been brought to an end, a youth belonging to the
household service of Pamphilus, as having been trained together by genuine
rearing and education of so great a man,

when he learned the vote against the master, cries out from the midst of the
multitude, asking that the bodies be given over to earth. =11.16= And the
other not a man but a beast and if anything more savage than a beast, neither
having accepted the reasonableness of the request nor having assigned pardon
to the young one of the age, only as having asked he learned that he confessed
himself a Christian, as if wounded by some arrow, having swollen in anger, he
commands the torturers to use against him with all power; =11.17= and when he
saw him refusing as he was bidding him to sacrifice, he commands him to be
scraped persistently no longer as fleshes of a man, but either stones or woods
or some of the other lifeless things, to the bones themselves and the inward
parts in the depth and in the recesses. And this happening for a long time, he
discerned that he was attempting in vain, the body being worn down by the
tortures for him voiceless and unfeeling, and little short of altogether
lifeless; =11.18= and having acquired a persistent pitilessness and
inhumanity, immediately as he was he pronounces that he be given over to a
long fire. And this one before the perfection of the master according to the
flesh, having come last to the contest, anticipated the release from the body,
those who were busy about the former still delaying; =11.19= and it was then
possible to see Porphyrius in the disposition of a sacred victor who has won
in the pancratium, dusted as to the body, but bright as to the face, walking
with bold thought and proud after such things to the death, and truly full of
a divine spirit, and clothed in a philosophic form with only the wrap about
him in the manner of an exomis, and with sober reasoning enjoining upon his
acquaintances concerning what he wished and nodding, and still keeping the
face bright even upon the stake itself, but even when the pyre had been
kindled outside from a long distance in a circle about him, snatching the
flame with the mouth from here and there and most nobly enduring in silence to
the last breath, and after one voice which he broke forth at the same time as
the flame fastened upon him, calling upon the son of God Jesus as helper.
=11.20= Such also was the contest of Porphyrius; and Seleucus having become a
messenger of the perfection according to him to Pamphilus, a confessor of
those from military service, as a servant of so great a message, is deemed
worthy of the lot together with them immediately. For immediately some
soldiers, taking hold of him as he announced the end of Porphyrius and greeted
one of the martyrs with a kiss, bring him to the governor; and he, as if
hastening him to become a fellow-traveler of the former of the journey into
the heavens, immediately commands him to be punished with capital punishment.
=11.21= This one was from the land of the Cappadocians, and of the chosen
youth in military services and having obtained no small honor among Roman
dignities; for in age and strength of body and in size and might he surpassed
the fellow-soldiers by very much, so that even the look was renowned to all
for him and the whole form admirable for the sake of size and beauty. =11.22=
At the beginnings then of the persecution having been distinguished through
endurance of scourges in the contests according to the confession, and after
the release from military service having established himself a zealot of the
ascetics of godliness, of orphaned desolates and unsupported widows and of
those cast aside in poverties and weaknesses he has been shown as a bishop as
it were and a helper in the manner of a father and guardian; whence reasonably
by the God who rejoices in such things more than the sacrifices through smoke
and blood he was deemed worthy of the paradoxical calling according to the
martyrdom. =11.23= This one as tenth athlete upon those declared was perfected
on one and the same day, on which, as it seems, a very great gate having been
opened for the martyrdom of Pamphilus worthily of the man, the passage of the
entrance into the kingdom of the heavens became easy together with him also
for others. =11.24= In the tracks then of Seleucus, Theodulus, a certain
solemn and godly old man, of the

gubernatorial household happening to be and having been honored by Firmilianus
more than all those in the house, this on the one hand for the sake of the age
and by his having been established a father of three generations, this on the
other through the goodwill which he was keeping about them and most faithful
conscience, having done the similar to Seleucus, having been brought to the
master and having sharpened him more than the former, accepted the same
martyrdom of the saving passion, having been given over to a cross. =11.25=
Upon these one still remaining, who would fill up the twelfth number for the
martyrs being declared, Julianus was present to fill this up. Having arrived
from a sojourn at any rate immediately and not even having entered the city
yet, immediately as he was from the road, having learned and having rushed
upon the spectacle of the martyrs, when he saw the tabernacles of the saints
upon the earth on the ground, having become full of joy, having embraced each,
he was greeting all. =11.26= As he was doing this the servants of the murders
seizing him again bring him to Firmilianus, and accomplishing things
consistent with himself he gives this one too over to a long fire. Thus then
Julianus also, leaping and jumping over and with a great voice giving
exceeding thanks to the Lord who deemed him worthy of such things, was deemed
worthy of the crown of the martyrs. =11.27= And this one too was by race
according to the flesh of the Cappadocians, and in manner most reverent and
most faithful and most genuine and zealous in all other things and breathing
of holy spirit itself. Such was the company of the band of those deemed worthy
to enter together with Pamphilus upon the martyrdom. =11.28= The sacred and
truly holy bodies of these by command of the impious governor were being kept
for four days and so many nights for food to the flesh-eaters; and when
paradoxically nothing, not a beast, not a bird, not a dog, approached them,
again by a dispensation of the providence of God having been taken unharmed
and having obtained the fitting care, they were given over to the customary
burial. =11.29= And while the movement concerning these was still being spoken
in the mouth of all, Adrianus and Eubulus from the country so called Batanea
having arrived at Caesarea as the remaining confessors, were themselves also
examined at the gate through the cause for which they had come; then having
confessed the truth, they are brought to Firmilianus. And he as he was, again
having deferred nothing, after very many tortures which he laid upon their
sides, condemns them as food of beasts. =11.30= Two days then having passed
between, Adrianus on the fifth of the month Dystrus, the third before the
Nones of March, on the birthday of the Tyche thought to belong to Caesarea,
having been thrown to a lion and after this slaughtered with a sword was
perfected; and Eubulus after another intervening day, on the Nones themselves,
which would be the seventh of Dystrus, the judge having entreated him much
that having sacrificed he might obtain the freedom thought among them, having
preferred the glorious death for piety to the temporary life, having become a
sacrifice after the beasts likewise as the former, last of the martyrs at
Caesarea sealed the contests. =11.31= And it is still worthy to remember here
of the account that not long after the heavenly providence having visited the
impious rulers with the tyrants themselves, the one who so insulted against
the martyrs of Christ, Firmilianus himself, having endured a last punishment
with the others, ends his life by the sword. And the martyrdoms accomplished
at Caesarea throughout all the times of the persecution were such; 12.1 And as
many things as after these happened to be accomplished in the time between
both concerning those set over the churches, how instead of shepherds of the
rational sheep of Christ, of whom they were not set over lawfully, the divine
justice having condemned them as worthy of these set them over as caretakers
of camels, an irrational animal and most crooked by the nature of the body,
and how it condemned them as bound to the presenting of imperial horses, and
as many things as of the sacred vessels and for the sake of the ecclesiastical

from the

the many upon these and the indiscriminate and unlawful ordinations and the
schisms among the confessors themselves, and as many things as the new

newer innovations upon new and unsparingly rubbing upon the disasters of the

this I think I shall adorn the outline of the whole account. The seventh year
of the contest against us was being completed, and somehow quietly the absence

come through into an eighth year, about the copper mines in Palestine no small
multitude of confessors having been assembled and using much boldness, so as
even to have built houses into churches, the ruler of the province, being some
terrible and wicked one and such as the things done against the martyrs
established him, having arrived there and having learned the conduct of those
there, communicates to the emperor, writing as it seemed for slander, 13.2
then the one appointed over the mines having come, as if from an imperial nod
having divided the multitude of the confessors, to some he assigned to inhabit
Cyprus, to some Lebanon, and having scattered others in other countries in
Palestine, he commands all to toil at various certain works. 13.3 Then having
selected four who especially seemed to be chiefs of them he sends them to the

and another presbyter, and upon these Patermouthius most known to all through
the zeal about all; whom the camp-commander, having asked a denial of
godliness and not having obtained it, gives over to the perfection through

through old age or through maimings or other weaknesses of bodies had been
released from the service in the works; of whom there was leading, originating

first day of the persecution and through the whole time having been
distinguished in all kinds of contests of confessions, had been kept to that
time, so that he might become last a sealing of the whole contest in
Palestine. 13.6 And with this one there were also more of those from Egypt,
among whom was also John, who in the virtue about memories surpassed all among
us. This one then had been deprived of the sights even formerly, yet even upon
the confessions in which he was distinguished, similarly to the others having
been made unseen as to the foot by cauteries, he had received the same cautery

the cruel and inhuman of the manner toward the pitiless and unsympathetic.
13.7 Such then being he, what would one wonder at of character and of a
philosophic life, not having appeared so paradoxical as of the virtue in
memories, whole books of the divine scriptures not on tablets of stone as the
divine apostle says, nor even on skins of animals or papers being destroyed by
moths and time, but on tablets truly of a fleshy heart and inscribed in a
translucent soul and a most pure eye of understanding, so as to bring forth,
whenever he also wished, through the mouth as if from some treasury of words
now a legal and prophetic scripture, now a historical, and at another time a
gospel and apostolic. 13.8

I myself confess I was once struck, having first seen the man standing in the
midst of a sufficient multitude of a church and going through certain parts of
divine scripture. For so long as it was possible for me to listen to voice
alone, I thought that someone was reading, as is custom in the assemblies; but
when having come nearest I understood what was being done, all the others
standing around in a circle with healthy eyes, and this one using only those
of the understanding and simply uttering as some prophet and surpassing by far
those sound in the body, I was not even able somehow to glorify God and to

persuasion, that in truth the only man is not the one thought in the apparent

maimed displayed a greater virtue of the power according to himself. 13.9 But
those declared spending time according to the place that had been set apart
and accomplishing the customary things in fastings and prayers and the
remaining exercises, God, God himself deemed them worthy to obtain the saving
perfection, furnishing them a right hand that hears, but the enemy hostile
one, no longer being able to bear them as being armed most at leisure against
him through the prayers toward God, thought to kill and to remove from earth
as if they were troubling. =13.10= And God granted even this to him as he was
attempting to do it, so that at once he himself might not be barred from the
wickedness according to his choice and those might at last receive the prizes
of the manifold contests. In this way then forty lacking one in number by a
nod of the all-unholy Maximinus on one day have their heads cut off. =13.11=
These then were the martyrdoms in Palestine brought to an end in eight whole
years, and such was the persecution in our time, having begun from the
demolition of the churches, and having advanced to a great point in the
uprisings of the rulers according to times, in which many-mannered and
many-formed contests of those who had contended for piety established some
unnumbered multitude of martyrs in every province in those stretching from
Libya and through all Egypt and Syria and those from the east and in a circle
as far as the regions toward Illyricum. =13.12= For the things beyond those
declared, all Italy and Sicily and Gaul and as many as toward the setting sun
upon Spain and Mauretania and Africa, not having endured the war for even two
whole first years of the persecution, were deemed worthy of a most swift
visitation of God and of peace, the heavenly providence making sparing of the
simplicity and faith of the men. =13.13= What at any rate has not even been
recorded in the things from of old of the Roman empire, this now first in our
time has happened beyond all hope; for at the persecution against us the
things of the empire are divided in two, and the brothers inhabiting in the
one part, the one declared before, enjoy peace, of those throughout the
remaining inhabited world having endured countless upon countless contests.
=13.14= But when the divine and heavenly grace was displaying also the
visitation concerning us as favorable and gracious, then indeed also the
rulers concerning us, those very ones through whom of old the things of the
wars against us were being effected, changing by a most paradoxical judgment
were singing a palinode, quenching the conflagration against us with good
proclamations concerning us and gentle ordinances; and the the palinode too
must be written down.
