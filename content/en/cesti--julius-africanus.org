things, so that the beautiful may not appear pure: one man is eloquent, but
not solemn; and another commands as general, but is not temperate; and someone
is brave, but a braggart did not keep his beauty unstained for himself; and
another in another way both has the advantage and goes wrong. Like also are
the fair and the base things in horses: swift, but amorous; hunters, but
hard-mouthed; walkers, but insolent; some do not receive their riders, others
shake them off; some rub against walls or plants; some are vicious from
nature, others have become so by their owners, they bite and kick and bolt and
rear, either hating or being arrogant toward their masters. And I can name
many, one and another destroyed in various ways by horses with one disaster
and form of evils. The arts of horse breakers, as far as they can, either
check or correct the vices, by little food the insolent, by gelding the
lovers, by muzzles those that bite, the hard-mouthed with wolves, the
disobedient with blows. But what would one do against the one that has bolted
and has chosen to obey in no way toward nothing, neither of commands nor of
lessons? For just as the wildest of beasts, when taken full grown, are not
tamed, but, even if they seem for a little to have become tractable,
nevertheless do not forget their former savagery, so also this creature is
hard to turn, when vice has grown old in it. Since then blow and threat and
art and feeding are weak toward such things, let vice of nature be corrected
by art of nature. Let writing also tame him, which he will not fear, which he
will not suspect, which wearing he will be mastered. In the hollow of the hoof

find the breadth of a river and <the height> of a wall Those moderately
possessed of the encyclical education have, as is likely, touched the Elements
of Euclid to a certain amount. It is not then hard through the first to
establish these also: to measure out the breadth of a river, the other bank
being impassable because of the enemies stationed on it, toward bringing on a
proportionate yoke to bridge, and by the same ratio to take the height of a
wall from a distance toward bringing on the city taking machines of equal
standing. And toward easy learning of the demonstration this theorem will
lead: "If of a right-angled triangle one of the <sides> about the right angle
is cut in two, and from the cut a <straight line> is raised at right angles,
and through the point at which it cuts the remaining side a parallel is drawn,
the remaining


sides of the triangle also are cut in two." For let there be a right-angled
triangle ABG, having the B angle right. And let AB be cut in two, at D. And at
right angles let DE be drawn. And through E let EZ be drawn parallel. I say
that the remaining sides of the triangle also are cut in two, AG at E, and BG
at Z. For let DZ be joined. Since AD is equal to DB, <and DB to EZ>, therefore
AD is equal to EZ and parallel. And those joining equals and parallels on the
same parts are equal and parallel. But also DE ZG are the parallelogram GEDZ.
Therefore DZ is equal to EG. But it was also equal to AE. <EG is therefore
equal to AE>. Again, since each of BD EZ, GE DZ is a parallelogram, DE is
therefore equal to each of BZ ZG; for it is opposite. So that BZ ZG also are
equal. The demonstrations are also according to every triangle. Following
these then the breadth of a river will be measured from a distance. Let there
be banks, opposite the one of the enemies on which is point A, and the one
toward us PH. A split dioptra is fixed in the region toward us, at I, so that
the interval of I as far as the bank of the river toward us is greater than
the river. And this is easy to guess. And at right angles two points are
sighted, one on the bank opposite, either a stone or a bush or some other
easily sighted mark, and let it be A, and the other the point toward us, from
the other line of the crossing, Y. And moving the dioptra over to Y I sight A
and I make a right-angled triangle. Let IY be cut in two at K. And from K let
KTh be <drawn> parallel to AI, and from Th let ThR be parallel to IY. Since
then of the right-angled triangle AIY IY has been cut in two at K and ThK is
parallel to AI <and ThR to IY>, AI too then is cut in two at R. Measure off
then the interval from I to R. Therefore that from R to A is also given. And
subtracting from this that from R to Ph we shall have also the remainder, that
is the breadth of the river. And if it seem to someone laborious to take a
greater interval standing off on our own land, when there the sight is of
necessity disturbed <and> what happens is confused, we would take, standing on
the same bank of the river, easily the size of the breadth in this manner. For
let again on the opposite part point A have been taken. And on the part toward
us let point B have been taken, so that AB is at right angles to the line
through the bank, BG. And some point has been taken on BG, D, on which let the
rod DE lie. And on the end of the rod let the gnomon E be aloft, so that, if
the rod DE should touch the surface of the water, the gnomon is on the
surface. And up to this the rod, at right angles <to DE>, let it be carried
along BG, until from some <point, G>, on the line BG, through a dioptra the
points EA are observed. And it will be proportional as BG to GD so AB to ED.
And the ratio of BG to GD is given. Therefore that of AB to DE is also given.
And DE is given. Therefore AB also is given. And by the same ratio the height
of a wall will also be taken when the same diagram is set upright. Let the top
of the battlement be A, and the base B, and the line from the wall to us
outside missile range BG. A dioptra hangs from a pole (which indeed is called
a "lampstand") being fixed at right angles at G. Let then the pole be line DG.
And tilting the dioptra, I sight the top of the wall, which is A. And going
over to the other vessel, on the same straight line I take a point <E. And the
triangle> AEB will be, and beside one of the sides AB the parallel GD.
Therefore the ratio which EG has to GD, this EB has to BA. And the ratio of EG
to GD is given; for each of them is given. Therefore also the <ratio> of EB to
AB <is given. And EB is also given>, as has been shown on the river. Therefore
BA also is given, which it was required to show. =1.16= Theft of sound The
