ORATION XXII.

Second peace oration, spoken in the city of Constantinople, upon the strife
which had arisen among the people, concerning certain bishops who had
quarrelled with one another.

A. Peace my beloved, the sweet thing and name, which I have now given to the
people and have taken in return; I know not whether from all comes a genuine
voice and worthiness of the Spirit, and not public covenants being annulled,
with God as witness, so that our condemnation be the greater. Peace my
beloved, my study and adornment, of which we hear both that it is of God,
and that God, the very God, is called its God, as in “The peace of God,” and
“The God of peace,” and “He himself is our peace;” and yet not even so do we
stand in awe of it. Peace my beloved, the good praised by all but kept by
few, where then did you leave us, so long a time already? and when will you
return to us? How I long for you and greet you above other men; and,
present, I behold you; absent, I recall you with many laments and tears,
such as neither Jacob the patriarch shed for that Joseph, sold by his
brethren and snatched away, as he supposed, by a wild beast; nor David for
Jonathan his friend, having become a work of war, or afterward for his son
Absalom. Of whom the one, torn in his fatherly heart, cried, A wild beast
has snatched Joseph, a wicked and untamed beast; and holding forth the
child's garment bloodied, he folded it about him as if embracing the child's
flesh, both burning and being comforted by the same. The other, now, curses
the mountains on which the war was, saying, Ye mountains of Gilboa, let
there not fall upon you neither rain nor dew; and, How are the mighty
fallen, and the bow of Jonathan! Now, again, he excuses the father-slayer as
having done no wrong, and libations are poured to the dead; grieving,
perhaps, for the child also on this account, that he had lifted his hand
against the father. For such is the father also: whom as an enemy he
requited while warring, as a friend he longs for when dead; and nature
conquers hatred, than which nothing is more violent.

B. Pitiable also is the ark held by the aliens, and Jerusalem laid low and
trodden by the nations, and the sons of Sion, the precious and gold-equal
ones, going into captivity, and even now scattered, being a stranger and
sojourner of the =35.1133= inhabited world. Grievous also are the things now
seen and heard: fatherlands upheaved, and myriads falling, and the earth
fainting with the blood and the corpses, and a people of strange tongue
traversing what belongs to others as their own; let no one bring the charge
of cowardice against those who fight in front; for these are they who all
but set in array the whole inhabited world; but it is because of our
wickedness, and of the impiety prevailing against the Trinity. Grievous are
these things, and beyond grievous. Who would dispute it, of those who,
knowing from what they have suffered or shared in suffering, make trial of
the calamities? But nothing yet is so great as that peace is persecuted, and
the comeliness of the Church taken from us, and the ancient dignity broken
down; and the order so far inverted, that before we became a people from
not-a-people, and a nation from not-a-nation; but now we are in danger,
being dissolved out of a very great people and nation, of becoming again
not-a-people and not-a-nation, as from the beginning, when Thou hadst not
dominion over us, nor were we gathered into one name and order.

C. And truly there is danger that it be easier to bear ill success than to
preserve good success; since even we, when we were warred upon, were made
strong by the persecutions and gathered together; when we were assembled, we
were dissolved. For who would not lament, thinking rightly of the things
present? And who will find a word to set before the calamity? That even
robbers make peace, whom wickedness bound together, and those ranged under a
tyranny, or partners of theft, or conspirators of sedition, or accomplices
of adultery; and further, the companies of dances, and the ranks of armies,
and the crews of ships; for I let pass the lots divided equally, and
constitutions opposed one to another, and successions of ministrations and
magistracies advancing in order and law; and that much-sung sophistical, or
grammatical, that I say not philosophical, wisdom, about which the emulation
of the young rages and is mad; to see this now quarrelling in a few things,
but in most at peace; while we are without conjunctions and without
connectives, and never able to come to the same, nor does any word appear a
healer of this sickness; but, as it were, being initiators and initiated of
wickedness, not of virtue, doing many things to fan the flame of division,
but little, or not at all, caring for concord. And yet when these quarrel,
their quarrel leads not to anything great; and for some it is even better to
quarrel than to be of one mind; for who, thinking rightly, would praise
gatherings together for evil? =35.1136=

D. But if anyone should ask us, What is that honored and adored among you?
it is ready to say, Love. For our God is love. The word of the Holy Spirit;
and God rejoices to hear this rather than any other thing. And what is the
head of the Law and the Prophets? Nothing else than this would the
Evangelist consent to answer. Why then do we, the people of love, so hate
and are hated; and we of peace war, wars both undeclared and irreconcilable;
and we of the corner-stone are at variance; and we of the rock are shaken;
and we of the light are darkened; and we of the Word are of such silence, or
unreasonableness, or folly, or, I know not what to call it, so full; so that
there is a satiety of food, and sleep, and song, even of the basest, as they
say; and fulness of all things, not of the painful only, but already also of
the sweetest; and all things pass over into one another and are turned
about; but for us there is no bound of casting and being cast, not against
those of other doctrine only, who are divided according to the word of the
faith (for it would be less painful, and the zeal an apology for things
praised, if it stood within bounds), but already also against those of the
same doctrine, quarrelling with the same and for the same (for this is the
basest, or most pitiable).

E. And what is the cause? Perhaps love of rule, or love of money, or envy,
or hatred, or disdain, or something of the things which we see not even the
impious suffering; and the elegant thing, the transposition of the votes.
When we are caught, we are pious and orthodox, and falsely flee for refuge
to the truth, as quarrelling for the faith; and we make this the only
praiseworthy thing, as among evils, if not also very shameful, that,
blushing at our wickedness, we pass over to a more reverend name, piety.
Thunderstruck and versatile, someone would say, and a device of the Evil One
and of the sophist of wickedness, whoever you are that suffer this; or, to
speak more truly, most foolish: yesterday he was pious to you, and how
impious today, having added nothing, nor taken away, neither in word nor in
deed, but standing upon the same things; who breathes the same air, and with
the same eyes looks upon the same sun; and, if you will, as one questioned
about numbers or measures, answers nothing else but the same? But also a
fornicator to you today, he who yesterday was a Joseph (for the strife
advances =35.1137= even to these things, like some flame running through
stubble and embracing the things around); and a Judas today, or a Caiaphas,
who yesterday was an Elijah, or a John, or some other of those ranged with
Christ and girt with the same girdle, and wearing the same rough garment,
grey or black, which the solemnity of life puts forth, according at least to
my law and word. And the fair flower of the heights, the paleness, or the
well-ordered and quiet voice, or the weighty and gentle gait, yesterday we
called philosophy, today vainglory; and the same power over spirits or
diseases we have attributed at one time to Jesus, at another to Beelzebul;
and we use them with no just balance, in our strife and wrath.

F. And as the same earth stands firm for the sound and unhurt, but is moved
for those who are giddy and turning round, the affection of the seeing
passing over to the thing seen; or, if you will, as the same interval
between pillars appears greater to those nearer and close by, less to those
farther off, the air stealing away the interval, and the sight joining
together the thicker objects; so we too are easily deceived through hatred,
and about the same things we know not the same, being friends and not
friends; and the season ordains for us, easily, many saints and many
impious, contrary to what is likely; or rather, all wretched; not only
because we look to the evil example (since wickedness is ready at hand even
without one who draws), but because we grant all things readily to all, for
the sake of one man's raging with us. And formerly it was not even safe to
utter a word of the superfluous things; now we rail even at the most pious;
and at one time it was not permitted even to read the law outside, nor to
call upon the confessions (which is, as it seems to me, the consent of the
people); now we use the things secret from the profane as arbiters, casting
holy things to the dogs, and throwing pearls before swine. Not only so, but
we feast their ears with our outrages upon one another; nor can we even see
this, that it is not a safe weapon to trust an enemy, nor one hating
Christians, with a Christian's word. For what we today reproached, tomorrow
we were reproached; and the enemy fawns upon what is said, not that he
praises, but that he bitterly lays up treasure, that in due season he may
vomit his venom upon him who trusted. =35.1140=

G. Why do we suffer these things, O ye men, and until when? When shall we
awake from drunkenness, or take away the film from our eyes, and look up to
the light of the truth? What darkness of night is this? What battle by
night? What whirlwind, not distinguishing the sight of friends and enemies?
Why have we become a reproach to our neighbours, a derision and mocking to
those round about us? What is this ambition for evil? How is it that we
labor so deathlessly? or rather we do not labor, but are grown strong in the
evil, the passion raging, and we are pleased while spending. And nowhere is
there a word, nor friend, nor ally, nor physician, either drugging or
cutting out the passion; nor an angel standing by, nor God; but, besides the
other things, we have shut out from ourselves even the philanthropy of God.
Why, Lord, hast thou stood far off? and how dost thou turn away to the end?
And when wilt thou make thy visitation of us? and where will these things
issue and stand? I fear lest the things present be the smoke of the expected
fire, lest upon these the Antichrist come, and take occasion of his own
dominion from our stumbles and weaknesses. For he will not happen to fall
upon the sound, nor upon those fortified with love; but the kingdom must be
divided against itself, then the strong one within us, the reason, be tried
and bound, then the vessels be spoiled; and we suffer these things which we
now see the enemy suffering at the hands of Christ.

H. For these things I weep, says Jeremiah in the Lamentations, and seek
fountains of tears sufficient for the passion for my eyes, and call the wise
women, that they may work, or co-work, the lament; and my belly aches, and I
set boiling my senses, and I have not how I may lighten the pain, nor with
what words. For this the old things are hushed, and the new are made comedy
(for to my enemies my tragedy is a comedy); for this we owe the Churches no
little, and we have made an addition to the stage; and these things in such
a city, which studies to play with things divine, as if it were something
else, and would sooner laugh at something of the things praised than behold
aught of the things ridiculous without laughter. So that I should wonder if
they did not laugh today even at me who say these things, the herald poured
forth for piety, who teach not that all things are to be laughed at, but
that there are things even to be taken in earnest. And why do I say laugh?
It would be a wonder if I were not even sued, wishing to do good. For such
are our things, that they grieve not me, neither Churches being possessed,
which perhaps someone of small mind would suffer, nor gold flowing to
others, nor =35.1141= wicked tongues making their own, speaking evil, since
they learned not to speak well.

I. For there is no fear lest the Godhead be circumscribed by place, or
become a saleable thing, so as to be wholly of the more wealthy. And me they
will not pay off, those who speak fair or speak ill (like those mixing myrrh
with mud, or mud with myrrh, and confounding the qualities in the mixture),
so that I feel the blasphemies grievously, as one disquieted. Would that
they had laid out much money upon my praisers, if by their praises they made
me better! Now it is not so; whence? But what I am, this I remain, both
spoken ill of and admired. But a mortal swims otherwise in words, says Job;
and as much as the foam flowing about a rock, or the breezes about a pine,
or some one of the shaggy and lofty, so much do the tongues flow about me;
and at the same time I philosophize with myself after this manner: If the
accuser is false, the word touches not me more than what is said of him,
even though he blaspheme me by name; but if true, I shall accuse myself
rather than the speaker (for of him is the speaking, not of me the being
such from the speaker); and having run past the voices, as being nothing, I
shall become myself, gaining from their wickedness only this, to live more
safely. But third, which has also something greatest and most majestic, is
the railing, that we are blasphemed together with God; for the same men both
set at naught the Godhead and insult the theologian. None then of these is
grievous, even if the many so think; but that no one is still believed to be
faithful, nor virtue unhistrionic and free of the stage, even if he were
very sound in soul and genuine in piety; but the one is openly wicked, and
the other has device and coloring for uprightness, that he may steal by
seeming.

J. And men are not thought wicked for the sake of certain such persons; nor
ill-born, or ill-favored, or unmanly, or licentious, when many are so; but
each is judged by himself, and is not made common, neither of the things
blamed, nor of the things praised. But the charge of wickedness is poured
out upon all easily, and the accusation is common to the whole, not only of
the many, but becomes even that of some. And most grievous, that it does not
stop at us only, but passes over to our great and reverend mystery. For of
those who judge our things, which all but happens to all, as judges of
things not their own, some are moderately gentle and philanthropic, others
very bitter and graceless. For some =35.1144= blame us ourselves for the
wickedness, letting go the doctrine of the blame; others bring the
accusation against the law itself, as a teacher of wickedness, and
especially when they light upon many wicked who have been thought worthy of
its charge.

K. Why these things, O ye men, and until when? Shall we not be sober at
least late? Shall we not awake? Are we not ashamed? Shall we not, if for
nothing else, guard the tongues of our enemies, which lightly swagger even
upon false things? Shall we not cease from excessive quarrelsomeness? Shall
we not come to know which of the things sought are attainable for us, and
until when, and which beyond our power? And which of the present season, and
of the confusion below that oversees the mind; and which of the age to come,
and of the liberty from thence; that the former we may embrace for a while,
but from the latter be purified, as hereafter to be perfected and
established in the enjoyment of them? Shall we not divide among ourselves,
which things are not even to be sought at all, which moderately beyond
these, which to be conceded and let go to the contentious, as it may fall
out, doing no harm to our word? And which to be given to faith alone, and
which also to the reasonings? And for which must we also war eagerly,
logically, but not soldier-fashion? For even the lifting of hands is wholly
outside our court, and to be cast away upon those who hate us.

L. Shall we not hold one definition of piety, to worship Father, and Son,
and the Holy Spirit, the one Godhead and power in the three, holding nothing
above it in reverence, nor anything below (that I also may imitate a little
those who are wise about these things; for the one is impossible, the other
impious), nor cutting apart the one greatness by novelties of names? For
nothing is greater or less than itself. This at least being defined, we
shall also think the same about the rest, we at least who are of the same
Trinity and of nearly the same doctrine and body; and the superfluous and
useless offshoots and by-ways of the present questions, as it were some
common disease, we shall cut out and take away. Or was it not enough for me
(for I let pass the things still farther back), the wicked spirit of
Montanus against the Holy Spirit, and the boldness of Novatus, or =35.1145=
rather an unclean cleanness, beguiling the many by the fairness of the word;
and the madness of the Phrygians still even now, ministering and ministered
to, little unlike the ancients; and the folly of the Galatians, being rich
in many names of impiety; and the conflation of Sabellius, and the division
of Arius, and from these the under-division of the present sophists,
differing as much as a talkative tongue from the more idle? But still even
we ourselves differ somewhat with ourselves, we who are sound about the
head, quarrelling about the same things and with the same.

M. I speak then of the yoke-fight lately risen up among us, a brotherly one,
from which both God is dishonored and man. The one not even born for us at
all, nor nailed to the cross; plainly neither buried, nor risen, as it
seemed to some of those who love Christ ill; but honored only here, of which
the dishonor of the honor has been established, and on this account being
cut into two sons, or put together; the other not wholly received, or
honored, but cast aside with the greatest and estranged, if the greatest in
the nature of man is that according to the image, and the power of the mind.
For it was necessary, since Godhead is united, to divide the manhood, and
for those wise in other things to play the fool about the mind; and not that
the whole be saved, the whole having stumbled and been condemned from the
disobedience of the first-formed, and the theft of the adversary; so that
the grace is lessened for God, and for us the salvation. Not only so, but
that our war is for men with those saved of God, and so much remains to us
of the factious spirit, that we have used even the emulations of others for
this, and for the thrones of others we take up private hatreds; committing
two greatest offenses in one, both fanning the love of rule of those men,
and ourselves taking this as a stay of our own passion, like those hanging
over precipices holding to the near rocks, or the thicker of the bushes.
=35.1148=

N. It was right to make even those weaker through our own quietness. For
thus we would have shown them more favor than by warring for them. But now
they ally and are allied, very pitiably, as it seems at least to me; so that
even into two opposing parts the world has now been divided, and that with
labor, hardly, and gathered together little by little, and with the blood of
many. And as for the peaceful and middle, they suffer ill from both, either
despised, or even warred upon. Of which we too are today, being those who
accuse these things, and therefore having received this seat, the assailable
and envied; it would be nothing strange if we were worn away by both, and
removed from the midst, after the many sweats and labors, that they might
cast at one another from near, and with all their wrath, there being nothing
in the way of wall or hindrance between. These things then to define and to
establish belong first to God, who joins together all things; then also to
men, with whom the beautiful has been striven for, and the good of concord
is known; beginning from the Trinity, whose nothing is so peculiar as the
one in nature, and peaceful toward itself; taken up by the angelic and
divine powers, which are at peace both toward God and toward one another;
and having gone forth to the whole creation, whose ornament is to be without
sedition; and having lived its life among us, in the soul according to the
following after and communion of the virtues, in the body according to the
well-harmonizing and symmetry of the members or of the elements toward one
another; of which the one is called beauty, and the other health.

O. But I praise also that of Solomon, who legislates a season, as for every
thing, so also for war and peace. This only I will add, that the season of
both must be observed, since it is at times fine also to war, according to
his law and word; but as far as it is allowed, we must incline rather toward
peace; for this is loftier and more God-like. As it is absurd to suppose
concord best in private, but not most profitable in public. And that a house
and city is best governed which quarrels with itself in nothing or least, or
if suffering this, quickly returns and is healed; but that for the common
body of the Church something else is better and more seemly. =35.1149= And
that each one himself, how he may be at peace with himself, must strive (and
peace is the chosen thing in each, and the lordship over the passions); but
toward others not to appear the same, but to hold as glory of himself the
dissolution of his neighbor. And to bid God forgive even those sinning
against us, not seven times only, but many times so much, since the
forgiving procures the being forgiven; but we swagger more eagerly even upon
those who wrong us in nothing, than we are benefited by others. And such
blessedness to know is laid up for the peace-makers, that they alone are
saluted sons of God in the rank of the saved; while we ourselves, even
hating enemies, then think we act friendly toward God, him who suffered for
us, that he might be at peace with himself, and dissolve the war in us.

P. By no means, O friends and brethren, let us think so. Let us reverence
the gift of the peace-bringer, peace, which going forth from here he left
us, as some other farewell-gift. Let us know one war, that against the
opposing power. Let us say, brethren, even to those who hate us, if haply
they receive it. Let us concede something small, that we may take hold of
the greater, concord. Let us be conquered, that we may conquer. Behold the
laws of the contest, and the wrestlings of the wrestlers, who often by lying
low conquer those above them. These let us emulate, not the more insatiable
of the banqueters, or of the merchants; of whom the one, filled beyond
measure with the things set before them, and the other, having laden the
ship, sooner burst and sank together with it, than enjoyed anything of their
insatiable desire, that they might gain the little, the great things having
been lost. I then both cry out these things and adjure, and doing the part
of the Scripture I will not cease: For Sion's sake I will not be silent, and
for Jerusalem's sake I will not rest; for my soul fails for the slain, we
who are wounded not with the sword, nor wounded with famine, but wounded
with love of glory or love of rule; so that even to be pitied rather than
hated does not befall those who fall. But you, if you receive my words, this
is better for both; but if you spit them out and send them away, the passion
conquering the reason, for me sufficiently are dedicated both the things
toward God and the things toward men; for nothing further, I suppose, will
anyone seek, not even of those very peaceful and God-loving; but you would
know the things that follow. For I will add nothing of the =35.1152= rougher
things, since to spare children is a fatherly law. But may you find merciful
and peaceful the great Judge, both now and in the day of retribution, in
Christ Jesus our Lord; to whom be the glory and the power unto the ages of
ages. Amen.
