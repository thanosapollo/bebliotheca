all expecting that the homily would be spoken on the beginning of the ninth of
Acts, that Paul's calling is a proof of the resurrection.

1. Are these things bearable? Are these things tolerable? Each day our
assembly shrinks toward the less; and the city is full of people, but the
church is empty of people; the marketplace is full, and theaters, and the
promenade; but the house of God is deserted; or rather, if one must speak the
truth, the city is deserted of people, and the church is full of people. For
one must not call people those in the marketplace, but you who are in the
church; not those who are idle, but you who are in earnest; not those who gape
after the things of this life, but you who prefer the spiritual to the
worldly. For it is not if someone has a human body and a voice that this one
is a hu =51.114= man; but if someone has a human soul, and a soul's
disposition. And of a human soul there is no such proof as to love the divine
oracles; just as there is no such mark and sign of a brutish and irrational
soul as to despise the divine oracles. Do you want to learn that those who
despise the divine hearing have, through this contempt, lost even being
people, and have fallen from that nobility itself? I will not tell you my own
word, but I will tell a prophetic saying that confirms my judgment, so that
you may see that those who do not love spiritual words cannot even be people;
so that you may see that the city is deserted of people for us. For the most
loud-voiced Isaiah, the beholder of paradoxical sights, the one who was
counted worthy to see the se =51.115= raphim while still in the flesh, the one
of that mystical song, this man, entering the Jews' city, the populous
metropolis, I mean Jerusalem, standing in the middle of the marketplaces, the
whole people standing around him, wanting to show that the one who does not
hear the prophetic words is not a person, cried out saying: I came, and there
was no person; I called, and there was none who would listen. And that he said
this not because of the desertion of those present, but because of the sloth
of the hearers, after saying, I came, and there was no person, he added, and
there was none who would listen. So they were present, but they were not
counted as present, because they did not hear the prophet; therefore, since he
came, and there was no person, he called, and there was none who would listen,
he turns the word toward the elements, and says, Hear, O heaven, and give ear,
O earth. For I, he says, was sent to people, to people who have a mind; but
since they have neither speech nor perception, for this reason I converse with
the elements that have no perception, as an accusation of those who have been
honored with perception, yet do not use the honor. So also another prophet
says, Jeremiah. For he too, standing in the midst of a multitude of Jews, in
the city itself, cried out as if no one were present: To whom shall I speak
and testify? What are you saying? Seeing so great a multitude, you ask to whom
you will speak? Yes, he says; for it is a multitude of bodies, but not a
multitude of people; it is a multitude of bodies that do not have hearing. For
this reason he also added: Their ears are uncircumcised, and they cannot hear.
Do you see that all these, because they do not hear, are not people? That one
says, I came, and there was no person; I called, and there was none who would
listen; this one says, To whom shall I speak and testify? their ears are
uncircumcised, and they

cannot hear. And if the prophets say that those present, because they did not
attend with eagerness to what was said, are not even people, what would we say
about those who not only are not heard, but do not even endure to set foot on
these sacred thresholds, about those who wander outside this sacred flock,
being far from this motherly house, in streets and alleys, like the disorderly
and idle among children? For those too, leaving the father's house, wander
somewhere outside, spending the day in childish toys; for this reason such
children have often fallen even from freedom and from life. For falling into
the hands of kidnappers or robbers, they have often paid death as the penalty
of their sloth. For those men take them, and stripping off the golden
ornament, either drown them in river currents, or, when they plan something
more humane concerning them, leading them away into a foreign land, they sell
their freedom. This is what these too suffer. For when they wander from the
father's house and from the sojourn here, they fall in with the mouths of
heretics, and the tongues of the enemies of the truth; then, like kidnappers,
those men take =51.116= them, and stripping off the golden ornament of the
faith, they drown them at once, not throwing them into rivers, but sinking
them in the turbid doctrines of their own stench.

2. It would be yours to provide for the salvation of these brothers, and to
bring them back to us, even if they drag the other way, even if they resist,
even if they cry out against it, even if they lament. This contentiousness and
sloth belong to a childish mind. But you, correct their soul that is still in
a more unfinished state; it is yours to persuade them to become people. For
just as we would not say that one who turns away from human food, and feeds on
thorns and herbs with the cattle, is a person; so too we would not say that
one who hates the true food proper to a human soul, the food from the divine
oracles, and sits always in worldly gatherings and councils full of indecency,
feeding on lawless words, is a person. For a person, as we take it, is not if
someone is nourished by bread only, but if someone, before that food, shares
in divine and spiritual oracles. And that this is a person, hear Christ
saying: A person shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that goes
out through the mouth of God. So the food of our life is twofold, the one
lesser, the other better; and one must especially lay claim to this latter, so
as to nourish the soul as well, and not overlook it wasting away with hunger.
It would be yours, then, to make the city full of people for us. Since, then,
this great and populous city is deserted of people, you would be right to
contribute this gift to the fatherland, and one must draw the brothers in, if
you carry what is from here over to them. For we persuade people to enjoy a
table then, not only when we praise the table, but also when we have something
of the foods from it to give to those left behind. Do this yourselves now, and
one of two things will certainly be: either you will persuade them to return
to us, or, remaining in the same contentiousness, they will be fed through
your tongue, or rather they will come back altogether. For they will not
choose to be fed as a matter of favor, when it is possible to enjoy this
paternal table with the father's authority. But that you are doing this, or
have done it, or will do it, I am fully confident and I believe; for I myself
have not ceased continually advising these things; and you are filled with all
knowledge, able to admonish others also. It is time, then, to set before you
our table, this cheap and poor one, full of much poverty, yet having the best
relish, your

desire as hearers. For it is not the costliness of foods alone that makes a
table most pleasant, but also the appetite of those invited; so a cheap table
will appear splendid, when those present do not attack it with hunger; so also
the cheap is shown costly, when it receives the diners hungry. Which another
also, perceiving that the costliness of tables is judged not by the nature of
the foods but by the disposition of those feasted, says somehow: A soul in
satiety mocks at honeycombs, =51.117= but to a needy soul even bitter things
appear sweet; not because the nature of what is set out is changed, but
because the disposition of those feasted cheats the sense. And if bitter
things appear sweet because of the desire of those invited, much more do cheap
things appear costly. Therefore we too, living with extreme poverty, imitate
the ambitious among hosts, calling you to our table at each assembly. And we
do this, not trusting in the abundance of our own means, but taking courage
from your hearing.

3. We have paid you the whole debt of the title, the title, I mean, of the
Acts of the Apostles. It would follow next to take hold of the beginning of
the book as well, and to say what this is: The first account I made concerning
all things, O Theophilus, that Jesus began both to do and to teach. But Paul
does not let me use this order of sequence, calling our tongue toward himself
and his own achievements. For I desire to see him led into Damascus, bound,
not in an iron chain, but by the Master's voice; I desire to see this great
fish caught, the one that was churning up the whole sea, the one that was
raising countless waves against the Church; I desire to see him caught, not
with a hook, but by the Master's word. For just as some fisherman sitting on a
high rock, lifting the rod, from on high lets the hook down into the deep; so
too our Master, who showed the spiritual fishing, sitting as on a high rock of
the heavens, letting down this voice from above as a hook, and saying, Saul,
Saul, why do you persecute me? so he caught this great fish. And such a thing
as happened in the case of the fish that Peter caught according to the
Master's command, something of that kind happened in this case too. For this
fish also was found having a stater in its mouth, a stater, but a counterfeit
one; for he had zeal, but not according to knowledge. Therefore God, granting
him the knowledge, made the coin genuine; and what happens in the case of fish
that are caught, this also happened in his case. For just as those, drawn up
at once from the deep, are blinded, so he too, receiving the hook at once, and
being pulled up, was at once blinded; but that blinding made the whole world
look up. All this, then, I desire to see. For if a barbarian war had
surrounded us, and on the battle line the enemies were giving us countless
troubles, then the general of the barbarians, the one bringing on countless
engines, and confounding all that is ours, and filling us with much tumult and
disturbance, and threatening to raze the city itself, and to hand it over to
fire, and holding slavery over us, had suddenly been bound by our king and was
being led captive into the city, we would all, with women and children, have
rushed out to that spectacle. Since, then, a war has been joined now as well,
Jews throwing everything into tumult and disturbance, and bringing many
engines against the Church's security, and the head of the enemies was Paul,
the one doing and saying greater things than all, the one throwing everything
into tumult and disturbance, and our Lord Jesus Christ, the king who is

ours, bound =51.118= him, bound him and led in as captive this man who was
overturning everything, shall we not all go out to this spectacle, so as to
see him being led captive? For the angels then, seeing him from the heavens
bound and being led in, leaped; not because they saw him bound only, but
because they considered how many people he was going to loose from bonds; not
because they beheld him being led by the hand, but because they reckoned how
many people that man was going to lead by the hand from earth to heaven. For
this they rejoiced, not because they saw him disabled, but because they
considered how many he was going to lead out from darkness. For go, he says,
to the nations, and delivering them from darkness, you will transfer them into
the kingdom of the love of Christ. For these reasons, leaving the preface, I
am eager to leap into the middle. For Paul and the longing for Paul compelled
us to make this leap. Paul and the longing for Paul. Forgive me, or rather do
not forgive me, but even emulate this love. For the one who loves an unseemly
love reasonably asks pardon; but the one who loves such a love, let him take
pride in the longing, and make many partners of the desire, and prepare
countless fellow-lovers of it. For if it were possible, going by the road and
advancing in order, both to speak the earlier things and to arrive at the
middle, we would not have left the beginning and come at once to the middle;
but since the law of the Fathers commands that after Pentecost the book be
laid aside, and with the end of this feast the reading of the book also is
dissolved, I feared that while we were busy and lingering over the prefaces,
the sequence of the history might run ahead of our arrival. For this reason I
ran from the beginning of the narrative, and as if holding the preface of the
history from behind by the head, I ordered you to wait and stand at the
beginning of the road. For having taken hold of the head of the narrative, I
will with confidence go through all the rest, even if the feast passes; for no
one will charge us with untimeliness, the very necessity according to the
sequence freeing us from the charges of untimeliness; for this reason I ran
from the prefaces to the middle. For that it was not possible, going by the
road, to come to Paul, but that the book would have run ahead of our tongue,
and would have shut the doors on us, I will make clear from the preface
itself, even if this has already become evident.

4. For if, reading and explaining only a title to you, we spent half the
feast, if we had also attempted, starting from the preface toward the very
deep of the book, to let the discourse go, how much time would we have spent,
so as to arrive at the narratives concerning Paul? Or rather I will try to
make this clear to you from the preface itself. The first account I made
concerning all things, O Theophilus. How many questions do you think there are
here? First, why he reminds him of the former book. Second, why he calls it an
account, and =51.119= not a Gospel; and yet Paul calls it a Gospel, saying
thus, Whose praise is in the Gospel through all the Churches, speaking of
Luke. Third, why he says, Concerning all things that Jesus did. For if John,
the beloved of Christ, who enjoyed such freedom of speech, who was counted
worthy to recline on that holy breast, who drew the springs of the Spirit from
there, this man did not dare to say this, but used such caution as to say that
If everything that Christ did were written one by one, I do not think the
world itself would contain the books being written; how did this man dare to
say that The first account I made concerning all things, O Theophilus, that
Jesus did? Does this question seem to us a small one

to be? And there, Most excellent Theophilus, the name with praise; for this
has not been said simply to the saints. And perhaps we have shown this in part
as well, that not even one iota, nor one tittle, is to be seen lying simply in
the Scriptures. If, then, in the preface there are so many questions, how much
time were we going to spend going through all that follows? For these reasons
I was compelled, running past what is in the middle, to come to Paul. Why,
then, after stating the questions, did we not also add their solution?
Accustoming you not always to receive the food already chewed, but in many
places to bring the solution to the thoughts yourselves, which is what doves
do. For they feed the nestlings, as long as they remain in the nest, with
their own mouth; but when they are able to lead them out of the nest, and see
their wings set, they no longer do this thereafter, but they bring the grain
on the mouth and show it; and when the nestlings, expecting it, come near, the
mothers, dropping the food on the ground, order them to pick it up by
themselves; so we too have done, taking the spiritual food on the mouth, we
called you, as if we were going to show you the solution in the usual way; but
when you came forward and expected to receive it, we let it go, so that you
might take up the thoughts through yourselves. For this reason, leaving the
preface, we hurry toward Paul. And we will speak not only how much he
benefited the Church, but also how much he harmed it; for this account is
necessary for us. We will speak how he fought against the word of the
preaching, how he made war on Christ, how he persecuted the apostles, how he
thought the thoughts of enemies, how he gave the Church more trouble than all.
But let no one be ashamed hearing these things about Paul; for they are not
accusations against him, but a subject of praises. For it is not an accusation
for him that, being bad before, he later became good, but that, being earnest
before, he later changed toward wickedness; for things are always judged from
the end. For even pilots, even if they suffer countless shipwrecks, when they
are about to put into harbor, if they bring in a merchantman filled with
cargoes, we do not say they have fared badly, the end hiding the things that
have passed; and athletes again, even if they are countless times before
=51.120= defeated, but win the bout for the crown, we do not deprive them of
the praises for the victory because of the former things. Let us do so in
Paul's case too. For he too suffered countless shipwrecks, but when he was
about to put into harbor, he brought in the merchantman filled with the
cargoes. And just as Judas was not helped at all by being a disciple before
this, then becoming a traitor; so this man was not harmed at all by being a
persecutor before this, and after these things becoming an evangelist. These
are praises of Paul, not because he razed the Church, but because he himself
built it again; not because he sacked the word, but because, having sacked the
word, he himself increased it again; not because he made war on the apostles,
not because he tore the flock apart, but because, having torn it apart, he
later himself knit it together.

5. What could be more paradoxical than this? The wolf became a shepherd; the
one who was drinking down the blood of the sheep did not cease pouring out his
own blood for the salvation of the sheep. Do you want to learn how he drank
out the blood of the sheep? how his tongue was bloodied? But Saul, still
breathing threat and murder against the disciples of the Lord. But this man
who was breathing threat and murder, and pouring out the blood of the saints,
hear how he poured out his own blood for the saints. If according to man, he
says, I fought with beasts at Ephesus; and again, I die daily; and again, We
were reckoned as sheep of slaughter. And he said these things who was present
when Stephen's blood was being poured out, and was consenting

to his killing. Do you see how the wolf became a shepherd? Are you not
ashamed, hearing before this that he was a persecutor and a blasphemer and an
insolent man? Did you see how the former accusation made his praise greater?
Did I not tell you in the former assembly that the signs after the cross
became greater than those before the cross? did I not show you both from the
signs, and from the goodwill of the disciples, and how before this Christ by
command raised the dead, but later the shadows of his servants did this? how
then he himself, giving orders, worked wonders, but later his servants, using
his name, did greater things? did I not tell you about the enemies, how he
shook their conscience? how he mastered the whole world? how the signs after
the cross were greater than those before the cross? A brother of that
discourse is the one that has entered today. For what sign could be greater
than the one concerning Paul? For while he was living even Peter denied him,
but when he had died Paul confessed him. And greater than raising the dead
through the shadows was the sign of drawing and capturing Paul's mind. For
there nature followed, and did not contradict the one who commanded; but here
choice was mistress of being persuaded and of not being persuaded; whence the
power of the one who persuaded is shown to be great. For to change choice was
much greater than to set nature right; so the sign became greater than all the
=51.121= others, that Paul came to Christ after the cross and the tomb. For
this is why Christ let him display all the enmity, and then called him, that
he might make the proof of the resurrection, and the word of teaching, free of
suspicion. For Peter, speaking about him, was perhaps suspected; for one of
the shameless might have said something. And I said "of the shameless,"
because even there the proof was clear. For that man too denied him first, and
denied him with an oath, but nevertheless later, confessing this same one, he
even gave his own soul. And if he had not risen, the one who denied him living
would not, so as not to deny him when he had died, have endured countless
deaths; therefore even in Peter's case the proof of the resurrection was
clear. Still, the shameless had that to say, that because he was his disciple,
because he shared a table with him, and spent three years with him, because he
enjoyed teaching, because he was flattered by him, being deceived, for this
reason he preaches his resurrection; but when you see Paul, who did not know
him, who did not hear him, who did not share in the teaching, who even after
the cross made war on him, who was killing those who believed in him, who was
confounding and disturbing everything, this man suddenly changed, and in the
labors for the preaching outstripping all the friends of Christ, what pretext
of shamelessness will you have left, tell me, disbelieving the word of the
resurrection? For if Christ has not risen, who would have drawn and brought to
himself one so savage and inhuman, one made an enemy and made wild? For tell
me, O Jew, who persuaded Paul to come to Christ? Peter? or James? or John? But
all these feared him and shuddered, and not only before this, but even when he
had become one of the friends, when Barnabas, taking him by the hand, brought
him back to Jerusalem, they were still afraid to join themselves to him; and
the war had been dissolved, but the fear remained with the apostles. Those,
then, who still feared him when he had been reconciled, did they dare to
persuade him when he was an enemy and a foe? did they endure even to come
forward at all, or to stand, or to open a mouth, or even to appear at all? It
is not so, it is not so; what happened was not human eagerness, but of divine
grace. If, then, Christ was dead, as you say, and his disciples came and stole
him, how did the signs after the cross become greater? how was the

proof of the power more? For he not only transferred the enemy, and the leader
of your battle; and yet if he had done this only, it was of the greatest power
to take the enemy and foe captive; but now he has not done this only, but also
much greater than this. For he not only transferred him, but made him so much
his own, so drew him toward his own goodwill, as even to entrust to him all
the affairs of the Church. For a vessel, he says, of election he is to me, to
bear my name before nations =51.122= and kings, and to persuade him to labor
more than the apostles for the Church that was formerly warred on by him.

6. Do you want to learn how he transferred him? how he made him his own? how
he drew him? how he ranked him among the first of the friends? To none of
people did he dare to speak so many secret things as to Paul. And from where
is this clear? I heard unspeakable words, he says, which it is not lawful for
a person to speak. Did you see how much goodwill the enemy, the foe,
displayed? For this reason it is necessary to speak his former life as well;
for it shows us both the loving-kindness and the power of God;
loving-kindness, because he willed to save the one who had worked so many
evils, and to draw him to himself; power, because, having willed, he was able.
And this also shows Paul's soul, that he did nothing toward contentiousness,
nor was he preoccupied with human glory, as the Jews were; but he was fired
with zeal, not a right zeal, but zeal nonetheless; which he himself also cried
out saying, that For this reason I was shown mercy, because I did it
ignorantly in unbelief; and, struck with amazement at God's loving-kindness,
he said: That in me first Christ might show all the long-suffering, for a
pattern of those who are going to believe on him unto eternal life; and
elsewhere again he said, that He showed the working of his power especially
toward us who believe. Did you see how Paul's former life showed both the
loving-kindness of God, and the power, and the unbribable character of his own
mind? This, at any rate, writing to the Galatians, he brought forward as a
proof, that he was not changed because of people, but was transferred by
divine power. For if I were still pleasing people, he says, I would not be a
servant of Christ. And from where is it clear that, not pleasing people, you
transferred to the preaching? You heard, he says, of my former conduct in
Judaism, that beyond measure I persecuted the Church of God, and sacked it.
And he would not, if he wanted to please people, have shifted toward the
faith. Why? He was honored among Jews, and enjoyed much freedom from fear, and
was counted worthy of a front seat; he would not then have transferred to the
dangerous life of the apostles, the one full of ill repute, the one stuffed
with disasters; so that this (sic) sudden change and transfer, and the leaving
of the honor among Jews and the unwarring life, and the exchanging of it for
the life of the apostles, which has countless deaths, has become the greatest
proof that Paul was not transferred for any human pretext. For this reason we
too wanted to bring his former life into the middle, and to show the fired
zeal against the Church, so that, when you see the great eagerness on behalf
of the Church, you may wonder at God who does all things and recasts them. For
this reason Paul's disciple also narrated the former things to us with
accuracy, and with much emphasis, saying thus: But Saul, still breathing
threat and murder against the disciples =51.123= of the Lord. I myself, then,
wanted to begin the preface today, I wanted to throw myself into the beginning
of the narrative, but I see a sea of thoughts from the name alone. For
consider how great a question this, Saul, at once stirs for us; for in the
Letters I see another name lying; Paul, a servant of Jesus Christ, called an
apostle; Paul and Sosthenes; Paul

called an apostle; See, I Paul say to you; but now as Paul, and everywhere he
is called Paul, but he is not called Saul. Why, then, was he called Saul
before this, and after these things Paul? The question is not a bare one; for
Peter too runs up at once; for he too was called Simon before this, and after
these things was named Cephas; and the sons of Zebedee, James and John, were
renamed Sons of thunder. And not in the New only, but also in the Old we find
Abraham called Abram before this, and after these things Abraham; and Jacob
now Jacobcalled, and after these things Israel; and Sarah called Sara before
this, and after these things Sarah; =51.124= and the changing of the names
furnishes us much inquiry; and I fear that, suddenly letting loose many
streams of rivers, I may drown the word of the teaching. For just as in a plot
that has moisture, wherever one digs, springs leap out from every side; so
also in the plot of the divine Scriptures, wherever you open, you will see
many rivers going out, whence to let them all loose at once today is no small
fear. Therefore, blocking our own channel, I will send your love on to the
sacred spring of these presidents and teachers, this pure and drinkable and
sweet one, the stream that goes out from the intelligible rock itself. Let us
then prepare the mind for the reception of the teaching, to draw the spiritual
streams, so that there may be in us a spring of water leaping up unto eternal
life; which may we all obtain by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord
Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom to the Father be glory, honor, power,
together with the Holy and life-giving Spirit, now and always, and unto the
ages of ages. Amen.

To those who accuse us over the length of what has been said, and to those who
are displeased over brevity, and concerning the appellation of Saul and of
Paul, and why the first person was called Adam, that it was usefully and
advantageously, and to the newly illuminated.

1. What then must we do today? For seeing your multitude, I am afraid to
stretch the word toward length. For when the teaching advances farther, I see
you being trampled, cramped, and the distress from the cramped space damaging
the accuracy of the hearing; for a hearer who does not enjoy ease would not be
able to attend with eagerness to what is said. Seeing your multitude, then, as
I said, I am afraid to stretch the word toward length; but reckoning your
longing I fear to contract the teaching. For the one who is thirsty, if he
does not first see the cup filled, would not even bring it to his lips with
pleasure; but even if he is not going to drink it all, he desires to see it
all full. For this reason I do not know how I should use the address. For I
want by brevity to cut short your toil, and by the length of the word to fill
your desire. But I have often done both of these, and not even once have I
escaped the blame. I know that often, sparing you, I dissolved the word before
the end, and those who have an insatiable soul cried out against us, those who
continually enjoy the divine streams, yet are never filled, those Blessed
ones, who hunger and thirst for righteousness, and fearing the outcries from
these I again came forward stretching the teaching to a great length, and for
this I underwent blame. For those who love brevity, meeting us, begged us to
spare their weakness, and

to contract the length of what is said. Whenever, then, I see you cramped, I
drive the word toward silence; =51.124= but whenever I see you cramped, and
not standing off, but hanging on for a greater course, I desire to wake the
tongue. I am cramped from every side. What am I to suffer? For the one who
serves one master, and is forced to serve one mind, can please the master with
ease, and not miss the mark; but I have many masters, being forced to serve so
great a people that has a differing mind. And I have said these things not
being displeased at the service, far from it, nor running away from your
mastery. For nothing is more august to me than this service. A king does not
think so highly of the diadem and the purple as I now take pride in the
service of your love. For that kingship death succeeds; but this service, if
it is completed well, the kingdom of the heavens awaits. For blessed is the
faithful and prudent servant, whom his master set to give the food-measure to

REBUKE Against those who were left behind, exhortation to those present, to
care for the brothers, and on the preface of the letter to the Corinthians,
"Paul called," and concerning humility.

1. When I look to your fewness and behold the flock being lessened at each
assembly, I both am despondent, and I rejoice; I rejoice because of you who
are present, but I am despondent because of those who are absent. For you are
worthy of praises, not even becoming more idle from the fewness; but those are
liable to accusations, not even being awakened into eagerness from your
earnestness. For this reason I bless you and say you are to be emulated,
because nothing of their =51.144= faintness has harmed you; but I count those
wretched and I weep, because nothing of your earnestness has benefited them.
For they have not heard the prophet saying: I have chosen to be cast aside in
the house of God, rather than for me to dwell in tents of sinners. He did not
say, I have chosen to dwell in the house of my God, nor, to spend time, nor,
to enter; but, I have chosen to be cast aside. It is beloved to me even to be
ranked among the last; I am content in this, even if I am counted worthy to
set foot on the thres =51.145= holds, he says; I consider it a greatest gift,
even if someone should number me among the last in the house of my God.
Longing makes the common Master its own; for such is love. In the house of my
God. The one who loves does not desire to see the beloved only, but also that
man's house only, but also the gateway; not the gateway of the house only, but
also the alley itself and the street; and if he sees a garment, if a sandal of
the beloved, he thinks the beloved himself is present. Such were the prophets;
since they did not see God the bodiless, they saw the house, and through the
house they imagined that one's presence. I have chosen to be cast aside in the
house of my God rather than for me to dwell in tents of sinners. Each place,
each plot, toward the comparison of the house of God, is a tent of sinners,
even if you say a lawcourt, even if a council-chamber, even if each one's
house. For even if prayers come to be there, even if supplications, still
there is necessity that contentions also come to be and fights and insults,
and gatherings over worldly cares; but this house is clean of all these; for
this reason those are tents of sinners, but this is a house of God; and just
as a harbor freed from winds and waves furnishes much security to the ships
that put in, so also the house of God, snatching those who enter as from some
winter of the outside affairs, furnishes them to stand with much calm and
security, and to hear the divine oracles.

This plot is a subject of virtue, a school of philosophy; not in the assembly
only, when there is a hearing of Scriptures and spiritual teaching, and a
sitting of reverend Fathers; but also in all the remaining time, only set foot
on the thresholds, and at once you have put away the worldly cares. Enter
inside the thresholds, and like some spiritual breeze it stands around your
soul. This quiet leads into awe, and teaches to philosophize; it raises the
mind, and does not let one remember the present things, it transfers you from
earth to heaven. And if without an assembly the gain of being present here is
so great, when prophets cry from every side, when apostles evangelize, when
Christ stands in the middle, when the Father accepts what is coming to be,
when the Holy Spirit furnishes his own gladness, with how much benefit do
those present go away filled! and those absent, how great a loss they undergo!
I wanted to know where those who have despised the assembly spend their time,
what held them, and led them away from this sacred table, about what the
conversation is. Or rather I know clearly; for either they converse about
out-of-place and laughable affairs, or they are nailed to worldly cares; and
the sojourn of both has been deprived of pardon, and has the last punishment.
And concerning the former not even a word is needed, nor a proof; but that
those too who put forward to us the affairs of the house, and speak of the
unbearable necessity from there, not even these can obtain pardon, being
called here once in the week, and not even then enduring to prefer the
spiritual to the earthly affairs, is clear from the Gospels. For those called
to the spiritual mar =51.146= riage put forward these pretexts, one that he
bought a yoke of oxen, another that he purchased a field, another that he took
a bride, but they were punished nonetheless. The causes are necessary; but
when God calls, they do not have a defense; for after God all things are
necessary for us. After that one's honor, then let the others enjoy eagerness.
For what household servant, tell me, before he completes the master's
ministry, will ever take care of the things according to his own house? How
then is it not out of place, in the case of a person, where the mastery is a
bare name, to furnish so much respect and obedience to the lords, but not to
count the true Master, not of us only but also of the powers above, worthy
even of the same service as our fellow-servants? Would that it were possible
for you to have entered into their conscience, and then you would have seen
well, of how many wounds they were full, how many thorns they had. For just as
earth, not enjoying farming hands, lies fallow and runs wild with wood; so
also a soul, not enjoying spiritual teaching, sends out thorns and burs. For
if we who each day share in the hearing of the prophets and apostles scarcely
hold anger, scarcely bridle wrath, scarcely check desire, scarcely cast out
the wasting of envy, singing continual charms from the divine Scriptures to
our passions, scarcely check the shameless beasts; those who never enjoy this
healing, nor listen to the divine philosophy, what hope of salvation will they
have, tell me? I wanted to be able to show their soul to your eyes, and you
would have seen it filthy, squalid, poured down, and humbled, and without
freedom of speech. For just as bodies that do not enjoy baths are full of much
squalor and filth; so also the soul, not enjoying spiritual teaching, has a
great stain of faults lying around it. For the things here are a spiritual
bath, wiping off all filth by the heat of the Spirit; or rather the fire of
the Spirit is not wiping off filth only, but also a dye. For if, he says, your
sins are as scarlet, as snow I will

whiten them; and even if, he says, with the same accuracy the filth of the
faults bites the substance of the soul, so as to stand into an unmoved habit
of dye, even so I can set it into the opposite quality; for it is enough to
nod, and all the faults vanish.

2. I say these things, not so that you may hear; for you do not need the
drugs, through the grace of God; but so that those may learn through you. And
if I were able to know the places in which they gather, I would not have
troubled your love; but since it is impossible for me, being one, to know so
great a people, I put into your hands the healing of your brothers; take care
of your own brothers, draw them, call them. I know that you have often done
this; but it is nothing to do this often, but to do it until then, until you
persuade and draw. I know that you have troubled, that you have often been
thought burdensome, that =51.147= you have not persuaded; and this has made
you more shrinking; but let Paul console you saying: Love hopes all things,
believes all things, love never falls away. Do your own part; and even if that
man does not receive the healing, you have the wage with God. For on the earth
if you throw down the seeds, and it does not bring out the ears, you must go
away with empty hands; but upon the soul not so; but you throw down the
teaching, and even if that soul is not persuaded by what is said, you have the
wage completed, and so much as if it had been persuaded; for God, attending
not simply to the end of the affairs, but to the mind of those who toil, is
accustomed so to define the exchanges. I exhort you, then, what those raging
about the theaters do toward the contests of the horses, this you too do. And
what do those do? From evening they arrange with one another, and they meet at
one another's houses toward dawn, and they mark off other places for
themselves, so that, being gathered together, they may go up with more
pleasure to that satanic spectacle. As those are in earnest against their own

and save one another together, and when an assembly is about to come to be,
meet at the brother's house, and wait outside the thresholds, and when he
comes out hold him; and even if ten thousand necessities call, do not yield,
nor permit him to touch anything of the worldly things, before bringing him
into the church and persuading him to share in the whole assembly; and if he
contends, and if he contradicts, and if he puts forward ten thousand pretexts,
do not be persuaded, nor put up with it, but having said and taught that the
other things too will then be made easier for him, when, having completed the
assembly, and having shared in prayers, and having enjoyed blessings of
Fathers, he so walks toward those, and having bound him with these and with
more other words than these, so lead him to this sacred table, so that you may
have a double wage, both for your own presence and for that man's. Certainly,
if we use so much earnestness and eagerness about the hunt of the more idle,
we will obtain the salvation. For even if they are ten thousand times faint
and shameless and murderous, blushing at the continuity of your choice, they
will sometime stand off from the sloth. For they are not harder than that
judge who did not know God nor was ashamed of people, even if they are ten
thousand times unfeeling; but nevertheless that cruel one, the wild, the iron,
the adamant, the continual sitting-by of one widow woman put to shame. Of what
pardon then would we even be worthy, if a widow woman was able to bend a cruel
judge, who neither feared God nor regarded people, and to persuade him to give
the favor, and we should not be able to draw the brothers who are much more
bearable than that man, and more moderate than that man, exhorting them over
their own

goods? I have said these things often, and I will not cease saying, until I
see those who are sick becoming healthy. =51.148= I seek them each day, until
I am able through your earnestness to find them. And I beg you too, with the
same pain with which I say these things now, with the same toil to make the
search of the more idle. For not to me only, but also to you Paul has
commanded to take thought for your own members. For Exhort, he says, one the
one in these words, just as you also do; and again: Build one another up. For
the wage is great for those who care for the brothers, and the punishment
greatest for those who neglect and despise their salvation.

3. Whence I am extremely confident and have believed that you will do what has
been said with much eagerness; and for this reason, having stopped this
exhortation here, I will try to lead you to Paul's table. Paul, called an
apostle. These things you too have often heard, and we have read; but one must
not only read, but also recognize what is said; since there will be no gain
for us from the reading. For a treasure trampled from above does not display
the wealth, but one must first dig through it, and go down below, and so find
all the abundance; so also in the case of the Scriptures; the reading alone is
not enough to show the treasure of the goods stored, if you do not search out
the depth. If reading were enough, Philip would not have said to the eunuch,
Do you even know what you are reading? if the reading were enough, Christ
would not have said to the Jews, Search the Scriptures; and the one who
searches does not stand as far as the surface, but goes down toward the depth
itself. For I see a great sea of thoughts in the prefaces. For in worldly
letters the addresses come to be simply, showing service only; but here not
so, but the preface is full of much wisdom. For it is not Paul who is
speaking, but Christ who moves that man's soul. Paul, called. This Paul is one
name, and a bare name; but it has so great a treasure of thoughts stored, as
you have known through the experience. For if indeed you remember, you know

through which, being called Saul before this, he was after these things called
Paul, and why, having transferred to the faith, he did not receive the
appellation at once, but remained until long having the name which the parents
set from the beginning; and from this we displayed much wisdom and care of
God, both having come to be about us, and about those saints. For if people do
not simply set the names upon their own children, but calling one from the
father, another from the grandfather, another from other ancestors; much more
did God not simply, nor without some reason, set the appellations upon his own
servants, but with more wisdom. For people, both for the honor of those who
have gone away, and often for their own consolation, call their own sons by
the names of those who have died, devising some consolation of the death of
those who have gone through the appellation of the children; but God a
reminder of virtue and a teach =51.149= ing, as on a bronze stele, deposits in
the appellation of the saints. Peter, at any rate, he called so from the
virtue, depositing the proof of the firmness about the faith in his name, so
that he may have a continual teacher, the appellation, of such firmness. This
he also called John and James, from the loudness of voice according to the
preaching. But so that I may not again trouble by saying the same things,
leaving these, I will say that, even the names of the saints being said by
themselves, are both reverend to those who love God, and fearful to those who
sin.

Onesimus, at any rate, having become a runaway and a thief, and having taken

the old man. Do you see that even the names themselves are reverend to the
faithful and god-loving? For if the appellation of a beloved child, being
named, has often persuaded even an unwilling father to give the favor through
the affection toward the name, much more was this likely to happen in the case
of the saints. And that they were also fearful to those who sin, as to idle
children the names of the teachers, hear how writing to Galatians he hinted
this very thing. For since they had declined into Jewish weakness, and were in
danger about the faith itself, wanting to raise them and to persuade them to

Paul say to you, that if you are circumcised, Christ will benefit you nothing.
You said, I; why adding the name? for the I was not sufficient to show the one
writing? But so that you may learn that even the insertion of the name itself
is sufficient to take hold of those who hear, for this reason he adds the
appellation, bringing them into a reminder of the teacher. And we too suffer
this same thing; for when we are reminded of the saints, even if we are in
sloth, we are raised up, even if in contempt, we fear. When, at any rate, I
hear Paul the apostle, I consider the one in afflictions, the one in cramped
spaces, the one in blows, the one in prisons, the one who became a
night-and-day in the deep, the one snatched into a third heaven, the one who
heard the unspeakable words in paradise, the vessel of election, the bridesman
of Christ, the one who prayed to be anathema from Christ for the brothers. And
like some golden chain, the string of the achievements with the memory of the
name comes in upon those who attend with accuracy; and no small gain comes to
us from this.

4. It was possible to say even more than these into the appellation; but so
that we may take hold of the second word as well, having stopped the
contemplation about the name here, we will walk toward that one thereafter.
For just as the name Paul furnished us much abundance, so also the Called, if
we should want to learn it with the belonging earnestness, would fill us with
equal or even more contemplation. And just as from an ornament, or a royal
diadem, someone taking out one stone could buy splendid houses and costly
fields, and herds of household servants, and many other things much more than
these, selling that pearl; so also in the case of the divine sayings, if you
want to unfold the thought of one expression, it will furnish you a great
subject of spiritual abundance, bringing not houses, nor slaves, nor acres of
earth, but sending starting-points of piety and philosophy into the souls of
those who attend. Consider then this very Called, into how great a history of
spiritual affairs it leads us by the hand. And one must first learn what the
Called itself is; and second examine why writing to Corinthians only and to
Romans he wrote thus, and to no other. For he does not do this simply, nor at
random. For if we do not make the addresses of letters simply, but writing to
inferiors, So-and-so to so-and-so, we write, but when to those of equal honor,
we even call the one receiving the letter master in the

address; and when they much exceed our rank, we also add many other names,
having a greater service; if then we have used so much providence, and do not
write to all in one manner, but we make the appellations corresponding to the
difference of the persons receiving; much more did Paul not simply, nor at
random, send thus to these, and otherwise to those, but with some spiritual
wisdom. That, sending to none of the others, he called himself called in the
preface of the Letter, it is possible for us, going through the beginnings of
the Letters themselves, to learn this. And why he did this, it would be ours
thereafter to say, when we first show what the called is, and what Paul wanted
to teach us through this expression. What then does he want to teach us
through calling himself called? That he did not himself come forward to the
Master first, but being called he listened; he did not himself seek and find,
but he was found wandering; he did not himself look up to the light first, but
the light let its own rays go toward that man's eyes, and disabling the
outside eyes, so opened the ones within. Wanting, then, to educate us that of
all his achievements he reckons nothing to himself, but to God who called, he
calls himself called. For the one who opened the gates of the pit for me, he
says, and the stadium, this one also becomes cause of the crowns; the one who
furnished the beginning and throwing down the root, this one also furnished me
the starting-points of the fruits that sprouted for me after these things. For
this reason also elsewhere again, having said, I toiled more than all, he
added, Yet not I, but the grace that is with me. The name Called, then, is an
indication of nothing =51.151= else, than that Paul thinks nothing of his own
achievements to be his own, but lays all things up to the Master God. Which
Christ also taught the disciples saying, You did not choose me, but I chose
you; and the Apostle hints this same thing again upon the same Letter saying:
Then I will know, just as I also was known; for now, he says, I did not know
first, but I myself was known first. For while he was persecuting and sacking
the Church, Christ called him, saying: Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?
For this reason he calls himself called. Why did he send thus to Corinthians?
This Corinth is a metropolis of Achaia, and it was leafy with spiritual gifts;
and very reasonably; for it first enjoyed Paul's tongue, and just as a vine,
enjoying some best and careful farmer, is leafy with many leaves, and is
continually heavy with much fruit; so also that city, having first shared, as
of some best farmer, in Paul's teaching, and having luxuriated for a long time
in this man's wisdom, bloomed with all the goods; and this was not only a
surplus of spiritual gifts, but it also had a great plenty of worldly
advantages. For in wisdom of words outside, and in wealth and power it
mastered the other cities. And these things puffed it and lifted it toward
arrogance, and through the arrogance split it into many parts. For such is the
nature of pride; it tears apart the bond of love, and splits the neighbor off,
and makes each one who has acquired it be by himself. And just as a wall
puffed dissolves the building, so also a soul puffed does not endure the
joining toward another, which Corinth suffered then; and they were in faction
toward one another, and they cut the Church into many parts, and they set
countless other teachers over themselves, and having come to be according to
clans and companies they harmed the dignity of the Church. For the dignity of
a Church is when those gathered keep a sequence of a body toward themselves.

enjoyed Paul's teaching, that they were filled with spiritual gifts, that they
also mastered in worldly advantages, that through these things having become
arrogant they were torn from one another, and some assigned themselves to
these, and some to those. That they first enjoyed Paul's teaching, hear how
Paul hinted this very thing: For if, he says, you have many pedagogues in
Christ Jesus, yet not many fathers. For in Christ Jesus through the Gospel I
begot you; and the one who begot first leads what is born into light. And

that in everything you were enriched in him, so that you are not lacking in
any gift. And indeed that they shared in the wisdom =51.152= outside, from the
many and long words he stretches against the wisdom, he made clear to us. For
nowhere having done this quickly in another Letter, here he has used even much
accusation; and very reasonably. For since from here the inflammation came to
be, there he also brought on the cutting, saying thus: For Christ did not send
me to baptize, but to evangelize, not in wisdom of word, so that the cross of
Christ may not be emptied. See how great an accusation of the wisdom outside,
that not only does it appear contributing nothing toward piety, but it even
becomes a hindrance and a block. For just as the splendid among bodies, and
faces well-formed and beautiful, if they receive some addition of adornment
from outside, are harmed into the glory of their own shapeliness, the
paintings and the under-drawings and the other artifice dividing toward
themselves the praise of the beauty; but if you bring nothing upon them, you
more show forth their shapeliness, the bloom wrestling naked by itself, and
enjoying the wonder whole; so also in the case of piety and the spiritual
bride, if you bring in upon her something of the things outside, either
wealth, or power, or force of words, you emptied her glory, not having let her
wonder appear whole, but having divided her glory into many; but if you let
her wrestle naked by herself, having stood all the human things off, then
accurately all her beauty will appear, then the unconquerable force will shine
out clearly, when, having needed neither wealth, nor wisdom, nor power, nor
nobility, nor any other of the human things, she can master and prevail over
all, through cheap and low and resourceless and poor and private people, even
over impious rhetors and philosophers and tyrants, and mastering the whole
world. For this reason Paul also said: I did not come according to excellence
of word, preaching to you the testimony of God; and, God chose the foolish
things of the world, so that he may put the wise to shame. He did not say
simply, the foolish things, but, The foolish things of the world; and it is
not altogether, that the foolish things of the world are also foolish with
God; but many of those who seem here to be mindless are wiser than all the
others with God; just as also many of those living in poverty here are more
abundant than all with God. Since that Lazarus too, being poorer than all in
the world, was more abundant than all in the heavens. He calls, then,
foolishness of the world those who do not have a sharpened tongue, those who
have not shared in the wisdom outside, those deprived of fluency. And these,
he says, God chose, so that he may put the wise to shame. And how, tell me,
are those put to shame through these? Through the experience of the facts. For
when you examine the widow sitting outside and begging, and often even being
maimed in the body, about

immortality of soul, about resurrection of bodies, about providence of God,
about the exchange according to worth, about the accounts there, about the
fearful lawcourt, about the goods stored for those who achieve, about the

answers with accuracy and much full-assurance; but the philosopher who thinks

even gape, nor has he to open a mouth about these things; then you will know

again, The Lord knows the reasonings of people, that they are vain.

6. But that the Corinthians shared in wisdom is clear from these things; and
that they thought great things and were puffed, again is clear from the Letter

added saying: And you are puffed. And that from the arrogance they were
quarreling toward one another, he made this itself clear again saying: For
where there is among you strife and zeal and divisions, are you not fleshly,
and do you walk according to a person? And what is the manner of the strife?

this, that each of you says: I am of Paul, and I of Apollos, and I of Cephas.

and Apollos, but by these names he wants to hide those who have become causes
of the faction, so as not, having set them in the open, to make them more
contentious, and to lead them into greater shamelessness. For that they did
not assign themselves to Paul and Peter and Apollos, but to some others, this
too is clear from what follows. For having accused them over this split, he
again added saying: And these things, brothers, I transferred in shape unto
myself and Apollos because of you, so that in you you may learn not to think
above what is written, so that one may not be puffed for the one against the
other. For many of the private men, not having a lifting from home, nor a
biting of the neighbors, having set some rulers in front of themselves, used
those men's advantages toward pride against others; and the wisdom of those
teaching them became a pretext of arrogance for them against others; which was
of extreme glory-madness, not having from home something to pride themselves
in, to use the superiorities of others toward contempt against the brothers.
Since, then, they were also lifted into arrogance and were in faction, and
they cut themselves into many parts, and they thought great things over the
teaching, as having found it from home and from themselves, but not as having
received from above and from the grace of God the doctrines =51.154= of the
truth, wanting to check their puff, he at once from the prefaces called
himself called, all but saying: If I the teacher found nothing from home, nor
did I myself come forward to God first, but being called then I listened, how
can you the disciples, having received the

doctrines from me as well, think great things, as having become finders of
these yourselves? For this reason also going forward he said to them: For who
distinguishes you, and what do you have that you did not receive? and if you
also received, why do you boast as not having received? This Called, then, is
nothing else but a teaching of humility, and a taking down of conceit, and a
checking of all boastfulness; for there is nothing, nothing that could so hold
us together and keep us, as humility, and to be moderate, and to have been
checked and never never to imagine anything great about ourselves. Which
Christ also knowing, and beginning that spiritual teaching, began the
exhortation first from humility, and opening the mouth, he first brought in
this law, saying thus: Blessed are the poor in spirit. For just as someone
about to build a great and splendid house throws down a foundation in
proportion, so as to be able to bear the weight laid on after these things; so
also Christ, raising that great building of philosophy in their souls, as some
foundation and a safe keel and a firm and unmoved base, throws down in advance
the exhortation of humility, knowing that, this being rooted in the minds of
those who hear, all other parts of virtue can be built with security. Just as,
then, this being absent, even if someone achieves all the remaining virtue, he
has toiled at random and in vain and unto nothing needed, according to that
man who built the house upon the sand, who underwent the toil, but did not
enjoy the gain, because he did not throw down a safe foundation; so the one
who pursues any good without humility has lost all things and corrupted them.
And I mean humility, not the one in words, nor the one upon the tongue, but
the one in the mind, the one from the soul, the one in conscience, which God
alone can see. This advantage is enough, even appearing by itself often, to
make God gracious. And the tax-collector showed this; for having no good, nor
being able to appear from achievements, having said only, Be gracious to me
the sinner, he went down justified above the Pharisee; and yet those words

great about himself; but of well-disposition, when, being a sinner, he
confesses this very thing. And if the one conscious of no good to himself,
because he confessed this, what he was, so drew God into goodwill, of how much
freedom of speech will they enjoy who have many achievements of themselves to
say, yet forgetting all those, and numbering themselves among the last?
=51.155= Just as Paul also did; for being first of all the righteous, he said
he was first of the sinners; and he did not only say it, but he was also
persuaded, having learned from the Teacher that after doing all things one
must call oneself useless servants. This is humility, emulate this man you who
have achievements, and the tax-collector you who are full of faults, and let
us confess as we are, and let us strike the breast, and let us persuade the
mind to imagine nothing great about ourselves. For if we are so disposed, this
is enough for us unto an offering and a sacrifice; just as David also said: A
sacrifice to God is a crushed spirit; a crushed and humbled heart God will not
despise. He did not say simply humbled, but also crushed; for the crushed is
also broken; for not even, if it wants, can it be lifted. So let us too not
only humble our soul, but also crush it and =51.156= sting it; and it is
crushed remembering its own faults continually. If we humble it so, not even
wanting toward conceit will it

be able to stand up, as if some bridle of the conscience taking hold of it
when it is being awakened, and checking it and persuading it to be moderate in
all things. So we will be able with God also to find grace; For as great as
you are, he says, so much humble yourself, and before the Lord you will find
grace. And the one who has found grace with God will sense nothing hard, but
both here he will be able with the grace of God to run through all those
terrible things with ease, and to flee the punishments lying there for those
who sin, the grace of God going before everywhere, and making all things
propitious for him; which may we all enjoy in Christ Jesus our Lord, through
whom and with whom to the Father be glory, together with the Holy Spirit, now
and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
