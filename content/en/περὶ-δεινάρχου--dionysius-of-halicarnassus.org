* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Having said nothing about Dinarchus the orator in the writings on the ancient
orators, because the man was neither inventor of a style of his own, as Lysias
and Isocrates and Isaeus were, nor the perfecter of what others had invented,
as we judge Demosthenes and Aeschines and Hyperides to be, but seeing that
this man too has been thought worthy of a name among many for force of
speeches and has left public and private speeches neither few nor
contemptible, I thought I ought not to pass him over, but to go through both
his life and his style and to distinguish the genuine and the false speeches,
which I think more necessary than all things, or at least than most, for those
who do not practice rhetoric from an apron. At the same time, seeing that
neither Callimachus nor the grammarians from Pergamon have written anything
exact about him, but have erred through examining none of the more accurate
things about him, so as not only to have been false in many points but also
for speeches that in no way belong to him to be assigned to this man as of
Dinarchus, and those written by him to be said to be of others; but Demetrius
of Magnesia, who seemed to have become very learned, speaking also about this
man in the treatise on namesakes and giving the impression that he would say
something exact about him, was cheated of that reputation. And nothing
prevents setting out the man's words as well. The things written by him are
these: /And we met with four Dinarchuses; of whom one is from the Attic
orators, one has compiled the mythologies about Crete, one is older than both
of these, Delian by race, having treated now epic, now affairs, and a fourth
who has composed a discourse about Homer. And I wish to go through each in
turn, and first about the orator. This man, then, according to my judgment at
least, falls short of the charm of Hyperides in nothing, so as to say: and he
would even have overtaken him. For he brings a persuasive enthymeme and a
figure of every kind, and he has come so well into persuasiveness that he
makes it stand before the hearers that the matter has not happened otherwise
than as he himself says. And one would think simple those who supposed the
speech Against Demosthenes to be this man's; for it is far from the style. But
nevertheless so much darkness has spread over the surface, that it has
happened that his other speeches, being somewhere above one hundred and sixty,
are all unknown, and the one not written by him is thought to be of him alone.
And the diction of Dinarchus is ethical in the strict sense, moving passion,
falling short of the Demosthenic style in almost bitterness and tension only,
and lacking nothing of the persuasive and the proper./

From these things it is possible to find nothing exact, nor even true; for he
has shown neither the man's race nor the times in which he lived nor the place
in which he spent his time, but he was eager only about common and circulating
names and stated a number of speeches agreeing with none of the [lost] . And
it ought to have been the opposite. The things, then, which I myself have
grasped through my own inquiry are these: Dinarchus the orator was son of
Sostratus, Corinthian by race, and having come to Athens at the time when the
schools of the philosophers and orators were flourishing, he associated with
Theophrastus and with Demetrius of Phalerum. And being gifted in political
speeches, while those about Demosthenes were still at their peak he began to
write speeches and advanced little by little into reputation. And he
flourished most after the death of Alexander, when Demosthenes and the other
orators had fallen into perpetual exiles and deaths, and no man worthy of
account was left after them. And he continued for a period of fifteen years
writing speeches for those who wished it, until Cassander occupied the city.
And in the archonship of Anaxicrates, in which those about the kings Antigonus
and Demetrius abolished the garrison in Munychia set up by Cassander, having
an accusation together with the most distinguished of the Athenians, though he
himself was a foreigner, of overthrowing the people, seeing the Athenians
stirred up and suspecting him especially because he was rich, lest through
this he suffer some terrible thing, he did not wait to go into a lawcourt, but
having left the city and gone to Chalcis in Euboea, he spent there the time
from Anaxicrates until Philip, which became fifteen years, if some might come
about for him through Theophrastus and the

other friends' return, waiting. And when the king granted that he too come
back with the other exiles, having arrived at Athens and been lodged with one
of his friends, Proxenos, he loses the gold, being already old and weak in his
sight. And as Proxenos was careless toward the search, he brought a suit
against him about the money, himself never having come forward into a lawcourt
before. This is the man's life. And each of these things is shown both from
the histories of Philochorus and from what he himself wrote about himself in
the speech Against Proxenos, which was spoken after the exile and has this
indictment attached: /Dinarchus son of Sostratus, Corinthian, against
Proxenos, with whom I live, for damage of two talents. Proxenos damaged me,
having received me into his own house in the country, when having been exiled
from Athens I was coming back from Chalcis, of gold two hundred and
eighty-five staters, which I brought from Chalcis with Proxenos knowing and I
entered his house having them, and silver plate worth not less than twenty
minae, having plotted against these./ Nevertheless also in the speech itself,
at once in the beginning he has shown concerning his never having entered any
suit before, and in the earlier parts after these, first in the proem he has
shown the damage that came to him through Proxenos, and in what follows he
goes through both the exile and all the rest, from which the things said
before become clear, and still besides these, that he remained a foreigner and
spoke the speech being already an old man, from what he has said at the end of
the suit.

These things Dinarchus himself says about himself. And Philochorus in the
Attic histories speaks thus about both the exile of those who overthrew the
people and about the return again: /For in the archonship of Anaxicrates the
city of the Megarians was taken at once; and Demetrius the king, having come
back from Megara, made his preparations toward Munychia and having torn down
the walls gave them back to the people. And later many of the citizens were
impeached, among whom also Demetrius of Phalerum. And of those impeached,
those who did not wait for the trial they condemned to death by the vote, and
those who answered, having heard, they acquitted./ These things, then, are
from the eighth. And in the ninth he says: /And when this year had passed and
another was coming in, such a sign happened on the acropolis: a dog, having
entered the temple of Athena Polias and gone down into the Pandroseion, having
gone up onto the altar of Zeus Herkeios under the olive, lay there. And it is
ancestral for the Athenians that a dog not go up onto the acropolis. And about
the same time also in the sky by day, the sun shining out and the weather
being clear, a star became visible for some time. And we, being asked about
both the sign and the apparition, toward what it tends, said that both
foretold a return of exiles and that this would not be from a change of
affairs, but in the established constitution; and it happened that the
judgment was fulfilled./

These things having been said beforehand, one thing still remains and the most
necessary, to mark off his age, in order that we may also have something clear
to say about the speeches both genuine of his and not. We set him down, then,
as having come back from the exile in his seventieth year, as he himself also
says, calling himself an old man; from which time we have also been accustomed
especially to call those in this age. And these things being laid down by a
rough reckoning (for we do not have the exact), he would have been born in the
archonship of Nicophemus. And if someone shall say that he is either older or
younger than the times said, besides saying nothing sound he will also take
away many speeches, or rather all except five or six, saying that he is older
than some and younger than others. Nevertheless, saying that he began to write
speeches from the twenty-fifth or twenty-sixth year we would not err,
especially as those about Demosthenes were then at their peak. And the
twenty-sixth from Nicophemus is Pythodemus. So that as many speeches
circulating as his as we find older than this archon, if someone shall set
them down as genuine, we would reasonably disbelieve; and still also the
contests completed from Anaxicrates until Philip we would likewise list among
the false; for people would not sail to Chalcis for the sake of speeches,
whether private or public; for they were not so completely at a loss for
speeches. And since the man's time has been found as exactly as possible,
toward which we shall also distinguish of the speeches those genuine and not,
it is already the moment to speak also about his style. And it is hard to
define. For he had neither

anything common nor anything of his own, neither in the private nor in the
public contests, but he also becomes like the speeches of Lysias in some
places and like those of Hyperides and of Demosthenes. And of these one would
have many examples to set out. Of the Lysian style both in the speech
concerning Mnesicles and in that against Lysicrates for Nicomachus and in many
others. And of the Hyperidean, which is somehow more exact in arrangements and
more noble in constructions than the Lysian, it is possible to find examples
in more than thirty speeches of Dinarchus, and not least also in that
concerning the diamartyria of Agathon. Nevertheless also of the Demosthenic
style, which he imitated most, one would be able to say far more, especially
also in that against Polyeuctus. For he makes the proem similarly to that man,
and through the whole speech he has remained close.

How then could one be able to recognize the speeches genuine to him? First if
he should know the styles of the others, then he should bring the speeches
resembling Lysias to this man, and those that seemed to some to be of
Hyperides he should say are of Dinarchus, bidding a long farewell to the
titles of the books, and those that have approached the Demosthenic style he
should as far as possible affirm to be this man's. For in the case of the
other orators whom he has imitated, the greatest recognition is the uniformity
of the speeches. For example Lysias in both the private and the public
contests is consistent with himself [lost] and as to the department of
diction, according to the clearness of the words and a composition that seems
to be natural and smooth, but differs from every speech according to the
pleasure. And Hyperides is inferior to Lysias according to the choice of
words, but differs according to the department of subject-matter. And he
narrates in many ways, sometimes according to nature, sometimes going from the
end to the beginning. And he proves not by enthymeme only, but also broadening
out by epicheireme. And Demosthenes, surpassing both these and all the others,
having imitated all things and chosen out the finest of all, is clear even by
the voice alone, and clear also by what is fitting in each speech one by one,
and nevertheless also by the composition and by the winding of the figures and
by the arrangement and by the passion and, the greatest thing, by the force.
And Dinarchus is neither similar in all things nor inventor of anything of his
own, through which one will know him accurately, or in this way: for he
greatly displays both imitations and their difference as toward the archetype
of the speeches, as also in the case of the pupils of Isocrates and of
Isocrates himself.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

Let there be, then, certain speeches titled as being of Dinarchus, having much
likeness toward the Lysian. Of these let the one who wishes to make the
discrimination first consider the peculiarity of that man, then if he sees
both virtue and charm blooming upon the speeches and the choice of words being
present and that nothing of the things said is lifeless, let him say with
confidence that these are of Lysias. And if he finds neither the charm similar
nor the persuasive and the exactness of the words nor that which touches the
truth, let him leave them among the speeches of Dinarchus. Likewise also in
the case of those of Hyperides: if he has the strength of the diction, the
simplicity of the composition, the timeliness of the matters, and of the
construction that which is not tragic nor puffed up (for these are the
greatest peculiarities of that man), let him say they are of Hyperides. And if
he is more deficient in these very things, even if all the rest has been
written not poorly, let him list them again among those of Dinarchus. And we
suppose the same also about Demosthenes. If both the magnificence of the
diction and the variation of the composition and the living quality of the
passions and the bitterness that runs through every stroke and the
intelligent, both the breath and the force, attend upon all, let there be no
longer anything preventing listing them among those of Demosthenes. And if the
peak in each of these is lacking, or the similar through every form, let them
remain among the Dinarchan. And to speak generally, one would find two manners
of the difference of imitation as toward the ancient models; of which one is
natural and taken from much instruction and companionship, and the one next to
this from the precepts of the art. About the former, then, what would one even
say? And about the second what would one have to say except that upon all the
archetypes some natural charm and bloom is conspicuous, but upon those
constructed from these, even if they come to the peak of imitation, there is
present nevertheless something studied and not existing from nature. And by
this precept not only do orators distinguish orators, but also painters the
works of Apelles and of those who imitated that man, and modelers the works of
Polyclitus, and sculptors the works of Pheidias.

And those who say they imitate Plato and are not able to take the ancient and
the lofty and the graceful and the beautiful, but bring in dithyrambic and
vulgar words, are easily convicted according to this. And those who say they
emulate Thucydides and take with difficulty the tense and the solid and the
forceful and the things like these, but take up the solecism-appearing figures
and the unclear, would be caught very readily from this precept. Just as also
in the case of the orators those who imitate Hyperides, having missed that
charm and the rest of the power, became some dry men, such as the Rhodian
orators about Artamenes and Aristocles and Philagrius and Molon have become;
and those who wished to stamp Isocrates and the things of Isocrates, supine
and frigid and loosely-wound and untrue; and these are those about Timaeus and
Psaon and Sosigenes. And those who took up Demosthenes and pursued that man's
virtues were praised for the sake of the choice, but they were not able to
take the strongest of that orator's works. Of these one would set Dinarchus
down as having become the best. And he falls short of Demosthenes according to
the choice of words by the force, according to the composition by the variety
of the figures and by the variation, according to the invention of the
epicheiremes by not taking new and unexpected things but things obvious and
lying in the middle, according to the arrangement by the order and by the
workings-out of the epicheiremes and by the preparatory constructions and by
the approaches and by the other technical precepts which belong about this
form, and he falls short of him most by the proportion and by the timely and
by the fitting. And I say these things not in the general manner, as if he
succeeds in none of these, but in the more common and as for the most part.
For through this very thing some also said he was a rustic Demosthenes, having
taken this opinion about him according to the deficiency of the arrangement;
for the rustic differed from the civic body not in form, but in dressing and
in some disposition of the form.

The things, then, which it was possible to find and to write about the man's
style are these, and I shall turn to the discrimination of the speeches. To
the genuine, then, the listing itself alone will be attached, and to the false
the things of both the proof and the cause worked out exactly, through which
we reject each of them. And since the discrimination of the times is necessary
toward these things, we shall set out the archons at Athens, from the time
from which we supposed Dinarchus to have been born until the return given him
after the exile, being seventy. And they are these: Nicophemus, Callimedes,
Eucharistus, Cephisodotus, Agathocles, Elpines, Callistratus, Diotimus,
Thudemus, Aristodemus, Theellus, Apollodorus, Callimachus, Theophilus,
Themistocles, Archias, Eubulus, Lyciscus, Pythodotus, Sosigenes, Nicomachus,
Theophrastus, Lysimachides, Chaeronides, Phrynichus, Pythodemus; in this man's
year we supposed him first to write speeches for a lawcourt. And after this
Evaenetus, Ctesicles, Nicocrates, Nicetes, Aristophanes, Aristophon,
Cephisophon, Euthycritus, Hegemon, Chremes, Anticles, Hegesias, Cephisodorus,

Against Theodorus, audit speech: /Least of all, gentlemen./ It is older than
the age of Dinarchus. For it was spoken in the archonship of Theophilus or
Themistocles, in the third or fourth year after the archon Theellus, as
becomes clear from the speech itself, when he was not yet in his fifteenth
year, as we showed. Against the Kerykes: /If the father, gentlemen./ This
contest was spoken in the archonship of Eubulus or of Lyciscus after Eubulus,
when he was not yet in his twentieth year [lost] for the speech about someone
voted off happened in the year of Archias after Themistocles. And each of the
things said becomes clear from the speech itself. Against Moschion, Nicodicus
having registered him: /Of those who voted off, gentlemen, this Moschion here,
having contributed./ And this speech too was spoken in the same times as the
former. And it is shown both from the very beginning of the speech and the
things of what follows. Against Menecles, of an apagoge: /Gentlemen of the
jury, and of the laws according to which./ And this too was spoken while
Dinarchus was still a child. For the one being judged is Menecles who
convicted the priestess Ninos, and the accuser is the son of Ninos. And these
things are older than the prime of Dinarchus. For the speech of Demosthenes
concerning the name, which we have shown, in which he mentions these things,
was completed in the archonship of Theellus or Apollodorus, as we have shown
in the writings on Demosthenes. And if Demosthenes mentions him there as of
Menecles already dead, saying: /for you all saw him associating, while he
lived, with Menecles,/ it is some old speech. And that this is the Menecles,
the accuser has shown in the speech itself. Adjudication for the Athmoneis
concerning the myrtle and the smilax: /I pray then to Demeter and to Kore./ It
is older than the prime of Dinarchus. For it was spoken in the archonship of
Nicomachus, as becomes clear from the speech itself, when the orator was in
his twenty-first year. These, then, are the spurious speeches circulating as
his that are older than his prime. And after the withdrawal from Athens to
Chalcis these: Adjudication of the priestess of Demeter toward the hierophant:
/Of many and unexpected things, gentlemen of the jury./ This speech was spoken
when he had already gone into exile, as becomes clear from it. For the speaker
mentions in it the oligarchy that had taken hold. Impeachment against
Timocrates for overthrow of the people: /You do deeds./ This one is clear even
from the title itself as being spurious. Against Spoudias: /And I promised
that I would accuse also in the assembly./ After the overthrow of the
oligarchy this too was spoken and already Dinarchus appears as being in exile,
as becomes clear especially from the speech itself. Adjudication of the
Eudanemoi toward the Kerykes concerning the kanoun: /By no means such
matters./ And this too was spoken in the same times, the orator already having
gone into exile, as is shown again in the speech itself. Attic: /Of all they
were likewise./ And this too was spoken in those times, as becomes clear also
in the speech itself. Aetolian: /And we, Aetolian gentlemen, ambassadors./
This was spoken while the oligarchy was established, by the exiles from Athens
asking the Aetolians to help them, since Cassander also was attacking them
though they were free, as this becomes clear in the speech itself. Therefore
it is not likely that Dinarchus, being a friend of those who established the
oligarchy, would contend together with those attempting to overthrow it, nor
is it possible that they from Athe ns speeches † to overtake. Demegoric for
Diphilus, asking for gifts: /Because it is not easy./ I was persuaded that
this speech had been written by Demosthenes, because Demosthenes proposed the
gifts for him, as Dinarchus has shown in the speech Against Demosthenes, and
because at the end of the speech Diphilus calls Demosthenes as advocate. And I
think it unconvincing that Demosthenes, being so well-disposed toward Diphilus
as to write the honors in this way, would overlook taking a speech from
Dinarchus. Defense for Hermias, superintendent of the emporium, concerning the
things accused: /I ask you, gentlemen./ From the style itself one would find
that the speech is not of Dinarchus (for it is watery and weak and frigid),
but one would rather set it down as of Democlides or of Menesaechmus or of
some one of the others of that sort. And I take from him also both speeches
for Menesaechmus, of which one is about the sacrifice at Delos: /we supplicate
you and/; and the one toward Pericles and Democrates, whose beginning: /we
think, gentlemen/ both through the style (for it is watery and poured-out and
frigid) and because the one speaking them, being neither without fame and
having taken over the administration of the public funds after Lycurgus, and
himself having been examined often, as he indicates about all things in the
speeches, would not have been unable in both private and public contests, so
as to use Dinarchus as speechwriter. On not surrendering Harpalus to Alexander
/it is not worth wondering then./ Nor does this speech show the style of
Dinarchus. For if nothing else, one would find in it at least much that is
silly and sophistical, which is furthest from the styles of Dinarchus. Delian:
/Of Apollo and of Rhoio daughter of Staphylus./ This is not of the orator but
of some other writer. And it shows from the manner and the style that it is
archaic and runs around the local history of Delos and Leros. Against
Demosthenes for illegal proposals: /You are accustomed, gentlemen./ This is
carried in the Pergamene tables as of Callicrates. And I do not know if it is
that man's (for I have not met with any of the works of Callicrates), but that
it stands very far from the speeches of Dinarchus, being cheap and empty and
not keeping away from a private person's babble, I am persuaded.

Special plea toward Pedieus: /According to this law./ This speech was spoken
in the archonship of Aristodemus, as becomes clear from the speech itself. For
the cleruchs sent out to Samos were sent out in this archon's year, as
Philochorus says in the histories. And Dinarchus at that time did not yet have
his tenth year. Toward Melesander concerning the trierarchy: /As the laws
command./ of which the speech [lost] but the speaker makes the speech as of
the wrong having happened in the archonship of Molon. And he says that he
enters the contest in the following year in the archonship of Nicophemus, in
whose year Dinarchus is found to have been born. Toward Boeotus concerning the
name: /With no busybodying./ Even if those who take this speech from
Demosthenes and attach it to Dinarchus were not refuted by the other things,
they would at least be shown lying by the time. For he mentions the expedition
to Pylae as having happened recently, and the expedition of the Athenians to
Pylae happened in the archonship of Thudemus, when Dinarchus was in his eighth
year. Toward Mantitheus concerning a dowry: /Of all things it is most
grievous./ This follows the former speech and has many such things according
to the diction as would be of the same orator, outside the age of Dinarchus
[lost] the accuser has contested the contest not many years later, but two or
three, as we have shown more exactly about them in the writing on Demosthenes.
