* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Isocrates the Athenian was born in the eighty-sixth Olympiad, when Lysimachus
was archon at Athens, in the fifth year before the Peloponnesian War,
twenty-two years younger than Lysias, and he was the son of Theodorus, one of
the moderate citizens, who owned slave flute-makers and had his livelihood
from this work. Having met with a seemly upbringing and having been educated
no worse than any of the Athenians, as soon as he became a man he desired
philosophy. Having become a hearer of Prodicus of Ceos and Gorgias of Leontini
and Tisias of Syracuse, who then had the greatest name among the Greeks for
wisdom, and as some relate also of Theramenes the orator, whom the Thirty
killed as seeming to be on the people's side, he was eager to act and speak in
public affairs, but when nature opposed him, having taken away the first and
most sovereign things of the orator, both daring and magnitude of voice,
without which it was not possible to speak in a crowd, he stood off from this
choice; but desiring glory and to be first among the Greeks in wisdom, as he
himself has said, he took refuge in writing what he thought, not making his
choice about small things nor about private contracts nor about the things for
which some others of the sophists of that time, but about Hellenic and royal
and political affairs, from which he supposed that both the cities would be
better governed and private men would have increase toward virtue. For these
things he writes about himself in the Panathenaic speech. And having received
the practice of speeches mixed up by the sophists around Gorgias and
Protagoras, he first went from the eristic and physical to the political, and
he continued being eager about that science itself from which, as he himself
says, deliberating and speaking and doing what is advantageous comes to those
who have learned. And having become the most distinguished of those who
flourished at the same time and having educated the strongest of the young
both at Athens and in the rest of Greece, of whom some became best in forensic
speeches, and some excelled in taking part in the city and doing public
business, and others wrote the common actions of Greeks and barbarians, and
having made his own school an image of the city of the Athenians according to
the colonies of the speeches, having acquired as much wealth as none of those
who made money from philosophy, he ended his life in the archonship of
Chaeronides a few days after the battle at Chaeronea, having lived a hundred
years lacking two, having used the judgment to end his own life together with
the good things of the city, it being still unclear how Philip would use his
fortune after taking the rule of the Greeks. These then are the things related
about him in summary.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

The diction which he has used has some such character. It is pure no less than
that of Lysias and puts no word at random, and it is precise in the dialect
that is among the very common and most customary. For this too has fled the
tastelessness of archaic and marked words, and in figurative expression it
differs a little from that of Lysias and is mixed in due measure, and it has
the clear similar to that one and the vivid, and it is ethical and persuasive
and fitting. But it is not compact, as that one is, and hammered together and
well suited to forensic contests, but it is rather supine and poured out
richly, nor indeed so concise, but both long-legged and slower than the mean.
For what cause it suffers this, I will say after a little. Nor does it display
the natural and simple and agonistic composition, as that of Lysias does, but
one made rather toward pompous solemnity and varied and in some places more
seemly than that one and in some more overwrought. For this man pursues
elegance from every side and aims at speaking smoothly rather than simply, and
he avoids the parallel placements of vowels as loosening the harmonies of the
sounds and spoiling the smoothness of the tones, and he tries to enclose the
thoughts in a period and a circle that is very rhythm-like and not far from
poetic metre, and he is more proper to reading than to use. Therefore his
speeches bear the displays at the festivals and the viewing from the hand, but
they do not endure the contests in assemblies and lawcourts. The cause of this
is that much of the pathetic must be in those; and a period admits this least.
And the assonances and equalities and the antitheses and all the ornament of
such figures is abundant in him and often hurts the rest of the construction
by striking against the ears.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And in general, there being three things, as Theophrastus says, from which the
great and solemn and extraordinary in diction comes, both the selection of
words and the harmony from these and the figures that enclose them, he selects
very well and puts the strongest of the words, but he fits them
overelaborately, straining the musical euphony, and he figures vulgarly and
for the most part becomes frigid either by taking from far off or by the
figures not being fitting to the subjects because of not mastering the mean.
These things however also make his diction longer often, I mean both fitting
all the thoughts into periods and enclosing the periods in the same types of
figures and pursuing good rhythm from every side. For not all things admit
either the same length or a similar figure or equal rhythm. So that it is
necessary to use fillers of words that help nothing and to lengthen the speech
beyond the useful. I say this not as if he does these things throughout (I am
not so mad; for he also sometimes composes the words simply and looses the
period nobly and flees the overwrought and vulgar figures, and most of all in
the deliberative and forensic speeches), but as being for the most part a
slave to the rhythm and to the circle of the period and placing the beauty of
the delivery in the extraordinary I have spoken more commonly about him.
According to these things then I say that the diction of Isocrates falls short
of that of Lysias and still according to the charm. And yet Isocrates is
flowery, if anyone else is, and leading the hearers by pleasure, but he does
not have the same charm as that one. And he falls short of him in this
excellence by as much as bodies assembled by added ornaments fall short of
those beautiful by nature. For the diction of Lysias is born to have the
graceful, but that of Isocrates wishes it. In these excellences then he is
behind Lysias, at least according to my judgment. But he is ahead in those
about to be said: he is higher than that one in interpretation and far more
magnificent and more dignified. For wonderful and great is the height of the
construction of Isocrates, proper to a heroic rather than a human nature. It
seems to me then that one would not be off the mark who likened the rhetoric
of Isocrates to the art of Polycleitus and Phidias according to the solemn and
the grand in craft and the dignified, and that of Lysias to that of Calamis
and Callimachus for the sake of the fineness and the charm. For just as of
those some are more successful than the others in the lesser and human works,
and some are more dexterous in the greater and more divine, so also of the
orators the one is wiser in the small, and the other more extraordinary in the
great, perhaps being even by nature some high-minded man, and if not, at any
rate by choice pursuing the solemn and the wonderful. These then about the
diction of the orator.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

The theorems in the subject-matter place are some similar to those of Lysias,
some stronger. The invention of the enthymemes that fit each subject is
abundant and dense and falling short of that one in nothing. And judgment
likewise coming from great intelligence. But arrangement and divisions of the
matters and the working-out according to argument and the interrupting of
sameness by private changes and foreign episodes and all the other goods that
there are about the practical economy are far greater in Isocrates and
stronger, and most of all the choice of the speeches about which he was eager,
and the beauty of the hypotheses in which he made his studies. From which he
would make those who give him their mind not only clever at speaking but also
serious in character, useful to house and city and all Greece. For the
strongest teachings toward virtue are to be found in the speeches of
Isocrates. And I at least say that those who are going to acquire not some
part of political power but the whole of it ought to have this orator through
the hand. And if anyone practices the true philosophy, not loving only the
theoretical of it but also the practical, and not choosing those things from
which he himself will have a painless life, but those from which he will help
many, I would exhort him to imitate the choice of that orator.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Who would not become a lover of the city and a lover of the people, or who
would not practice political nobility, having read his Panegyric? In which
going through the virtues of the ancients he says that those who freed Greece
from the barbarians were not only clever in war but also noble in character
and ambitious and temperate, who at least cared more for the common than for
the private, and desired the things of others less than the impossible, and
judged happiness not toward silver but toward good repute, thinking they would
leave to their children a great wealth and an unenvied honor among the masses;
and they held a seemly death better than an inglorious life, and they looked
not how the laws would be well and precisely for them, but how the moderation
of daily practices would go out from none of the ancestral things; and so they
had their relations toward one another ambitiously and politically, that they
even made their factions toward one another over which of them would do more
good things for the city, not over which, having destroyed the others, would
themselves rule the rest. And using the same eagerness also toward Greece they
won over the cities by serving them and they held them by persuading with
benefactions rather than by forcing with arms, using words more trustworthy
than now oaths, and thinking it right to abide by the treaties rather than by
necessities, and thinking it right to judge such things about their inferiors
as they would have thought it right for their superiors to think about
themselves, and so prepared in their minds as having their own cities
privately, but inhabiting Greece as a common fatherland.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And who that is a man of greatness and leading some power would not love the
things written by him to Philip the Macedonian? In which he thinks it right
that a man who is a general and master of such great authority should
reconcile the cities that differ but not dash them against one another, and
should make Greece great from small, having looked down on ambition about
small things and should attempt such works from which, if he succeeds, he will
be the most distinguished of all leaders, and if he fails he will at least
acquire the goodwill from the Greeks; of which those who obtain it are far
more to be envied than those who have subdued great cities and many lands. And
further he exhorts him to imitate the choice of Heracles and of the other
leaders who campaigned with the Greeks against the barbarians, and he says
that those who differ from others ought to choose the actions that have
greatness, and to accomplish them with virtue, considering that we have a
mortal body, but become immortal through virtue, and we are vexed at those who
are insatiably disposed toward some other of the goods, but we praise those
acquiring honor greater than what they have, and that of the other things
about which there are human eagernesses, wealth and rule and dominion, it
often happens that enemies become masters, but of virtue and of the goodwill
among the masses the household of each inherit. For there is much necessity
that the dynasts who read these things be filled with greater spirit and
desire virtue more.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

And who would more turn toward justice and piety both each man privately and
the cities whole in common than the speech On the Peace? For in this he
persuades the Athenians not to desire the things of others, but to be content
with what is present, and to spare the small cities as if they were
possessions, and to try to hold the allies  [lost]  both by benefactions, but
not by necessities nor by violences. And to imitate of the ancestors not those
who lived before the Decelean affairs, who all but destroyed the city, but
those before the Persian, who continued practicing nobility. And he shows that
it is not the many triremes nor the Greeks ruled with force that make the city
great, but the just choices and helping those who are wronged. And he calls
them to make the goodwill of the Greeks their own for the city, thinking it
the greatest portion toward happiness, and to be warlike in preparations and
practices, but peaceful by doing injustice to no one in nothing, teaching that
toward wealth nor toward glory nor wholly toward happiness would nothing
contribute so great a power as virtue and the parts of this; and blaming those
who have not supposed these things, who held injustice profitable and
advantageous toward daily life, but justice unprofitable and more helpful to
others than to those who have it. For of these I do not know if anyone could
speak speeches either better or truer or more fitting to philosophy.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And who having read the Areopagitic speech would not become more orderly, or
who would not wonder at the attempt of the orator? Who dared to converse about
constitution with the Athenians, thinking it right to change the democracy
then established as greatly harming the city, about which none of the
demagogues attempted to speak, seeing it had gone forward into such great
disorder that not even the magistrates still ruled the private men, but each
both doing and saying whatever became according to his pleasure, and untimely
frankness being thought by all a democratic liberty, and to recover the
constitution established by Solon and Cleisthenes. Going through the choice
and the characters of which he says that the men of that time held it more
terrible to speak against their elders than  [lost]  and they thought
democracy not licentiousness but temperance. And they placed the free not
in despising the magistrates but in doing the things commanded, and they
granted authority
to none of the licentious, but assigned the offices to the best, supposing
that the others would be such as those who administer the city are. And
instead of repairing their private substances from the public they expended
their private riches into the common. And besides these the fathers took more
care of their sons when they had become men than they used to when they were
children, considering that the common is helped not from that education but
more from this temperance. And they supposed good practices better than
precise legislation, looking not how they would restrain those who err by
punishments, but how they would prepare each to practice nothing worthy of a
penalty, and thinking that the fatherland ought to live in great liberty, but
that it should be permitted to private men to do nothing that the laws forbid.
And to endure the terrible things and not be struck out of their wits by
disasters.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

And who would more persuade both city and men than the orator in many other
places, but most of all in the speech written to the Lacedaemonians, which is
entitled Archidamus, and has taken as its hypothesis that about not giving up
Messene to the Boeotians nor doing what is commanded by the enemies? For the
Lacedaemonians had fared ill both in the battle at Leuctra and in many others
after that, and the affairs of the Thebans were blooming and had gone forward
into greatness of empire, but those of Sparta had become low and unworthy of
the ancient hegemony. At last then, in order that the city might obtain peace,
it was deliberating whether it must stand off from Messenia, the Boeotians
putting this necessity upon it. Seeing it then about to do things unworthy of
the ancestors he composed this speech for Archidamus, who was young and not
yet king, but had many hopes of obtaining this honor. In which he goes through
first that the Lacedaemonians acquired Messene justly, both the children of
Cresphontes having handed it over when they were thrown out of the rule, and
the god having commanded them to receive it and to avenge those who were
wronged, and besides these the war having confirmed the possession, and time
having made it held and firm. And he teaches that they will furnish the city
as a base and a refuge not to Messenians who no longer exist but to slaves and
helots. And he goes through the dangers of the ancestors, which they endured
for the sake of the hegemony, and he reminds them of the glory existing about
them among the Greeks, and he exhorts them not to fall together with the
fortunes nor to despair of the changes, considering that many already having
greater power than the Thebans were mastered by the weaker, and many shut up
into a siege and suffering more terrible things than the Lacedaemonians
destroyed those who campaigned against them. And he makes an example of the
city of the Athenians, which having become uprooted from much happiness
underwent the last dangers, in order that it might not do what was commanded
to the barbarians. And he exhorts them also to endure upon the present things
and to be of good courage about the future, knowing that cities set such
disasters right by a good constitution and by experiences of wars, in which
Sparta excelled the other cities. And he thinks that it is not those who are
faring badly who ought to desire peace, for whom there is hope from innovation
of changing the affairs toward the better, but those who are fortunate; for in
the absence of danger is the guard of the present goods. And having gone
through many other things besides these, as many splendid deeds as were done
both in common and privately by the most distinguished of them in the wars,
and of how much shame they will do things worthy, and that they will also be
slandered among the Greeks, and having calculated also that from every side
some help will be for them if they make the contest, both from gods and from
allies and from all men to whom the power of the Thebans growing is invidious,
and having shown the disorder and confusion holding the cities while Boeotians
are stewards of Greece, at the end, even if none of these things is going to
happen and no other hope of safety is left, he bids them leave the city,
teaching them that they must send children and women and the rest of the crowd
both to Sicily and Italy and the other friendly places, and themselves, having
occupied a place which is strongest and most suitable toward the war, plunder
the enemies both by land and by sea. For no power will think it right to come
to close quarters with men strongest of the Greeks in war, and disposed
desperately toward living, and having a just anger and a seemly pretext of
necessity. For these things I at least would say that he advises not for
Lacedaemonians only but also for the other Greeks and for all men, far better
than all philosophers, who make the end of life virtue and the noble.

And although I have many other speeches of his to go through, written to
cities and dynasts and private men, of which some call the masses toward
concord and temperance, and some lead the dynasts toward moderation and lawful
rule, and some make the lives of private men orderly, suggesting what each
must do, fearing that the speech be lengthened for me beyond what is needed, I
will let these go, and for the sake of the things said before becoming more
easy to follow for me, and for the sake of the difference by which he differs
from Lysias, having gathered their excellences into a shorter speech I will
pass over to the examples.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

First then I said that an excellence of speeches is the pure interpretation,
in which I found no difference in either. Next the precision of the dialect
then customary; and this too I saw similar in both. After these I reckoned
that both have used the proper and customary and common words, but the diction
of Isocrates, having taken on something of the figurative construction, went
forward as far as not to hurt. I declared that both master clearness and
vividness, but in bringing out the thoughts concisely I thought Lysias
succeeded more. In the amplifications I thought Isocrates succeeded better. In
twisting the thoughts together and bringing them out compactly as suited to
true contests I accepted Lysias. In the character-drawings I found both
dexterous, but of the charm and the pleasure I awarded the first prizes to
Lysias without dispute. I saw the magnificent in Isocrates. Of the persuasive
and the fitting I thought neither fell short. In the composition of the words
I judged Lysias simpler, and Isocrates more overwrought, and the one a more
persuasive likeness of truth, and the other a stronger athlete of
construction.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

These things I said about the diction of each. And making the examination of
the subject matter, I found the invention wonderful in both and still the
judgment. But in the arrangement of the enthymemes and the divisions of the
arguments and the working-out according to each kind and all the other
theorems in the subject-matter place I thought Isocrates far ahead of Lysias,
and according to the splendor of the hypotheses and the philosophic of the
choice to differ more than a man from a boy, as Plato has said, and if one
must speak the truth, even from all the other orators who have stood over this
learning philosophically. The circular of the leading of the periods, however,
and the boyish of the figurings of the diction I did not approve. For the
thought is often a slave to the rhythm of the diction and the true falls short
of the clever. And the strongest practice in political and agonistic dialect
is that most like what is according to nature. And nature wishes the diction
to follow the thoughts, not the thoughts the diction. And for a counsellor
speaking about war and peace and for a private man running the danger about
his life before judges I do not know what help these clever and theatrical and
boyish things could furnish, or rather I know that they would even become
causes of harm. For all jesting that happens in earnest and in evils is an
untimely thing and most hostile to pity.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

And this is not my speech first, by Zeus, since many even of the ancients had
this opinion about him. For Philonicus the dialectician, while praising the
rest of the construction of the man's diction, blames the emptiness of it and
the vulgar, and he says he is like a painter adorning all the paintings with
the same clothes and the same figures; /all at any rate found his speeches
having used the same manners of diction, so that in many things, working out
the particulars technically, he appeared altogether unfitting in the wholes
because of not speaking in a way proper to the characters that underlie/. And
Hieronymus the philosopher says that one could read his speeches well, but
could not altogether harangue raising both the voice and the tone and speak in
this construction with the fitting delivery. For the greatest and most moving
of the crowds has been omitted, the pathetic and the living; for he is a slave
to smoothness throughout, and he has thrown away the mixed and varied with
tension and slackening and that which is divided by pathetic excesses. And in
general he says that having sunk into the voice of a reading-boy he can bear
neither tone nor passion nor delivery. And these things and things near to
these have been said by many others as well, about whom I need write nothing.
For from the diction of Isocrates itself when it is set out both the rhythm of
the periods pursuing the smooth from every side and the boyish of the figures
worn out about the antitheses and equalities and assonances will be clear. And
I do not blame the kind of the figures (for many both historians and orators
used them, wishing to flower the diction), but the excess.

* Chapter 14

For by not happening in due time nor in season  [lost]  I say they strike
against the ears. At any rate in the Panegyric, the celebrated speech, he is
abundant in such things; /I think them causes of most goods and worthy of
greatest praises/; for here not only is clause equal to clause, but also the
words to the words, most to greatest, goods to praises, causes to worthy. And
again: /nor did they enjoy them as private, and neglect them as another's/;
for the second clause is equal to the clause and of the words enjoy is
antithetical to neglect, and private to another's. To which he adds: /but they
cared for them as their own, and held off as one must from things that belong
in no way./ For again here too cared is opposed to held off, and their own to
things that belong in no way. And these are not yet enough, but in the period
after these he again turns /both he himself would be most well reputed/
against the following /and would leave great glory to his children/, and /nor
did they emulate the boldnesses of one another/ against what is joined to it
/nor did they practice their own daring/. And not even having left a little he
adds to these: /but they thought it more terrible to be spoken of badly by the
citizens than to die well for the fatherland./ So then a second time badly is
the reverse of well, and to die is equal to to be spoken of. If he were
moderate up to here, he would be bearable, but he will not let go. At any rate
again in the period after it he puts: /that for the good among men there
will be no need of many writings but of few tokens, and they will be of
one mind both about the common and about the private./ So then writings
and tokens are equal
and many and few and common and private are antitheses. Then as if he had said
no such thing he floods with equalities in a mass, adding these: /and they
administered the things of the others serving but not outraging the Greeks and
thinking they must be generals of them but not tyrants of them and desiring
more to be called leaders than masters and saviours but not to be named
ruiners, winning over the cities by doing well but not subduing them by force,
using words more trustworthy than now oaths, and thinking it right to abide by
the treaties as by necessities./ And why must one lengthen by going through
the particulars? For nearly the whole speech has been made clever by him with
such figures. The speeches written at the end of his life, however, are less
boyish, as having received, I suppose, complete intelligence from time. And
about these things these are enough. And it would be time both to take hold of
the examples and to show by these what the strength of the orator is. It is

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

/All who come forward here are accustomed to claim that those things are
greatest and most worthy of zeal for the city about which they themselves are
going to counsel. Nevertheless if it was fitting to preface such things even
about some other affairs, it seems to me to be proper also about the things
now present to make the beginning from here. For we have come to sit in
assembly about both war and peace, which have the greatest power in the life
of men and about which those who have deliberated rightly must fare better
than the others. Such then is the magnitude of the things about which we have
come together. But I see you not making the hearing of the speakers on equal
terms, but giving your mind to some, and of others not even enduring the
voice. And you do nothing wonderful. For in the rest of time too you have been
accustomed to throw out all the others except those who speak along with your
desires. Whom one would also justly blame, because, knowing that many and
great houses have been uprooted by those who flatter, and hating in private
affairs those who have this art, in the common you are not similarly disposed
toward them, but while accusing those who admit and enjoy such men you
yourselves appear to trust these more than the other citizens. And indeed you
have made the orators study and philosophize not the things that will benefit
the city, but how they will speak speeches that please you; toward which even
now the mass of them has flowed. For it was clear to all that you will be more
pleased by those who call you to the war than by those who counsel about the
peace. For the ones put in an expectation that we will recover the possessions
in the cities and will take up again the power which we happened to have
before, but the others hold out nothing such, but that one must keep quiet and
not desire great things against the just, but be content with what is present,
which is the hardest of all things for most men. For we are so hung from hopes
and so insatiably disposed toward the seeming advantages, that not even those
who have acquired the greatest riches are willing to remain upon these, but
always reaching after the more they run risks about what they have. Wherefore
it is worth fearing that we too now become liable to these follies. For some
seem to me to have started toward the war too much, as if not chance men had
counselled but they had heard from the gods that we will succeed in all things
and will easily master the enemies. But those who have mind must not
deliberate about the things they know (for that is superfluous), but do as
they have decided, and about the things they would deliberate, they must not
think they know what will happen, but as using opinion, and as whatever may
chance being about to happen, so they must think about them. Of which you
happen to do neither, but you are disposed as turbulently as possible. For you
have come together as needing to choose the best from all the things said, but
as if already knowing clearly what must be done you are not willing to hear
except those who harangue toward pleasure. And yet it was proper, if you
wished to seek what benefits the city, to give your mind more to those who
oppose your judgments than to those who gratify, knowing that of those who
come forward here those who say what you wish can easily deceive; for what is
said toward favor overshadows the seeing of the best. But from those who
counsel not toward pleasure you would suffer nothing such. For there is no way
they could change your mind unless they make the advantageous clear. And
besides these how could men be able to judge well about the things that have
happened or to deliberate about the future, if they did not examine the
speeches of those who oppose beside one another, and themselves furnish
themselves as common hearers to both? And I wonder at the older men, if they
no longer remember, and at the younger, if they have heard from no one, that
through those who advise holding on to the peace we have never yet suffered
any evil, but through those who choose to make war easily we have already
fallen into many and great disasters. Of which we make no mention, but we are
ready, doing nothing forward for ourselves, to man triremes and to make
contributions of money and to go to help and to make war, as if we happened to
be running risks in another's city. And the cause of these things is that,
though it is proper for you to be eager about the common likewise as about the
private, you do not have the same judgment about them, but when you deliberate
about the private you seek counsellors who think better than you yourselves,
but when you sit in assembly about the city you distrust and envy such men,
and you praise the most wicked of those who come forward to the platform and
you think more democratic those who are drunk than those who are sober and
those who have no mind than those who think well and those who distribute the
things of the city than those who perform liturgies for you from their private
substance. So that it is worth wondering if anyone hopes that the city using
such counsellors will increase toward the better. And I know that it is uphill
to oppose your thoughts and that, democracy being, there is no frankness
except here for the most mindless and those who care nothing for you, and in
the theatres for the comic poets. Which is also the most terrible of all
things, that to those who carry out to the other Greeks the faults of the city
you have as much thanks as not even to those who do well, but toward those who
rebuke and admonish you you are so disposed harshly, as toward those who are
working some evil on the city. Nevertheless even with these things being I
would not stand off from what I thought. For I have come forward not to
gratify you nor to court a vote but to declare what I happen to know, first
about the things which the prytanis puts forward, then about the other affairs
of the city. For there will be no use of the things now decided about the
peace, if we do not deliberate rightly about the rest. I say then that one
must make the peace not only with Chians and Rhodians and Byzantines and Coans
but with all men, and use not these treaties which some have now written, but
those which came about toward the King and the Lacedaemonians, and which
command that the Greeks be autonomous and the garrisons to go out from the
cities of others and each to have their own. For we shall find none more just
than these nor more beneficial to the city./

coming from elsewhere and not corrupted together with us, but suddenly
standing over the things that are happening, would not think us mad and out of
our minds, who are ambitious over the deeds of the ancestors and think it
right to praise the city from the things then done, but do none of the same
things as those, but everything opposite? For they continued making war

Such then is the man in the deliberative speeches. And in the forensic he is
in the other things very precise and true and having come nearest to the
character of Lysias, but in the composition of the words having that
smoothness and seemliness, less than in the other speeches, yet having it at
least. And let no one suppose me ignorant either that Aphareus, who became
stepson and adopted son to Isocrates, in the speech against Megacleides about
the Exchange defines that no hypothesis into a lawcourt was written by the
father, or that Aristotle says that very many bundles of Isocratean forensic
speeches are carried about by the booksellers. For I know these things said by
those men, and I neither obey Aristotle wishing to soil the man nor do I join
with Aphareus fabricating a seemly speech for this reason at least. But
thinking the Athenian Cephisodorus sufficient confirmer of the truth, who both
lived with Isocrates and became a most genuine hearer and made the very
wonderful apology for him in the writings against Aristotle, I believe that
some speeches were written by the man for lawcourts, not however many, and I
use as an example from them one (for more is not possible), the so-called
Trapezitic, which he wrote for some foreigner of his pupils against Pasion the
banker. And the speech is this:

/The contest is great for me, gentlemen of the jury; for I run a risk not only
about much money, but also about not seeming to desire the things of others

for me even if I am deprived of these. But if I should seem to have claimed so
much money with nothing belonging, I would be slandered for the whole of life.
And it is, gentlemen of the jury, the hardest of all things to meet with such
adversaries. For the contracts toward those at the banks happen without
witnesses, and those who are wronged must run risks toward such men as have
acquired many friends and handle much money and seem trustworthy through their
art. Nevertheless even with these things being I think I will make it clear to
all that I am robbed of so much money by Pasion. And from the beginning I will
relate to you, as I am able, the things that have been done. For I, gentlemen
of the jury, have as father Sopaeus, whom all who sail to the Pontus know as
so intimate toward Satyrus that he rules much land and takes care of all that
man's power. And inquiring both about this city and about the rest of Greece
he desired to go abroad. And my father, having filled two ships with grain and
having given money, sent me out at once for trade and for viewing. And
Pythodorus the Phoenician having introduced Pasion to me I used this man's
bank. And later in time, a slander having come about toward Satyrus, that both
my father was plotting against the rule and I was associating with the exiles,
he seizes my father, and he sends word to those from the Pontus sojourning
here to take the money from me and to bid me myself sail in, and if I do none
of these things, to demand me from you. And being in so many evils, gentlemen
of the jury, I tell Pasion my own disasters. For I was so intimate toward him
that I trusted this man most not only about the money but also about the other
things. And I thought that if I gave up all the money I would run a risk, if
that man suffered anything, of being deprived both of the things here and of
those there and becoming in want of all, but if, agreeing that it was when
Satyrus sent word, I did not hand it over, I would set myself and my father
into the greatest slanders toward Satyrus. So as we deliberated it seemed best
to hand over the visible of the money, but about the things lying with this
man not only to deny, but also to appear owing both to this man and to others
at interest and to do all things from which those men were most going to be
persuaded that I had no money. Then then, gentlemen of the jury, I thought
Pasion was counselling all these things through goodwill. But when I had
managed these things toward those around Satyrus, I knew him plotting against
my things. For when I wished to recover my own and to sail to Byzantium, this
man, thinking a most beautiful occasion had fallen beside him (for the money
lying with him was much and worth shamelessness, and I had in the hearing of
many become a denier of possessing nothing and it was clear to all that I was
being asked for money and was agreeing that I owed to others); and besides
these, gentlemen of the jury, thinking that if I attempted to remain here I
would be given up by the city to Satyrus, but if I turned somewhere else
nothing of my speeches would concern him, but if I sailed into the Pontus I
would die with my father; calculating these things he was intending to rob me
of the money and toward me he pretended to be at a loss in the present and not
to be able to give it back. But when I, wishing to know the affair, send
Philomelus to him and Menexenus to demand it, he denies toward them that he
has nothing of mine. And with so many evils having fallen upon me from every
side what judgment do you think I had? I for whom it was, if I was silent, to
have been robbed of the money by this man, but if I spoke, to recover these
things no more, and to set myself and my father into the greatest slander
toward Satyrus. So I thought it strongest to keep quiet. After these things,
gentlemen of the jury, men arrive announcing to me that my father has been let
go and that Satyrus so regrets all the things done that he has given him the
greatest pledges and has made the rule still greater than he had before and
has taken my sister as wife for his own son. And Pasion, having learned these
things and knowing that I will now act openly about my own, makes the boy
