=8.10= But since from the claw we have shown the lion thus far, and this in
part; for not even this whole achievement of yours have I gone through; for as
I said before, I fear to enter into the boundless seas of your other
achievements; and besides we did not set out now to speak an encomium of your
holy soul, but to prepare a drug of consolation; come, let us take up again
the things said before. And what have we said before? Being rid of calculating
what so-and-so sinned and what so-and-so offended, consider continually your
contests of endurance, of patience, of fasting, of prayers, of the sacred
all-night watches, of self-control, of almsgiving, of hospitality, of the
varied and grievous and dense trials. Reckon how from first age until the
present day you have not ceased feeding Christ when hungry, giving him drink
when thirsty, clothing him when naked, gathering him when a stranger, visiting
him when sick, going to him when bound. Consider the sea of your love, which
you opened so great as to arrive with much rush even to the very ends of the
inhabited world. For not only was your house opened to every one who came, but
also everywhere on land and sea many enjoyed this munificence through
hospitality. Gathering all these things then, feast and rejoice in the hope of
these crowns and of the prizes. And if you also desire to see these lawless
and blood-devouring men, who have worked things far more grievous than these,
paying the penalty, you will see this too then. Since Lazarus also saw the
rich man being fried. For even if the place was marked off for them because of
the difference of life and the chasm kept them apart and the one was in the
bosom of Abraham, the other in the unendurable furnaces, still Lazarus both
saw him and heard a voice and answered. These things will be then in your case
also. For if one who overlooked one man pays such punishments, and for the one
who scandalized one it is profitable that a millstone be hung from the neck
and that he be sunk in the sea, those who scandalized so great an inhabited
world and overturned so many Churches and filled all things with uproars and
confusion and outdid the cruelty and inhumanity of robbers and barbarians and
were thus by force driven frantic by the devil who commanded them and by the
demons who worked with them, so as to make this fearful dogma, full of
holiness and worthy of the one who gave it, a laughing-stock both to Jews and
to Greeks, those who sank myriads of souls and worked myriads of shipwrecks
everywhere in the inhabited world, those who kindled so great a pyre and split
the body of Christ and scattered his members in many places: "For you," he
says, "are the body of Christ and members in part." Rather why do I strive to
set forth their madness, which cannot be interpreted in a word? How great a
punishment then do you think these destroyers and blood-devourers will undergo
then? For if those who did not feed Christ when hungry are condemned with the
devil to the unquenchable fire, those who also handed over choirs of monks and
virgins

only did not visit them but even crushed them still more, and not only did not

then you will see them being fried, being burned, being bound, grinding their
teeth, wailing, lamenting at last to no purpose and repenting without profit
and without gain, just as that rich man. These too will see you again in that
blessed lot, wearing a crown, dancing with the angels, reigning with Christ,
and they will cry out much and will lament, repenting of the things in which
they rioted against you and adding a supplication to you and remembering mercy
and loving-kindness, and nothing more will there be for them.
=8.11= Calculating all these things then, sing them continually to your soul
and you will be able to scatter this dust. And since there is also something
else, as I at least think, that especially pains you, come, for this thought
too let us prepare the drug together with the things said and the things we
will now say. For I think you suffer not only because of these things, but
also because of being separated from our nothingness, and that you lament this
continually and say to all: "We do not hear that tongue, nor do we enjoy the
accustomed teaching, but we have been shut up in famine; and what God then
threatened the Hebrews, this we now undergo, not a famine of bread, nor a
thirst of water, but a famine of divine teaching." What then shall we say to
these things? That it is possible for you especially, even when we are absent,
to converse with our books. And we too will make haste, if we meet with
letter-carriers, to send you continual and dense and long letters. And if you
also wish to hear the things from us from a living voice, perhaps this too
will be and you will see us again, God permitting; rather not perhaps, but
altogether; and do not doubt at all. For we will remind you that we have not
said these things in vain, nor deceiving you and cheating you, but you will
hear also from a living voice the things now through the letters. And if the
delay grieves you, consider that not even this becomes without gain for you,
but it will bring you much wage if you endure and utter no bitter word, but
also glorify God for these things, which you also continue doing. For this
contest is not small, but also needs a soul exceedingly young in vigor and a
philosophic mind, so as to bear the separation of a loved soul. Who says these
things? If anyone knows how to love genuinely, if anyone understands the power
of love, he knows what I say. But lest we go about seeking those who love
genuinely (for this is rare), let us run to the blessed Paul and he will tell
us how great this contest is and of how great a soul it has need. For this
Paul, who stripped off the flesh and laid aside the body and went about the
inhabited world with a soul almost naked and banished every passion from the
mind and imitated the impassibility of the bodiless powers and dwelt the earth
as heaven and stood above with the Cherubim and shared with them that mystic
melody, bore all the other things easily, as suffering in a body not his own,
both prisons and chains and leadings away and scourges and threats and death
and being stoned and being sunk and every kind of punishment. But being
separated from one soul loved by him, he was so confused and disturbed as at
once even to leap away from the city in which, expecting to see the loved one,
he did not find him. And Troas, which was left by him for this reason, since
it did not have that man to show him then, is conscious of these things with
him. "For coming," he says, "to Troas for the gospel of Christ and a door



having been opened to me in the Lord, I had no rest in my spirit, because I
did not find Titus my brother, but taking leave of them I went out into
Macedonia." What is this, O Paul? Bound to wood and dwelling a prison and
having scourges laid on and your back flowing round with blood you both
initiated and baptized and offered sacrifice and did not despise one who was
about to be saved; but coming to Troas and seeing the field cleaned out and
ready to receive the seeds and the fishing filled and furnishing you much
ease, you threw so great a gain from your hands, though you had come even for
this. "For coming," he says, "to Troas for the gospel," that is, because of
the gospel, and with nothing opposing; "For a door," he says, "having been
opened to me," you leapt away at once? "Yes," he says; "for I was held by a
great tyranny of despondency and the absence of Titus confused my mind
exceedingly and so mastered me and prevailed as to compel me to do this." For
that he suffered this because of despondency we need not guess anything, but
learn this too from him. For he has also set the cause of the withdrawal,
saying: "I had no rest in my spirit, because I did not find Titus, but taking
leave of them I went out."
=8.12= Did you see how greatest a contest it is to be able to bear mildly the
separation of one loved? and how painful a thing and bitter? how it needs a
high and vigorous soul? This contest you yourself also are accomplishing now.
And by how much the contest is greatest, by so much also the crown is greater
and the prizes more brilliant. Let this be a consolation to you of the delay,
and that we shall altogether see you again teeming with the wage from this and
being crowned and being proclaimed. For it is not enough for those who love
only to be bound together in soul, nor do they content themselves with this
for consolation, but they also need bodily presence; and if this is not added,
no small part of the gladness has been cut away. And this again, going to the
nursling of love, we will find to be so. For writing to the Macedonians he
spoke thus: "But we, brothers, being orphaned from you for a season of an hour
in face, not in heart, were more abundantly eager to see your face; I Paul
both once and twice, and Satan hindered us. Therefore no longer bearing it we
were well pleased to be left in Athens alone and we sent Timothy." O the power
of each word; for it shows with much clarity the flame of love stored in his
soul. For he did not say "being separated from you," nor "being torn from
you," nor "standing apart," nor "being left behind," but "being orphaned from
you." He sought a word sufficient to show the pain of his soul. And yet he
himself was in the rank of a father to all, but he utters the words of orphan
children who in untimely age have lost the one who begot them, wishing to show
the excess of despondency. For nothing is more painful than untimely
orphanhood, when both their age suffices them for nothing, and those who would
genuinely stand over them are not, and many who will set upon them and plot
appear all at once, and like lambs they are thus set in the midst for wolves
who from every side tear them and card them. No one can set forth by speech
the greatness of this misfortune. For this reason Paul too, going about and
seeking a saying indicative of desolation and grievous misfortune, in order to
set forth what he suffered when being separated from those loved, used this
word; then he also intensifies it again through what follows: "For being
orphaned," he says, "not a time but for a season of an hour, and being
separated not in mind but in face only, not even so do we bear the pain from
this, though we have a sufficient consolation, being bound together in soul,
that you are in our heart, that yesterday and the day before we



saw you, but none of these things rid us of the despondency." But what do you
wish and desire, tell me, and desire with excess? Their very sight. "For more
abundantly," he says, "we were eager to see your face." What do you say, you
high and great one? You who have the world crucified and have been crucified
to the world, you who have been rid of all fleshly things, you who have become
almost bodiless, you became so captive under love as to be brought down to the
clayey flesh, the flesh from earth, the sensible? "Yes," he says, "and I am
not ashamed to say these things, but I even pride myself in them; for having
love, the mother of good things, teeming for me, I seek these things." And he
does not simply seek bodily presence, but he desires especially to behold
their face. "For more abundantly," he says, "we were eager to see your face."
You are in love then with sight, tell me, and you desire the viewing of a
face? "And exceedingly," he says, "where the gathering of the senses is. For a
soul naked by itself, coming together with another soul, will be able neither
to say anything nor to hear; but if I enjoy bodily presence I will both say
something and will hear from those who are loved. For this reason I desire to
see your face, where there is also a tongue sending forth a voice and
reporting the inward things to us and hearing receiving words and eyes
painting the movements of the soul; for through these it is possible to enjoy
the company of the longed-for soul more exactly."
=8.13= And that you may learn how he is kindled toward this viewing, having
said "we were more abundantly eager," he was not content with this word, but
added: "in much desire." Then, not bearing to mix himself in with the others,
but showing that he loves more vehemently than the others, having said that
"We were more abundantly eager and wished to come to you," he tore himself
from the rest and, setting himself alone, added: "I Paul both once and twice,"
showing that he was eager more than the others. Then since he did not attain
this, he is not content with letters but sends the head, Timothy who was with
him, who would be to him instead of letters; therefore he also adds saying:
"Therefore no longer bearing it." O again the nobility of the word; o the
power of the saying, making clear his ungovernable and unendurable love. And
as one being set on fire and as one being burned and seeking to find some
consolation of the burning moves everything, so then this man too, being
kindled, being choked, being burned, invented the consolation possible
according to what was allowed. "For no longer bearing it," he says, "we sent
Timothy the servant of the gospel and our fellow-worker, tearing the most
necessary member from our company and exchanging grief for grief." For that he
did not bear even that man's absence easily, but chose this heaviest thing
because of these men, he himself also made this clear saying: "We were well
pleased to be left alone." O a soul made one with love itself with exactness.
For since he was separated from one brother, he says he has been left
solitary, and this though having so many with him. These things then do you
yourself also study continually, and by how much the thing is painful to you,
by so much consider it more profitable if you bear it thankfully. For not only
blows brought upon the body but also pain of soul bring unspeakable crowns,
and pain of soul more than of body, when those who are struck bear it
thankfully. Just as therefore if, being carded in the body and being scourged,
you bore it nobly, glorifying God for these things, you would have received a
great recompense, so with the soul suffering these things now await many
repayments. And expect also that we shall altogether see you again and be rid
of this pain and receive back the great trade that has come from the pain both
then and now. These things suffice you for consolation, or rather not you
only, but even if someone were mindless and of a soul all stone.



But where there is so great understanding and a wealth of piety and a height
of philosophy and a soul that has trampled the fantasy of worldly things, the
ease of the healing is greater. Show then even in this the love concerning us,
that even when we write a great letter we have power with you, and so great as
when we are present. And you will show it clearly, if we learn that something
more has come to you from our letters, or rather not more only, but so much as
we desire. And we desire that you be now in the same gladness in which we also
saw you when we were spending time there. And if we learn this, we ourselves
too will harvest no small comfort of the desolation in which we now are. So
that if you wish to set us also in greater good cheer (and I know that you
wish and have been exceedingly eager), make it clear that you have driven out
all the rubbish of despondency and that your affairs are in a calm, and give
us this recompense of the goodwill and love concerning you. For you know, you
know clearly how you will restore us if you accomplish this and make it clear
to us through letters with truth.

9.t LETTER IX
9.1 Why do you lament? Why do you beat yourself and demand penalties which not
even your enemies were able to demand of you, thus giving over your soul to
the tyranny of despondency? For the letters which you sent us through
Patricius made clear these wounds of your mind. Therefore I also suffer
exceedingly and am in pain that, though you ought to move everything and
contrive so as to drive despondency from your soul, you go about gathering
painful thoughts and fashioning the things that are not (for this you said)
and carding yourself idly and in vain and to the greatest harm. For what
grieves you, that you were not able to transfer us from Cucusus? And yet on
your part you did transfer us, having moved and contrived everything. And if
the work did not come to an end, one must not suffer even for this. For
perhaps it seemed good to God that the double courses of the races be set
longer for me so that the crowns also might become more brilliant. Why then do
you suffer over these things from which we are proclaimed, when you ought to
leap for these things and dance and be crowned, because we were counted worthy
of so great a thing, exceedingly beyond our worth? But does the desolation of
the things here grieve you? And what is sweeter than the sojourn here? Quiet,
calm, much freedom from business, vigor of body. For if the city has neither a
marketplace nor things for sale, this is nothing to me. For all things flow in
upon me as from springs. For I have my lord the bishop here and my lord
Dioscorus making this their work throughout, our rest. And the good Patricius
will also tell you how we live in good cheer, in gladness, in much attendance,
at least as far as the sojourn here goes. And if you lament the things in
Caesarea, you do this too unworthily of yourself. For there again brilliant
crowns were plaited for us so that all proclaimed us, announced us, wondered,
were struck at the things we suffered badly when we were driven out. But let
no one know these things as yet, even if many chatter them. For my lord
Paeanius made it clear to me that the presbyters of this very Pharetrius are
present there who said they commune with us and have nothing in common with
the adversaries, nor associate with them, nor share communion. So that we may
not disturb them, let no one know these things; for the things that happened
to us are exceedingly grievous. And if I had suffered no other terrible thing,
the things that happened there would have sufficed to procure myriads of
prizes for me; so the danger for us came to be about the last things. And I
entreat, let them be secret with you and I will narrate them to you in brief,
not that I may grieve you, but that I may gladden you. For these are the
matters of my trade, this is my



wealth, this is the spending of my sins, to travel continually through such
trials, and to have these brought upon me by those from whom I in no way
expected it. For when we were about to set foot on the country of the
Cappadocians, having been rid of the Galatian, and he too having almost
threatened death to us, many met us along the road saying that "The lord
Pharetrius is waiting for you and goes about everywhere, lest he miss your
meeting, and he does and contrives everything so as to see you and embrace you
and show all love; and he has moved the monasteries of men and of women." And
I, hearing these things, expected none of these, but I formed the opposite
things in myself; yet to none of those reporting these things did I say
anything of these.
9.2 And when at last I set foot in Caesarea, worn out, pickled, lying in the
very peak of the flame of the fever, being in distress, suffering the last
things, I obtained a lodging lying toward the very outskirts of the city and I
was making haste so as both to meet with physicians and to quench that
furnace; for it was the very peak of the tertian. And there was added also the
misery of the road, the toil, the crushing, the desolation of those who would
attend, the lack of necessities, that no physician was present with us, that I
was worn down by toil and heat and sleeplessness, and being almost a
self-corpse I entered into the city. Then indeed the whole clergy arrived, the
people, monks, nuns, physicians, I enjoyed much attendance, all serving and
ministering everything to us. But even so, being held by much stupor of the
flame, I was in the last terrors. At last little by little the sickness
slackened and abated. But Pharetrius was nowhere; but he was waiting for our
departure, I do not know what seeming good to him. When therefore I saw the
evil gently abating, I was counseling thereafter about going abroad, so as to
take hold of Cucusus and rest a little from the misfortunes of the road. And
while we were in these things, Isaurians are all at once reported, an
unbounded multitude overrunning the country of the Caesareans and having
burned a certain great village and having disposed the last things. Hearing
this the tribune, taking the soldiers whom he had, went out. For they feared
lest they should also assault the city and all were in fear, all in agony,
being in danger about the very ground of the fatherland, so that even the old
men themselves took in hand the watch of the walls. While the affairs were in
these things, all at once toward dawn a droungos of monks (for so one must
speak and show their madness by the word) stood upon the house where we were,
threatening to burn it, to set it on fire, to dispose the last things for us,
if I should not go out. And neither the fear of the Isaurians, nor the
sickness so vehemently lying upon me, nor anything else made them more
reasonable, but they pressed on breathing so much wrath that even the
provincial officials themselves were afraid. For they also threatened them
with blows and they prided themselves as having already shamefully beaten many
provincial officials. Hearing these things the provincial officials fled to
us, and they entreated and begged that "Even if we are about to fall among
Isaurians, rid us of these beasts." Hearing it the governor ran down upon the
house wishing to help us. Not even when that man entreated did the monks bear
it, but he himself also grew weak. Seeing therefore the affairs in much
perplexity and neither daring to counsel us to go out to a clear murder, nor
again to remain within because of so great a madness of those men, he sent to
Pharetrius entreating him to yield a few days, both because of the sickness
and because of the danger lying upon us. And not even so did anything more
come about, but even on the next day they arrived more vehement and none of
the presbyters dared to stand by and help, but being ashamed and blushing (for
they said these things were happening according to the mind of Pharetrius)



they hid, they escaped notice, being called by us they did not obey. What need
is there to say much? Even with so many fears lying upon us, and death almost
clear and the fever working me down (for I had not yet even been rid of the
evils from this) in mid midday, throwing myself into the litter, I was led out
from there, the whole people wailing, crying aloud, cursing the one who had
done these things, all moaning and lamenting. And when I went out of the city,
some of the clerics also going out quietly escorted us, mourning. And hearing
some saying: "Where are you leading him away to a clear death?" another of
those who love us exceedingly said to us: "Go, I beg you; fall among
Isaurians, only be rid of us. For wherever you fall, you fall into safety, if
you escape our hands." Hearing these things and seeing, the good Seleucia, the
freedwoman of my lord Rufinus (for she attended us exceedingly), entreated and
begged that we lodge at her suburb, being five miles before the city, and she
sent men with us and we went off there. 9.3 But not even there was this 9.3
plot about to stand off from us. For when Pharetrius learned it, he made clear
to her, as she said, many threats. And when she received me into her suburb, I
too knew nothing of these things; but going out to us, she hid these things
from us, but made clear to her steward being there to furnish us all rest, and
if any monks should come upon us wishing to insult us or crush us, to gather
farmers from her other places and thus array themselves against them. And she
also entreated us to take refuge into her house which had a castle and was
unconquerable so as to flee the hands of the bishop and of the monks. But this
I did not bear, but I was in the suburb knowing nothing of the things being
prepared after these. Nor did this suffice them for standing off from the
madness against us. Then in mid nights, I knowing nothing of these things (for
Pharetrius pressed much, threatening her with threat, as she says, compelling,
pushing, so as to cast us out even from the suburbs), the woman not bearing
his burden, I not knowing, made it clear there that the barbarians had come
upon us, being ashamed to say the necessity which she underwent. And in mid
nights Euthius the presbyter coming in to me, having woken me sleeping, said
these things with much cry: "Rise, I entreat, barbarians have come upon us,
here nearby." Consider what I was hearing these things. Then as I said to him
also what one must do: "We cannot take refuge into the city lest we suffer
things more grievous than what the Isaurians are about to do to us," he
compelled us to go out. It was a moonless night, the night mid, gloomy, dark;
and this itself again was full of perplexity for us; and no one present, no
one helping, for all had abandoned us. Nevertheless being pushed together by
the fear and expecting to die at once, I rose up worn out, having ordered
torches to be kindled. But the presbyter ordered even these to be quenched,
lest, he says, the barbarians, being called by the light, set upon us; the
torches too were quenched. Then the mule that carried our litter (for the road
was exceedingly rough and uphill and stony) being brought down upon its knee
brought me down being inside and I was all but about to perish; then leaping
out, being dragged I walked about under Euthius the presbyter (for he too
jumped down from the beast) and thus being led by the hand I went, or rather
being hauled; for it was not possible even to walk into so great a difficulty
of ground and grievous mountains in mid night. Consider what I was likely to
suffer, being held by so many evils and the fever lying upon me and knowing
nothing of the things prepared, but fearing barbarians and trembling and



expecting to fall into their hands. Do these sufferings alone not seem to you,
even if nothing else should have happened to me, to be able to dissolve many
of our sins and to furnish me much occasion of good repute? And the cause, as
I at least think, all at once when I entered Caesarea, those in offices, those
from vicars, the sophists from governorships, those from tribunes, the whole
people saw me each day, attended, carried me on the pupil of the eyes; these
things I think nettled Pharetrius and the envy that drove us from
Constantinople did not stand off from us even here, as I at least think; for I
do not declare but I suspect. What could one say of the other things along the
road, the fears, the dangers? Which, myself remembering each day and carrying
about in mind, I fly under the pleasure, I leap as having a great treasure
stored; for I am so and am so disposed. Therefore I also entreat your Honor to
rejoice over these things, to be glad, to leap, to glorify God who counted us
worthy to suffer such things. And I entreat you to have these things with
yourself and to speak them out to no one, even if the provincial officials
especially have to fill the whole city, they themselves also having been in
danger about the last things. Yet from your piety let no one know this, but
also restrain those who speak.
9.4 And if you suffer because of the remnants of the maltreatment, learn
clearly that I have been purely rid of all of them and I have the body more
vigorous than when spending time there. And why do you fear the frost? For
both suitable dwellings have been prepared for us, and my lord Dioscorus does
and contrives everything so that we may not even take a small perception from
the frost. And if it is possible to guess from the preludes, the air now seems
to me to be eastern and nothing less than Antioch, so great the warmth, so
great the good mixture of the air. And you grieved me exceedingly saying that
"Perhaps you are even grieved toward us as having been careless." And yet many
days before I wrote to your Honor entreating you not to move me from here. And
I had this to consider, that you need much defense and many sweats and toil,
in order to be able to defend yourself toward this word. And perhaps in part
you have also defended yourself saying that "I simply calculate this for the
sake of increasing the affliction." But this again is a greatest charge, to
say that "I even add pains to the thoughts by way of extra ambition." For
though you ought to do and contrive everything for the sake of dissolving the
affliction, you do the will of the devil, increasing the despondency and the
grief. Or do you not know how great even despondency is? And on account of the
Isaurians fear nothing thereafter; for they have returned into their country;
and the governor did everything for this; and we are in safety much more being
here than when we were in Caesarea. For thereafter I fear no one as the
bishops except a few. Wholly then on account of the Isaurians fear nothing;
for they have withdrawn and with winter having overtaken them they are shut up
at home, if indeed thereafter after Pentecost they go out. And how do you say
