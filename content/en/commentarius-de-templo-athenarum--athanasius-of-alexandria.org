* Commentary on the Temple at Athens

On the temple and on the schools and the theaters in Athens.
Exegetical work of Athanasius the Great concerning the temple in
Athens.
Those who do not know the divine scriptures we ought thereafter to
persuade concerning the knowledge of God from the nature of things
itself. For we see certain essences in creation serving one another
not according to nature, but above nature. As an example I mention:
the nature of waters is an essence flowing and downward-tending; how
then do we see the so-called siphons 108 bringing water from the sea
up to the clouds? And more marvelous is that the salty, as it rises,
comes down upon the earth sweet through the rains. And again, how
does the nature of bodies, being sinkable according to nature, appear
unsinkable and unsubmerging in the waters of the Marmarican
Pentapolis? And not only this, but also the nature of water and of
fire, being contrary, was at one time together on the mountain in
Lycia called Olympus, as myriads of men have seen (and until now they
behold that thing). And there are countless other paradoxes observed
and marveled at in creation, which would not thus have been about to
come about above nature, unless some essence of God were mastering
them and bidding them not to array themselves against one another. O
children of the Greeks, when a hard thunder comes about, how does all
human nature tremble, shudder, and stand astonished, declaring
through that bearing that it is under a master who works the
thunders. And these things are examples bringing the simpler among
the Greeks toward the knowledge of God. But toward the wise among
them, certain wise men of the Greeks from among the ancient and able
philosophers declared many testimonies concerning piety, and they
even dimly foretold the economy of Christ. For many years before the
advent of Christ, a certain wise man named Apollo, moved, as I think,
from God, founded the temple in Athens, having written on its altar:
To an Unknown God. In it, then, the first philosophers of the Greeks
were gathered, that they might ask him about the temple and about
prophecy and piety; whose names we will say 109 are these: first
Titon, second Bias, third Solon, fourth Cheilon, fifth Thucydides,
sixth Menander, seventh Plato. These seven philosophers said to
Apollo: Prophesy to us, prophet, O Apollo, what is this temple, and
whose would this altar be after you? To whom Apollo said: Whatever
you have been stirred to do toward virtue and adornment, do. For I
proclaim the thrice-one high-ruler, whose unutterable Word will be
conceived in an unbound maiden; like a fire-bearing bow, having taken
the whole world captive, he will bring it as a gift to the Father;
and Mary is her name. And this is the solution of the prophecy ***
concerning the temple, then, is the first saying: whatever, he says,
it is possible for you to do toward the adornment of the temple and
beauty, do the things pleasing to God and to men. For I take there to
be in heaven a great, most-high king in three hypostases; of this one
the beginningless God and Word is enfleshed in an unmarried maiden
and like a fire-bearing bow, or as something more powerful, he will
appear to the whole world, fishing for men as for a fish from the
depth of unbelief and ignorance, whom he will bring as a gift to his
own Father; and Mary is her name. And this Apollo said, prophesying.
Titon said: The young girl will come, having for us the heavenly
offspring, the child of God and the Father; the manless maiden
conceives. Bias said: This one has stepped from 110 the heavens, an
exceeding immortal fire of flame; whom heaven and earth and sea
tremble at, Tartaruses and demons of the deep; self-father,
thrice-blessed. Solon said: Late at some time upon this much-divided

earth would God drive; and without error he will become flesh; by the
bounds of unwearying godhead he will loose the corruption of
incurable passions. And toward him the envy of a people will become
bitter, and having been hung on high, as one condemned to death, he
will suffer all things mildly. Cheilon said: The unwearying nature of
God will come to be; and from him his Word is given being.
Thucydides said: Honor God and learn; but do not seek who he is and
how; for whether he is or is not, honor and learn this one as being;
for he is pious in mind who wishes to learn God. Menander said: The
old is new and the new ancient; the father is offspring and the
offspring father; the one is three and the three one; the fleshless
is of flesh; earth has borne the heavenly king. Plato said: Since God
is good, he is not cause of all things, as the many say; but of many
things he is not cause; and of good things, of nothing else, we say
he is cause; only of the beautiful, but of evils no longer. Again
these seven spoke and said: So be it concerning the economy of Christ
and concerning the holy Trinity. 111 And another of the wise among
the Greeks, together with certain others, called Asclepius, asked
Hermes, more a philosopher than all the philosophers, to give them a
word concerning the nature of God. And Hermes, taking a pen, wrote
thus: If there were not some providence of the Lord of all, he would
not have wished to reveal this word, nor would he have occupied you
with such works that you might ask about these things. For it is not
attainable to furnish such mysteries to the uninitiated, but
listening with the mind, listen: there was one only; intellectual
light before intellectual light; and there was to him a union from
the mind with light and spirit; all things from him and to him; a
fertile one, having come down from a fertile one upon fertile water,
made the water pregnant. You know how the children of the Greeks
prophesied and proclaimed beforehand both the God before all
beginning and his co-beginningless Son and Word and his co-enthroned
and consubstantial Spirit, and they proclaimed beforehand the
honorable sufferings of the cross; to him be the glory and the might
with the beginningless Father and the All-holy Spirit unto the ages
of ages; amen.
