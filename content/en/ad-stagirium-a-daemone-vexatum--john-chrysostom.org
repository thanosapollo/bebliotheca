Of the same, an exhortatory discourse to Stagirius the ascetic
possessed by a demon
=47.423= First Discourse

1. We ought, Stagirius, dearest of all to me, both to be present with you now
and to share the hardship, encouraging by word =47.424= and serving by deed,
and taking part in all the rest, so as to lighten some portion of the
despondency =47.425= as far as was in our power. But since the weakness of the
body, and the trouble that has fallen on the head, have forced us to stay at
home and have robbed us of a ministry that has so much gain, we will not
hesitate to bring in, as far as we can, what remains both for your consolation
and for our own benefit. Perhaps there will be some further help toward your
bearing the present things nobly. And if we accomplish nothing of that kind,
at least the fact that nothing of what belongs to us has been left undone will
make us easier in mind for the rest. For he who has fulfilled all that he
thinks can tend to the loosing of the griefs that hold him, even if he finish
nothing, is at least freed from the charge that comes from his thoughts, and
having laid aside the burden of accusations, he is compelled to bear only the
burden of despondency. If I had been one of those who are approved before God
and who can do great things, I would not have ceased begging and entreating
for the head that is precious to me. But since the multitude of sins has taken
from me that freedom of speech and that strength, I will try to bring you the
consolation that comes through words. For in the case of those who are ill in
body, it belongs to physicians to wipe out the pains and stop the disease, but
even the household servants who are about the care have not been deprived of
the work of encouraging; rather these especially are the ones who spend many
words on such things, when they happen to be well disposed toward those who
own them. If something is said by us that can quench this immoderate pain,
what we pray for has come to pass. If we find nothing of that kind to utter,
at least He who commanded through the blessed Paul that we weep with those who
weep and be carried along with the lowly will altogether accept our eagerness.
One cause of the despondency seems to you to be the frenzy of this wicked
demon. But one would find many griefs born in sequence from this root. And I
do not say this now from myself, but from what I often heard you lamenting to
us when you were with us. First, that while you lived the earlier time in a
more worldly way you suffered nothing of this kind, but when you crucified
yourself to the world, then you perceived the disease: this is enough to throw
one into turmoil and cast one into helplessness. Second, that many even of
those living in luxury who have been sick with the same things as you, after
enduring a little time, have been freed from this infirmity and have returned
to such perfect health as even to enter into marriage, become fathers of many
children, enjoy the pleasant things of the present life, and suffer nothing of
this kind again; while you, drawing out so long a time in fasts and vigils and
the rest of the hard training, have found no release from the terrible things
that hold you. Third besides these, that that holy man, who displayed such
power in other men, was able to do nothing of this kind in the case of your
love, neither he nor those after him, and these more powerful than he, but all
alike went away blushing. After these you said you were also otherwise in
great pain, because the power of despondency so prevailed over your soul
=47.426= that you often almost rushed even to a noose, and to a river, and to
a cliff. And there is also a fifth after these, that those



of your own age who began this way of life with you live in good cheer, while
you still lie in a most harsh swell and inhabit a prison more pitiable than
all. For you said that those bound with iron ought not to be wept for as much
as those who wear this chain. You told me there was yet another thing besides
these that most of all throws you into turmoil: that you fear for your father
and tremble lest, if he ever perceive it, he work great evils on the holy men
who received you at the first. For trusting in his power and wealth, and
mastered by despondency, he will dare everything against them and will hold
back from none of those who fall in his way. And up to the present your mother
has been able both to cover what has happened and often to put him off when he
seeks you. But if more time comes, her pretense will be detected, and he will
be unbearable both to her and to the monks. And the crown of the evils is that
you do not even have confidence about the future, nor know clearly whether
there will ever be a release and riddance of this disease, because after often
expecting this you have fallen into the same things again. These things, then,
are enough to throw a soul into turmoil and fill it with much confusion, but a
soul that is slack and untrained and sluggish. If we are willing to look a
little sharply and stir up the pious thoughts, we will shake off these causes
of despondency like a fine dust. And do not think that because I stand outside
this toil and this storm I now promise you these things with ease. For even if
I seem to some to say things hard to believe, still I will speak: for you at
least will not disbelieve us along with the rest. When that foul demon first
leapt upon your soul and threw you down while you were praying among all, I
happened not to be present, and I thank the God who loves mankind, but I
learned everything with accuracy as if I had been present. For my friend and
yours, Theophilus of Ephesus, came and related everything to me clearly: the
twisting of the hands, the distortion of the eyes, the foam from the mouth,
that abominable and inarticulate voice, the trembling of the body, the long
insensibility, the dream that appeared that night. For he said that a wild
swine, stained with much mire, kept leaping on you and wrestling with you;
then the one sleeping beside you, troubled by the sight, woke and found you
again being moved by the demon.

2. When he reported these things, he poured
over us a mist as great as this demon poured over your love. And when after a
long time I recovered, nothing remaining of the terrible things in this world
seemed to me =47.427= terrible, nor any of the pleasant things pleasant.
Having already condemned much of the vanity in life, I suffered this far more
then, and a greater longing for your piety entered me. For such is the nature
of griefs: they are always accustomed to intensify friendships, and this is
clear even from their being able to dissolve enmities easily. For there is no
one so wretched or so foolish that, seeing his enemy in pain, he will be able
to keep his hatred toward him. And if we pity enemies and make them friends
when we see them undergo something they did not wish, consider what I was
likely to suffer, seeing the dearest of all to me, whom I count equal to my
own head, in things incurable, as far as the account of despondency goes. Do
not think that we stand outside your affliction, nor on that account suspect
my consolation. For even if by the grace of God I have been freed from being
torn and thrown down by the wicked demon, still I bear an equal share with you
of the despondency and the pain that come from these things, and no one who
knows how to love as one must love would disbelieve me. Come then, let us
shake off the dust. For so the nature of this despondency will be bearable and
light, if only we are not willing, simply giving ourselves over to the
passion, to be dragged down cliffs, but take care both to come to ourselves
and to think some of the things that are needed. For many of the things that
seem to be terrible



appear great and unbearable before they are examined well. But if someone
looks them through with reason, he will find them far short of the suspicion:
which I hope will happen now as well. But rouse yourself, and do not,
following the vain and irrational opinion of the many, make the enemy more
violent against us. If my speech were toward some unbeliever, or toward those
who think all things are carried along of themselves, or toward those who
assign the providence of the world to wicked demons, I would have needed much
labor, so that first, after casting out the wandering opinion and persuading
them to know the true providence of the whole, I might then turn the speech to
consolation. But since by the grace of God you have known the sacred writings
from infancy, and having received the true and saving doctrines from your
ancestors you believe accurately that God cares for all, and most of all for
those who believe in him, leaving this part we will make the beginning from
another place. When God made the angels: or rather let us take the speech
higher. Before the angels came to be, and the other powers in the heavens, God
was, never having taken a beginning of being. Being always without need, for
such is the Divine, he made angels, archangels, and the other bodiless
substances, and he made them for no other reason, but for goodness alone. For
of those whose service he did not need he would not even have become creator,
if he were not exceedingly good. After their creation he also makes man again
for this same cause, and this whole world. And having filled him with
countless goods he set that small and cheap one over so many works, declaring
him on earth what he himself is in the heavens. For the word, =47.428= Let us
make man according to our image and according to likeness, is nothing other
than that he too was taken up to the rule of the things on earth. And having
made him and set him in so great an honor, as palaces for a king he assigned
him paradise, the fairest of the things on earth. And besides this, wishing to
show him from another side as well how far he had been raised above the
remaining animals, he brings them all to him and commands him to give names to
them all. And yet he gives him none of them for help; and he adds the cause:
For there was not found, it says, a helper like him. And having taught him
through these things also the interval between either nature, and that he
himself is more honorable than all, and that nothing in so great a multitude
is equal to him, then indeed he also creates the woman, in this again honoring
him and making it plain that she came to be because of him, as Paul also says:
For man was not created because of the woman, but woman because of the man.
And he did not only dignify him with these things, but he also granted speech
to him alone of all, and thought him worthy of his own knowledge, and gave him
to enjoy converse with himself, as far as it was possible for that one to
enjoy it, and promised to provide immortality, and filled him with much
wisdom, and placed in him a spiritual grace, so that he even prophesied
something. And he bestowed all these things when no achievement had gone
before on his part. What then did that one do after so many and so great
goods? He thought the enemy more trustworthy than the one who had given these
things, and making light of the command of the one who made him, he preferred
the deceit of the one who was eager to wipe him out altogether and to cast him
at once from all the goods. And he preferred it, though that one had shown
nothing, either great or small, by way of benefaction toward him, but had
brought him words only. Did God then wipe him out, when he had shown such
ingratitude from the beginning and, so to speak, from the very starting line?
For this, according to the account of justice, would have been the
consequence, that the one who had suffered countless good things, and in
return had made disobedience and thanklessness the very preludes of his life,
should perish and be thrown out of the midst. But God continued benefiting him
no less than before, showing that even if we sin ten thousand times and turn
away from him, he himself does not cease arranging the things that tend to our
salvation, so that if we turn we may also be saved; and if we remain in



vice, at least this much remains, that he himself does his own part. Now the
casting out of paradise, and the barring from the tree of life, and the
handing over to death, seem to belong to one who is punishing and taking
vengeance; but they belong no less to one who is providing than they did
before. For even if what is said seems paradoxical, still it is true: for the
things that happened were contrary to one another, but the ends of both were
in agreement and consistent. What I mean is this. The shutting out from
paradise, the settling opposite, the turning away from the tree of life, the
forbidding, the making him mortal, the declaring him for a time above the
sentence: all these, both those from the former things and those from the
latter, came to be for his salvation and honor. And about the former =47.429=
I would say nothing, for it is clear to everyone, but one must give an account
of the things after these.

3. How then shall we know that the second things
also were for what was profitable to him? If we consider what he would have
suffered if these had not happened. What then would he have suffered? If,
after the devil promised after the transgression to show them equal to God, he
had remained in the same honor, he would have fallen into three last evils.
First, he would have thought God to be envious, and a deceiver, and a liar;
second, he would have thought the one who is truly a deceiver, and father of
the lie and of envy, to be both a benefactor and a friend; and besides these
he would have continued sinning immortally for the rest. From all these God
freed him by casting him out of paradise then. So too a physician, if he
leaves the sore, makes the rot greater, but if he cuts it out he prevents the
evil of the wound from going further. What then? someone says. He did not stop
at these things, but he also added sweats and toils, because nothing is so
useless toward relaxation as the nature of men is. For if even with these
lying upon us we do not cease sinning, what would we not have dared, if God
had let us go toward luxury and idleness? For idleness, it says, has taught
every evil. And the things that happen day by day bear witness to the word,
and also the things that befell our ancestors. For the people sat down, it
says, to eat and drink, and they rose up to play; and again: He grew fat, he
grew thick, he was broadened, and the beloved kicked. And in harmony with
these the blessed David also speaks, saying: When he slew them, then they
sought him, and they turned and rose early toward God. And to Jerusalem
through Jeremiah God says: Be instructed continually, Jerusalem, that my soul
may not depart from you. And that not only for the worthless, but also for the
good, to be humbled and bent is for salvation, the prophet again says: It is
good for me, Lord, that you humbled me, that I might learn your statutes. And
after him Jeremiah also cries out these same things, even if not in the same
words: It is good for a man when he takes up a heavy yoke from his youth; he
will sit alone and be silent. And he even begs concerning God himself, saying:
Do not become one who spares me unto alienation in an evil day. And the
blessed Paul too, who shone so much in grace and went beyond human nature,
still himself also needed the help of this good; therefore he also said: There
was given me a thorn in the flesh, an angel of Satan, that he might buffet me,
that I might not be lifted up. For this I begged the Lord three times, and he
said to me: My grace is sufficient for you; for my power is perfected in
weakness. And yet it was possible for the preaching to be accomplished even
apart from persecutions and afflictions and toils and sweats, but still Christ
did not wish it, sparing those who preach. Therefore he says to them: In the
world you will have affliction. And those who wish to go into the kingdom he
commands to do this through the constricted road, as if it were not possible
to come there from another place. Thus afflictions and trials and the griefs
that befall us display to us the care of God no less than the good things. And
why do I speak of the afflictions =47.430= here? For the threat of Gehenna
presents his love of mankind no less than the kingdom of the heavens. For if
Gehenna had not been threatened,



no one would quickly have attained the goods in the heavens. For the promise
of good things alone is not enough to urge toward virtue, unless the fear of
terrible things also pushes those who are more slack toward it. Therefore he
also cast the first-formed out of paradise at the beginning, because the honor
given him would have made him worse, if that honor had remained unshaken and
firm after the command had been moved. And why do I speak of Adam? For what
would Cain not have done, turning about in paradise and enjoying such luxury,
when even deprived of these things, and seeing his father's punishment at his
feet, he was not even so brought to sense, but fell into greater vice, himself
first both inventing and daring murder, and the most accursed of all murders?
For he did not come to this new pollution little by little, nor in much time,
but all at once and suddenly he leapt straight to the very peak, lying in wait
for and killing the one who had shared the same birth-pangs with him, who had
wronged him in nothing, unless he thought it a wrong to honor God. But you,
learn here again the love of God toward mankind. For when Cain insulted him,
he admonishes him through words and consoles him when he is grieving; but when
he raged against his brother, then he proceeds against him and brings on the
punishment. And yet the former things also were worthy of the same punishment,
or rather even of a harsher one. For if among men, when one of the household
servants keeps the better things for himself and brings the worse to the
master, the thing seems terrible and an insult, how much more in the case of
God? And Cain did not only sin this, but also another no less, being
displeased at the honor of his brother. For if as one who had sinned he was
repenting, he was acceptable for this fairest change; but as it was, the
confusion was not of repentance, but of jealousy and envy, and the end showed
it. For he all but was angry at God, because God, having been insulted, did
not honor him, and set the unbridled before the reasonable; but still, even
so, when the sins demanded a violent punishment, he used the offender far less
than he deserved, and tries to settle his inflamed soul. For that despondency
was from anger; therefore he also says to him: Be still. And he said these
things, knowing to what evil it would go, but wishing to cut off every pretext
from the ungrateful. For if he had been punished at the beginning, many would
have said many such things: was it not possible to console with a word, and
admonish, and rebuke first, and then go after the one who remained in the same
things? This punishment is of much cruelty and harshness. Therefore God is
long-suffering toward the insults against himself, at once stopping their
mouths, and at once showing that he also trained Cain's father through
goodness, and besides leading those after these things to repentance through
such kindness of his. But when according to his hardness =47.431= and
unrepentant heart he treasured up wrath for himself, then he proceeds against
him. And if even after the murder he had remained unpunished, he would have
gone on to another greater evil. For one cannot say that he offended through
ignorance: what the younger understood, how would this one have been ignorant
of? But still, if you wish, let it even be granted that the former thing was
of ignorance: after hearing Be still, after obtaining pardon, from what
ignorance was he coming to the murder, and polluting the earth, and
overturning the laws of nature? Do you see that not even the former things
were of not knowing, but of vice and wickedness and last pollution? What then
is the punishment of these things? Groaning you shall be, it says, and
trembling upon the earth. Now the punishment seems bitter; but not if we
reckon the sin, and look through the punishment itself well. For when he
offered badly, he was pained that after insulting he was not honored by the
God who had been insulted; he spat on the one admonishing him; he dared murder
first, or rather a pollution far more accursed than murder; he grieved those
who had begotten him; he lied to God. Am I my brother's keeper? he says; and



in return for all these he is punished only with fear and trembling. But still
I would say that the kindness of God appears not only from the punishment
being milder than the sin, but also from its having no ordinary gain. And the
gain was that all those after him were brought to sense, and became better
through the rebuke against that one. Therefore he did not kill him: for it was
not the same to hear that a certain Cain, having killed a brother, died, and
to see the killer paying the penalty. For then this, being said, might even
have been disbelieved because of the excess of the defilement; but now,
appearing and being present, and making many witnesses of the punishment for a
long time, he made what had happened evident and credible both to those then
and to those after. What then is this to that one? someone says. First of all,
he provided even for his salvation, when, as far as it belonged to him, he
checked his rage beforehand through the exhortation of words. And if one were
also to examine the punishment itself, he will see from this too that the
benefit is great. For if God had killed him at once, he would not have given
him any appointed time for repentance and improvement; but then, living with
trembling and fear, if he were not exceedingly insensible, and a beast rather
than a man, he would have gained much from this life. And apart from these
things, through this punishment he made the coming punishment lesser for him.
For the griefs brought upon us by God in the present life, or the
chastisements, cut away no small part of the torments there. And one could
bring the witnesses of this from the divine Scriptures. For Christ, conversing
with the disciples and relating the things concerning Lazarus, says that
Abraham, being entreated by the rich man to drip a little with the tip of his
finger on the burning tongue, said to him: Child, remember that you received
your good things in your life, and Lazarus the evil things; but now this one
is comforted here, and you are in pain. And Paul (and when I say Paul, I say
again the commands of Christ; for he himself was moving that blessed soul),
writing once to the Corinthians about the =47.432= one who had committed
fornication, commands that such a one be handed over to Satan for the
destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of our Lord
Jesus Christ. And conversing again with the same about those who share in the
mysteries unworthily: For this reason, he says, many among you are weak and
sick, and a sufficient number sleep. For if we judged ourselves, we would not
be judged; but being judged by the Lord we are trained, that we may not be
condemned with the world. Did you see unspeakable love of mankind, and
infinite wealth of kindness? Did you see how God does and manages everything,
so that even when we sin we may be able to undergo a milder punishment than is
owed, or even be freed altogether?

4. But if someone should say, Why then did
he not wipe out the one who deceived from the beginning? this too is because
he cares exceedingly for us. For if that foul one were mastering by force, the
question would have some reason; but if he has been deprived of this power,
and only persuades, and not being persuaded is in our power, why do you take
away the occasion of approval, and cut out the ground of the crowns? And
besides this, if knowing him to be unconquerable, and about to master all, he
then let him go, it would not even so be right to be at a loss about these
things: for even then it was from us that that one mastered and conquered
those who were not forced, but who of their own will bowed under him; still
not even this would have been enough for those who wish to be ungrateful. But
if many already have prevailed over his power, and many again will prevail,
why do you deprive those who are about to be approved, and display a brilliant
victory, of so great an honor? For this is why he allowed it, that those
formerly defeated by him might throw him down: which is greater than every
punishment for him, and enough to bring him to last condemnation. But not all
will prevail over him, someone says. And what is this to what is being said?
For it is far more just that the noble take



occasions in which they will display their own choice, and that those who are
not such be punished from their own slackness, than that those be abused
because of these. For now the worthless one, even if he is wronged, is
mastered not because of the antagonist, but because of his own sluggishness;
and the multitude of those who conquer him makes this plain. But then, if the
earnest were abused because of the worthless, not having anywhere to use their
courage because of those, it would have become like this, as if some president
of the games, having two athletes, one ready both to close with the antagonist
and to display much endurance and to bind on a crown, the other preferring
idleness and luxury to that hardship, should take the antagonist out of the
midst and send both away from him with nothing done. =47.433= Thus the earnest
one was abused because of the worthless, but that one is no longer bad because
of the noble, but because of his own vice. And now the question seems to them
to be about the devil; but according to the sequence this speech, going
forward, will accuse his providence to many and blame it, and will slander the
whole creation of God. For it will accuse even the fashioning of the mouth and
of the eyes: for through these many desire what they ought not, and fall into
adultery; and through that others blaspheme, and bring forward the destructive
doctrines. Was it necessary then for this reason that men come to be without
tongue and eyes? Shall we then also cut off the feet, and amputate the hands,
since these are full of bloods, and those run toward vice? But not even the
ears will be able to escape the harshness of this speech: for they also
receive a vain hearing, and send on the corruption of doctrines to the soul;
so that we will take these away too. And if this, then also foods, and drinks,
and heaven, and earth, and sea, and sun, and light, and moon, and the choir of
the stars, and all the kinds of the irrational: for where will these be
useful, when the one for whom all things came to be has been cut up so
pitiably? Do you see the laughter and the absurdity into which the speech is
forced to turn? For the devil is bad to himself, not to us: for if we should
wish, we will even harvest many good things through him, against his will and
not wishing it; and the wonder is greater even in this, and the excess of the
love of God toward mankind. For that men become better bites him even by
itself and grieves him; but when this is also procured for us through him, he
will not even be able to bear the abuse. How then is it procured through him?
Fearing his cruelty and his continual plots, and his successive devices, we
beat off much sleep, we are sober, we remember the Lord continually. And that
this is not my speech, but that of the blessed Paul, hear how through nearly
the same words he rouses those of the faithful who are sleeping. For writing
to the Ephesians he says somehow thus: Our wrestling is not against blood and
flesh, but against the principalities, against the authorities, against the
world-rulers of the darkness of this age, against the spiritual things of
wickedness in the heavenly places. And he said these things, not wishing to
throw them down, but to make them stand up. And Peter too: Be sober, he says,
watch, because your adversary the devil walks about as a roaring lion, seeking
whom he may swallow; whom resist, firm in the faith. And he said this wishing
to make them more vehement, and to persuade them to have greater intimacy
toward God. For he who sees the enemy standing over him runs more to the one
who can help and closes with him. So too little children, when they see
something fearful, fleeing into the mother's bosom, and hanging from her
garments, hold them securely, and though many often pull them the other way,
they do not stand off; but when there is nothing that throws them into
turmoil, they do not even endure her when she calls and draws them, but they
even spit on her when she calls, and turn away from her as she devises many
things to draw them, and they despise a table that is set. =47.434= Therefore
many of the mothers, when



they can do nothing by encouraging, fashion bogeys and scare-things, and so
they contrive their return, and persuade them to run to them again. And this
happens not only in the case of children, but also in ours. For when the Evil
One frightens us and throws us into confusion, then we are brought to sense,
then we recognize ourselves, then we run to God with much eagerness. But if
this one had been wiped out at the beginning and had been put out of the way,
perhaps the many would not even have believed about the things that have
passed: for example, that he deceived the man, and cast him out of those many
goods; but they would have said that God did this by envy and jealousy. For
where even now there are those who dare these things after so great a
demonstration of his deceit, if they had not taken experience of that one's
villainy at all, what would they not have said, what would they not have
uttered? And if one must attend with accuracy to what has happened, the devil
does not even push us into all things, but he himself works many evils, and we
also work many for ourselves by slackness and contempt alone. For where, to
return again to the beginning, did the devil approach Cain, and advise him to
do the murder? For with this one's mother he appears conversing and
constructing the deceit; but here no longer, unless someone should say that he
suggested the wicked thoughts, and this too is from the one who received them
and was persuaded and gave him the beginning of the attack. But still God did
not even so leave him, but he continued training and admonishing him through
the things by which he seemed to be punishing him. And why do I speak of the
punishment of Cain, one man, when even the flood, where the destruction of so
many men occurred, could show us the care of God? For first he did not bring
those terrible things simply or unexpectedly, but he also foretold, and not a
short time before, but even a hundred and twenty years before. Then, that they
might not, because of the length of the prophecy, fall into forgetfulness and
slackness, he prepared the ark to be built before their very eyes, crying out
more clearly than any voice the threat of God. For Cain had already fallen out
of the thought of men, but that ark, lying before their sight, continually
stirred up the memory of the things threatened. But not even so were they
brought to sense, but they continued calling forth and drawing the evils upon
themselves. For God wished neither to threaten a flood nor to bring it on,

first age, and who expired before they came to distinguishing good or evil?
And what terrible thing do these suffer, tell me, not yet having the
perception of what they suffer, nor having learned to grieve and to rejoice?
And I do not solve this account by this only, but because parents and brothers
and kinsmen also have been brought to sense from such disasters; and this is
no small gain, that from things in which another has been wronged in nothing,
another is benefited most greatly. And it is likely that there is also some
other unspeakable cause, apparent to the one who made us alone.

9. One account
still remains, why those who walked straight before the trials fell after the
trials. Who then is the one who well knows those who walk straight, except the
one who molded our hearts alone, and understands into all our works? For many
often of those who seem to be good are more wicked than all. And this has
already been shown also in the present



life, but in the case of some, when some circumstance and necessity has come.
But when the one who tests hearts and kidneys, the living and energetic and
sharper than every two-edged sword, who reaches as far as division of soul and
body, of joints and marrows, the judge of thoughts and notions, sits judging
us, then indeed, then you will see not some few out of many, but all such
uncovered; and neither a sheep's skin could hide the wolf, nor a whitewashing
of a tomb the pollution within. For there is no creation unapparent before the
one who then judges, but all things are naked and laid by the neck to his
eyes; which Paul also making plain said to the Corinthians: So that you do not
judge anything before the time, until the Lord comes, who will also light the
hidden things of the darkness, and will make apparent the counsels of the
hearts. And that leaving the hypocrites we may come in the speech to those who
live rightly, from where is it clear, if the other things have been succeeded
in by them, but the chief of the goods, humility, has been neglected? And for
this God left them, that they might learn that they did not succeed in those
things by their own power =47.446= but by the grace of God. And if someone
should say it is better to succeed and be lifted up than to be humbled by
falling, he is much ignorant both of the damage of arrogance and of the gain
of humility. For know clearly that a man succeeding with madness, if indeed
this is succeeding at all, will quickly fall into last destruction. And the
one allowed to slip, and having learned to be moderate from the fall, will
both rise and recover himself more quickly, if he should wish; but the one who
seems to do well with arrogance, and suffers nothing terrible, will never even
take perception of his own lawlessness, but will even increase the terrible
thing, and will escape notice going away from here empty: as that Pharisee,
who went up seeming to be rich in all the goods, and came down having learned
that he was even poorer than the tax-collector. And there is also another form
that has much strength toward emptying the good things gathered with many
sweats and toils, the wind of vainglory. For like a wind truly it comes in and
blows out all the treasures of virtue. Behold then a second occasion also has
appeared to us of those who walk straight, as you said. For many among us
seeming to have undergone many toils for virtue, and undergoing them, since
looking toward the honor from men, and not toward God, they did all things,
were allowed to fall into trial, so that being deprived of the glory from the
many through which they were damaged in all things, and having learned its
nature, how it is disposed no better than a flower of grass, they may attend
to God alone for the rest, and do all things because of him. And there are
also other accounts besides these, much more than these, unapparent to us, as
I said, but known to the God who made them. Therefore let us not be vexed at
the things that happen, but let us give thanks: for this belongs to
well-minded household servants. But you, wondering that not before, when you
lived in luxury and wore the worldly show, but now, when you have thrown all
those things and dedicated your whole self to God, then this foul one leapt
on, you do something similar as if you wondered why no one troubles the
spectators, but to the one who has registered himself for boxing, and been
trained, and gone down into the contest, to this one alone of all the
antagonist comes on, striking the head, and beating the face. This then is not
wonderful nor worthy of despondency, if when we have chosen to box, that one
afflicts and grieves and weighs down: for this is the law of those who box;
but if he overturns and throws down and takes away the prize, this is the
terrible thing. And as long as he cannot do this, he has not only harmed
nothing, but has even benefited most greatly by making us more august through
the violent closing. For even in the camps that one is better than all, who
can display more wounds, and has taken up the single combats toward the more
vigorous of the enemies; and of the athletes we wonder at those who



close with the unconquerable (for so they call the more contentious of the
antagonists); and this hunter especially is the one who is mastered, who
undertakes the hard-to-catch of the beasts. Is this demon shameless and
unastonished? =47.447= For this I do not cease being astonished at you and
wondering, that being allotted such an antagonist you did not fall, nor give
yourself up, but remained unbending, overturned from nowhere.

10. And that I
do not say these things now flattering you, but that you have truly harvested
the greatest things from this affliction, endure us speaking with freedom of
speech: for I would not otherwise be able to provide the proof of this. You
know somewhere and remember your former conduct, I mean that before this
trial: therefore examine that well, and set it against the present after the
trial, and then you will see how great the gain has been for you from this
closing. For now both in fasts and vigils, and in the eagerness about reading,
and in the endurance about prayers, there has been much intensification, and
the unscattered and humble has been succeeded in by you with excess; but
formerly there was no account of books for you, but all your care and toil was
spent on the tending of the plants in the garden. And I heard many then also
mocking you into madness, and blaming by this the brilliance of the family,
and the glory of the father, and having been reared in much wealth; and the
slackness in the vigils you yourself know clearly. For often when others rose
at once after deep evening, you yourself remained held by deep sleep, and were
displeased at those who woke you. But now all those things have been loosed,
and have been changed toward the better, from the time you took up this battle
and this war. And if you should ask me why, when you were living in luxury and
fluttering toward the affairs of the world, God did not allow this demon to
stand against you, I would answer this, that this too was of his care: for he
knew that finding you then easy to conquer he would quickly destroy you.
Therefore not even when you had come at once to the life of the monks did he
call you to this wrestling, but he let you be trained in and wait a long time,
then when you had become most enduring, then he drew you to this toilsome
stadium. Will you still remember those turned about in the world, and will you
bring into the midst your household servant? For I think you hint at that one,
when you say that many of worldly men who fell into this disease obtained a
quickest and perfect =47.448= riddance. But the household servant, dearest
one, yours and as many as obtained the same things as that one, did not close
with him on the same terms as you now; for to that one and to such God let
this beast loose only to frighten, and by the fear to make them better; but to
you, so that you might contend nobly, and conquer brilliantly, and bind on the
crown of endurance. And victory is not when someone, while the theater is
still standing, having dragged himself wrestling leads him away from the
antagonist, but when he lets him close continually, then wrestling and through
the despondency leading into absurd thoughts he is able to do nothing. And
that these things are so, I will reason well from this. It is clear to
everyone, I suppose, even if you yourself wish to be moderate, how much better
your life has differed from the life of the household servant. Therefore there
is also more account of you with God and care, than of that one. And this
being clear, that too will likewise be apparent, that if this permission were
of hatred, he would not have extended it upon the one he loved more, snatching
this one who was much less quickly from the demon. And I would not insist on
this from this only, but also from the very things in which you seem to have
been left, from these especially I will try to show that God cares exceedingly
for your things. For if you had not made much eagerness, nor sent yourself on
long journeys, so as to be with holy men sufficient to loose such bonds,
perhaps someone of the many would have been at a loss, the cause not being
very evident, why he allowed so long a time; but since



you also took the places of martyrs, where many even of those who eat men were
healed, and you lived a long time with those wonderful and noble men who have
never missed, and having left out nothing at all of the things that tend to
the riddance of the passion, you returned again having the enemy with
yourself, you have brought a proof of the providence of God about you that is
apparent and evident even to the exceedingly mindless. For he would not have
hindered so great a grace, nor would he have endured his own servants to be
put to shame, if he were not looking exceedingly more toward your approval and
benefit. So that what seems to be a sign of the abandonment of God, this is a
sign of his much affection and eagerness about you.

1. Let these things have been said by us about the
providence of God, and that not turning away nor hating, but even loving
exceedingly, he exercises in this manner. But since you said you were also
otherwise in great pain, so that the demon often even persuades you to wipe
yourself out into the sea or down cliffs, or in some other such manner to end
the present life, I wish to converse a little about these things from the
thoughts. For this counsel is not of that one only, but also of your
despondency, and =47.448= of this more than of that one, and perhaps even of
this alone. And it is clear from the fact that many even of those not
possessed by demons, from grieving only, counsel such things. Therefore cast
this out and drive it from the soul, and that one will have no strength
remaining, not only toward persuading such things, but not even toward
advising at the beginning. For now, just as house-breakers, when night takes
them, having quenched the light, could both steal the things that are there,
and slaughter the masters of these with much ease: =47.449= so also this one,
having scattered despondency instead of night and darkness, tries to steal all
the thoughts that tend to safety, so that taking the soul deserted and without
help he may wound it with countless blows. But when someone, having scattered
this darkness by the hope toward God, and having fled to the sun of
righteousness, is eager to set those rays into his own soul, he will transfer
the turmoil from his own thoughts onto the robber: for those who work such
evils, when someone convicts them and shows the light, tremble, grow dizzy,
are thrown into confusion. And how could someone, he says, be freed from this
pain, if he has not first been freed from the demon that moves it? It is not
the demon who moves the despondency, but that which makes the demon strong,
and suggests the wicked thoughts. And the blessed Paul would bear witness to
us here: for not fearing some demon, but the immoderation of this, he wrote to
the Corinthians to spare at last the one who had offended in the things in
which he sinned, Lest by the more abundant grief, he says, such a one be
swallowed. And if you wish, let us for the time suppose in the account that
the demon lies upon you, but the despondency has been driven out of your soul:
what harm will there be remaining? What by himself could this one wrong us,
either small or great? But that, even when this one is not present, would work
many terrible things; and we will find the greater number of those who have
fastened nooses, or been destroyed by a sword, or been drowned in rivers, or
have otherwise somehow destroyed themselves, to have been pushed from
despondency toward these violent deaths; and if some of those possessed by
demons also are among these, the destruction of these too must be reckoned not
to the demon, but to the



tyranny and force of despondency. And how is it possible, he says, not to be
despondent? If standing off from the opinion of the many about the thing you
think the things above. For now, since the thing is thought by the many to be
terrible, it seems so to you too; but if you should be willing, having been
freed from the vain and wandering preconception, to look down on it by itself
with accuracy, you will find it having no occasion of despondency, as has
already often been shown us. And because of those of the same age (for I think
that seeing their good cheer and their freedom of speech toward the brothers
you are confused and fall down) I would say this, that if, while these were
living in self-control and orderliness and the rest of the philosophy of life,
you yourself spent all the time in brothels and dice and revels, the pretext
of the despondency would have had reason; but if you still walk the same road
with them, what is it that grieves you? For I, if my speech were toward some
other of those easily lifted up, would even have been silent about these
things which I am about to say to you now; but since I have much confidence
that, even if someone should praise and wonder at you ten thousand times, you
will never stand off from being moderate, but will remain even so ranking
yourself among the last, I will say all things holding nothing back. For I
hear that so great an increase of your piety has come to be, that your contest
is no longer toward those youths, but toward the great and wonderful men. For
they say you fall short of those in nothing, not according to the account of
fasting: for how, being fed =47.450= on water and bread only, and this day by
day? not according to the time of the vigils, but likewise with them you drag
through many nights in a row sleepless. And in the conduct by day they say you
have already even hidden many of them; for I hear those coming from there
relating that all your time is spent on prayers and tears; and just as those
who practice silence, or who have even shut themselves alone in a little
house, speak nothing to no one, so they say you also continue, being turned
about in so great a multitude. And the contrition of your heart, and the
drought, and the pain, they have even shuddered relating, and they have stung
many here announcing your things: He does not look, he says, at any of those
who enter there, nor does he let himself go from the continual toils; we have
often feared lest he maim the eyes with the wailings, lest he strike the
membranes with the violence of the sleeplessness and the unyielding and
continual eagerness about reading.

2. Do these things then grieve you and
throw you into turmoil, that you have so outstripped those of the same age,
that having a violent and shameless antagonist you have by much measure
overshot those who run with you? Was I not reasonably saying that this
despondency is of preconception only, but when it is searched it also provides
us many occasions of good cheer? For what is the benefit, tell me, of not
being demon-possessed, when the life has been neglected? And what harm from
being demon-possessed, when the things of the way of life happen to have been
made exact and well put together? But perhaps you are ashamed and blush, when
it throws you down with some present? But this again happens to you from the
same ground, that you have entrusted the thing to the opinion of the many, but
never to reason. For to fall is not this which you say you suffer, but to fall
into sin: at this fall it is good to be ashamed and to be in pain. But now we
sink at the things that have no shame, and doing the things truly shameful and
full of much laughter and last punishment, we think we suffer nothing
terrible; but when the soul falls each day under the working of the sins,
there is no one who mourns; and if ever this body should suffer something, it
seems terrible and unbearable. Is this not to be demon-possessed, for the soul
to be so disposed and to miss in the judgment of things? For if you suffered
this from drunkenness, one ought to sink and be despondent, for the charge was
of choice; but if the thing is of another's force, it is not the one abused,
nor the one undergoing the force, but the one doing it who ought to be ashamed



one must. For in the marketplace, if ever a fight has happened and one has
pushed the other and thrown him down, we all blame the one who threw down, not
the one who fell. It is good to blush, but when we do something of the things
that bring us punishment with the one then going to judge us; and as long as
we are conscious of nothing of this kind in ourselves, why do we hide our
faces? For if someone taking you alone were simply beating you and having
nothing to accuse, or again were throwing you down to the earth, and you,
having borne all things gently, were freed, the thing was not of shame, but of
philosophy and of the greatest =47.451= praises. Then when men abuse, to bear
the abuse is praise; but when the one more wicked than all does this same
thing, will the one who has borne that one's frenzy nobly sink as doing a
blameworthy thing? And what could become more irrational than this
contradiction? For if rising from that fall you were led to do or say
something absurd, not even I would have hindered you from being cut down and
being in pain over these things; but if you bear all things with thanksgiving,
and at once turn yourself to prayers, what then is it that makes the shame?
But perhaps the reproaches from the others are painful. And what could become
more reproachful than these, who could not even know well what one ought to
reproach? For these truly are the ones who are mad and demon-possessed, who
have not learned to see things as they have by nature, but blaming the things
worthy of praises, and transferring the reproachful things into the rank of
those praised. Those sick with phrenitis also say many evils to those present,
but those who hear do not think they are insulted. Therefore do not you
either, hearing those who have stood out of themselves, think the thing to be
shame and insult, lest you ever truly make yourself reproachful by provoking
God. For when you think the things brought on by God toward training and
benefit to be a reproach, see where the evil ends.

3. For if you wish to see
those truly worthy of shame and reproach, I will try to show you a few out of
many by the speech. Look at those fluttering about the beauties of women,
those mad about money, those who love power and glory, and choose both to do
and to suffer all things for these, those wasting away under envy, those
plotting against those who have wronged nothing, those melancholy simply,
those always raging about the vain things of life: these and such things are
works of madness and worthy of punishment, these of reproach and shame and
laughter. But if someone troubled by a demon should even so display much
philosophy in life, he would be just not only not to be reproached, but even
to be wondered at and crowned by all, running so toilsome a race with so many
bonds, and going up the steep and rough road of virtue. But I do not know how
another thing has all but escaped me, in which you have the advantage of the
brothers: and this is that easily, even if something has been offended by you,
through this affliction you strip off even these sins. And this has become
clear to us from the things I already conversed about, when I spoke about
Lazarus and the one among the Corinthians who had committed fornication. But I
fear for my father, he says; for even if I can bear my own things gently, how
shall we easily bear his disasters and his frenzy, if he should ever perceive
something of these? For the time he has not perceived. And it is of much
smallness of soul already to harvest the despondency and beat oneself over the
things that will sometime later happen or will not happen at all. For from
where is it clear to us that he will even perceive? Or rather let it be clear,
and let it be granted, if you wish, both that he will perceive, and that he
will work great terrible things: I praise you for being in pain =47.452= over
his evils, but I do not think it right to do this with your own damage; for
those who think the things above and not the things on the earth must master
not only anger and desire and the other passions, but also despondency. For
this too will work more terrible things for us than those, and one must stand
against it nobly if one is not going to perish altogether. For if



you yourself were going to be the cause of the griefs that befall the father,
you would well have feared and trembled, having become the cause of so great a
destruction; but if he himself wishes to pierce himself round with the last
evils, there is no account of these toward you, except so far as one must
share pain with a father. And apart from these things we do not even know how
he will bear this hearing: for many things contrary to what was expected have
often come out; and that is of the things that happen paradoxically and
rarely, but this is both of the likely and of the exceedingly possible. From
where is this clear? He makes much account of the illegitimate, and the
affection toward those is enough to overshadow this despondency of his. Do not
then vainly pain yourself with the cares. And if one must also be in pain over
the father, it is over the irrational expenses, over the revels, and over the
conceit, and over the tyranny, over the adultery now. Or do you think it a
small evil, when your mother is inside, to waste himself on another girl, and
to beget children from lawless marriages? These things are worthy of mourning,
these of tears, the apparent evils that have come out to an end; but the
things that will happen in your case perhaps will be terrible, perhaps not
terrible, and it is of much irrationality already to undergo an agreed torture
over things so unclear. And let us grant that they will also meet with much
violence, but they will have a quickest end, and before they are well kindled
they will be quenched. For a man attending to such luxury, and caring for many
affairs, and feeding parasites and flatterers, and so burning about that girl
from whom he has the brothers half to you, even if he takes perception of your
things, will take a short one and worthy of nothing. And this I guess not from
what I have said only, but also from the things already happened: for you
know, you know clearly, that loving you exceedingly before these things, and
thinking your head everything, when you changed to the life of the monks, he
quenched all that longing, and thought the thing shameful, and unworthy of the
brilliance of the ancestors, and said you were putting his glory to shame; and
if the necessity of nature had not held him, perhaps he would even have
disowned you. So that, if what is said is not exceedingly insensible, I think
he would even be pleased at what has happened, as if you had paid a penalty
for the things in which, when he often encouraged and wished to lead you away
from this endurance of life, you did not endure the counsel even as far as
words.

contest and toilsome, if indeed we are going to enjoy the eternal rest and the

pleasant things here, and the rewards stored up there for those who toil, he



cheats himself and deceives. For just as in the case of athletes the one who

necessary to rest that unaging ease, then will wail and grind the teeth, and
will suffer the last terrible things; but the one who here has borne the
afflictions eagerly will both here and there be brilliant and renowned with
the immortal and true glory. For if in worldly things the one who confuses the
timely season of affairs will fall out of all the things set before him, and
will throw himself round with countless disasters, much more in spiritual
things will he suffer this who does not know the order of the times. Christ
said that You will have affliction in the world. The blessed Paul said: And
all who wish to live piously in Christ Jesus will be persecuted; not speaking

luxury and rest; if in the time in which we were commanded to contend and toil

for I am persuaded by the word of Christ only, which says that Narrow and
constricted is the road =47.454= that leads away into life; and that it is not

suppose. For where in the outer contests no one bound on a crown without
sweats, and this when he has the antagonists sharing the same nature, where

5. But up to what point do we
examine these things from thoughts, when it is possible to flee to the blessed
and noble athletes who came to be in the former times? For examine all those
then renowned, and you will see all to have had freedom of speech toward God
from the afflictions. And if you wish, let us first come to the child of the
first-formed, the lamb of Christ, Abel, who wronged nothing, but underwent the
things of those who have worked the most terrible things. For we pay the
trials as penalties of sins; but that righteous one was punished for no other
thing, but that he was righteous. And as long as he displayed nothing great,
the brother recognized him; but when he shone from the sacrifice, he was
ignorant even of nature itself, blinded by envy. From where then do you know
if now too the same cause has raised the devil, and the brilliance of your
life has called him out to this battle? And if you laugh at these words of
mine, I praise you for the humility, yet I will not cease being so disposed.
For if offering fat that one was so approved, the one who dedicated not the
things from outside, but his whole self to God, much more would he have
provoked the enemy against himself. And God allowed him to come on; for not
even then did he hinder the murder from happening, but he let the righteous
fall into the hands of the murderer, and he did not snatch away the one being
taken away because of him and the honor toward him; for he did not wish the
crowns to be lessened for him; therefore he let him run as far as the end. And
what punishment is death? he says. Would that I too had undergone this
vengeance now. You say these things, beloved, now, but formerly the thing
seemed to all to be most harsh, and more pitiable than every punishment.
Therefore also in the



law of Moses those who had done the greatest wrongs and things greater than
pardon underwent this punishment. And among the outer lawgivers still even now
all who are caught in the most shameful evils are punished in no other way;
but still the righteous suffered the same things as the lawless, or rather
even much harsher, inasmuch as he also received the blow from a brother's
right hand. And what of Noah? For this one too was righteous and perfect, and
when all had been corrupted he alone was well-pleasing to God, all the others
having offended him. He underwent countless afflictions, many and evil. For
this one did not die at once like that one, nor did he suffer this which seems
to you to be light, but he endured a long life, in so many years being
disposed no better than those who bear burdens and are pressed by a heaviest
load continually. And this I will make apparent to you at once, saying that
first. A whole year this one inhabited a prison, a strange and fearful prison.
For to leave aside the multitude of the beasts and the reptiles, with whom he
lived so long a time shut into such =47.455= constriction, what do you think
he suffered from the crash of the thunders, from the noise of the rains? An
abyss below was broken open, and one from above was carried down; and he sat
inside alone with the sons. For even if he had confidence about the end, still
by the violence of the things that happened he had already died beforehand
from fear. For where we, having houses founded upon the earth, and inhabiting
cities, still when we see a rain carried down a little more violently than the
usual, we fall down and contract ourselves, what would that one not have
suffered, being inside alone, and considering that most shuddering abyss and
the various kinds of those drowned? It is enough then to astonish a soul even
that one city or even a house alone, being flooded, was covered by the waves,
but that the whole inhabited world suffered this, one cannot even say how it
would have disposed the one being carried about in the midst of the swell.
That year then he was held by so great a fear. And when at last the flood
ceased, the fear gave way remaining, but the things of despondency were
intensified for him, and another winter no less than the former received him
peering from the ark: seeing the much desolation, and that violent death, and
the bodies of those taken away mixed with the slime and the mud, and all
together, both men and asses and the kinds more dishonored than these, buried
in one most pitiable burial. For even if those who had suffered these things
were exceedingly sinners, still Noah was a man, and he suffered something
toward the same race. For Ezekiel too, being righteous, and knowing the
Israelites to be most wicked of all, when he saw them being slaughtered and
falling, suffered this common thing and wept; and yet for this God taking the
lead had uncovered all their impiety to him, and had prepared him to oversee
it with his own eyes, so that when he sees them being punished, he may bear
the disaster nobly. But still even when so great a consolation had been
prepared for him beforehand, he was even so convicted, and falling he cried
out saying: Woe is me, Lord, do you wipe out the remnant of Israel? And he did
not suffer this once only, but also again when he saw Jeconiah being taken
away. So that Noah too, even if he was conscious of countless evils in them,
was neither braver than this one, nor than Moses. For that one too often
suffered the same things as the prophet, and seeing them sinning, when they
ought to be punished, he was in pain, and was more pained than those being
punished themselves. And in the case of Noah the disaster was also harsher:
for that death came to be such for him alone. Held then by so many evils, by
the isolation, by the sympathy toward the same race, by the multitude of those
destroyed, by the manner of the end, by the desolation of the earth, and with
the despondency being raised from all sides and blooming violently, at once
there is also added the insult of the child, an unbearable thing, and full of
much shame, and of much pain. For as much as the reproaches from friends are
heavier than those from enemies, so much more



still more unbearable are those from children than these themselves. For when
he whom he begot, whom he reared, whom he trained, for whom he endured many
labors and toils and cares, he sees this one using him more insultingly than
all =47.456=, the one who has suffered this will not even be able to bear the
much mourning of the soul. For insult to the free is even by itself
unbearable, but when it also comes from children, it could even lead the one
suffering into a standing-out: so heaviest it is. But do not consider only
that daring, but from this also learn how this insulter used him through all
the time before. For he who still had in his hands the fear of the things that
had happened, and had just been freed from that prison, and was still looking
at the disasters of the inhabited world, and not even so was brought to sense,
but insulted the one whom least of all he ought, and became better neither by
the death of the many, nor by the desolation, nor by the wrath of God, nor by
any other of the things that then befell, what was he before the flood, when
he also had many encouraging him toward vice? Then, then that righteous one
underwent a harsher swell than the one that happened after these things at the
flood, both because of this one, and because of all the others. For at the
flood a multitude of waters surrounded him only, but before this he was held
from all sides by an abyss of vice, and the plots of the wicked men broke upon
him more violently than the waves. For left alone into so great a multitude of
lawless and foul men, even if he suffered nothing terrible, still he bore much
laughter and many mockeries, if not before, yet when he conversed about the
ark and the disasters about to be theirs. And how much power this has toward
throwing a soul into confusion, the one sanctified from the womb would bear
witness to us, who for this was even going to stand off from the prophecy. For
I said, he says, I will not prophesy. And apart from these things, the mere
having no one of the same soul, nor of the same manner, how much
unpleasantness, how much despondency did it construct for him? And not this
only, but also by sharing pain with them he suffered countless terrible
things. For surely the righteous do not so grieve looking at the wicked only
when they die, but no longer when they sin, but they are bitten much more by
this than by that; and this too one could learn well from the prophets. For
one cried out bitterly: Woe is me, soul, because a pious one has perished from
the earth, and the one who succeeds among men is not. And another said toward
God: Why have you shown me to look upon toils and labors? And beating himself
exceedingly over those wronged, he lamented saying: You will make men as the
fishes of the sea having no leader. And if then these things happened, even
when there were laws, and rulers, and courts, and priests, and prophets, and
punishments, consider with how great excess all things were dared in the time
of Noah, when none of these held them. And in the case of the prophets the
time of life was not even much, but seventy years and eighty; but then the
life of men was stretched even to six hundred years and more. Therefore, that
I may leave aside all the things said, =47.457= how many affairs was he
compelled to have, traveling so long a road, and being eager not even a little
in so great a length to be turned aside, when there were many hindrances in
between? And why do I say many, when the whole road through and through was
such, from the ends of the earth to the ends, filled with all reefs and thorns
and beasts and drought and plague and frost and criminals? For it was easier
to travel a worn path in the deepest of the night continually, than the road
of virtue in those times: so many were those forcing his steps to turn aside.
For when it is permitted to all to do whatever they wish, and one only is the
one walking the contrary to them, how will he be able to come to the end when
all push backward and beat him back? For how much difficulty there is in
succeeding when one is with many, those who have now taken the deserts make
plain, when by



the grace of God the good life is to be seen scattered everywhere, when there
is also no small concord and affection toward one another. But then none of
these things was, but all were disposed more wildly than beasts toward him.

6.
What then could become more painful than this life? What more worthless than
this living? I promised then to show him disposed no better than those who
bear burdens continually and never breathe; but the speech has done much more,
showing him to us not only no better, but even much more burdensome than
those. Now it seems to many that Abraham completed all the time in good cheer,
whence they are also accustomed to compare to him those who prosper and
flourish from all sides. Come then, let us examine the things that befell him.
For to me this one is much even than Noah and than Abel; or rather I will say
nothing, until the examination of the affairs sets the vote for us. The things
in Persia, then, and if anything grievous befell him up to the seventy years,
no one could know clearly. For neither did the blessed Moses write us the
history of that time, but leaving all this age, he has made the beginning of
the narrative from the things after these. And that he too was likely to
suffer the same things as Noah, wishing to be pious alone among so many
impious and barbarians, this is no longer unclear like the other things, but
even the exceedingly mindless could easily guess it. Still let these things
too be left aside now; and let us make the beginning of the speech from his
journey abroad, having first examined this, how far the land of the Chaldeans
stands off from Palestine, or of what sort the construction of the roads was,
how the things of intercourse with one another were disposed for men, what the
manner of the constitution was. For do not, because that righteous one obeyed
easily, let the thing now seem to be easy, nor because Moses related what
happened shortly and in brief, think that the work also imitates the shortness
of the words: for to say these things and relate them is easy, but to do them
is no longer similarly easy, but even exceedingly hard. The length of the road
then and how great the interval is of these places those who have crossed from
there to us could say with accuracy, if indeed there =47.458= are some; but
we, having been with none of those, but with someone of those who arrived at
the outer country, and having asked the time itself, how much he was compelled
to travel, heard that a number of thirty-five days; and Babylon he said he did
not know, but he had heard from those who had been there that there is again
another so great a road from there. And the distance was so great both then,
and is now; but the construction of the roads is no longer similarly disposed
as both now and then. For now it is divided both by continual stations and
cities and fields, and one walking would meet many travelers, which is no less
than a station and a city and a field in the account of safety. And besides
these the rulers of the cities from the country, having arrayed men differing
from the others in strength of body and able to suffice with javelin and sling
as much as archers with arrows, and the hoplites with spears, and having set
rulers over them, have occupied them for nothing else, but have entrusted only
the guard of the roads. And with these they have invented again another safety
more exact than these. For having built dwellings at an interval of a thousand
steps beside the road, they have stationed night-guards in them, walling the
assaults of the criminals with their sleeplessness and leisure as a greatest
hindrance. But then none of these things was, no continual fields, no cities,
no stations, no lodging, no quickly seeing a fellow-traveler, no other of
these things at all; for I leave aside now the roughness of the path and the
unevenness of the airs, although they are enough, when those are not present,
to grieve those traveling sufficiently. And those who use yokes and beasts of
burden would bear witness to me, and who do not even so dare to go upon the
usual



road, unless they first strew it with stones, and having filled the ravines
from the streams thus make it level. But then it was more deserted than the
uninhabited, more without a way than the mountains, more slippery than the
pits and the cliffs. And the harshest of all I have not said, how the things
of intercourse were disposed, which also provided them much more and greater
affairs than the road, all being divided by nations, or rather by city. For it
was not as now one rule is stretched over the greater part of the inhabited
world, and all are slaves to one man, and are administered by the same laws,
so also then; but as one body cut into many parts, so also the race of men was
torn, and the righteous was compelled to exchange enemies for enemies, before
escaping the former, always falling into others, there being now many-rule,
now no-rule. What then is harsher than this life? For he did not fear and
tremble for himself only, but also for father and wife and for a brother's
son. And the care of the household servants was not even small, even if they
happened to be on their own, much less when they were compelled to be on the
foreign continually. And if he knew clearly where the things of the wandering
would stop for him, the unbearable of the cares would not have been so; but
now having heard simply and indefinitely Land, not this or that, but, Which I
will show you, =47.459= he went about everywhere with the mind, and received
much turmoil in the soul, being able to stand nowhere with the thought, but
having many things to suspect, and being compelled to be anxious. For it is
likely that he expected even to arrive at the very ends of the inhabited
world, and at the ocean itself; so that even if he did not go through all the
earth, still he underwent the care of so great a traveling. For he had not
prepared the soul as going to come as far as Palestine, but as going to follow
everywhere, even if he should be commanded to go out of the inhabited world,
even to come to the islands outside this. And if the indefinite of the command
also persuaded him to expect the contraries, this again is hard. For the one
about to undergo something burdensome, it is much lighter to know clearly what
he will stand, and toward what he will prepare himself, than to wander
everywhere with the thought, and now expect the good things, now the
contraries, and to have confidence about none of these, but to disbelieve both
alike.

7. And these things were before he came into the promised land; but
when at last he took hold of Palestine, and remaining expected to rest, then
indeed he found a greater winter in the harbor. And this is not small in the
account of pain, but even more unbearable than all, when someone thinking he
has been freed and has come to the very end, and having laid aside all care
and anxiety, again from above finds another beginning of the difficulties. For
the one still prepared toward the terrible things would more easily bear the
bringing-on of these; but the one who has slumped and loosed the thoughts from
the cares, if something of this kind happens again, as also before, is somehow
doubly thrown into confusion, and becomes easy to master both by the
unexpected and by all the eagerness and the preparation having been dissolved
for him. What then was the winter? A famine so violent then took Palestine, as
to raise him at once from there, and bring him down into Egypt. And coming
there as going to find a release of the evil, he fell again into a disaster
harsher than the famine, being compelled to be in danger about the last
things; and he then came into so great a fear, that what is more unbearable
than all to all, that their own wives be insulted, this he chose. For he then
came into such necessity, as even to serve the pretense, than which what could
become more pitiable? For what sort of soul do you think he had, when he was
compelled to advise the wife such things? I know I that you are a fair-faced
woman; it will be then that when the Egyptians see you, they will say that,
This is his wife; and they will kill me, but they will keep you. Say then
that, I am his sister, that it may be well for me because of you, and my soul
will live for your sake. And



these words he uttered who for God had left fatherland, and house, and
friends, and kinsmen, and all the rest at the house, who had undergone such
hardship and so great a toil in this long time along the traveling; and still
he said none of these things, such as that God has left me, he has turned
away, and has cast me out of his own providence; but he bore all things nobly
and faithfully, and he whom one ought most of all to be angry when the wife
was insulted, from the much force, did all things so that the insult might not
even become apparent. And how much despondency and pain this has, it is not
possible to set beside by a word, but those know who =47.460= have had wives
and have fallen into a suspicion of jealousy. And Solomon also bears witness
to this passion, saying thus: For the anger of her husband is full of
jealousy, he will not spare in a day of judgment, nor will he exchange the
enmity for any ransom, nor will it even be dissolved by many gifts. And again:
Love is strong as death, jealousy hard as Hades. And if the one who is jealous
is so kindled, the one mastered by so many evils as to be compelled even to
flatter the insulter, and to do all things, whom one ought to defend oneself
against, so that the adulterer may easily enjoy his own wife, than whom was he
not more pitiable? And when these evils also took an end, other terrible
things were again born, and a war succeeded that famine. For I leave aside now
the fight of the shepherds and the distribution toward the brother's son,
although these, examined upon others, are enough to throw in much despondency.
For when the one saved by him and become master of so many goods, and whom one
ought to have stood aside from all things for him, and to have rebuked his own
shepherds, this one, when a choice is put forward, runs upon the more fertile
of the places, and leaves the more deserted parts to that one: who could
easily bear, I do not say the damage, but the being dishonored after honoring
and the bearing the less, which seems to be harsher than every damage? But
still I leave aside all these things now: for the speech is about the
patriarch, not about someone of the many.

8. That famine then is succeeded by
the war of the Persians, and he is compelled to campaign against them, not at
the very beginning while both parts were still unharmed, but when all had been
turned in flight, and the victory was with the enemies, and no one was still
able to bear them, some having been cut down utterly, some hiding, some being
slaves among them. But still none of these things persuaded him to remain at
home, but struck by the violent despondency of the things that had happened he
went off to share those evils with them, and to throw himself into a death in
full view. For to array himself with three hundred household servants and a
little more against so great an army was of one reckoning these things, and
prepared toward captivity and toward punishment, and toward countless deaths.
He went off then as himself also going to enjoy the barbarian cruelty; but
having been saved by the love of God toward mankind, and having returned with
the spoil and the kinsman, he was compelled again to lament his own evils, I
mean the childlessness, and having no successor of the inheritance. For do
not, because you then heard him lamenting toward God and saying, What will you
give me? and I perish childless; on this account think this grief also to be
fresh: for this anxiety and this care entered the house of the righteous with
the bride, or rather even before the bride herself. For it is a custom for us
all, when we only begin to counsel and converse about marriage, to be pricked
by all the cares of the thing, of all of which the chief is that of the
children, and this fear from that day shakes our souls. And if =47.461= it
should happen, when the marriages have come to be, that a first year or a
second or even a third pass, the things of despondency are intensified, and
those of the better hopes become weaker; and when another more time has been
added, those stand off remaining altogether, and this masters the soul,
overshadowing all the most pleasant things,



and allowing perception of nothing. So that even if none of so many griefs had
befallen him, but all things had come to be according to mind, the despondency
of childlessness following all the things that happen would be enough to
overshadow all good cheer and throw it down. For the promise of God came
toward extreme old age, when the things of nature had been despaired of; but
through all the time before, so great as it was, he continued being in pain
and beating himself, and as much as he saw the wealth increasing, so much more
he mourned, there being no one who would succeed to this. For what do you
think he suffered, when he heard: Your seed will be a sojourner in a land not
its own, and they will enslave them, and they will maltreat and humble them
four hundred years? And the wife now commanding him to take her own
maidservant, now when he had taken accusing and blaming, and calling God
against him, and compelling him to cast out the one who had shared the bed,
and who was remaining about to bear him a child, into what last despondency
would she not have thrown? For even if these things seem to someone small now,
having reckoned that whole houses have been overturned because of these, let
him wonder at the righteous man. For even if he bore nobly because of the fear
of God, still he was a man, and he was bitten and pained by all these. And the
servant-girl of the master returns again into the house, and bears him the
illegitimate, and Abraham becomes a father after that long time; and the thing
had some pleasure, but a greater despondency. For the illegitimate reminded
him of the genuine, and threw in a greater desire of the thing. For the word,
This one will not inherit you, but he who will come out from you, he thought
to have been said about Ishmael: for nothing had yet been said about Sarah.
And when remaining he also received a clearer promise about Isaac, and a time
was defined for the birth, before he was again pleased with these hopes, the
sufferings of the Sodomites poured much mist of despondency over him. For that
the thing did not confuse the righteous man as it chanced, is clear to
everyone, I suppose, from the words themselves and the supplication which he
set toward God on their behalf; and when he also saw that most shuddering rain
carried down, and all things become dust and ash suddenly, he was no longer
even in himself remaining. For if seeing houses burning from far we stand out
and fall down from the despondency and the fear, what would that one not have
suffered seeing whole cities and countries with the inhabitants being burned
down not with the usual burning, but with a strange and fearful one? Does the
suffering of the righteous not seem to you to imitate the continuity of the
successive waves in the sea? For just as there, the former not yet having
vanished, the second are peaked, so also it happened through and through upon
the life of this righteous man. For still while the evils in Sodom were
blooming, the king of Gerar attempted to dare the same things as Pharaoh
against Sarah; and again the wife was compelled to play the pitiable =47.462=
pretense, and the insult would have come out into a work, if God had not again
hindered. And together while the child and the wife and the whole house were
being of good cheer at the birth of the child, the righteous man alone in so
great a pleasure was compelled to grieve and be despondent, being compelled to
cast out the concubine with the child. For even if Ishmael was illegitimate
and from a maidservant, still the tyranny of nature became no less from the
unworthiness, nor did the low birth of the mother make weaker the necessity of
the paternal bowels; and this one can learn from the Book itself. For the firm
and manly one, who undertook to slaughter the only-begotten with his own
hands, this one was in pain when the wife commanded these things to him, and
he would not have yielded nor been persuaded by Sarah, although she then had
more freedom of speech, and was speaking toward him, if the fear of God had
not bent him exceedingly. So that when you hear that, God having commanded, he
sent out, do not think the pain to have been loosed (for this was impossible),
but wonder at the violent obedience, that even being bent by the sympathy he
did not speak against God, but



he sent out the little child with the mother, not even knowing where he would
travel, but he endured and bore being in pain: for he was not stronger than
nature.

9. And this he suffered also upon the genuine child. For let no one
say that he was not in pain, nor underwent the things of fathers, nor, wishing
to show him philosophizing beyond measure, let him deprive him of the chief of
the praises. For if those caught in the most shameful things, and having
enjoyed this life a long time, and being unknown, and never even seen by us,
these we, seeing suddenly in the marketplace being led away to death, contract
ourselves and are in pain, and often even weep: the one commanded to sacrifice
and burn with his own hands his genuine son, the only-begotten, the one born
against hopes, the one after so great a time, the one toward extreme old age
(for these things also raise the flame more), this one still being young, did
he suffer nothing human? And what is more laughable than those who say these
things? For if he were a stone, if he were iron, if he were adamant itself,
would he not have been bent and broken toward the season of the child (for he
was bringing the very flower of the age), toward the understanding of the
words, toward the piety of the soul? He asked the father then saying: Behold
the wood and the fire, where is the sheep? He heard that, God will see for
himself a sheep for a whole-burnt offering, O child; and he was over-curious
about nothing more. He saw the father binding him, and he did not stretch
against; he was set upon the wood, and he did not leap off; he saw the knife
being carried against him, and he was not thrown into confusion. What could
become more pious than this soul? Will someone still dare to say that Abraham
suffered nothing from all these? For if he were about to sacrifice an enemy
and a foe, if it were a beast, would he have done these things without pain?
It is not so, it is not: do not condemn the righteous man of such cruelty; for
he was in pain and was cut through. God, he says, =47.463= will see a sheep
for himself for a whole-burnt offering, child. Of how much sympathy do you
think these words are? But still he held the pain down and pressed it, and he
did all things with so great an eagerness, as those held by none of these
hindrances. Having sacrificed him then (for he did sacrifice in the
eagerness), he hands him over healthy and sound to the mother; and she, having
taken him, before she was well filled with the child, ends her life. And that
too grieved Abraham not little. For even if she had been with him a long time,
nothing was enough to persuade him to bear the disaster more easily; this
itself then provided him more despondency: for those who have lived together a
long time, and have given us much proof of their own both friendship and
virtue, these we seek most of all. And that these things are so, the patriarch
himself made plain, having mourned and lamented her. And what would one say of
the cares over the child, those over this one's wife, those over the brothers
of this one, all the other things, which if someone should wish to examine
according to accuracy, he will see the life of the righteous much more painful
than what has now been shown and full of more cares? For Scripture, having
said only the more chief things, has left all the other things for us to
examine, as many as are likely to happen each day in a house, where there is a
multitude of household servants and a man and a wife and little children and
care of many affairs. Yes, he says; but for that one the greatest consolation
in each of these griefs was that he underwent all those things because of God.
Therefore this will also console you now: for the one who allowed this trial
to come upon you is no other, but God. For if those wicked demons would not
have dared to leap upon swine, if he had not then permitted, much more upon
your precious soul. As then for that one the bearing all things nobly and
thankfully brought much reward, so it will also bring for you, only if you are
not displeased nor indignant, but give thanks for all things to the God who
loves mankind. Since the blessed Job also suffered what he suffered, God
having allowed, but what brought him the



crowns was not the suffering only, but also the standing nobly toward all the
terrible things; and we all wonder at him, not because the devil deprived him
of all things, but because in all these he did not sin, not even with his
lips.

10. And since I have remembered Job, I wished indeed also to set out in
the speech his long laments, and the violence of the sufferings; but for the
sake of not making the length much I return toward Isaac. And you, if you wish
to learn his things with accuracy, taking his book in hand peer toward the
abyss of the disasters, and you will find in those much consolation of your
things. For even if the righteous man is much better than we, still he also
arrayed himself toward a much greater working: for the Evil One then blew more
violently against that one. And the successes are judged not by the measure of
the trials, but by the power of the things done. So that even if this present
contest is lesser, this will be able to harm you nothing toward the crowns.
For the one who brought the two talents brought no less than the one who
displayed the five. Why? Because even if the income =47.464= is not the same,
still the eagerness is the same, whence also each obtained the same honor,
having heard, Enter into the joy of your Lord. What then of Isaac? He did not
send himself on a long journey abroad, as the father, nor was he compelled to
leave his own land, as that one, but he himself also underwent the chief of
the evils, the fear of childlessness. And when he loosed the terrible thing by
praying, another fear greater than that received him: for it was not equal to
tremble over childlessness, and over the root itself. For she was so cut
through by the birth-pangs as to undergo a life more bitter than every death;
and hear her saying this, If it is going to be thus for me, why is living
mine? And he himself also took experience of famine; and if he did not go down
into Egypt, still what the father all but suffered there, these things this
one also underwent and was in danger over his own wife. And all the neighbors
honored the father, but this one they drove as an enemy and a foe and they did
not let him enjoy his own toils, but setting that one in much constriction,
they themselves with authority lived in luxury in his labors. And when at last
this one also acquired these as friends, and saw his own children come to be
in age, then indeed, then, when he expected to find much consolation, and to
have good tenders of old age, he fell into the last despondency. For first the
elder, taking a wife from the foreigners against what seemed good to the
father, by this itself exceedingly pained him, and by bringing fight and war
into the house. For those women threw them round with countless evils, all of
which Scripture, declining to say, hinted the whole through one word, saying
that They strove with Rebecca; and it left those who have a house and children
already married to know what was said. For those most of all know accurately
how great an evil it is for the mother of the bridegroom and the bride to be
disposed contentiously, and especially when both inhabit one house. This then
was the continual terrible thing. And what happened upon these, the maiming of
the eyes, how great an evil it is those who have suffered know only; and after
this, the missing about the blessings of the child, by which he was so struck
in the soul, as to cry out more bitterly than the one who underwent the deceit
himself, and to make a defense toward him: for he said he had not wronged
willingly, but having been deceived. And the things from here showed the
tragedy upon the stage, and copied the drama of the Theban youths. For here
too the elder, having despised the age and the maiming of the father, cast the
younger brother out of the house. And if he did not kill as that one, the
wisdom of the mother hindered this; for this one too threatened the murder,
and waited for the end of the father. And the mother, having learned, and
having conversed with the father, snatched him from that one's hands, and they
were compelled to exile the one who served them and was disposed with goodwill
=47.465=, but the



worthless one who made life unlivable for them (for this Rebecca herself says)
they held among themselves continually. That one then having run away who had
been reared continually at the house (for he was simple, inhabiting a house,
and for the most part keeping house with the mother), how many things was
Rebecca likely to wail and lament, remembering the child continually, and
seeing the husband disposed no better than the dead both because of the age
and because of the disease? And with how much mourning was the old man held,
being compelled with his own to lament also the disasters of the wife? And
when she was about to die, what then did she not say, what did she not utter,
things enough even to melt a stone, not seeing the child standing by and
weeping, and wiping eyes, and taking hold of the mouth, and laying out, and
caring for all the other things, which seem to those who have begotten to be
more bitter than the death itself? And Isaac, seeing her so breathing out,
what sort of soul did he possess both then and after her end?

11. Such has the
one who seemed to many to be of better cheer been shown us. And the life of
Jacob the very declaration of Jacob is enough to show even without
examination: for conversing then with Pharaoh he said: Small and evil are my
days, and they have not arrived at the days of my fathers; that is, I have
lived both a shorter and a more worthless life. And apart from the declaration
his disasters are so very apparent, that not even some of the many are
ignorant of them. For his grandfather, even if he sent himself on the long
journey abroad, still God commanding, which brought him the greatest
consolation; but this one fleeing a brother plotting and murdering against
him; and that one never wailed over the supply of the necessary things; but
this one thought it desirable and to be prayed for, to have plenty of a
garment and bread only. And having been saved, and having been freed from the
evils of the traveling, and having come to his own kinsmen, he who had been
reared in so great abundance was compelled to be a slave. And you know that
everywhere slavery is bitter; but when someone is compelled among those of
equal honor to undergo the things of household servants, and this never having
taken experience of the thing, but having spent all the first age in freedom
and luxury, the terrible thing becomes unbearable; but still he bore all
things nobly. And you hear him relating the hardships in his shepherding, and
saying: I paid from myself thefts of day and thefts of night; I became burned
with the heat of the day and with the frost of the night, and sleep stood off
from my eyes. These things for me twenty years. These things he suffered who
had lived the simple life, who had inhabited a house continually, and after so
many labors and the damages and that long time, he underwent the bitter deceit
about the wife. For if he had not been a slave the seven years, if he had
suffered none of the things he wailed toward the father-in-law, if he did not
love the girl, the mere having the better pledged to him, but the worse given
instead of that one, how much despondency, how much turmoil, how much abuse
did it provide that blessed one then! Another then if there were someone,
would not easily have borne =47.466= the abuse, but would have overturned the
whole house of the in-laws, slaughtering himself with them, or even destroying
himself together in some other manner; but since Jacob was forbearing and
long-suffering, he did not do this, nor even counsel it at the beginning; and
being commanded to be a slave another seven years again, he obeyed readily: so
gentle and reasonable he was. And if the love of the girl took part with the
reasonableness of the manners, again you tell me the excess of the
despondency. For consider how great a pain he underwent, when he was deprived
of the one so loved, and expecting to have her inside, he was beaten back into
a delay of seven years again, and the frosts and the heats and the
sleeplessnesses and the continual damages. And having taken this one too
later, and living with the father-in-law a hardship-filled and toilsome life,
he was envied even so, and underwent a second deceit



about the wages, as he himself also convicted him saying that, You have
cheated me with ten lambs. And with the father-in-law the brothers of the
wives also became more beast-like toward him than the father-in-law. And the
harshest of all was, the beloved, for whom he chose to be a slave twice seven
years, was being choked by despondency, seeing the sister bearing, but herself
not even having a hope of the thing, and she was then carried out into such a
standing-out by the despondency as even to accuse and blame the husband, and
to threaten death against herself, if she should not bear: For give me
children, she says; and if not, I will die. What then was able to make him of
good cheer, when the one so longed for was wailing such things, and her
brothers were plotting against him and doing all things, so that he might come
into last poverty? And if the things given to wives without sweats, when they
are taken away, bring much despondency, how would the one in danger over the
things acquired by his own toils have borne this damage gently? Perceiving
then that they had him under suspicion and looked askance, he withdrew
secretly as a fugitive: than which what could become more pitiable? For having
journeyed abroad both from his own and from the foreign with fears and dangers
he was compelled to fall into the same pits again. For fleeing the brother he
came to the father-in-law, and being driven again by this one, he was forced
to fall into the brother; and he underwent that prophetic thing, which Amos
says about the day of the Lord: As if someone should flee from the face of a
lion, and a bear should fall upon him, and he should leap into his house, and
lean his hand upon the wall, and the serpent should bite him. And what need is
there to speak of the trembling which he underwent being overtaken by Laban,
the afflictions along the traveling, so many flocks and children following
him? But when he was about to see the brother's sight, did he not suffer the
same as those who see the head of the Gorgon fashioned by those among the
poets? did he not arrange all things as going away to death? Hear his words,
and learn the flame stored in his soul: Deliver me, he says, O God, from the
hand of Esau my brother, because I fear him, lest somehow coming he strike me
and mother upon children. And you said to me, I will do you well. How much
good cheer would this fear not have cast out, even if he had completed all the
time before in good cheer? But now =47.467= all his life even from that day,
in which about to receive the blessings he had already died beforehand from
fear, was woven through disasters and plots. For so great a fear then held
him, that not even after the meeting, although the brother used him well and
with love of mankind, did he have confidence and lay aside the agony. When
that one then encouraged him to go away together, as about to be freed from
some beast, he so thought it right and contrived the withdrawal, saying: My
lord, you know that the little children are more tender, and the sheep and the
oxen are giving birth upon me; and if then I drive them hard, in one day all
the cattle will die. Let my lord go forward before his servant, and I will
strengthen myself in the road, according to the leisure of the journey
opposite me, and according to the feet of my little children, until I come to
my lord into Seir. And having breathed a little from those dangers, he fell
again into another much harsher fear. For the daughter having been seized,
first he was in pain at the insult of the girl; and when the son of the king
consoled what had happened, promising to take her as wife by the law of
marriage, and Jacob approved the judgment, those about Levi confused the
agreements, and having taken away the city man and all they set the one who
begot them into so great a trembling, that he even moved house from there
because all had been made enemies toward him. For Jacob said, it says, to
Simeon and to Levi: You have made me hated, so that I am evil to all who dwell
the land, both among the Canaanites and the Perizzites, and I am very few in
number, and gathering



against me they will cut me down and I will be rubbed out, I and my house. For
the neighbors would even have slaughtered them all utterly, if the love of God
toward mankind had not held that one's anger, and set an end to those evils:
For there came to be, it says, a fear of God upon all the cities round about
them, and they did not pursue behind the sons of Israel. What then after the
riddance of these? Did he breathe? Then indeed the crown of the evils came
upon him, the death of the beloved, being both untimely together and violent.
For Rachel bore, it says, and she had a hard time in the birth. And it came to
be in her bearing hardly, the midwife said to her, Take courage, for this too
is a son for you. And it came to be in her letting the soul go (for she was
dying), she called his name Son of my pain. And while the mourning was still
blooming, Reuben added to the despondency by insulting into the father's bed;
which he bore so harshly, as even dying to curse the child, when especially
parents are more sympathetic toward the offspring, and this when he was
first-born of all the others; and this is not small toward the account of
affection. But still the force of despondency pushed aside all these things,
and calling him he said: Reuben my first-born, my strength, and beginning of
my children, hard to bear, and hard, self-willed; you have insulted, that you
may not boil over as water; for you went up upon the bed of your father; then
you polluted the mattress which you went up. And when the son of the loved
wife came to be in age, and he expected to have him as consolation of the
grief over her, then many-formed disasters were constructed for him through
this one: for =47.468= the brothers, having dipped that one's little tunic in
blood and having shown it to the father, worked a varied mourning for him. For
he mourned not the end only, but also the manner of the end, and many were the
things confusing his soul: that the son of the loved one, that the better than
all the others, that the one loved most, that in the very flower of the age,
that sent by him, that not even at the house, nor upon the bed, nor with the
father standing by, nor having said something and heard, that not with the
common death of all, that living he was torn by the cruelty of the beasts,
that he did not even find to gather the remains and give them to the earth,
that he did not even undergo these things in youth, when it was possible to
bear, but in extreme old age. And it was a sight more pitiable than all to
see, gray hair being put to shame with dust, and an aged breast being bared
when the tunic was torn through, and laments greater than consolation: For
Jacob tore, it says, his garments, and put sackcloth upon his loin, and he
mourned his son many days. And all his sons and the daughters were gathered,
and they came to console him, and he did not wish to be consoled, saying that,
I will go down toward my son mourning into Hades. And as if it were necessary
that his soul never be clean of despondency, so, when this blow began to be
healed, a famine coming on and seizing all the earth first grieved him
exceedingly; and when the sons coming up from Egypt brought a consolation of
the evil, they led it mixed with another grief, and the pleasure from the
loosing of the famine was confused by the journey abroad of the child. And not
this only, but that they also demanded Benjamin from him, whom alone he had as
a soothing both of the wife who had gone and of the child eaten by beasts. And
not these things only made him hold to Benjamin, but also the things of the
age, and the things of the rearing. For my son will not go down, he says, with
you, because his brother has died, and he alone has been left, and it will
happen that he becomes weak in the road in which you travel, and you will
bring down my old age with grief into Hades. First then because of all these
he declined, and said he would not give him; but when the famine fell on
violently, and he perceived a greater necessity, although wailing exceedingly,
For why did you do me evil, he says, announcing to the man if you have a
brother? and this more painful than all



Jacob underwent, uttering the pitiable words, Joseph, he says, is not, Simeon
is not, and will you take Benjamin? upon me have all these things come to be;
and wailing that after Joseph and Simeon they attempted to tear Benjamin also
from him, and showing that he will suffer all things rather than hold off from
the child, later, being conquered, he himself gave him over with his own
hands, saying: And take your brother, and rising go down toward the man; and
may my God give you grace before the man, and send your one brother, and
Benjamin; for I, as I have been made childless, =47.469= have been made
childless. So exceedingly was he mastered by the many evils, that even while
the bowels were being torn, and the children were being lessened for him
little by little, he underwent all things by the excess of the greater; for a
greater despondency held him over these than over Joseph. For the disaster
that does not have an expectation of correction, even if it brings us violent
despondencies, still quickly pushes them out, having thrown the thought into
despair; but the one still in suspense does not let the soul rest, always
intensifying and renewing the agony for us by the unclarity of the things to
come. And this one could learn well from the blessed David, who while the
child was still living lamented, but when he had died let himself go from the
mourning. And when the children were at a loss about this, and asked him the
cause, he said this thought, which I now say. Reasonably then that one too
feared more over these and trembled. But the longed-for sight and the seeing
Joseph provided him good cheer later. And what is the gain? For just as limbs
burned down by a violent fire, even if one should cool them ten thousand
times, one benefits nothing; so also the soul become very pained for that one,
and burned down exceedingly by the flame of despondency, nothing was enough to
recover, and especially when the perception was not even likely to be clear;
which Barzillai also, declining David, said: How many will the days of the
years of my life be for me there, that I may go up with the king into
Jerusalem? I am a son of eighty years today; will I know between good or
between evil, will your servant taste as many things as he eats, or as many as
he drinks, or will I hear a voice of men singing or of women singing? And why
does your servant become a burden upon my lord the king? But what need is
there to guess these things from others, when it is possible to hear from the
one who has suffered himself? For after seeing the son, it says, being asked
by Pharaoh about his life: Small, he says, and evil are my days, and they have
not arrived at the days of my fathers; so blooming in his soul he had the
memory of the things that had happened continually.

12. But this brilliant and
renowned son Joseph, whom did he not hide by the disasters? For his father had
only one brother plotting, but this one far more; and the one was reared all
the first age in abundance and much ease, but this one on the foreign and
still being a little child was compelled to bear the hardships of the
traveling. And for Jacob the mother was present lightening the plot; but this
one still being young, and when he especially needed the mother, then was set
in a desert of this help. Besides these Esau pained Jacob as far as a threat
only; but these also brought the plot out into a work, and before the plot
they continued envying and slandering him: than which what could become
harsher, to have those living together always as enemies? For they also
brought down a wicked blame against him, and seeing that his father loves him
above his other sons, they hated him, and they could not speak anything
peaceful to him. For neither to come under the merchants =47.470= nor under
the eunuch would I say is so great an evil: for these used him much more with
love of mankind than the brothers. But still not even so did the winter of the
disasters become milder: for a more violent squall falling again sank him.



Perhaps someone thinks I will speak of the plot of the mistress; but I will
speak of another harsher before this. For it is terrible, truly terrible, both
to be slandered in such things, and to be condemned, and to inhabit a prison
so long a time, a free and well-born child and inexperienced of this
necessity; but much heavier than all these I think the winter from the age
came to be for him. For if, being troubled by no desire, he spat upon that
one's love, I do not praise exceedingly, nor do I wonder at the man, being
persuaded by Christ. For he says that not those eunuched from nature, but
those who have eunuched themselves are thought worthy of the kingdom of the
heavens; and if this were not, what victory would he have taken? against whom
would he have bound on a crown? whom having conquered would he have been
proclaimed, there being no one wrestling against him, and attempting to throw
him down? For we do not praise those who do not mix with the irrational in the
account of self-control, because the desire of that mixing is not even laid in
the nature. If then this fire did not even trouble that blessed one then, why
do we wonder at him for self-control? But if, when the flame is raised more
violently than all the times (for he was remaining bringing a twentieth year),
and when the tyranny is unbearable even with no one increasing it, then the
unbridled one set upon the youth, adding to the flame from her own charms and
adornments as much strength as she had from nature: how could one relate the
winter of the soul and the turmoil and the agony, nature and age throwing him
into confusion from within, and the devices from the Egyptian woman striking
from without, and these not as far as one day and a second, but for a long
time? For I think he then trembled not for himself only, but also was in pain
over her, as she was being driven to come to so great a cliff; and this is
clear to us from the things he answers toward her with much reasonableness.
For it was possible, if he had wished, also to converse more insultingly and
more boldly toward her: for she would easily have borne all things because of
the love; but he neither uttered nor thought anything of this kind, but having
stirred up pious thoughts, and through the things by which he thought he would
only make her ashamed, he added nothing more. For behold, he says, my lord
knows nothing because of me of the things in his house, and all things as many
as he has he has given in my hand, and nothing in this house is above me, nor
has anything been taken away from me except you, because you are his wife; and
how shall I do this wicked word, and sin before God? And after so great a
reasonableness and the display of self-control he was slandered, and God
allowed it. He was bound, and not even so did he convict the plot, and the
unjust slander of the woman; for he wished both the rewards to become many for
him and the crowns more brilliant, and therefore even when the slaves of the
king were released he himself still remained inside. But do not tell me the
love of mankind of the prison-keeper, but examine his words, and you will see
the pain of the =47.471= soul. For after loosing the dream he said toward the
cup-bearer: But remember me through yourself, when it is well for you, and you
will do mercy upon me, and you will remember about me toward Pharaoh, and you
will lead me out of this stronghold, because I was stolen by theft from the
land of the Hebrews, and here I have done nothing, but they have thrown me
into the house of this pit. For even if he bore being shut in easily, still
the living with such men, tomb-robbers, thieves, father-beaters, adulterers,
man-killers (for this dwelling was filled with all these and such), was
heavier than all things for him. And not this only grieved him and afflicted
him, but also seeing many being pickled there in vain and to no purpose. But
still the household servant, which you too now wail, was freed from the bonds,
but the free man remained still being in hardship. And if someone should speak
of the kingship, again he will tell me the swarm of



cares and of the sleeplessness and of the countless affairs, all of which for
those who embrace the quiet and unoccupied life are not exceedingly =47.472=
according to pleasure. And apart from these things, for those even if
something good happened, still the kingdom had not yet been made clear, nor
was the promise of the things to come apparent; but here where so many goods
are set forth, and the thing is clear to all, will someone be in pain, tell
me, even if he will enjoy no good thing according to the present life, and
will he think anything of the things that happen here good at all, knowing
those? And what could become more lowly than this soul, if expecting a little
later to move house toward heaven, it should seek the eases here, and the good
cheer that differs nothing from a shadow? For vanity, it says, of vanities,
all things are vanity. And if the one who most of all men took experience of
the pleasure of life brought out such a declaration against it, much more must
we be so disposed and think, who have nothing in common with the earth, but
have been enrolled into the city above, and have been commanded to move all
the mind there.

=47.471= Of the same, to Stagirius, on despondency
Third Discourse

1. The things said then are enough to quench the flame of despondency, and to
persuade the soul to be disposed more easily; but so that the consolation may
come to be from abundance, I have also known to add this speech for you,
asking you that first: for tell me, if someone called you into a kingdom on
the earth, then before entering into the city, and binding on the crown, you
were going to lodge in an inn, in which there is both mire and smoke, and
turmoil of travelers, and confusion of robbers, and constriction and much
affliction, would you have turned away toward those griefs, and not have
despised all of them as being nothing? How then is it not absurd, when about
to master the earth, not to contract at any of the difficulties that fall in,
exulting in the hopes of the kingdom, but when sending oneself toward the
heavens to fall down and be thrown into turmoil at each of the griefs that
befall us in this lodging? For this constitution of this life has differed
nothing from a lodging and a stay in an inn; and the holy ones, wishing to
show this, called themselves strangers and sojourners, training us through
these words to laugh at both the good and the grievous things in the present
life, and having been freed from the earth to bind the whole soul to heaven.
Come then, let us come upon the holy ones themselves, having transferred the
speech from Joseph to Moses. For this one, the most gentle of all upon the
earth, is born when his own are doing badly, and having been alienated
=47.472= from those who bore him, and having been ignorant of those who begot
him, he is reared all the first age by barbarian men: than which what could
become harsher for a Hebrew youth who has a mind, even if he should seem ten
thousand times to be a king's son? And then this was not the only terrible
thing, but that he also saw all the same race in the last disasters. For he
who did not endure even to live, nor to be written in the book of God, without
their salvation, how would he have enjoyed the good things in the palaces,
seeing all lying in so great a swell? For if so great a pity of that
child-killing enters us who have come to be after so great a time and have no
such claim of sympathy toward Jews, what would that blessed one not have
suffered, bound with much longing toward the whole nation, and taking their
disasters with his eyes, and being compelled to write as parents those who
worked these evils? For I think him then to have mourned those wretched
children even more than those who bore them



; and this is clear from the things he did after these. For when he neither
had it to persuade, nor was able to compel the one who seemed to be father to
dissolve the beast-like and tyrannical command, he himself remaining went
together with their evils. And I do not wonder at this so much now, but
guessing from the murder how great a flame of despondency he nourished in the
time that passed, I am more astonished at this. For he who broke out that
birth-pang into a murder made plain the former things through the last. For he
would not have defended them with so great a violence, if he were not wasting
more than the fathers at their evils. What then, when he defended, and rested
the soul a little from that despondency? Was he able to enjoy perfectly that
=47.473= consolation that came from the vengeance? A second day then did not
arrive, and another pain harsher than the former received that blessed one,
and so great a fear as even to drive him from all Egypt. It is terrible then
even to hear badly from anyone, but when the one doing this is also one of
those benefited, and reproaches and brings forward the benefactions toward
himself, For do you wish, he says, to take me away, in the manner you took
away the Egyptian yesterday? then indeed, then the insult becomes unbearable,
and it could even choke the one insulted: so much along with the despondency
it also brings on the anger. And in the case of Moses a third other thing also
was added to these, the fear of the king, which so mastered the soul of the
righteous man as even to cast him out of all the country. The king's son then
becomes a fugitive: so that if someone now blesses him because of the rearing
in the palaces, let him remember that abundance, and he will see it to have
become an occasion of countless despondencies and difficulties for the
righteous man. For it is not equal, having been reared in a private house, and
having undergone many labors, and many journeys abroad and hardships, to
endure long wanderings, and to undergo the evils of the foreign, and never
having come even into a short experience of these, but having continued all
the time in luxury, to suffer this same thing: for this one will perceive the
flight much harsher than that one, if ever it should happen to him to be set
in this necessity; which indeed then also happened to that one. And having
become a fugitive, he is brought down with an idolater and a foreigner; and
this too is not small in the account of despondency, to be a guest with the
one consecrated to demons so long a time. And having been entrusted with the
care of that one's flocks, he so completed the forty years. And if this seems
to someone to be nothing terrible, let us examine those who do not journey
abroad and hide because of fear and dread, but those who of their own will are
torn a little from the house, how they are in distress, how they are
displeased, of how many goods they set the return. And when fear also is added
and a hardship-filled life, and these burdensome and heavy things appear
lighter than the most pleasant return, look at the varied of the disaster. For
do not simply hear the word, he shepherded, but remember now the words of
Jacob, which he wailed toward the father-in-law saying: I paid from myself
thefts of day and thefts of night; I became of the day burned with the heat,
and with the frost of the night, and sleep stood off from my eyes. For all
these things were likely to befall this one too, in more years and with
greater violence, inasmuch as the country was more deserted than the country.
And if Moses did not wail these things, neither would that blessed one have
said them, if he had not been set in much necessity of the thing, and by the
ingratitude of the father-in-law been forced to come to these words. This
foreign land then is enough even by itself to humble the man, even the one
journeying abroad only because of need. For as a bird, it says, when it has
flown out from its nest, so a man is enslaved, when he has been made a
stranger from his own =47.474= places. And then besides this he did not even
have confidence about his own salvation, but as some



household servant who has run away from a cruel master trembles continually,
and fears lest he be caught: so also the blessed Moses lived with continual
fear; and it is clear from the fact that even when God after so great a time
commanded him to go, he draws back and hesitates, and this having heard that:
The one seeking his soul has died.

2. And when at last he was persuaded and
went, and was compelled to leave the wife and the children, again there were
blames and insults and threats from the one then mastering Egypt, and
accusations and curses from those being benefited themselves. The one then
said: Why, Moses and Aaron, do you turn the people aside from their works? Go
away each of you toward his works; and the Israelites: May God see you, they
say, and judge, because you have made our smell abominable before Pharaoh, and
before his servants, to give a sword into their hands to kill us. These things
then are grievous and heavy, but the more burdensome of all was that, having
entered and promised countless goods, freedom, riddance of the evils lying on,
he seemed to be a deceiver. For not only was the weight of the slavery lying
on not lightened for them, but it was even intensified toward the harsher, and
the one expected to be a liberator of the whole nation, and having promised
this, seemed to be a cause of the rackings and the scourges, and a plotter and
a destroyer. Who would not easily have been swallowed by the despondency,
having promised a release of so many evils, and after the promise having seen
an addition of other harsher ones? He was despondent then as it is likely for
the one hearing and seeing these things to be despondent, but he was not
turned aside by the passion, but remained unbending, although the things that
happened did not agree with the promises, but even fought. And coming to God,
and conversing about these things, and wailing much: for he said, it says,
Lord, why have you maltreated your people, and why have you sent me? and from
when I have gone in toward Pharaoh to speak upon your name, he has maltreated
this people, and you have not delivered your people; having lamented these
things then, and having heard again the same things as also before, he
announces them again to the Israelites, but those do not receive them because
the souls are mastered by the weariness and the despondency. For they did not
listen to Moses, it says, from the despondency and from the hard works. And
this too afflicted him not as it chanced. And when it came to the signs, and
having often been mocked by Pharaoh he bore this jeering also nobly, having
been freed from Egypt, and having confidence remaining with the Jews, before
breathing perfectly, he was taken by the former fear, or rather even much
greater than the former. For not yet whole three days, and they saw all the
barbarians standing near them with the arms, and they suffered the same, as if
some runaways on the foreign had suddenly fallen into the eyes of the masters;
or as if these very ones, if it ever happened they had luxuriated in good
dreams, and had imagined this riddance, then having woken found themselves in
Egypt again, and in the like evils. Or rather I do not know what one would say
they thought a dream, the three days of the riddance, or that present
shuddering and fearful sight; so great a mist of despondency was scattered
upon the eyes of all. And Moses =47.475= was held by a greater darkness. For
he did not fear the Egyptians only, as also the remaining Israelites, but also
these with those. For both remaining attended to him as a deceiver and an
ironist, the ones with laughing and insulting upon, the others with being
bitten and being despondent. But what need is there to guess the despondency
of the man from these, when it is possible from the voice brought from above
to learn all his pain? For to him being silent and not even daring to open the
lips, God says: Why do you cry toward me? showing us the turmoil of his soul
through this one word.

3. And when this dread also was loosed, the terrible
things were again raised greater. For even than the Egyptians and Pharaoh
those who were being led by him and through him had enjoyed countless goods

10. But these things, as I said,
I leave for you to go through with all accuracy at leisure; and I will come

again: Woe is me! because I have become as one gathering stubble in a harvest,

his bread upon dung-cakes

11. But I do not know how in the midst of the prophets I
have passed by the heaven-high soul, the one who so inhabited the earth, as
the heaven: for nothing more than the sheepskin was his. What then of this
great and wonderful man, if indeed one must call him a man? After the freedom
of speech toward Ahab, after the sending down of the fire, after the slaughter
of the priests, after the shutting and opening of the heaven, according to
whichever time he wished both, after so many and so great successes, he was so
mastered by the fear and by the excess of despondency, as to utter these
words: Take my soul from me, because I am not better I above my fathers. These
things that one who has not even now died. And not these only, but also having
gone away into the desert he slept, bent down by the heaviness of soul. And
this one's disciple received not only the double spirit of the teacher, but
also much greater afflictions. These then a little before the blessed Paul
showing us and numbering their hardships said: Of whom the world was not
worthy. And this blessed one has come in for us timely: for the one who even
seen alone and sufficing toward consolation, this one having entered after the
others, what despondency and grief would he not make vanish? The hunger then,
and the thirst, and the nakedness, and the shipwrecks, and the stays in the
deserts, and the fears, and the dangers, and the plots, and the prisons, and
the blows, and the sleeplessnesses, and the countless deaths, and the other
things as many as he underwent for the sake of the preaching, I do not even
think one must say. For these things even if they provided some afflictions,
still also provided him pleasures; but when all those in Asia turned away from
him, when Galatians were turned aside, a whole nation and one that had been
approved, when Corinthians cut the Church among them into many parts, and
having flattered the one who fornicated led him into insensibility, what do
you think he suffered? With how much darkness was the soul held? But what need
have we of thoughts, when it is possible to hear their words? For writing to
the Corinthians he said that From much affliction and constriction of heart I
wrote to you through many tears; and again, Lest somehow when I come God
humble me, and I mourn many of those who have sinned before and have not
repented. And to Galatians, My little children, whom I again travail, until
Christ is formed in you. And about the Asians he wails toward the disciple.
And not these things only pained him, but also the thorn given him so
afflicted him and constricted him, as often over the loosing of this



to beg God; for the three times here is the often. And in short when was he
able to breathe, who even at the absence of a brother was beaten? Because I
did not find, he says, Titus my brother, I have not had ease. And at a disease
again he suffered this same thing. For God pitied him, he says about =47.488=
Epaphras he said to the Philippians, and not him only, but also me, that I
might not have grief upon grief. And being exceedingly heavy-souled over the
deceivers and those who speak against him, writing to Timothy he said:
Alexander the coppersmith has displayed many evils to me; may the Lord repay
him according to his works. What small pause then of despondency and pain was
he able to have? For not even the things said only were those besieging that
one's soul, but also others besides these, which provided him the despondency
continually; and these he himself again uncovered saying: Apart from the
things besides, my daily gathering, the care of all the Churches. Who is weak,
and I am not weak? who is scandalized, and I am not burned? If then at each of
those scandalized he himself was burned, it was not possible for this burning
to be quenched from that one's soul. For those being scandalized did not
cease, and providing the ground to the fire. For where cities and often whole
nations fell through, much more was it likely that one and a second were those
suffering this continually, there being so many Churches according to the
inhabited world. And let us grant, if you wish, in the speech, that no one was
even scandalized, nor ever separated from him, nor that any other of these
grievous things befall him; not even so can I find him clean of despondency;
and in this again I need to take no other witness, but the one who has
suffered himself. For what does he say? I prayed to be anathema from Christ
over my brothers, my kinsmen according to flesh, who are Israelites. And what
he says is such: It was more longed for by me to fall into Gehenna, than to
see the Israelites disbelieving. For the word, I prayed to be anathema, is
this; and he who chose the punishment in Gehenna for the sake of being able to
bring all the Jews, it is very clear that not obtaining this, he spent more
heavily than those being punished in Gehenna, since that was more according to
his mind than this.

12. But you, at each of the things said, do not reckon
only the ground from which the despondency was born for those men, but also
the measure of the grief, and you will see these much greater than your pain.
For this is what is now being examined, if they were in pain more harshly; and
the measure of despondency is accustomed to be tested not from the pretext
that births it only, but also from the words and from the affairs. Many then
having lost money only have been in pain more greatly than your love, and some
have thrown themselves into the sea, and some have also fastened nooses, not
having borne the damage; and of others the excess of this despondency has even
quenched the eyes; and yet to lose money seems to be less and lighter than to
be troubled by a demon, but still many have borne this, but have been defeated
by that. For do not test these things now from your own soul, nor, because you
laugh at the =47.489= loss of money, think already that the others too are so
disposed: for the taking-away of these has led many even into madness and into
last damage. For neither of these can throw down a noble soul; but the weak
and more worldly disposed is bitten more by that than by this. Why then?
Because it is not equal to fear hunger continually and to be troubled through
some days by this disease. For here in a short part of time is all the force,
as of some fever, or shivering, or even of some other period that has struck,
or rather it even holds a time much less than these; and if it conquers by the
violence, I would be able to show many of those with fever standing out much
more than those possessed by demons, when they are held by that fire. And
since the fear of want, of the lack of the necessary things, as a continual
moth,



so sitting beside continually eats the soul of those in poverty. And why do I
speak of poverty? For if we should wish to number all the disasters of men,
not we only, but perhaps you yourself also will laugh at your own laments and
wailings. All then, or rather not even the many-th part of them is possible
for us to say: for we neither know them, nor, even if we knew, would the whole
time have sufficed us toward the relating. But having gathered a few from
many, from these I will provide you according to power to guess also those not
said. For remember that dearest old man, I mean Demophilus, the one from the
great and brilliant house. This one has remaining a fifth and tenth year, from
which he is disposed no better than the dead toward working, except so far as
he trembles continually, and speaks, and has a clear perception of his own
evils; and he lives with last poverty, and he has as the one serving a youth
one, good indeed and master-loving, yet not enough to console that disaster:
for he can neither defend against the poverty, nor stop the trembling of the
palsy, but only put a morsel into the mouth of the master (for not even toward
this do the hands suffice to help him), and bring a cup, and wipe him when he
has a running nose he could, but bring in more than these nothing. But this
one has remaining fifteen years being in hardship so, as I said; and I
consider the one who has spent thirty-eight years into this passion. And
besides these reckon for me also Aristoxenus the Bithynian: for this one's
body has not been dissolved as that old man's, but a disease lies on much
harsher than that paralysis. For through the abdomen some spasms and toils
more bitter than every pain, sometimes piercing worse than spits, sometimes
eating through more violently than fire, and troubling through each day and
night, among those who do not know the passion they put round him a reputation
of one who is mad; so distorting the pupils, so strangling the hands with the
feet, they set him voiceless. And the cries and the wailings (for he also
often cries out after that voicelessness) conquer the howlings of those in
birth-pangs, and often those inhabiting far, having sick people, and being
troubled by much sleeplessness, have sent accusing over those who are ill, as
having been disposed worse by this =47.490= one's voice. And it happens to him
not from long intervals, but often of the day, often of the night, and
remaining this is a sixth year, from which he has been given over to this
wicked scourge, and neither is a household servant present who will tend him,
nor a physician who will console, this because of the poverty, this because
the art has been convicted by them by the passion: for many having toiled many
things (for that one happened before this to have much money) benefited
nothing. And the more terrible, not even any of the friends wishes remaining
to see him, but all have left him, even of those formerly much benefited by
him. And if someone should even enter sometime, he has at once leapt off: with
so great a stench has the room been filled, because there is no one who cares.
For one little girl sits beside him only, serving as many things as it is
likely a woman to be able, and alone, and being fed from the work of the
hands. Of how many demons then are that one's evils harsher! For even if none
of these troubled him, what would he not have suffered considering the long
time, which he lies upon the bed, the much expense that has thrown him into
last poverty, the contempt of the friends, the desert of those who will tend,
the not even knowing if these terrible things will ever stop, which you always
mourn, or rather the being persuaded accurately, that they will not even ever
stop, while he is living and still breathing? For the intensification of the
disease and the going forward each day toward the worse for him, this says.



13. And that I may not, numbering one by one those who have been so
unfortunate, seem to wear out those who hear, go away toward the one entrusted
with the oversight of the hospice, and command him to lead you in toward those
lying there, that you may see every root of passions, and strange manners of
diseases, and all kinds of occasions of despondency. From there having come
into the prison, and having learned all the things in this dwelling, walk upon
the forecourts of the baths, where some using both dung and reed instead of
garments and houses lie naked, besieged by frost and disease and continual
hunger, encouraging those who pass by with the sight only and the trembling of
the body and the noise of the teeth striking together, and able neither to
send out a voice, nor to stretch out hands, because of having already been so
melted by so many evils. But do not even stand as far as these, but also go
out toward the lodging of the poor before the city, and then you will see
well, that the despondency that seems to hold you now is a fair-weather
harbor. For what would one say of the men being consumed little by little by
elephantiasis, the women being eaten by cancer? For both these diseases are
both long and incurable; and the one of them also drives those who have them
from the city, and it is not lawful for them to share either bath, or
marketplace, or any other of the things inside. And not this only is the
terrible thing, but that they do not even have confidence about the abundance
of the necessary things. And what of those often condemned in vain and to no
purpose to inhabit the mines? For all these have a pain much greater than
those possessed by =47.491= demons. And if you disbelieve, it is no wonder.
For we are not accustomed to judge both our own and the foreign evils with the
same mind, but those having learned by word and sight only, these by
experience and perception and with our own sympathy; therefore we think them
more unbearable than those, even if they are exceedingly light and bearable.
And if someone, having been freed from all these, should both learn their
nature, and look through those who have fallen into them, this one will bring
us an unbribed vote about these things. But perhaps you will say that, that
all those passions are turned about the body only, but this disease touches
the soul, and is harsher than all these. By this itself then especially we
will find it happening to be lighter than those. For this neither corrupts the
body, as those, and it troubles the soul toward a short time; but those which
we have now numbered grow in the flesh, but they do not set all the damage as
far as that, but they also send it on upon the soul, racking it continually
and corrupting it through the pain and the much despondency. For just as, it
says, vinegar draws unprofitably, so a passion having fallen in a body grieves
a heart. Therefore do not say only that this is born from the body, but if it
does not send on all the damage and the corruption to the soul, show this.
Since the plague too, not being born from the bodies, corrupts the bodies, and
the venom of the reptiles comes forward from those, but damages us; so also
upon these passions: it is born from the body, but it pours the venom of its
own vice into our soul. For the excess of despondency is more harmful than
every demonic working; since the demon too, in whatever he masters, masters
through this; and if you take this away, no one will suffer anything terrible
from that one. And how is it possible not to be despondent, he says? And I
will ask you, how is it not possible not to be despondent? For if adultery and
murder, or some other such thing of the things that shut out from the kingdom
of the heavens, has been dared by you, be despondent and mourn, there is no
one who will hinder; but if by the grace of God you have stood far from all
these, why do you vainly beat yourself?

14. For God has placed despondency in
our nature, not so that we may use it simply and untimely and in the things
contrary to it, nor so that we may corrupt ourselves, but so that we may gain
the greatest things from it. And how is it possible to gain? When we take it
in season; and a season of despondency is not when we suffer badly, but



when we do badly; but we have overturned the order, and have exchanged the
times, and doing countless evils, we do not even contract for a short time;
and if we suffer some little from anyone, we fall down, we grow dizzy, we are
eager to be freed and to stand off from life. Therefore because of this the
thing seems to us to be burdensome and heavy, just as also the anger and the
desire. For these too those who have not used well nor unto what is needed
have slandered; and the same happens, as upon the medicines given by the
physicians; and =47.492= those too, when they are applied not to the
corresponding, and over which they have been prepared, but to others instead
of others passions, not only have not freed the one who is ill from what
grieves, but have even more worn the disease; which then despondency also
does, and reasonably. For being a harsh and eating-through medicine, and as
one would say a purgative of the wickedness in us, when it is provided to a
soul idle and living in luxury and having much the rubbish of the sins, it has
benefited the one who received most greatly; but when to one contending and
wrestling and toiling and being anxious and suffering badly, besides
benefiting nothing, it has also harmed greatly, having worked it weaker and
easy to conquer. Therefore to those standing and wrestling Paul writing said,
Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice; but to those who have
been poured out and have much the inflammation, And you are puffed up, and
have not rather mourned. For the one being fattened with the fat of the sins,
let him be made thin and be carded down by this medicine; but the one in good
condition and keeping himself in the fitting constitution, why should he
corrupt the good condition through the despondency? For this is so effective,
that even to those who need it, applied more time than is needed, it has
worked great evils. Which the blessed Paul also fearing, quickly commanded to
take it away after it has done its own, and he added this cause, which I now
say: Lest somehow, he says, by the more abundant grief such a one be
swallowed. And if it knows how to destroy those set in need when it has fallen
with excess, what will it work on those who do not need it at all, when they
draw it much to themselves? Yes, he says, I know this myself too, but how I
will beat it off, and stand it off from my own soul, I do not know. And of
what difficulty is this, O friend? For if it were some desire, and an absurd
love of bodies, or a tyranny of vainglory, the hard-to-conquer evil, or some
other of such passions, you would well have been at a loss about the riddance;
for those caught by such it is in no way impossible, but still difficult, to
run away from the nets. Why then? Because they have the pleasure working with
them and helping them; and this is what throws round from all sides many ropes
to those who have been caught; and this first it has toward being succeeded in
as hard, to persuade the soul at all to wish and to will the riddance; and it
becomes similar, as if someone owing to lay aside a scab or an itch should
take pleasure in the disease, and push himself inside the passion. But toward
the withdrawal of the grief this has been accomplished for us no small thing,
the being for the time unpleasantly disposed toward it. For he who is weighed
down by something will also be eager to throw it off quickly. What then, if he
is eager, but cannot? Let him not cease being eager, and he will quickly be
able. For consider that the Christian, if he should ever grieve, must have two
occasions only of despondency, either when he himself, or when the neighbor,
has offended God; and the present grief having no root of these pretexts, you
vainly =47.493= pain yourself. And from where is this clear, he says, that we
do not pay this as a penalty of offenses? First it is clear, still for the
time I do not insist on this. But let it be, if you wish, not even unclear, as
you said, but even exceedingly clear, that this is a repayment of the sins;
therefore then are you in pain, tell me? And yet it is worthy of good cheer
that the sins are dissolved here, and that one is not condemned with the
world. For the one in pain must not over the things for which he is punished,
but over the things in which sinning he provokes God,



be in pain: for the sins stand God off from us and make him an enemy, but the
things of the punishments both reconcile him to us and prepare him to be
gracious and near. And that this sweat and this toil is not even a repayment
of sins, but a ground of crowns and prizes, is clear from that. For if having
lived the former life shamefully and prodigally, you so transferred yourself
upon the way of life of the monks, it was not even so possible to suspect this
suspicion. For if God brings on the punishments for this, that he may turn
toward repentance those who are unyielding, when the repentance has been
displayed, the things of the punishment remaining are superfluous for this at
least. For God stands off so much from wishing to punish us, that even those
often doing things worthy of vengeance, and needing much turning, he brings to
sense through a threat only and fearful words. And this you would see both
upon Israel, and upon the city of the Ninevites. For not only did he not bring
on the punishment, when those displayed the repentance, but he also at once
loosed the threat. For he himself wishes much more than we ourselves that we
suffer nothing evil, and no one would spare himself as God spares all of us.
Then the one who frightens with words those who have often sinned, not
punishing, and when they have repented frees them even from this agony, you
who have displayed so great a piety and virtue, not only has he not freed from
the threat, but he also gave you over to the punishment through the works? and
how would someone believe this? Still, if your life before this had been
corrupted and wicked, as I said, it would have been possible for someone even
to come into this thought; but now that was less than the present, but it too
was orderly and full of much =47.494= gravity, so that this is apparent to us
from all sides, that these wrestlings are of crowns and of a greater ground.
One must then, as I said, both stir up these and such thoughts, and one must
with these, or rather even before these, scatter this darkness with prayers
and supplications. For the blessed David too, that wonderful and great man,
used these medicines continually, and so beat off the many pains of the
afflictions, now praying and saying: The afflictions of my heart have been
multiplied; from my necessities lead me out; now stirring up pious thoughts:
Why are you very grieved, my soul, and why do you throw me into confusion
together? Hope upon God, because I will confess to him. And again, turning
from the thoughts upon the prayers: Let me be, he says, that I may cool before

foolish women? If we have received the good things from the hand of the Lord,

trials, now saying: And if you are outside of training, then you are
illegitimate, and not sons; for who is a son, whom a father does not train?
now praying, For God is faithful, he says, who will not allow you to be tried
beyond what you can; and again, If it is righteous with God to repay
affliction to those who afflict you, and ease to you who are afflicted. If
then you yourself also wish to use these arms, and fence yourself well from
all sides, with the thoughts walling off the assault of despondency, and with
the prayers both your own and those of others making this wall strong, you
will quickly perceive the fruits of the training. For you will not only gain
this, to bear the present things nobly, but having been well trained in them,
you will remaining be invulnerable toward all the grievous things of life.
