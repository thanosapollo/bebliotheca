HOMILY VIII. That virtue according to the soul is to be preferred above all
things.
=64.473=
Often numbering to myself the goods of life, and finding creation filled from
every side with many things, and supplying countless goods to the race, I see
nothing better than to love the beauty of the soul, nor anything more fitting
to the nature of man. For what wealth so befits humanity as the abundance of
pious hopes? Who is so splendid from power as one honored from the mind by the
King of all? What pleasure from luxury is so great as that of a conscience
feasted on life-giving teachings? Whatever good you may bring into the midst,
you would find a man made honorable from none as from a godly mind. For of the
other goods there is a certain sharing even for the irrational with us: as the
fair enjoyment of the ray, yet it is set before the cattle also; the good
supply of foods, yet of these they too have a share; the lovely graces of
beauty, yet you would find beauty flashing even from birds; the much-famed
gift of strength, yet oxen too have the advantage of it; the much-prayed-for
gathering of wealth, yet you would see even oysters rich with treasures of
pearls. The nature of the irrational is not without share in any of our
fleshly boasts, nor is it inferior in any bodily advantage among us.
From one thing alone is there a yielding of them toward us; by one advantage
alone they are defeated by us. What, then, is this? The dignity of the mind,
through which man is like God, using intellect as helmsman of the things that
are, administering the affairs of creation by understanding, traversing heaven
by reasoning, leading what is subject by thought, with wisdom steering the
unmanageable of the disorderly sea, by providence preserving what grows, by
knowledge showing the things that appear, ruling the fair craftsmanship of
beings, as the Creator does. And making these things clear from the beginning
concerning us, God cried out: "Let us make man according to our image." And
the honor of the image is both fearful and loving toward man: fearful as an
image of a dread archetype. For it is no small danger, lest one have a
collision, having stained the image with the stain of sin; and loving toward
man, as a great gift before sweats, which the Creator prepared for us before
birth. For he says, "Let us make man according to our image." Before the
fashioning we are honored with care. For to each of the beings, granting
existence, he kept the authority of one who commands, and he created all the
nature of things seen from a command, speaking imperatively to things that
were not: "Let there be light; let there be a firmament; let there be lights
for shining; let the waters bring forth creeping things of living souls; let
the earth bring forth four-footed things according to =64.476= kind!"
But when man had to be created, leaving off the commander, he took up the
craftsman himself; leaving off the one who sketches the composition of beings,
he deliberated the generation of man; leaving off saying to the earth, "Bring
forth man!" As if unable to create man alone, he also calls partners to the
fashioning, and as if needing fellow-workers he has cried: "Let us make"; not
needing practice for the art, not requiring fellow-workers for the forming,
but by the figure of deliberation and cooperation showing that he is
fashioning a race cared for by him and altogether great. And in the honor a
certain training toward piety is hidden for the race. For he cast all creation
out of deification, handing it over to the image of his own monarchy; for
everything great is reckoned as belonging to the king's image. Second: "Let us
make man according to our image"; and though he foresaw the ingratitude, he
did not blunt the affection; for the sun ought not fail to shine because of
those blind toward the ray; for this reason, even knowing the transgression
beforehand, he made loving-kindness rise; and seeing the future, he did not
take away the honor; and knowing the insolence beforehand, he did not hold
back the grace, loving the beauty of those being fashioned, so that even as a
temporary being he might unlearn greed, and give God occasion for a second
gift toward an everlasting beginning. For the end is a terrible chastener of
greed. For what profit of the insatiable creation to him who will shortly lie
down? For him who goes through life as a road, the burden of superfluities is
vain. For we have life as a road, not as a homeland. Thus the Lord naming it
in the Gospels: "Be well-disposed toward your adversary while you are on the
way with him."
Reasonable is the name of a road for life; for life is measured, as a road, by
intervals; it is a temporary inn of rich and poor; a short rest of those who
pass by; "For our days pass as a shadow." Where are the first years of our
life? They have passed as a shadow's appearance; they have run through as the
shading of a figure. How great for us is the interval of remaining life? Set a
generous length of years, a hundred-year life; and if you will, give a
tripling in years, and you will grant permanence to none of these things of
life; even if it is ambitious in wealth, even if it is flooded with luxury,
even if it shines with height of thrones, even if it carries about whatever of
life's splendors. For all things are like the witherings of flowers, all are
liable to a mournful succession, being unsound even in the season in which
they bloom; for they have throughout an agony of dangers. Wealth worries about
robberies, luxury is dangerous with diseases, changes with powers; and having
wrestled with dangers of many kinds and been wasted by countless cares, he has
death kept for him, an unbreakable adversary, unbounded by places, unflattered
by gifts, unmoved by laments, a sudden plunderer. He who yesterday was in
=64.477= wealth, today is suddenly in a tomb; he who yesterday was in a
banquet of luxury, today is in the roll of the dead; he who yesterday was on
the thrones, on the day after this is in winding-sheets; he who yesterday was
among flatterers, after a little is among worms. Where is the joy of the
counted purses?
Where is the boast of the income of possessions? Where is the difference of a
rich and a poor corpse? Where is the interval between king and beggar? Death
is the successor of both, the tomb is the conclusion of each; except for
burial-clothes, no companionship for anyone. Well said is Paul's voice:
"Godliness with contentment is great gain; for we brought nothing into the
world, and it is clear that we can carry nothing out." An unconquerable
argument for contentment, that nothing of what was acquired goes out together
with the owners; for, he says, "we shall be able to carry nothing out." Why,
he says, do you take out with you what you do not transfer? Why do you hold
what is not held? What is beyond contentment is unprofitable to those who have
acquired. For whatever a man may gather will end in covering and food for him,
and of all the rest life is the heir. And we owners are beguiled by wills,
granting by letters what we have no strength to take; seeming to let go what
it is not possible to hold, as if, had it been possible to hold, we would not
have given it over to another. And the prologue of the will shows the mind:
"May I live," he says, "and may I be master of my own." The writing writes a
prayer of one who clings and yields with his voice, and it prays against the
work of the writing: "May I live, and may I be master of my own." Not even
when our soul is breaking do we stand off from the love of money; "and if I
suffer," he says, "what is according to man, I wish there to be heirs."
He inherits even if you do not wish; nature has arranged that you be inherited
even unwilling. Even if you do not write an heir to the money, the money will
find an heir; even if you leave it to no one, you will be able to carry out
nothing of what you acquired. I do not say that making a will is a poor thing;
for somewhere a prophet also advises a man: "Set your house in order; for you
are dying, and you will not live"; but I mock our earthly choices, that we
judge the money passed from these to those more honorable than the things that
have no successor. Truly godliness is great gain; it is not subject to
successions of death, it is not scattered by letters of wills, it is not
inherited by others after leaving the one who acquired it. The God-loving man
is himself his own chest, himself heir of his own substance; he travels with
the purse of piety, he takes along the wealth of hopes, he bears as he
journeys the income of the virtues, he finds heaven as a house, he is mixed
into the banquet of the righteous, he enjoys an eternal dignity of a kingdom,
he approaches the King with boldness crying: "You are he who restores my
inheritance to me." =64.480= Toward those things is the aim for Christians;
for those things birth is blessed, because one will attain a divine vision
beyond thought: the sudden opening of men, the paradoxical alteration of the
earth, the trumpets raising the dead from the age, the righteous borne through
air, the angelic revels visible to the eyes, the descent of the Master upon
the earth under the sight of the inhabited world. O how great it is that those
who have loved him warmly now should cry out, coming forward at the meeting
then: "Remember, Lord, how we walked before you in truth and did the things
pleasing before you," and that those crying should at once hear in answer him
who cries: "And I know my own, and I am known by my own."
Let us then lift our heads from the much care about the things of life; let us
lighten the soul of earthly loads; let us pass through to the future in
thoughts; let us love the treasures that have no successor; let us keep the
dignity of the royal image with the purple of virtue; having been appointed to
reign over the things of life, let us not turn the kingship into slavery;
before all wealth let us seek the protector of wealth; "For the rich," he
says, "have become poor and hungry, but those who seek the Lord shall not be
diminished of any good"; to him be the glory to the ages of ages. Amen.
