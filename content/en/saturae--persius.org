* Prologue
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I never rinsed my lips in the nag's spring, and I do not remember
dreaming on two-peaked Parnassus so as to come out a poet all at once.
The girls of Helicon and pale Pirene I leave to those whose busts the
clinging ivy licks. I myself, only half a villager, bring our song to
the bards' rites. Who loosed the parrot's "hello," and taught the
magpie to try our words? The belly, teacher of the art and lavish
giver of talent, a craftsman at chasing voices never given it. But if
the hope of tricky coin has flashed, you would think raven-poets and
poetess-magpies were singing the nectar of Pegasus.

* Satire 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

"O the cares of men! O how much emptiness there is in things!"
"Who will read this?" "You talking to me? Nobody, by Hercules."
"Nobody?" "Two, maybe, or nobody." "Shameful and pitiful!" "Why?
For fear Polydamas and the Trojan women prefer Labeo to me?" Trash.
If muddy Rome makes light of something, do not go over and correct the
crooked tongue on that scale, and do not look for yourself outside
yourself. For at Rome who has not... I would say it, if it is lawful.
But it is lawful, now that I have looked at our grey hairs and that
grim living of ours, and whatever we do once the nuts are put away,
when we put on the uncle's wisdom. Then, then, forgive me. I do not
want to. What can I do? I have a jeering spleen: I cackle.

We shut ourselves in and write, one in meter, another in free feet,
something grand for a lung too lavish of breath to pant out. Of course
you will read this to the people, combed, in a fresh robe, at last
white with your birthday sardonyx, from a high seat, once you have
rinsed your flexible throat with a liquid gargle, broken with a
leering eye. Then you may see huge Tituses, neither in decent fashion
nor with a sober voice, shivering as the poems enter the loin and the
inmost parts are tickled with a trembling verse. You, old man, are you
gathering bait for other men's ears, ears to which you, with your hide
ruined, might also cry "enough"? What was the learning for, unless
this yeast, and the wild fig that once grew inside, should burst the
liver and come out? Look at the pallor and the old age!

"O the manners! Is your knowing nothing, then, unless another knows
that you know it?" But it is a fine thing to be pointed at and to have
it said, "That's him." Do you count it nothing to have been the
dictation of a hundred curly-headed boys? Look: among the cups the
well-fed sons of Romulus ask what the divine poems have to tell. Here
someone with a hyacinth cloak around his shoulders, having mumbled
some rancid bit from a lisping nostril, strains out Phyllises,
Hypsipyles, and whatever is weepy in the bards, and trips the words on
a soft palate. The men have agreed. Is not that poet's ash happy now?
Does not the tombstone press the bones more lightly now? The guests
praise him. Will not violets now spring from those shades, from the
mound and the lucky cinders?

"You laugh," he says, "and you indulge your hooked nostrils too much.
Will there be anyone who refuses to have earned the people's mouth,
and, having spoken things worthy of cedar, to leave poems that fear
neither mackerel nor incense?" Whoever you are, O you whom I have just
now made speak from the other side, when I write, if by chance
something more apt comes out (since this is a rare bird), still, if
something more apt comes out, I will not be afraid of praise. My heart
is not horn. But I refuse to have the end and last bound of the right
be your "bravo" and "nicely done." Shake this "nicely done" out
entirely: what does it not have inside? Is not Attius's Iliad here,
drunk on hellebore? Are there not whatever little elegies the raw
nobles have dictated? Is there not, in short, whatever is written on
citrus-wood couches? You know how to serve a hot sow's udder; you know
how to give a shivering companion a worn cloak; and you say, "I love
the truth; tell me the truth about myself." How can anyone? You want
me to say it? You play the fool, bald man, when your fat paunch sticks
out a foot and a half in front of you. O Janus, whom no stork pecks
from behind, nor a mobile hand mimicking white donkey-ears, nor a
tongue as long as a thirsty Apulian dog's! You, O patrician blood,
whom it is lawful to live with a blind nape, meet the jeer at the back
door.

"What is the talk of the people?" "What else, except that poems now at
last flow in a soft measure, so that the joining lets a strict nail
run over the smoothness? He knows how to stretch a verse just as if
he were aligning a red line with one eye. Whether the work is to speak
against manners, against luxury, against the dinners of kings, the
Muse gives our poet grand matter." Look, now we see men who used to
trifle in Greek bringing in heroic thoughts, no craftsmen at planting
a grove or praising a rich countryside, where there are baskets and a
hearth and pigs and the hay-smoke of Pales's feast, the place Remus
came from, and you, Quintus, wearing the plowshare in the furrow, when
your wife, hurrying, dressed you as dictator before the oxen, and your
lictor carried your plow home. Bravo, poet! There is now someone whom
the veiny book of Brisaean Accius holds; there are those whom Pacuvius
delays, and warty Antiope, her grief-making heart propped on
miseries. When you see bleary fathers pouring these warnings into
their boys, do you ask where this fry-up of speech has come onto
tongues, where that disgrace has come from, in which the light young
blood whoops for you along the benches?

Does it not shame you that you cannot beat dangers from a grey head
without wanting to hear this lukewarm stuff prettily? "You are a
thief," says someone to Pedius. Pedius, what? He balances the charges
in antitheses; he is praised for having laid learned figures. "This is
pretty." Pretty, this? Or, Romulus, are you shaking your rump? Should
it move me? Indeed, if a shipwrecked man sings, should I bring out a
penny? You sing, when you carry yourself painted on a broken plank
from your shoulder? The man who wants to have bent me with a complaint
will weep a truth, not one got up at night.

"But grace and joining have been added to raw numbers. So Berecynthian
Attis has learned to close a verse, and the dolphin that used to part
blue Nereus. So we have stolen a rib from the long Apennine." "Arms
and the man!" Is this not frothy and with a fat bark, like an old
stick baked under a swollen cork-oak? What, then, is tender and to be
read with a loose neck? "They filled the grim horns with Bacchic
booms, and the Bassarid about to snatch the head from the proud calf,
and the Maenad about to twist the lynx with ivy-clusters, redoubles
Bacchus; the recoverable echo sounds with them!" Would these things
happen if any vein of the father's manhood still lived in us? This
limp stuff floats in the spit on the lips, and the Maenad and Attis
are in the wet, and it does not beat the desk or taste of bitten
nails.

But what need to scrape tender ears with biting truth? See to it that
the thresholds of the great do not grow cold for you. Here the canine
letter sounds from the nostril. "For my part, let everything be white
straightaway; I do not care. Bravo! All of you, all of you, you will
be fine, wonderful things!" Does this please you? Here you say, "I
forbid anyone to make a stink." Paint two snakes: "Boys, the place is
holy; piss outside." I leave. Lucilius cut the city, you, Lupus, you,
Mucius, and broke his back tooth on them. Wily Flaccus touches every
vice in a laughing friend and, once let in, plays around the heart,
clever at hanging the people on a blown-out nose. Is it a crime for me
to mutter? Not in secret? Not with a ditch? Nowhere? Still, here I
will bury it. I have seen it, I have seen it myself, little book: who
has not ass's ears? This hidden thing, this laugh of mine, such a
nothing, I sell to you for no Iliad. Whoever you are, inspired by bold
Cratinus, who grow pale over angry Eupolis with the huge old man, look
at these too, if by chance you hear something more distilled. From
there let my reader glow with a steamed ear, not the man who is eager
to mock the sandals of the Greeks, a dirty fellow, and who can say
"one-eye" to a one-eyed man, believing himself somebody because,
puffed with an Italian honor, he has broken the unfair half-pints as
aedile of Arretium; nor the man who knows how to laugh, the sly one,
at numbers on the counting-board and cones cut in the dust, ready to
rejoice a great deal if a saucy street-girl plucks a Cynic's beard. To
these I give the edict in the morning, after lunch Callirhoe.

* Satire 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Mark this day, Macrinus, with a better stone, the bright one that
sets the gliding years to your account. Pour wine for your Genius.
You are not the man to ask with a huckster's prayer what you could
only trust to the gods in private. But a good part of the nobles will
pour from a silent censer. It is not easy for every man to lift
murmurs and low whispers out of the temples and live with an open
vow. "A sound mind, a good name, trust": these he says aloud, so a
stranger may hear. The rest he mutters inward, under the tongue: "O
if only my uncle would bubble out, a splendid funeral!" and "O if
under my hoe a jar of silver would clink, with Hercules on my side!
Or if I could strike off that ward whom I, next heir, am shoving; for
he is scabby and swells with sharp bile. Nerius is already burying a
third wife!" Is it that you may ask these things piously that you
dip your head in Tiber's flood at morning, twice and three times, and
wash the night in the river?

Come now, answer (it is a very little thing I am working to know):
what do you think of Jupiter? Would you care to set him above...
"Above whom?" Above whom? Would you have Staius? Or do you stick at
that? Who is a better judge, or who fitter for orphan boys? Well
then, say to Staius what you are trying to push into Jupiter's ear.
"By Jupiter! O good Jupiter!" he would cry. And will Jupiter not cry
out upon himself? Do you think he has forgiven you because, when it
thunders, an oak is sooner split by the sacred sulfur than you and
your house? Or because you do not lie, at Ergenna's bidding and a
sheep's liver, a grim and shunned thunder-spot in the groves, does
Jupiter therefore offer you his stupid beard to pluck? Or at what
price have you bought the gods' ears? With a lung and greasy
chitterlings?

Look: a grandmother, or an aunt who fears the gods, takes the boy
from the cradle and first cleanses his brow and wet little lips with
the ill-famed finger and purifying spit, to hold off burning eyes.
Then she shakes him in her hands and sends the lean hope, with a
suppliant vow, now into Licinus's fields, now into Crassus's house:
"May king and queen want him for a son-in-law; may the girls snatch
him; may whatever he treads become a rose." But I do not trust
prayers to a nurse. Refuse her these, Jupiter, though she has asked
you dressed in white!

You ask help for the sinews and a body faithful in old age. Be it so,
come; but the great dishes and thick sausages have forbidden the gods
above to nod to this, and they delay Jupiter. You long to pile up
wealth by slaughtering an ox, and you fetch Mercury with a liver:
"Grant that the household gods prosper, grant cattle and increase to
the flocks!" How, you worst of men, when the cauls of so many
heifers are melting for you in the flames? And still he strains to
win with guts and a fat cake: "Now the field grows, now the fold
grows, now it will be given, now, now," until, cheated and out of
hope, a coin sighs in vain at the bottom of the chest.

If I brought you silver bowls and gifts beaten with fat gold, you
would sweat, and from the left breast a heart too quick to rejoice
would shake out drops. Hence that thought has stolen in, that you
overlay the sacred faces with triumphal gold; for let those among the
bronze brothers rank first who send dreams most purged of phlegm, and
let them have a golden beard. Gold has driven out Numa's vessels and
the bronze of Saturn, and it changes the Vestal urns and Tuscan
earthenware.

O souls bent to the earth and empty of things heavenly! What good is
this, to send our manners into the temples and to draw what is good
for the gods from this criminal flesh? This flesh has dissolved cassia
for itself in spoiled oil, and boiled Calabrian wool in corrupted
purple; this has ordered the pearl to be scraped from the shell and
the veins of the glowing mass to be stripped from the raw dust. It
sins, this flesh, it sins, yet it uses the vice. But you, priests,
tell me: what is gold doing in the holy place? Exactly what dolls
given to Venus by a girl are doing. Why do we not give the gods above
what the blear-eyed spawn of great Messala cannot give from a great
platter: law and right composed in the soul, holy recesses of the
mind, and a breast steeped in generous honor. Give me these to bring
to the temples, and I will make offering with meal.

* Satire 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

"This, then, all the time? Already bright morning is coming in at the
windows and stretching the narrow cracks with light. We are snoring
enough to work off that untamed Falernian, while the fifth line is
touched by the shadow. Look, what are you doing? The mad Dog-star has
long been baking the dry harvests, and every beast is under a spreading
elm," says one of the companions. "Is it true? Really? Let someone
come here quicker. Nobody?" The glassy bile swells. I split, so you
would think the herds of Arcadia were braying. Now the book comes, and
the two-colored parchment with the hair laid aside, and papers into
the hands, and the knotty reed. Then we complain that a thick humor
hangs from the pen, but the black cuttle, water poured in, fades; we
complain that the pipe doubles diluted drops.

O wretch, and more of a wretch by the day, have we come to this? Or
why not rather, like a tender pigeon and like the boys of kings, ask
to be fed pap cut small, and in a rage refuse mother's lullaby?

"Am I to study with a pen like that?" Whom are you fooling? Why hum
those windings? The game is yours. You are leaking, out of your mind.
You will be despised. Struck, the flaw sounds; an unbaked jar of green
clay answers badly. You are wet and soft mud; now, now you must be
hurried and shaped without end on the sharp wheel. But on the
paternal farm you have a modest store of grain, a salt-cellar clean
and without stain (what are you afraid of?), and a dish that tends
the hearth without worry. Is this enough? Or is it fitting to burst
the lung with wind because you, thousandth in line, draw a branch
from a Tuscan stem, or because you salute your censor in the
purple-striped robe? Trappings to the people! I know you inside and
in the skin. Are you not ashamed to live after the fashion of ungirt
Natta? But this man is stunned with vice, and rich fat has grown over
the inwards; he is without fault; he does not know what he is losing,
and sunk deep he sends up no bubble again at the top of the wave.

Great father of the gods, may you choose to punish savage tyrants by
no other means, when dread lust, dipped in boiling poison, has moved
the mind: let them look on virtue and waste away at having left her.
Did the bronze of Sicilian bulls ever groan more, and did the sword
hanging from gilded panels ever more terrify the purple necks below,
than if a man should say to himself, "We go, we go headlong," and
turn pale inside, poor wretch, at what the wife next to him may not
know?

I remember, as a small boy I would often smear my eyes with oil if I
did not want to speak the grand words of dying Cato, much to be
praised by a master not in his right mind, which my father, sweating,
would hear with friends brought in. And rightly, for the summit then
was to know what the lucky six would bring, how much the ruinous dog
would scrape, not to be fooled by the neck of a narrow jar, and not
to have anyone more cunning at spinning the boxwood with a whip.

It is not untried for you to catch crooked manners, and what the wise
porch, smeared with trousered Medes, teaches, over which a sleepless
and close-cropped youth keeps watch, fed on husks and a grand porridge;
and the letter that split the Samian branches has shown you the path
rising on the right-hand track. Are you still snoring? And does the
loose head, with the joint undone, yawn yesterday from jaws unsewn on
every side? Is there something you are aiming at, and a mark you
direct the bow at? Or do you follow crows at random with potsherd and
clay, careless where the foot carries you, and live from the moment?

You may see men asking for hellebore in vain when the sick skin will
already be swelling. Meet the disease as it comes, and what need to
promise Craterus great mountains? Learn, O you wretches, and know the
causes of things: what we are and for what lives we are born; what
order was given, or where the soft turn of the post is and from where;
what limit there is to silver; what it is lawful to wish; what use
harsh coin has; how much it is decent to lavish on country and dear
kin; whom the god ordered you to be, and in what part of the human
business you have been stationed. Learn, and do not envy that many a
jar stinks in a rich store, the fat Umbrians having been defended, and
pepper and hams, monuments of a Marsian client, and that the sprat has
not yet failed in the first jar.

Here someone of the goatish tribe of centurions may say: "What I know
is enough for me. I do not care to be what Arcesilas is, and the
toiling Solons, with head tilted and eyes fixed on the ground, when
they gnaw murmurs with themselves and rabid silences, and weigh words
on a stuck-out lip, pondering an old sick man's dreams, that nothing
is born from nothing, that nothing can go back into nothing. Is this
what you go pale for? Is this why a man should skip lunch?" At this
the people laugh, and the brawny youth redoubles trembling cackles,
the nose wrinkling.

"Inspect: something in my chest is fluttering, and a heavy breath
rises from a sick throat. Inspect, please," says the man to a doctor.
Ordered to rest, after he has seen a third night run with the veins
composed, he asked, from a greater house, for mild Sorrentine to bathe
in, in a flask only moderately thirsty. "Hey, good man, you are pale."
"It is nothing." "Still, look to that, whatever it is: your yellow
skin is rising quietly." "But you are paler, worse. Do not be my
guardian." "I buried this one long ago; you remain." "Go on, I will be
silent." Bloated with feasts and white in the belly he bathes, the
throat slowly exhaling sulfurous stink. But a tremor comes among the
wines and knocks the hot cup from his hands; the false teeth rattle;
then the greasy morsels fall from slack lips. Hence the trumpet, the
candles, and at last the poor blessed one, laid on a high bed and
plastered with thick balsams, stretches stiff heels toward the gate,
and yesterday's citizens, caps on, take him up.

"Touch the veins, wretch, and put your right hand on the chest.
Nothing is hot here. Touch the ends of the feet and the hands: they
are not cold." If money has been seen, or a fair neighbor girl has
smiled softly, does your heart beat as it should? Hard greens have
been set in a cold dish, and flour shaken through the people's sieve:
let us test the throat. A tender rotten ulcer hides in the mouth,
which it would hardly be decent to scrape with a common beet. You go
cold when white fear has shaken the bristles from your limbs; now,
with a torch put under, the blood boils and anger sparks in the eyes,
and you say and do what even mad Orestes would swear was not the act
of a sane man.

* Satire 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

"Are you handling the people's business?" Take these to be the words
of the bearded master whom the dread draught of hemlock carries off.
What are you trusting in? Say it, ward of great Pericles. No doubt
talent and a quick prudence in affairs have come before the hairs; you
are skilled in what should be said or left silent. So when the little
people seethe with stirred bile, your spirit is eager to impose
silence on the hot crowd by the majesty of a hand. What will you
speak then? "Citizens, this, say, is not just; that is bad; that other
is more right." For of course you know how to hang the just on the
twin pan of a wavering balance; you pick out the straight where it
comes in among curves, or when the rule deceives with a bent foot; and
you can stick the black death-mark on vice. Why then, handsome in
the outer skin to no purpose, do you not stop tossing your tail before
the day to the coaxing little people, when it would be better for you
to swallow undiluted hellebore by the townful?

What is your highest good? To have lived always off a greasy dish, and
a little hide tended by constant sun? Wait: this old woman would
give no other answer. Go now, puff yourself: "I am Dinomache's son; I
am the fair one." Be it so, so long as ragged Baucis is not wiser,
when she has well cried up her basil to an ungirt house-slave.

That no one tries to go down into himself, no one, but the pack on the
back walking ahead is watched! You may ask, "Do you know Vettidius's
farms?" "Whose?" "The rich man at Cures plows more than a kite can
wander." "This man you mean, this man with the gods angry and a
left-handed Genius, who, whenever he sets the yoke at the soaked
crossroads, fearing to scrape the old slime from a little jar, groans
'May this be well,' while biting a coated onion with salt, and, the
boys clapping at a pot of groats, sucks the ragged dregs of dying
vinegar?" But if you lounge oiled and pin the sun on your skin, there
is some stranger near you who jogs you with an elbow and spits
bitterly: "These manners! Weeding the penis and the secrets of the
loin, spreading drooping private parts for the people! Then, when you
comb a balsamed rug on your jaws, why does a shaven little throat
stick out from the groin? Let five wrestling-trainers pluck these
plantings and loosen the boiled buttocks with a hooked forceps: still
that fern is not tamed by any plow."

We strike and in turn offer our shins to arrows. Life is lived on this
pact; so we have learned. You have a blind wound under the groin, but
a broad belt of gold covers it. As you prefer, cheat the words and
deceive the sinews, if you can. "When the neighborhood calls me
excellent, am I not to believe it?" If you go pale, you shameless man,
at the sight of a coin; if you do to the penis whatever comes to you;
if, cautious, you flog the money-pit with many a welt: in vain will
you have given the people thirsty ears. Spit out what you are not; let
the cobbler take back his gifts. Live with yourself: you will learn
how short your furnishings are.

* Satire 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

It is the custom of bards to ask a hundred voices for themselves, to
wish a hundred mouths and a hundred tongues for their songs, whether a
play is to be set for a sad tragedian to gape, or the wounds of a
Parthian drawing iron from the groin.

"Where is this going? Or how large are the lumps of stout song that
you, huge, need a hundred-fold throat to match? Let those about to
speak grandly gather clouds on Helicon, if there are any for whom
Procne's pot or Thyestes' will boil, often to be dined on by tasteless
Glyco. You do not press winds with a panting bellows while the mass is
cooked in the furnace, nor, hoarse with a shut murmur, croak some
solemn nonsense to yourself, nor strain swollen cheeks to burst with a
pop. You follow everyday speech, clever at a sharp joining, neat
with a moderate mouth, trained to scrape pale manners and to pin fault
with a freeborn play. Draw what you say from here, and leave Mycenae's
tables with the head and feet, and get to know the lunches of common
folk."

I do not study this, that my page should swell with dark-clad trifles,
fit to give weight to smoke. We speak in private. To you now, at the
Muse's urging, we give the heart to be shaken out, and how large a
part of our soul is yours, Cornutus, to you, sweet friend, it is a joy
to have shown, careful to tell by the ring what is solid and what is
the plaster of a painted tongue. Here I would dare to ask a hundred
throats, that I may draw out with a pure voice how deeply I have fixed
you in my winding breast, and that words may unseal all this that lies
hidden, not to be told, in the secret inwards.

When first the guarding purple left me, still timid, and the locket,
given to the short-girt household gods, hung there; when pleasant
companions, and the gown now white, let me scatter my eyes over the
whole Subura without penalty; and when the road is doubtful and life's
ignorant wandering splits trembling minds into branching crossways: I
put myself under you. You take up the tender years in a Socratic fold,
Cornutus. Then the rule, cunning to deceive, applied, stretches the
twisted manners, and the mind is pressed by reason and works to be
won, and takes an artist's face under your thumb. With you, I
remember, I spent long days, and with you plucked the first nights
for feasts. One work, and we both lay out rest alike, and we loosen
serious things at a modest table. Do not doubt this: by a sure compact
of both, the days agree and are drawn from one star. Either the Fate
that holds to truth hangs our times on an equal Balance, or the hour
born for the faithful splits the concordant lots of the two into the
Twins, and together we break heavy Saturn with our Jupiter. There is
certainly some star that tempers me to you.

A thousand kinds of men, and the use of things is many-colored. Each
has his own will, and life is not lived on one vow. This man under a
fresh sun trades Italian wares for wrinkled pepper and pale cumin
grains; this one, full, prefers to swell with well-watered sleep; this
one indulges the field; the dice boil this one dry; that one is rotten
in love. But when stony gout has made the joints into the boughs of an
old beech, then they have groaned, too late, that the thick days and
the marsh light have passed, and that life has been left behind.

But it is your joy to grow pale over night studies; for, a tiller of
the young, you sow purged ears with Cleanthes's grain. From here let
boys and old men alike seek a sure end for the mind and provisions for
wretched grey hairs. "This will be done tomorrow." The same will be
done tomorrow. "What? As if it were a great thing, you are giving me a
day, then?" But when another light has come, we have already used up
yesterday's tomorrow. Look, another tomorrow drives out the years, and
it will always be a little beyond. For though it is near you, though
under one pole you will chase in vain the rim that turns itself, since
you run as the hind wheel and on the second axle.

There is need of liberty. Not that kind by which any Publius of the
Veline tribe, once he has earned it, holds mangy grain by a little
ticket. Alas, barren of truth, you for whom one spin makes a Roman!
Here is Dama, a groom not worth three coppers, blear-eyed from flat
wine and a liar over a thin mash. Let the master turn him: in the
moment of a top he comes out Marcus Dama. Well now! Do you refuse to
trust your coins with Marcus standing surety? Do you go pale with
Marcus for a judge? Marcus said it; so it is. Assign the tablets,
Marcus. This is pure liberty; this is what the liberty-caps give us.

"Is anyone else free, except the man who is allowed to lead his life
as he has pleased? I am allowed to live as I wish: am I not freer than
Brutus?" "You reason badly," says this Stoic, his ear washed with
biting vinegar. "I take the rest; strike out 'it is allowed' and 'as I
wish.'"

"After I left the praetor as my own man by the rod, why should I not
be allowed whatever the will has ordered, except if Masurius's
red-letter has forbidden something?" Learn, but let wrath fall from
the nose, and the wrinkled sneer, while I tear the old grandmothers
out of your lung. It was not for the praetor to give fools the fine
offices of life, and to hand over the use of rapid life. You would
sooner fit a harp to a tall camp-servant. Reason stands against it and
chatters into the secret ear that a man may not do what he will spoil
by doing. The public law of men and nature holds this as right: that
weak ignorance should hold forbidden acts. You mix hellebore, not
knowing how to steady the scale-tongue at a sure point: the nature of
healing forbids this. If a booted plowman, raw to the morning star,
should ask a ship for himself, Melicerta would cry that shame had
perished from affairs.

Has skill taught you to walk upright, and are you skilled at
telling the look of truth, so that no false thing rings under
copper-mixed gold? And what should be followed and what avoided in
turn, have you marked the one first with chalk, then the other with
charcoal? Are you moderate in your vow, tight in your household, sweet
to friends? Can you now bind, now loosen the granaries, and pass a
coin stuck in the mud without swallowing greedy spit? When
you have truly said, "These are mine, I hold them," be free and wise,
with praetors and Jupiter on the right. But if, when you were of our
flour a little before, you keep the old little skin, and, polished at
the brow, you keep a crafty fox in a stale breast, I take back what I
had given above and pull in the rope. Reason has granted you nothing.
Put out a finger: you sin. And what is so small? But with no incense
will you win that a short half-ounce of the right should stick in
fools. To mix these is a crime; nor, when you are a ditch-digger
in the rest, can you be moved through even three steps of Bathyllus's
satyr.

"I am free." Where do you take this as given, you subjected to so many
things? Or do you know no master except the one the rod relaxes? "Go,
boy, and carry Crispinus's scrapers to the baths!" If he has snapped,
"Are you dawdling, trifler?" no harsh slavery drives you, and nothing
from outside comes in to stir the sinews. But if masters are born
inside, in a sick liver, how do you come off less punished than this
man, whom the whip and the master's fear have driven to the scrapers?

In the morning you snore, lazy. "Get up," says Avarice, "come, get
up." You refuse; she presses: "Get up," she says. "I cannot." "Get
up." "And what am I to do?" "You ask? Look, bring salt-fish from
Pontus, beaver musk, tow, ebony, incense, slippery Coan cloths. Take
the fresh pepper first from the thirsty camel. Turn something; swear."
"But Jupiter will hear." "Alas, you dolt, you will go on content to
bore with a finger the salt-cellar you have tasted again, if you mean
to live with Jupiter."

Already, girt, you fit hides and a wine-jar on the boys. "Quicker to
the ship!" Nothing stops you from snatching the Aegean in a vast hull,
except that crafty Luxury first warns you aside: "Where then, madman,
are you rushing, where? What do you want? Has a male bile swollen
under a hot breast, which an urn of hemlock will not put out? You,
jump the sea? For you let dinner be on a bench, propped on twisted
hemp, and a squat mug breathe reddish Veii wine, spoiled by stale
pitch? What are you after? That the coins you had nursed here at a
modest five percent should run, by sweat, to greedy eleven? Indulge
the Genius; let us pluck the sweets. What you live is ours; you will
become ash and shades and a tale. Live mindful of death; the hour
flies; this that I am speaking is already from it."

Look, what are you doing, splitting two ways on a double hook? Do you
follow this one or that? You must go under the masters by turns in
wavering obedience, and by turns wander off. Nor, when you have stood
once and refused to obey the pressing command, should you say, "I have
broken the chains now." For even a struggling dog rips the knot away,
but still, when she flees, a long part of the chain is dragged from
the neck.

"Davus, quick, I order you to believe this: I am thinking of ending
past pains," says Chaerestratus, gnawing a raw nail. "Am I to stand as
a disgrace to my dry kinsmen? Am I to break the father's property with
an ugly rumor at a filthy threshold, while I, drunk, sing before
Chryseis's wet doors with the torch out?" "Bravo, boy. Be wise; strike
a lamb for the gods who drive it off." "But do you think, Davus, she
will weep, left?" "You play the fool. You will be scolded, boy, with a
red slipper, so you may not want to jib and gnaw the tight meshes. Now
you are fierce and violent; but if she should call, no delay, you
would say, 'What then am I to do? Not even now, when she sends for me
and even begs, am I to go?' If you had gone out from there whole and
entire, not even now." Here, here is what we are looking for, here it
is, not in the straw that a silly lictor tosses.

Has that man a right over himself whom chalked ambition leads,
open-mouthed, by a caress? Stay awake and pour chickpeas generously on
the brawling people, so that sunny old men may remember our Floralia!
What is finer? But when Herod's days have come, and the lamps, set
along the oiled window and carrying violets, have vomited a fat cloud,
and a tunny's tail, hugging a red dish, swims, and a white jar swells
with wine, you move your lips in silence and go pale at the circumcised
sabbaths. Then black specters and the dangers of a broken egg; then
the huge eunuch-priests and, with a rattle, the one-eyed priestess
have driven in gods that inflate bodies, if you have not tasted three
heads of garlic, as prescribed, in the morning.

Say these things among varicose centurions, and huge Pulfenius
straightway laughs thick and bids a clipped hundred-piece for a
hundred Greeks.

Has winter already moved you, Bassus, to your Sabine hearth? Do the
lyre and the strings already live for you under a stern quill? A
wondrous craftsman at stretching the first sounds of the old speech to
numbers, and the male noise of the Latin lyre; then, an old man
excellent at stirring young jokes and playing with an honest thumb. To
me now the Ligurian shore is warm, and my sea is wintering, where the
cliffs give a huge flank and the beach draws itself back in many a
valley. "Know the port of Luna, citizens; it is worth the work": so
Ennius's heart bids, after he snored off being Maeonides, fifth from
the Pythagorean peacock.

Here I am careless of the crowd, and of what the south wind, unlucky
for the flock, is preparing; careless too that that corner of the
neighbor is richer than ours. And even if all born of worse men should
grow rich, I would still refuse to be bent and lessened by old-man's
worry on that account, or to dine without a relish, and to have
touched the seal on a stale flask with my nose. Let another disagree
with this. Twins, horoscope, you bring forth with a bent Genius. There
is one who, on birthdays only, wets dry greens with brine bought, the
sly man, in a cup, himself sprinkling the sacred pepper on the dish.
This lordly boy runs through a great estate with his tooth. I will
use, I will use; not therefore grand enough to set turbots for
freedmen, nor clever at knowing the thin juices of thrushes.

Live up to your own harvest, and grind the granaries; it is lawful.
What are you afraid of? Harrow, and another crop is in the green. But
duty calls: a poor friend, the hull broken, clutches the Bruttian
rocks, and has buried all his property and deaf vows in the Ionian; he
himself lies on the shore, and with him the huge gods from the poop,
and already the rib of the torn raft meets the gulls. Now break
something even from the living turf; give to the needy man, so he may
not wander painted on a sea-green board. But the heir, angry that you
have cut the property, will neglect the funeral dinner; he will give
the urn unscented bones, ready not to know whether the cinnamon
breathes dull or the cassias sin with cherry. "Are you, unharmed, to
lessen the goods?" And Bestius presses the Greek teachers: "So it
goes. After this wisdom of ours, with no salt water in it, came to the
city with pepper and dates, the hay-cutters have spoiled their porridge
with thick grease." Are you to fear these things beyond the ash? But
you, my heir, whoever you will be, listen a little more withdrawn from
the crowd.

O good man, do you not know? A laurel has been sent from Caesar for a
marked slaughter of German youth, and the cold ash is being shaken
from the altars, and already Caesonia is contracting arms for the
doorposts, already the cloaks of kings, already yellow rugs for the
captives, and chariots and huge Rhines. So for the gods and the
general's Genius I am putting on a hundred pairs for things done
outstandingly. Who forbids? Dare. Woe unless you wink! I am lavishing
oil and meat-cakes on the little people. Or do you stop me? Say
clearly. "Not so much," you say; "the field next door has been stripped
bare." Come, if no aunt is now left me from the father's sisters, no
cousin, no great-granddaughter of an uncle remains, a barren aunt on
the mother's side has lived, and nothing is left of the grandmother: I
go to Bovillae and to Virbius's hill; Manius is ready for me as heir.
"A son of the earth?" Ask me who my fourth father is. Not promptly,
yet I will say it. Add still one, still one: he is already a son of
the earth, and by the rite of the line this Manius comes out for me
almost a greater uncle. You who are ahead, why do you ask me for the
torch in the running? I am Mercury to you; I come here a god as he is
painted. Or do you refuse? Will you not rejoice in what is left?
"Something is missing from the sum." I lessened it for myself, but for
you whatever it is is the whole. Do not ask where that is which Tadius
once left me, and do not put back the father's sayings: "Let the wage
of interest be added; take the costs from there." "What is left?"
Left? Now, now, boy, oil more freely, oil the stalks! Am I, on a feast
day, to have nettle cooked, and a smoked half-head with the ear split,
so that that spendthrift of yours, stuffed someday on goose guts, when
a fussy vein hiccups in a wandering groin, may piss into a patrician
womb? Am I to be left a threadbare shape, and for him a priest's
belly to shake with caul?

Sell your soul for gain; buy and shake out cunningly every side of the
world, so that no other, fat, may be better at clapping Cappadocians
on a stiff platform. Double the property. "I have done it. Already
triple, already a fourth time for me, already tenfold it comes back
into the fold." Mark where I am to stop: Chrysippus, the closer of
your heap has been found.
