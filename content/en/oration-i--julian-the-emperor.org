* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I have long been eager, O greatest emperor, to hymn your virtue and deeds and
to number your wars, and how you have put down the tyrannies, turning the
bodyguard of the one away by speech and persuasion, and conquering the other
by arms; but the greatness of the deeds held me back, not because I judged it
terrible that the speech should fall a little short of the works, but that I
should seem to miss the subject altogether. For those who spend their time on
political contests and on poetry it is no wonder if they can readily attempt
the praises of what you have done; for they have in abundance, from practice
in speaking and from familiarity with displays, a just confidence. But as many
as have neglected that part, and have set out upon another kind of education
and a writing of speeches not pleasing to the crowd and not daring to strip
for theatres of every sort, would reasonably be more cautious toward displays.
For this is not unclear, that for the poets the Muses, and the seeming to
write poetry inspired from that source, furnish an unstinted license for
fiction; but for the rhetors their art has furnished equal freedom, taking
away invention, yet in no way forbidding flattery, nor judging it an
acknowledged shame for the speaker to praise falsely those not worthy of
praise. But the poets, whenever they bring some new myth, never yet conceived
by those before, composing it themselves, are admired the more, having charmed
the hearers by the strangeness; while the rhetors say they enjoy their art in
being able to go through small things in a grander way, and to take away the
greatness of deeds by speech, and in general to set the power of words against
the nature of the facts.

But if I saw that I myself at present had need of this art, I would keep the
silence proper to those unpracticed in such speeches, yielding your encomia to
those of whom I made mention a little earlier. But since the present speech
demands the very opposite, a plain narrative of the facts needing no imported
ornament, it seemed to me also to be fitting, when a worthy recounting of the
deeds has already appeared unattainable even to those who went before. For
almost all who spend their time on education hymn you in verse and in prose,
some daring to embrace everything in brief, others giving themselves to parts
of the deeds and thinking it enough if they should not miss the worth of
these. And it is right to admire the eagerness of all the men who have taken
up your praises. For some, that none of what you have done might be dimmed by
time, dared to undergo the greatest labor; others, because they expected to
miss the whole, put forward their own judgment in part, judging it better than
the safe prize of silence to offer you first-fruits of their own labors
according to their power.

If then I myself happened to be one of those who love epideictic speeches, I
would have had to begin the subject from that point, demanding equal goodwill
to that which you already have from us, and asking that you become a kindly
hearer of the speeches, and not set yourself up as a strict and inexorable
judge. But since, having been reared and educated in other studies, as in
pursuits and laws, we seem, not rightly, to dare the works of others, I think
I must make a little clear about these things also, setting a more proper
beginning to the speech.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

There is an ancient law, laid down thus by the first who showed philosophy to
men: that all, looking to virtue and to the beautiful, should practice in
speeches, in deeds, in associations, in all things simply of life, small and
greater, to aim in every way at the beautiful. And that virtue is the most
beautiful of all things, who among those who have sense would dispute with us?
This then he bids those to cling to who will not bear this name in vain,
having appropriated what in no way belongs to them. Now in declaring these
things the law prescribes no form of speeches, nor, as from some tragic
machine, he says, does it proclaim to those who meet it that they must hasten
toward virtue and flee wickedness, but it grants many roads toward this to
whoever has wished to imitate that nature. For it praises both good counsel
and the use of hortatory speeches and the rebuking of faults with goodwill,
and again the praising of what has been done well and, when it is the time,
the blaming of such deeds as are not of that kind. And it permits one, if he
should wish, to use the other forms also toward the best in speeches, bidding
us, I think, in every speech and deed to remember in what way they will give
account of whatever they happen to have said, and to say nothing that they
will not refer to virtue and philosophy. These then are the things from the
law, and others of such a kind.

But we, then, what in the world shall we do, being barred by the seeming to
make the eulogy for favor, and the whole kind of praises already standing
under a terrible suspicion because of those who pursue it not rightly, and
being thought to be ignoble flattery, but not true witness of the best deeds?
Or is it clear that, having trusted the virtue of the one praised, we shall
give ourselves over, taking courage, to the encomia?

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

What then would be for us the finest beginning and order of the speech? Or is
it clear that it is the virtue of the ancestors, through which it was possible
for you even to become such a man? And of rearing, I think, and education it
is fitting to make mention next, which contributed the most to the virtue that
is yours; and after all these, as tokens of the virtues of the soul, to go
through the deeds, and, putting an end to the speech, to make clear the habits
from which, setting out, you did and planned the finest of the works. For by
this, I think, the speech will differ even from all the others. For they stop
at the deeds, thinking it enough for the perfect eulogy to make mention of
these, but I think one must make the greater part of the speech about the
virtues, from which, setting out, you came to so great a measure of successes.
For most of the works, and nearly all, fortune and bodyguards and phalanxes of
soldiers and ranks of horsemen help to accomplish, but the works of virtue
belong to the doer alone, and the praise from these, being true, is proper to
the one who possesses it. Therefore, since these things have been clearly
marked off for us, I will begin the speeches.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Now the law of praises thinks it right to make mention of the fatherland no
less than of the ancestors. But I do not know whom one must first take as your
fatherland; for countless nations have been disputing about this for a long
time already. And the city that rules over all, being your mother and nurse
and having furnished you the empire together with good fortune, says that the
prize is hers by special right, not using the common claims that apply to all
the emperors; I mean that, even if they happen to be from elsewhere, by all
already sharing in the constitution and using the customs and laws shown to us
from there they have become citizens; not so, then, but as having borne your
mother and reared her royally and worthily of the descendants who were to be.
But the city on the Bosporus, named for the whole race of the Constantii, says
it is not a fatherland, yet agrees that it came into being by your father, and
will think it suffers terribly if anyone should by speech take from it even
this kinship. And the Illyrians, because you were born among them, will not
endure being robbed of the finest of their blessings, if anyone should assign
you another fatherland. And I myself hear that some of the easterners already
say that we do not act justly in taking from them the claim concerning you;
for they themselves, they say, sent your grandmother to the marriage of your
mother's father. And nearly all the rest, inventing pretexts small or greater,
have resolved to adopt you for themselves by every means. Let then the prize
belong to the one you yourself wish, and whom you have often named in praise
the mother and teacher of virtues, and let the rest each obtain what is
fitting according to worth. And I would wish to praise them all, being worthy
of glory and honor, but I fear that through the length, even if they seem very
proper to the present speech, they may appear foreign because of the occasion.
Of the others, then, I think I will let the praises go for this reason, but of
Rome you yourself, O emperor, having gathered the sum of the praises in brief
and having named her teacher of virtue, by giving the finest of encomia, have
taken away the speeches from the others. For what such other thing shall we
say about her? And what has anyone else to say? so that to me ... [truncated]

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

But concerning your nobility it is perhaps worth going through in brief for
the present. And here too I seem to be at a loss from where one must begin.
For you have ancestors and grandfathers and parents and brothers and cousins
and kinsmen, all kings, having acquired the rule lawfully themselves or having
been adopted by those in power. And of the ancient things why must one speak,
making mention of Claudius, and furnishing clear proofs of that man's virtue,
known to all, recalling the contests against the barbarians who dwell beyond
the Ister, and how he acquired the rule piously and justly at once, and the
simplicity of his way of life in the empire, and the plainness of dress still
seen upon the images? And the things concerning your grandfathers are more
recent than these, but brilliant no less than those. For both obtained the
rule, having been judged worthy through virtue, and when they came to the
affairs they were so well disposed toward one another and so pious toward the
one who shared the empire with them, that he confessed he had never planned
anything better than this, though he had found many other things that saved
the common interest, while they loved the partnership with one another more
than the rule of the whole, if it were possible, accruing to each. And being
so disposed in their souls they did the finest of the works, revering after
the stronger nature the one who furnished them the rule, treating the subjects
mildly and humanely, and not only driving the barbarians who had long been
dwelling and grazing our things without fear as if they were their own, but
fortifying forts against them they established for the subjects such peace
toward them as it did not then seem easy even to pray for. But concerning
these things it is not worth speaking in a side-work. Yet the greatest sign of
their concord toward one another it is in no way reasonable to omit, and
besides it is proper to the speech. For having devised the finest partnership
for their own children they arranged the marriages of your parents. And it is
fitting, I think, to go through these things also in brief, so that you may
appear heir not of the rule only, but of the virtue as well.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

How then your father took the empire after the death of his father, by that
man's own judgment and by the vote of all the armies, why must one now be
over-busy? But his strength for the wars one would know from the works rather
than through the speeches. For putting down tyrannies, but not lawful
kingships, he went over the whole inhabited world. And he established such
goodwill for himself among the subjects that those on campaign, still
remembering his greatness of soul about gifts and favors, continue to revere
him as a god; and the multitude in the cities and upon the fields, not so much
praying to be rid of the heaviness of the tyrants as to be ruled by your
father, prayed for the victory against them for him. And when he became master
of all, as from a drought of the greed of the one who had held power there was
a great lack of money and the wealth of the palaces had been driven together
in recesses, taking off the bolt he flooded everything at once with the
wealth, and he established a city named for himself in not even whole years
ten, greater than all the others by as much as it seems to fall short of Rome,
of which to have been ranked second appears to me far better than to be
thought first of all the others. It is perhaps fine here to make mention also
of famous Athens, which he continued all the time to honor in works and
speeches. For being king and master of all, he thought it right to be called
their general, and obtaining such an image with an inscription he rejoiced
more than if he had been thought worthy of the greatest honors. And in return
to the city for this he often gives ten thousand medimni of wheat each year as
a gift to enjoy, from which it was possible for the city to be in plenty, and
for him praises and honors from the best men.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Though many and fine works were done by your father, both those of which I
made mention and as many as I think I omit because of the length, the best of
all I at least would say, and I think all the others also will agree, was your
birth and rearing and education; from which it is possible for the rest not to
enjoy the best rule for a little, but for as long a time as is possible. He
seems at any rate still to rule. And this was not possible for Cyrus. For when
he died the son was seen far worse, so that the one was called father, but the
other was named master. But that you are milder than your father and in many
other things stronger I know clearly, and I will show when the occasion
appears in the speech. Yet I think it belongs to him to share even in this,
having given you a share of the best rearing, about which I will now try to
speak, having made mention of your mother and brothers.

For so much of nobility and beauty of body and virtue of ways was hers as one
would not easily find in another woman. Since I hear also the saying of the
Persians about Parysatis, that she alone became sister and mother and wife and
child of a king. But she at least was sister of the one who married her by
nature, and law granted the Persian to marry a sister. But your mother,
keeping these kinships unstained and pure according to the laws among us,
happened to be child of one, wife of another, and sister of another, and
mother of many emperors, not of one. Of whom one helped your father accomplish
the war against the tyrants, another, having conquered by arms, prepared a
secure peace for us with the Getae, and another kept the land untrodden for
the enemy, himself campaigning against them often, as long as those who a
little later paid the penalty of the wrongs against him permitted. And though
they had many conspicuous works, for which one might justly praise them, and
though there was abundance of the goods from fortune, there is nothing of the
others of such a kind, for which one blessing them would reasonably exalt
them, as that they have become descendants of some and offspring of others.
But that I may not, speaking at more length about them, spend the due occasion
of the speech on your praises, I will try henceforth as is worthy for us, or
rather, if one must speak holding nothing back, I will show you far more
august than the ancestors.

Rumors then and oracles and visions in sleep, and as many other things as they
are accustomed to chatter about those who have done things so brilliant and
conspicuous, Cyrus and the founder of our city and Alexander son of Philip,
and if any other such has arisen, I willingly let go; for these things seem
not far from the license of poetry. And to speak of what belonged to you at
the first birth as brilliant and royal is even simple-minded. But since the
occasion of the training among children has reminded us, you needed, I
suppose, the royal rearing, which would exercise the body toward strength and
vigor and good condition and beauty, and would prepare the soul in due measure
toward courage and justice and temperance and prudence. And these things are
not easy to have through a relaxed way of life, which breaks, as is likely,
the souls and the bodies, and makes the minds weaker toward dangers and the
bodies toward labors. Therefore the body needed gymnastics, and you adorned
the soul with the practice of speeches. And it is worth going through more
fully about both; for this became a beginning of the deeds after these.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Of the care then about strength you did not practice what fits displays,
having supposed that the much-talked good condition of those who have occupied
the wrestling-grounds is least becoming to a king who is about to take part in
the true contests, needing very little sleep and not much food, and this not
altogether defined either by quantity or by quality or by the time at which
one must take it, but whatever comes to hand, whenever the deeds grant the
occasion. Hence you thought you must make the exercises also toward this, many
and military, the dance in arms, the run in these, the horseman's art, all of
which you have continued from the beginning to use in season; and each of
these has been accomplished by you as by none of the other hoplites. Therefore
one of those, being good on foot, has not known the horseman's art, and
another, knowing how to use the things of horsemen, hesitates to go on foot
into battle. But to you alone it belongs to appear best of the horsemen when
equipped like them, and when you have changed into the hoplites to surpass all
in strength and speed and lightness of the feet. And so that the relaxations
may not happen to be idle nor without arms, you practiced shooting at a mark.

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

And the body through voluntary labors you prepared to be well for the
involuntary, but for the soul the practice of speeches led the way, and the
studies proper to those of such age. And so that it might not be altogether
unexercised, nor hear the speeches about the virtues as mere songs and myths,
and remaining so long a time inexperienced of good works and deeds, as the
noble Plato thought right, granting as it were wings to the children and
mounting them on the horses to lead them to the battles, to be spectators of
what not long after they must be set as combatants, I would reasonably say
that your father, having so intended, set you as guardian and king over the
nations of the Celts, still a youth, or rather a child altogether in time,
since in understanding and strength you were already a match for the fine and
good men. That your experience of war should be without danger your father
provided well, having ordered the barbarians to keep peace toward the
subjects; but persuading them to fight and to faction against one another, in
their disasters and upon their bodies he taught the general's art, planning
more safely than the wise Plato. For for him, if a foot army of enemies should
come on, the children would become spectators and partners of the works for
the fathers, if perhaps they should need them; but if the enemies prevail with
horsemen, it is time to devise for the youths a manner of safety hard to
invent. But to accustom the children in others' dangers to endure enemies
seems to have been planned both sufficiently for the use and for the safety.

In these things then you had practice toward courage. And of prudence the
nature which you have been allotted was a sufficient leader; and there were
present, I think, also the strongest of the citizens teaching the political
things. And the meetings with the leaders of the barbarians here furnished
experience of ways and laws and foreign pursuits. And yet Homer, choosing to
show Odysseus intelligent from every side, says he is of many turns and has
come to know the mind of many men and has gone to the cities, so that picking
from all he might have the strongest things and be able to associate with men
of every sort. But for him who did not rule there was need of experience of
varied ways; but him who was being reared toward so great a command one must
not teach somewhere in a little house, nor imitate the kingship, as Cyrus, in
play, nor do business with his agemates, as they say that one did, but
associate with nations and peoples, and simply order what must be done to
ranks of soldiers; and in general to be left behind in none of those things
which, having become a man, he must do in security.

Therefore when you had been taught well the things among these, going over to
the other continent you were set alone against the nations of the Parthians
and Medes. And when the war was already smoldering and was about not long
after to be fanned up, you quickly came to know the manner of this also, and
you imitated the strength of the arms, and toward the season of summer you
accustomed the body to endure. And I learn that Alcibiades alone of all the
Greeks bore changes so well by nature as even to imitate first the
self-control of the Lacedaemonians, when he had given himself to the
Spartiates, then the Thebans, and the Thracians later, and at the end the
luxury of the Persians. But he, changing his way together with the places, was
filled with much unpleasantness and ran the risk of casting off the ancestral
altogether, but you thought you must cling to the self-controlled way of life
everywhere, and accustoming the body to the labors you bore more easily the
going up from the Galatians to the Parthians than the rich who change their
dwelling with the seasons, if they should be forced against the time. And a
kindly god seems to me, wishing from the beginning to prepare your virtue
toward the command of the whole, to have led you round in a circle and shown
you the bounds and limits of the whole empire and the nature of places and the
size of land and the power of nations and the multitude of cities and the
nature of peoples and the strongest of those very things, the abundance of
which none who is being reared toward so great a rule must be left behind in.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

And the greatest thing nearly escaped me to say, that being taught from
childhood to rule all these, you learned better to be ruled, submitting
yourself to the rule that is best and most just of all, by nature and by law;
for you obeyed a father and a king at once; of which even if the one only had
belonged to him, it was altogether proper for him to rule. And yet whom could
one ever find of a royal rearing and education better than this, that has
arisen of old? For neither the Lacedaemonians of the Greeks, who indeed seem
to have had a share of the best rule, that of the kings, educated the
Heraclids thus, nor of the barbarians the Carthaginians, being ruled by kings
in a distinguished way, thought their one who would rule them worthy of the
best care; but the gymnasia of virtue from the laws and the teachings were
common to all, as to brothers, the citizens who were about both to rule and to
be ruled, and nothing different attached in the account of education to the
leaders than to the others. And yet how is it not simple-minded to demand an
unsurpassable greatness of virtue from the rulers, but to take no forethought
how they will be different from the many? And for the barbarians, this rule
being set before all in common, the care of the ways becoming similar might
furnish pardon; but Lycurgus, who preserved the kingship unshaken for those
from Heracles, if he found no superiority in the cares of the young, one would
very reasonably blame. For even if he thought all Lacedaemonians must be
athletes of virtue and nurslings, one must not think the private men worthy of
equal rearing and education with those who will rule. For such a habit,
slipping in little by little, bred in the souls contempt of the stronger; for
in general one must not even think stronger those who have obtained first
place not through virtue. And this, I think, often made the Spartiates also
harder for the kings to rule. And one might use as a clear proof of what has
been said the rivalry of Lysander toward Agesilaus and many others, going over
what the men have done.

But for these the constitution, preparing the things toward virtue
sufficiently, even if it granted them to practice nothing different from the
many, at least furnished them to be fine and good men; but of the
Carthaginians not even the common pursuits are worth praising. For the
parents, driving the children from the houses, ordered them to prosper through
the labors necessary toward use, forbidding them to do any of the things
thought shameful. And this was not to take out the desire of the young, but to
order them to try to do something unnoticed. For not luxury only is born to
corrupt character, but also a way of life lacking the necessary things, in
those for whom reason has not yet taken on judging and follows the needs,
being persuaded by desire, especially if he should not even master this
passion, being accustomed from children toward money-making and toward certain
returns of tradings and hucksterings, some finding them himself and some
learning from those who know, about which it is not worth not only speaking
but not even hearing for a free child, he would deposit very many stains in
the soul, of all of which even the decent citizen must be clean, and not the
king and general only.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

But it is not proper for me at present to rebuke those; I will show only the
difference of the rearing, using which you excelled in beauty and strength and
justice and temperance, wrapping yourself in good condition through the
labors, and acquiring temperance through the laws, and using a body more
vigorous through the self-control of the soul, and again a soul more just
through the endurance of the body, increasing from every side the goods from
nature, and always taking on besides those from the cares outside; and needing
nothing, but sufficing for others and granting great gifts, such as were
enough to show those who received them like the ruler of the Lydians, yourself
enjoying the goods at hand more scantily than the most temperate of the
Spartiates, but furnishing to others the means of luxury, and furnishing
yourself to those who wish to be temperate to imitate, ruling the others
mildly and humanely, but being ruled by your father temperately and as one of
the many you continued the whole time. These things belonged to you as a child
and a youth, and many more, about which to speak now would be longer than the
occasion.

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

And when you came to the age, and the divinity furnished your father a very
blessed fated end, you adorned the tomb not only by the multitude and beauty
of what was brought, paying the thank-offerings of birth and rearing, but much
more by being the only one of all his children who, while he was still living
and pressed by the disease, set out toward him, and when he had died
established the greatest honors, about which even the mention is enough. For
the deeds call us to themselves, reminding of the strength, the good courage,
good counsel at once and justness, in which you were seen unconquerable and
unsurpassable, having settled the things toward the brothers and the citizens
and your father's friends and the armies justly and temperately; except if
anywhere, forced by the occasions unwillingly, you did not prevent others from
going wrong; and having settled the things toward the enemies courageously and
magnificently and worthily of the glory of the race that was already yours.
With the one group you have been together the whole time through concord,
keeping the city unfactioned and always attending the brothers as
fellow-rulers, and giving the friends a share of equal speech and of frankness
unsparingly with the other goods, sharing with all what was at hand, and
giving a share of whatever each seemed to lack. And of these one might
reasonably use those men themselves as witnesses, and the facts are enough to
show to those left out of association with them the choice of the whole life.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

And one must speak already about the deeds themselves, putting off the speech
about the habits. The Persians, having long ago ruled all Asia and having
subdued most of Europe, I almost say having thrown the whole inhabited world
about themselves in a circle with their hopes, when they had been taken of the
empire by the Macedonians, having become a work of Alexander's generalship, or
rather a plaything, bearing the being slaves hard, when they perceived that he
had died, revolting from the successors they again set themselves in opposing
power against the Macedonians, and to us who acquired what was left of the
Macedonians' empire they seemed throughout to be enemies worth fighting. And
of the ancient things why must one now remind, of Antony and Crassus, generals
with full power, and how we thrust off those shames through long dangers, many
and temperate emperors fighting the failures over again? And why must one
mention the second misfortunes and the deeds of Carus upon them, who after the
disasters was chosen general? But those who ordered them to keep the wonderful
peace loved among all, those who held the empire before your father, did not
the Caesar, engaging by himself, come off shamefully? And when the ruler of
the whole inhabited world turned and directed the forces of the whole command
there and occupied the inlets beforehand with armies and rolls of old hoplites
and new-levied and preparations of every sort, in fear they were barely
content with the peace. Which, I do not know how, while your father still
lived they confused and threw into disorder, and they missed the punishment
from him, since he changed life in the preparations toward the war; but to you
they later paid the penalty of what they had dared.

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

And being about still to take up the contests against them that have often
arisen for you, I think it right that the hearers consider this much, that
having been established master of the third part of the empire, which in no
way seemed strong for the war, not in arms, not in the men on campaign, not in
any of the other things that must flow in unsparingly toward so great a war,
and besides this not even your brothers, for whatever cause, lightening the
war; and there is no one so shameless nor envious a sycophant who will not say
that you became most responsible for the concord toward them; and the war
being, I think, hard in itself, the affairs of the armies were in confusion
toward the change, crying out that they longed for their old leader, and
wishing you to rule; and countless other absurd and hard things shooting up
from every side furnished more difficult hopes about the war; the Armenians,
old allies, in faction, and a not paltry portion of them attaching themselves
to the Persians, overrunning the land bordering them with robbers; and what
alone in the present things appeared saving, that you should hold to the
affairs and take counsel, was not yet possible because of the treaties toward
the brothers in Paeonia, which you yourself, being present, so administered
that you furnished them no starting-point of blame. The beginning of the deeds
nearly escaped me, finer than all or equally admirable with the finest. For
that one taking counsel about so many affairs should seem in no way lessened,
