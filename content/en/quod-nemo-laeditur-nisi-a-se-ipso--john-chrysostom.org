* That No One Is Injured Except by Himself
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of our father among the saints John, archbishop of Constantinople, the
Golden-mouthed, a letter written from Cucusus in Cilicia, while he was in
exile, that no one will be able to harm the man who does not injure himself,
to the blessed Olympias and to all the faithful.

1 I know that to the coarser and to those who gape toward the present things
and are nailed to the earth and are slaves to sensible pleasures, and do not
cling much to the things of the intellect, this argument will seem some new
and paradoxical thing, and they will laugh abundantly and will condemn us as
saying unconvincing things from the prefaces of the promise. Yet we will not
for this stand off from the announcement, but even for this very reason most
of all we will go on to the proofs of what we promised with much eagerness.
For if those so disposed are willing not to make a tumult and not to disturb,
but to wait for the end of the argument, I know well that they will stand with
us and will condemn themselves as having been deceived the time before, and
they will sing a palinode and will apologize and will ask pardon for those
things in which they did not have a right vote about the matters, and they
will be grateful to us with much thanks, as the sick to the physicians,
whenever they are freed from the diseases that besiege their body. For do not
tell me the judgment that now holds with you, but wait also for the contests
of our words, and then you will be able to bring an unbribed vote, being in no
way harmed from ignorance toward judging the true things. Since even the
judges who sit over these worldly affairs do not endure, even if they see the
first speaker flowing strongly and flooding everything with his tongue, to
bring the vote without also hearing with long-suffering the one who stands
against him; but even if the first seems ten thousand times to speak just
things, they keep the hearing untouched also for the second, since this too is
a virtue of judges, after learning the things from each side with all
exactness, then to bring on the things from themselves. Since then even now,
in place of some orator, the common prejudice of the many, rooted in a long
time in the minds of the many, orates these things throughout the whole
inhabited world, saying: Everything, it says, has become upside down, the race
of men has been filled with much confusion, and many are those each day who
are injured, who are despitefully used, who are forced, who are harmed, the
weak by the more powerful, the poor by the rich; and as the waves of the sea
cannot be numbered, so neither can the multitude of those plotted against, of
those despitefully used, of those suffering evil, and neither correction of
laws, nor fear of courts, nor anything else has stopped this plague and the
disease, but each day the evil increases, and everywhere are wailings and
dirges and tears of the injured; and the judges appointed to correct such
things themselves stretch the storm and grind the disease; and many of the
more wretched and more senseless from this, raging with some new madness,
accuse the providence of God, seeing the reasonable man often dragged, torn,
strangled, and the bold and dishonored and from the dishonored growing rich
and clothed with power, become fearful to the many and inflicting countless
terrible things on the more reasonable, and these things dared in cities and
in countries and in deserts and on land and on sea: therefore this argument of
ours necessarily comes in, standing opposite to what has been said and a
contest


contested that is new, as I said when I began, and paradoxical, but useful and
true and profitable to those who are willing to attend and be persuaded. For
it promises to show, but do not be thrown into tumult, that none of those
injured is injured by any other, but suffers this from himself.

2 But that the argument may become clearer, come, first let us examine what
injustice is and about what sort of things its matter is accustomed to be
constituted; what human virtue is, and what it is that ruins this; and what
seems to ruin it, but does not ruin it. For example (for one must make the
argument complete from examples) each of the things has that which ruins it,
iron rust, wool the moth, flocks of sheep the wolves; and the harm of the
virtue of wine is to be turned and to fall into vinegar; and of honey to lose
the inborn sweetness and to be changed into a bitter flavor; and rust and
drought ruin the hair of standing crops, and the wicked army of locusts the
fruit and leaves and branches of vines, and the caterpillar other trees, and
varied diseases the bodies of irrational animals. And that we may not, going
through all things, make the argument long, also our own flesh has both fevers
and palsies and another swarm of diseases. As then each of these has that
which ruins its virtue, come, let us consider what also harms the race of men
and what it is that ruins a man's virtue. The many then think different
things. For one must also speak the deceived opinions and, having taken them
away, thus bring into the midst that which truly ruins our virtues and show
clearly that no one can injure us with this injury, nor ruin us with this
ruin, unless we ourselves betray ourselves. The many then, having deceived
opinions, think other things to be what ruin our virtue: some poverty, some
disease of body, some loss in money, others slander, others death, and they
continue bewailing and lamenting these things and pitying those who suffer and
weeping and being struck, they say to one another: What things so-and-so has
suffered; he was suddenly stripped of all his substance. Another about another
again: So-and-so, having fallen into a hard sickness, has been despaired of by
the physicians who go in to him. And one laments those who dwell in the
prison, another those who have fallen from the fatherland and migrated to
exile, another those who have fallen from freedom, another those snatched by
enemies and become captives, another the one drowned or burned, another the
one buried under a house, and they wail; but those living in wickedness no
one, but, what is harder than all, they often bless them, which is also the
cause of all the evils. Come then, but as I exhorted from the beginning, do
not make a tumult, let us show that none of the things said injures the sober
man, nor could it ruin his virtue. For what, tell me, did this loss harm
toward the virtue of the man the one who has lost all that is his, or has been
stripped of his goods by slanderers or robbers or even household evildoers? Or
rather, if you please, first let us sketch what a man's virtue is, having
first exercised the argument in other beings so as to make it more easily
learned and clearer to the many.

3 What then is a horse's virtue? Is it to have a golden bit and such straps
and a saddle-cloth composed of silk threads and varied and gold-spangled rugs
and jewel-set cheek-pieces and manes plaited with golden cords, or to be swift
and sound of leg and to walk rhythmically and to have hooves fitting a noble
horse and to possess the courage that belongs in long journeys, that which is
fitted for wars, and to be able also in battle-array to appear with much
spirit and, when a rout has happened, to save the rider? Is it not quite clear
that these are a horse's virtue, not those? And what of asses and mules


what sort of virtue would you say they have? Is it not to be able to bear
burdens with ease and to accomplish the roads readily and to have feet
imitating the hardness of rock? Shall we say that the things laid around them
from outside contribute anything toward their own virtue? By no means. And
what sort of vine shall we admire? the one luxuriant in leaves and in
branches, or the one heavy with fruit? And what do we say the virtue of an
olive is? when it has large boughs and much hair from the leaves, or when it
shows its own fruit abundant and scattered everywhere? Let us then do so also
in the case of men: let us distinguish the virtue that is the man's, and let
us think that harm to be only that which ruins it. What then is a man's
virtue? not money, that you should fear poverty; nor health, that you should
dread sickness; nor the opinion of the many, that you should look askance at
an evil reputation; nor living simply and at random, that death should become
fearful to you; nor freedom, that you should flee slavery; but exactness of
the true doctrines and rightness according to the life. These not even the
devil himself will be able to plunder, if the one who possesses them guards
them with the fitting exactness. And this even that most wicked and wild demon
knows. For this is why he also plundered the substance of Job, not in order to
make him poor, but in order to force him to cast out some blasphemous word,
and he cut the body to pieces, not in order to throw sickness around him, but
in order to trip up the virtue according to the soul. But nevertheless, having
moved all his devices and having made him poor instead of rich, this which
indeed seems to all of us most dreadful, childless instead of having many
children, and having torn his whole body, more harshly than the executioners
in the courts (for their nails do not so dig through the sides of those who
fall to them as the mouths of the worms tore his flesh) and having put around
him an evil reputation (for his friends present said to him: You have not been
scourged worthily of the things you sinned, and they stretched out many words
of accusation against him) and he did not only cast him out of the city, nor
of the house, and transfer him to another city, but having set the dunghill as
both house and city for him, not only did he ruin him in nothing, but he even
showed him more splendid through the things in which he plotted. And he not
only took away nothing of the things that are, though he took away such
things, but he even worked for him a greater wealth of virtue; for after these
things he also enjoyed more boldness, inasmuch as he had also contested a more
vehement contest. And if the one who suffered so many things has been injured
in nothing, and suffered not from a man but from the demon more wicked than
all men, what apology will those have left who say, So-and-so injured me and
harmed me? For if the devil, full of so much wickedness, having moved all his
instruments and having sent all his arrows and as many evils as were among
men, with much excess, and having emptied them both into the house of the
righteous man and into the body, injured the man in nothing but, as I said,
even benefited him more, how will some be able to accuse this man and that
man, as injured by them, and not as from home?

4 What then? he says; did he not injure Adam and trip him up and cast him out
of paradise? Not he, but the listlessness of the one injured and the not being
sober and not being awake. For the one who brought on such and so many devices
and was not able to bring Job down, how would he have prevailed over Adam from
lesser things, if that man had not betrayed himself through his own
listlessness? What then? the one who has fallen among slanderers and has had
his substance confiscated has not been injured, even when stripped of all his
goods and fallen from ancestral things and wrestling with extreme poverty? He
has not been injured, but he has even gained, if he is sober. For what, tell
me, did this harm the apostles? did they not continually wrestle with hunger
and thirst and nakedness? And for this very reason they were even exceedingly
splendid and notable and drew much help from God. And what did the disease and
the sores and the poverty and the desolation of those who would stand by


harm Lazarus? were not the crowns woven for him more greatly from this?
And what did it harm Joseph to acquire an evil reputation both in his own land
and in the foreign? For he was thought to be both an adulterer and a companion
of harlots. And what did slavery? and what falling from the fatherland? Do we
not especially admire him and are we not struck because of these things? And
why do I speak of the migration into exile and poverty and evil reputation and
slavery? For death itself, what did it harm Abel, even becoming violent and
untimely and dared by a brother's hand? Is he not sung because of this
everywhere in the inhabited world? Do you see how the argument has shown more
than it promised? For it has shown not only that no one is injured by anyone,
but also that those who attend to themselves gain more greatly. For the sake
of what then are penalties and punishments? he says; for the sake of what is
gehenna? for the sake of what so many threats, if no one is either injured or
injures? Why do you confuse the argument? for I did not say that no one
injures, but that no one is injured. And how is it possible, he says, when
many injure, that there is no being injured? thus, as I taught now. Since the
brothers did injure Joseph, but he himself was not injured, and Cain did plot
against Abel, but he himself was not plotted against; for this reason there
are penalties and punishments. For God does not take away the penalties
because of the virtue of those who suffer, but he sets the punishments because
of the wickedness of those who do evil. For even if those who suffer evil
become more splendid from the plotters, yet this is not of the mind of those
who plot. For this reason the prizes of philosophy are made ready for the one
set, and for those the punishments of wickedness are prepared. Have you been
stripped of money? say: Naked I came out from my mother's womb, naked also I
shall go away. And add the apostolic: For we brought nothing into the world;
it is clear that we cannot carry anything out. Have you been spoken of evilly
and have some washed you with countless revilings? Remember that saying which
says: Woe to you when all men speak well of you, and Rejoice and leap when
they cast out your name as evil. Have you been moved to exile? Think that you
do not have a fatherland here but, if you are going to philosophize, you have
been commanded to think even the whole earth a foreign place. But have you
been given over to a hard disease? Say that apostolic word: As much as our
outer man is destroyed, so much the inner is renewed day by day. But has
someone endured a violent death? Think of John and the head cut off in prison,
brought on a platter and become the wage of a harlot's dance. Think of the
returns from this. For all these sufferings, whenever they are brought on
someone unjustly by someone, both loose sins and work righteousness. So great
is the magnitude of their benefit in those who bear them nobly.

5 When then neither loss of money nor slanders and revilings nor being
transferred to exile, nor diseases and tortures, nor that which seems more
fearful than all, death, harms those who suffer, but even benefits them more
greatly, from where can you show me someone injured, when from this nothing is
injured? For I will try to prove the contrary, that those who are especially
injured and despitefully used and suffer the incurable things are those who do
these things. For what could become more wretched than Cain, who treated his
brother with such things? And what more pitiable than the wife of Philip, who
cut off John? And what than the brothers of Joseph who sold him and
transferred him to exile? And what than the devil who tore Job with so many
evils? For not only of the others, but also of this plot he will give no
ordinary penalty. Did you see how here too the argument has shown more than
the promise? Not only that those despitefully used endure no ruin from these
plotters, but also that the whole is turned upon the head of those who plot;
since neither wealth, nor being free, nor dwelling in a fatherland, nor the
other things I mentioned, are a man's


virtues, but the achievements of the soul, reasonably, whenever the harm
happens to these, human virtue is in no way harmed. What then if someone is
harmed in the very philosophy of the soul? not even here, if he is harmed, is
he harmed by another, but from home and from himself. How from home and from
himself? he says. Whenever he is scourged by anyone whatsoever or stripped of
his goods or, having endured some other hard despite, casts out some
blasphemous word, he has been harmed here and with a very great harm; yet not
from the one who despitefully used him, but from his own smallness of soul.
For what I said before, I will say also now. No one of men, even if he be ten
thousand times wicked, could attack anyone more wickedly than that accursed
demon and the devil who is irreconcilably disposed toward us, nor more
bitterly; but nevertheless this hard demon was not able, the one before the
law, the one before grace, having sent so many arrows against him and so
bitter from every side, to trip him up and throw him down. So great is the
nobility of the soul. And what of Paul? did he not suffer so many terrible
things, which it is not even easy to catalogue? Dwelling in prisons, having
chains laid around him, being led and led about, being scourged by Jews, being
stoned, having his back torn not with straps only but also with rods, being
drowned, often falling among robbers, enduring a civil war from enemies, being
struck continually by acquaintances, being plotted against with countless
plots, wrestling with hunger and nakedness, enduring other dense and
continuous circumstances and afflictions? Why must one say the many things?
dying each day, but nevertheless suffering so many and such things, he not
only cast out no blasphemous word, but he even rejoiced at these things and
boasted. And now he says: I rejoice in my sufferings, and now: And not only
so, but we also boast in tribulations. If then he himself, suffering so many
things, rejoiced and boasted, what pardon do you have, and what apology, not
enduring the smallest part of them and blaspheming?

6 But I am injured otherwise, he says, and am I not to blaspheme? having been
stripped of money, I become useless toward almsgiving, he says. These things
are a pretext and an excuse. For if you grieve because of this, learn clearly
that poverty does not become a hindrance of almsgiving. For even if you are
ten thousand times poor, you are not poorer than the woman who possessed only
a handful of meal and the one who had only two obols, each of whom, having
emptied all her substance into those in need, was admired, and so great a
poverty did not become a hindrance to so great a loving-kindness, but the
almsgiving from the two mites became so abundant and ambitious as to hide all
those who were rich and to outstrip those who cast down many staters by the
wealth of the mind and the surplus of the eagerness. So that not even here
have you been injured but you have even gained more greatly, having received
more splendid crowns through a small contribution than those who laid down the
many things. But since, even if we say these things ten thousand times, the
body-loving souls that roll gladly in the things of this life and take
pleasure in the present affairs would not readily endure to stand off from the
rotting flowers (for such are the bright things of this life) nor do they
endure to let go the shadows, but the more reasonable hold to both those and
these, and the more pitiable from a greater share of those, and of these from
a very lesser, come then, having taken off the bright and conspicuous masks
from the shameful and ill-formed look of these affairs, let us show the
loathsomeness of the prostituted woman. For such is such a life, the one that
attends to luxuries and wealth and powers: the shameful and ill-formed and
full of much loathsomeness, the unpleasant and burdensome and filled with
bitterness. And this indeed is especially what strips those caught of all
pardon, that this life, even filled with unpleasantness and much bitterness,
is desired by them and much sought. And full of countless dangers, bloodshed,
precipices, reefs


and murders and fears and tremblings and envy and jealousy and plotting and
continual care and anxiety, and having no gain, nor bearing any fruit of so
many evils, but only punishment and penalty and being tortured continually, it
seems to the many to be enviable and worth fighting for, which is of the folly
of those caught, not of the blessedness of the thing. Since even small
children gape and are fluttered toward playthings, but of the things fitted
for complete men they could not even take perception. But for those there is
pardon because of the untimeliness of the age; these have been cast out of
apology, having in complete age a child's mind, and being disposed more
senselessly than those. For why is wealth enviable? tell me; for it is
necessary to make a preface from this, since it has seemed to the many more
honorable than health and life and good report from the many and a good
opinion and fatherland and household and friends and kinsmen and all the
things of those who have been sick with this hard disease. And the pyre has
already gone up to the very clouds and this furnace has taken land and sea,
and there is no one quenching this flame; but those kindling it are all, both
those already caught and those not yet caught, in order that they may be
caught. And one might see each, both man and woman and household-slave and
free and rich and poor, according to his own power, carrying loads that
furnish much food to this flame both by day and by night, loads not of wood
nor of brushwood, for the flame is not of that sort, but of souls and bodies,
of injustice and lawlessness. For from these this nature of the flame is
accustomed to be kindled. For those who are rich nowhere stand still from this
absurd desire, even if they throw the whole inhabited world around themselves;
and the poor hasten to overtake those, and some incurable rabies and
ungovernable madness and uncorrectable disease holds the souls of all. And
this desire, having conquered every desire and pushed it aside, has cast it
out of the soul. And there is neither a word of friendship, nor of kinship.
Why do I speak of friendship and kinship? not of wife and children, than which
what could become more desired for men? but all things have been thrown to the
ground and trampled, this cruel and inhuman mistress having taken the souls of
all those caught. For as an inhuman mistress of all these and as a harsh
tyrant and as a cruel barbarian and as a public and expensive harlot, she
shames and stretches and punishes with countless dangers and penalties those
who have chosen to be slaves to her; and being some fearful and implacable
thing, wild and harsh and having a barbarian face, or rather a beast-like one
and wilder than wolf and lion, she seems to those taken captive by her some
gentle and desired thing and sweeter than honey. Forging a sword and weapons
against them each day and digging pits and leading them onto precipices and
reefs and weaving countless nets of punishment for them, she is thought to
make them enviable both to those themselves who have been caught and to those
who desire to be caught. And as a sow rolling in the drain and mire takes
pleasure and lives softly, and dung-beetles continually rolling dung, so also
those caught by love of money are more wretched than these animals. For the
loathsomeness here is greater, and the mire more foul-smelling. For spending
time in the passion, they think they harvest much pleasure from this; which is
not of the nature of the thing, but of the mind that is sick with such
unreason.

7 How then might we heal those so disposed? If they should be willing to open
the ears and unfold the mind and receive what is said. For to change the
irrational animals from the unclean pastime is not possible; for they are
empty of reasoning; but this most tame race, honored with both reasoning and
speech, I mean the human nature, if it should be willing, is both ready and
exceedingly easy to free from the mire and the foul smell and the dung and
that loathsomeness. For why does


wealth, O man, seem to you so much to be sought? because of the pleasure from
the tables altogether? because of the honor and the bodyguard from those who
attend? because of being able to avenge those who grieve you and to be fearful
to all? For you would not have other causes to speak of, but only pleasure and
flattery and fear and punishment. For it is not accustomed to make one wiser,
nor more temperate, nor more reasonable, nor more intelligent altogether, not
good, not humane, not stronger than anger, not better than the belly, not
higher than pleasures; it does not train one to be moderate, it does not teach
one to be contracted, it does not bring in and plant any other part of virtue
into the soul. Nor would you have to say why of these this is so much sought
by you and desired. For not only does it know how to transplant or farm none
of the goods, but even if it finds them stored, it ruins and hinders and
withers them; and some it even pulls up and brings in the opposites of these,
immoderate wantonness, untimely wrath, unjust anger, desperation, pride,
folly. But let me not speak about these; for those caught by this disease
would not endure hearing about virtue and vice, being wholly of pleasure and
because of this becoming slaves of it, to see themselves accused and at the
same time convicted. Come then, for now leaving the argument about these, let
us bring the rest into the midst and let us see if wealth has any pleasure, if
any honor. For I see the very contrary, and, if you wish, first let us examine
the tables of the rich and of the poor and let us ask those who feast, who
especially are those who harvest a pure and genuine pleasure: those who lie on
the couches toward a measured-out day and join the dinners to the lunches and
burst the belly and maim the senses and with the oversized load of foods sink
the boat and make the ship waterlogged and, as in a shipwreck of the body,
flood it and invent fetters and hand-fetters and tongue-fetters and bind down
their whole body, with a bond harder than an iron chain, that of drunkenness
and luxury, and choosing neither genuine and unmixed sleep, nor being freed
from fearful dreams, being more wretched than the mad and bringing in a
self-chosen demon to the soul and being set forth as a laugh to the theater of
the household slaves, or rather a tragedy and a subject of tears to the more
reasonable of them, and knowing none of those present, nor able to say
anything and to hear, but being carried from the couches to the bed? or those
who are sober and awake and mark the measure by need and sail with a fair wind
and have hunger and thirst as the greatest seasoning both upon the foods and
upon the drinks? For nothing so works both pleasure and health as for one who
is hungry and thirsty to touch what is set out and to know need alone as
satiety and not to overstep the lists of this, nor to set a burden on the body
greater than the power.

8 And if you distrust my argument, study the bodies of each and the soul of
each. Are not those of the ones who live in due measure (for do not tell me
what happens rarely, and if some are weak from some other circumstance, but
take the votes from the things that happen always and continually) are not the
bodies of those who feast in due measure vigorous and the senses made
distinct, fulfilling their own need with much ease; and those of the others
flabby and softer than any wax and besieged by a swarm of diseases? For gouts
also quickly fly upon them and untimely tremor and untimely old age and
headaches and distensions and maimings of stomachs and destruction of
appetite, and they need continual physicians and continuous drugs and daily
treatment. Are these then of pleasure? tell me. And who of those who know what
pleasure is would say so? For pleasure comes then, when with desire leading
you enjoy, since if there is enjoyment


but desire appears nowhere, the things of pleasure are gone and have vanished.
For this reason also those who are sick, even though foods much sought are set
out for them, nauseated and seeming to be troubled, so taste of them, since
there is no desire, which makes the enjoyment most pleasant. For neither the
nature of the foods, nor that of the drinks, but the appetite of those who
feast is accustomed to beget the desire and is naturally able to work the
pleasure. For this reason also a certain wise man, knowing the things about
pleasure exactly and understanding how to philosophize about these, said: A
soul filled mocks at honeycombs, showing that not in the nature of the table,
but in the disposition of those who feast, the things of pleasure stand. For
this reason also the prophet, cataloguing the wonders in Egypt and those in
the desert, has said among the others also this, that he fed them honey from a
rock, and yet nowhere does a rock appear having gushed honey for them. What
then is what was said? Since, worn with much toil and the journey and held by
a vehement thirst, they fell upon the cold streams, having thirst as a great
seasoning, wishing to present the pleasure of those streams, he called the
water honey, not as the nature having been changed into honey, but as the
pleasure of the water rivaling that sweetness because those who enjoyed it
fell upon it thirsting. When then these things have this manner and no one can
contradict, even if he is exceedingly unfeeling, is it not quite clear that at
the tables of the poor there is the pure and unmixed and exceeding pleasure,
but in those of the rich unpleasantness and loathsomeness and defilement? And,
as that wise man said, even the pleasant things seem to annoy.

9 But wealth prepares those who have it to be honored, he says, and to avenge
enemies with ease. Is it for this then, tell me, that it seems desired by you
and worth fighting for, that it nourishes the hardest passions in you, both
leading anger out into work and raising the bubbles of glory-madness into a
greater bulk and anointing and waking toward desperation? For these reasons
then especially one must flee it without turning, because it settles some wild
and hard beasts into our mind, stripping us of the real honor from all, and
dyeing that opposite one with the colors of that and so bringing it to the
deceived and persuading them to think this to be that, not being so by nature,
but seeming to be so by the look. For as the beauties of prostituted women,
composed of paints and under-drawings, have been stripped of beauty, and make
the shameful and ill-formed look seem both beautiful and well-formed among the
deceived, so also wealth, forcing flattery, shows it as honor. For do not look
at the good reports that happen in the open through fear and fawning (for
these are the colors and the inscriptions) but unfold the conscience of each
of those who flatter you with such things and you will see countless accusers
within crying out against you and turning away and hating more than the most
hostile enemies and warriors, and if ever a change of affairs coming on should
both make vanish and convict the mask composed from this fear, as the sun
those looks, having sent a hotter ray, so you will see well that through all
the time you are in extreme dishonor among those who attend, and you thought
you were enjoying honor from those who most of all hate you and wash you in
the mind with countless revilings and desire to see you in extreme disasters.
For nothing is so accustomed to make honor as virtue, honor not forced, honor
not fabricated, nor hidden by some mask of imposture, but true and genuine,
and convicted by no difficulty of times. 10 But do you wish to avenge those
who have grieved you? And for this very reason most of all, as I said before,
wealth is to be fled. For it prepares you to thrust the sword against yourself
and makes heavier the accounts in the age to come for you and makes
unendurable the


punishments. For so great an evil is avenging, that it even called back the
loving-kindness of God and canceled a forgiveness of infinite sins already
given. For the one who received the release of ten thousand talents and
enjoyed so great a gift from a mere entreaty, when he made a demand of a
hundred denarii from the fellow-slave, that is, when he demanded penalties of
the faults against him, in the vehemence toward the fellow-slave, brought out
the condemnation against himself. And for no other thing, but for this only,
he was both given over to the torturers and was racked and was commanded to be
demanded the ten thousand talents and enjoyed no pardon nor apology; but he
suffered the incurable things, having been commanded to lay down the whole
debt, which the loving-kindness of God, having anticipated, forgave. Is it for
this then, tell me, that wealth is so much sought by you, that it leads you
out into such a sin with ease? and indeed for this one must turn away from it
as an enemy and a warrior and full of countless murders. But poverty, he says,
prepares one to be displeased and often even to cast out blasphemous words and
to endure illiberal affairs. Not poverty, but smallness of soul. Since Lazarus
also was poor and exceedingly poor, and to the poverty there was also added a
sickness more bitter than all poverty, which also made this poverty harder;
and to the sickness a desolation of those who would stand by and a want of
those who treat, which also worked both the sickness and the poverty more
bitter. For each of these even by itself is painful, but when those who treat
are not even there, the terrible thing becomes greater, the flame harder, the
pain more bitter, the winter wilder, the surge more vehement, the furnace more
burning. And if someone should examine exactly, there was also another fourth
added to these, the license and luxury of the rich man living from the
neighbors. And if you wish to find also a fifth fuel of the flame, you will
see this too clearly laid around him. For that rich man not only lived softly,
but also a second and a third time, or rather even more often in the day
seeing him (for he had been thrown by the entrance, being a theater of a hard
tragedy and by the look alone able to soften even a stony soul) and
nevertheless not even this drew that inhuman man to the relief of that
poverty; but the one set out a Sybaritic table and had mixing-bowls crowned,
and unmixed wine simply poured out and armies of cooks, and parasites and
flatterers and choruses of singers, of wine-pourers, of jesters, and inventing
every form of prodigality and being drunk and surfeited and living softly in
clothing and table and many other things he continued all the time. And seeing
this man stretched each day by a hard hunger and a most bitter sickness and a
siege of so many sores and desolation, and by the evils from these, he never
even threw it into his mind, but the parasites and the flatterers were even
bursting beyond need; and the poor man, and so poor, and lying in so many
evils, did not even enjoy crumbs from that table, and this though greatly
desiring; and nevertheless none of these harmed him, he did not cast out some
word, not small, not great, he did not utter a blasphemous speech; but as gold
being more greatly purified by the more vehement firing becomes more splendid,
so, being troubled by these sufferings, he was higher than all both the
passions and the tumults born from this to many. For if simply poor men,
seeing rich men, melt with envy, and stretching with jealousy think life
unlivable, and this though being well supplied with the necessary food and
having those who attend; this poor man, being poor as no other was poor, and
not only poor but also weak and having no one who would stand by and comfort,
but lying in the midst of the city as in extreme desolation and melting with a
most bitter hunger and seeing all things flowing to that man as from springs
and enjoying no human consolation at all, and to the


tongues of the dogs laid out as a continual table (for so was the body palsied
and dissolved, that he could not even drive those away) what would he not have
suffered if he had not been exceedingly noble and a philosopher? Do you see
that the one who does not injure himself, even if he is injured by all,
suffers nothing terrible? for again I will take up the same argument.

11 For what did the sickness? and what the desolation of those who would stand
by? and what the attack of the dogs? and what the wicked neighborhood of the
rich man? and what did that much delicacy and pride and desperation harm this
athlete, or make him softer toward the lists on behalf of virtue? and what
ruined his vigor? Nothing anywhere, but it even strengthened him more and
became a subject of countless crowns for him and an addition of prizes and a
multiplying of repayment and a subject of greater returns, that multitude of
the terrible things and the cruelty of the rich man. For he was not crowned
because of the poverty only, nor because of the hunger, nor because of the
sores, nor because of the tongues of the dogs; but because, having such a
neighbor and being seen by him each day and being continually overlooked, he
bore this temptation nobly and with much endurance, both the poverty and the
sickness and the desolation contributing not a small but even an exceedingly
most vigorous flame. And what of the blessed Paul, tell me? for nothing
hinders remembering the man again. Did he not receive countless snowstorms of
temptations? what then was he harmed from this? was he not even more greatly
crowned from this? that he hungered? that he was stretched with frost and
nakedness? that he was often torn with scourges? that he was struck with
stones? that he was drowned? But that man was Paul, and called of Christ. And
yet Judas also was one of the twelve and himself also called of Christ. But
neither the being of the twelve, nor the calling, benefited him, since he did
not have a mind prepared toward virtue, but Paul, even wrestling with hunger
and wanting the necessary food and suffering so many things each day, ran with
much eagerness the road that leads to heaven. And that man, even having been
called before this and having enjoyed the same things which he also had and
having been initiated into the highest philosophy and having shared the sacred
table and those most dreadful dinners and having received so great a grace as
even to raise the dead and cleanse lepers and drive out demons; and having
often heard the words about possessionlessness and having been with Christ
himself so long a time and having been entrusted with the money of the poor so
as to have consolation of the passion from there (for he was a thief) not even
so did he become better, though he had enjoyed so great a condescension. For
since Christ knew that he was a lover of money and was going to perish through
desire of money, not only did he not then demand penalties of him for this,
but in order to grind down his passion he even entrusted to him the money of
the poor, in order that, having whence he might satiate the love of money, he
might not fall into that dreadful pit; checking the greater beforehand with a
lesser evil.

12 Thus everywhere the one who does not wish to injure himself, no other will
be able to injure; and the one who is not willing to be sober and to
contribute the things from himself from home, no one will ever benefit. For
this reason also the wondrous history of the scriptures, as in some high and
great image having much breadth, wrote up the lives of the ancients,
stretching the narration from Adam until the presence of Christ. And it shows
you both those tripped up and those crowned, in order that it may train you
through all things that the one not injured from himself no other will be able
to injure, even if the whole inhabited world fans a hard war against him. For
neither difficulty of affairs, nor changes of times, nor despites of those in
power, nor snowstorms of plots, nor a crowd of disasters, nor the refuse of
all the evils among men being gathered will be able to shake even


a little the noble and sober and wakeful man, just as again the listless and
collapsed and betrayed from home, even if countless treatments are brought on,
they make nothing better. This then also the parable about those men intimated
to us, of whom one built his house upon the rock, and the other upon the sand;
not in order that we should understand sand and rock, nor a building of stones
and roofs, nor rivers and rain and wild winds falling upon the buildings, but
in order that from these we should take virtue and vice and see from this too
that no one injures the man who does not injure himself. Therefore neither the
rain, though it was borne vehemently, nor the rivers falling on with much
rush, nor the wild winds throwing themselves in with a vehement force, shook
that house at all; but it remained both unconquered and unmoved, in order that
you may learn that no temptation can shake the one who does not betray
himself. And the house of the other was borne down easily, not because of the
assault of the temptations (for otherwise the other also would have suffered
the same) but because of its own folly. For it did not fall because a wind
blew upon it, but because it had been built upon the sand, that is upon
listlessness and wickedness, it suffered this. For even before that storm
attacked, it was both weak and ready to fall. For such buildings, even when no
one troubles them, are borne down of themselves, the very foundation being
drawn from under and flowing apart; and as spider-webs are torn of themselves,
when no one troubles them, and adamant even when struck remains undissolved,
so also those who do not injure themselves, even if they are struck by
countless, become stronger; and those who betray themselves, even if there is
no one troubling, are borne of themselves and flow apart and perish; as also
Judas, not only when no such temptation troubled, but even having enjoyed much
treatment. 13 Do you wish me to show you this argument also shining through
upon a whole people? Of how much providence did the people of the Jews enjoy?
was not all the visible creation prepared toward their service? and was some
new and strange manner of a way of life introduced for them? For they did not
even throw themselves into the market and so enjoy the wares, not laying down
silver, nor did they cut furrows, nor drag a plow, nor tear the earth, nor
cast down seeds, nor need rains and the seasons of the year, not a sun-ray,
not a moon's course, not a nature of air, nor any other of such things; they
did not prepare a threshing-floor, they did not rub fruit, they did not need
winds toward separating the wheat from the chaff, they did not turn a mill,
they did not build an oven, they did not bring wood and fire into the house,
they did not need the baker's art, they did not handle a mattock, they did not
sharpen a sickle; they did not need any other art, weaving and building and
that which furnishes sandals, but all things were for them the word of God and
they had a table made ready, and freed from sweats and toils. For such is the
nature of the manna: both fresh and recent and nowhere furnishing them
troubles, nor stretching them with toil; and their garments also and their
sandals, and the very nature of the body, forgot its own weakness. For in so
long a time, neither were those worn, nor were their feet, though they walked
so many things, made callous. Of physicians and drugs and the other zeal about
such an art there was not even some memory among them. So all sickness was out
of the way. For he led them out with silver and gold, it says, and there was
not in their tribes one who was weak. But as having left this world and
migrated to another better inhabited world, so they ate, so they drank, and
neither did a ray becoming hotter strike their heads (for a cloud walled off
the flame, being raised from every side and becoming a portable roof for all
those peoples) nor at night did they need a lamp dividing the darkness, but
there was


for them the pillar of fire a fountain of unspeakable light, furnishing two
needs, both that from shining and that from directing the journey for them.
For it was not only light-like, but also more exactly than any guide it led by
the hand that infinite people according to that mainland. And they walked, not
upon earth only, but also upon the sea, as upon earth, and they dared the
bounds of nature, going on foot over that hard deep, as journeying through
some firm and resisting rock. And when it was spread under them, the element
imitated firm earth and level plains and fields, and when it received the
enemies it worked the things of the sea, and for them it became a vehicle, and
for those warring against them a tomb, sending the ones through with ease, and
drowning the others with much vehemence; and the unordered rush of the waters
displayed both good order and obedience of rational men, now holding the rank
of a guard, now of an executioner, and in one day showing the opposites all at
once. What would one say of the rocks which sent forth rivers of waters? and
what of the clouds of birds which hid the whole earth with the multitude of
their bodies? what of the wonders in Egypt; what of the paradoxes in the
desert? what of the trophies and the victories that happened without
bloodshed? For as dancing, not as warring, they overcame those who fell
against them. And of their own masters they prevailed without weapons; and of
those who fought them after Egypt, trumpeting and singing psalms they so got
the better. And what happened was a dance rather than a war, a mystic
initiation rather than a battle. For all these marvels happened not only for
this, that it might fulfill the need for them, but also that they might keep
more exactly the teaching of the knowledge of God which Moses placed in them;
and voices were borne from every side proclaiming the Master. For the sea also
cried these things, now being walked on foot, now becoming sea, and the Nile
streams sent forth this voice, being changed toward a nature of blood. And the
frogs and that army of locusts and the caterpillar and the rust conversed
these things with the whole people; and the marvels upon the desert, the
manna, the pillar, the cloud, the quail, all the other things, became for them
in place of a book and of letters never wiped out, furnishing them each day a
ringing memory and sounding in their mind. But nevertheless after so much and
so great providence, after those unspeakable benefactions, after the magnitude
of those wonders, after the unutterable care, after the continual teaching,
after the instruction from the words, after the exhortation from the deeds,
after the splendid victories, after the paradoxical trophies, after the
abundance of the tables, after the plenty of those streams, after the
unspeakable glory which they had thrown around themselves among every race of
men, since they were thankless and unfeeling, they worshiped a calf and served
a head of an ox and sought gods to be made for them, having fresh the
reminders of the benefactions of God in Egypt, and still enjoying many other
things also.

14 And the people of the Ninevites, being barbarian and of another tribe,
having shared none of these, not small, not great, not words, not wonders, not
works, not sayings, when they saw a man saved from a shipwreck, who had never
been with them before, but then first seen, having entered and said: Yet three
days and Nineveh shall be overthrown, so from these mere words they were
changed and became better and, having put off the former wickedness, they
traveled toward virtue through repentance, so as to call back a sentence of
God and to make a shaking city stand and to beat off a god-sent wrath and to
be freed from all wickedness. For God saw, it says, that each stood off from
his way, the wicked one. How did they stand off? tell me; and yet their
wickedness was great, their evil unspeakable, the


sores hard to heal. And making this clear the prophet said: Their wickedness
went up until heaven, showing by the interval of the place the magnitude of
their wickedness. But nevertheless so great an evil, so crowned and raised as
even to reach until heaven, this in three days, in a brief turn of time, from
a few words which they heard from one man, unknown, a stranger, who had become
a shipwrecked man, they so dissolved, they so made vanish, they so made to be
out of the way, as to enjoy such a voice, that God saw that each stood off
from their way, the wicked one, and he repented of the wickedness which God
said to do to them. Do you see that the sober and wakeful man is not only
injured in nothing by men, but even overturns a god-sent wrath? and the one
who from home betrays himself and harms himself, even if he enjoys countless
benefactions, gains nothing great? Therefore neither did so many signs benefit
those, nor did the not sharing in these harm these themselves; but since from
home they were well-minded, having taken hold of a small turn they became
better, though being barbarians and of another tribe and unhearing of all the

15 And what of the three children, tell me, did the evils that came on ruin
their virtue? being young and altogether young, in an untimely age did they
not endure that hard captivity and journey that long journey and, having come
into the foreign land, were they not separated from fatherland and house and
temple and altar and sacrifices and offerings and drink-offerings and even
from the very singing of psalms? For not only was the house impassable for
them, but also many forms of the worship from this. Were they not given over
to barbarian hands and to wolves rather than to men, and what is hardest,
having been settled into a land lying so far and barbarian and by a most hard
law of captivity, they had no teacher, no prophet, no ruler? For there is not,
it says, a ruler, nor a prophet and a leader, nor a place to bear fruit before
you and to find mercy. But also into the royal house, as onto some reef and
precipice and a deep full of underwater rocks and crags, they were so led in,
without a steersman and a prow-man and sailors and sails being forced to sail
that hard sea and, as in a prison, shut up in the royal courts. For since they
knew how to philosophize and were higher than the affairs of this life, and
they trampled all the human puffing, and they worked their wing light, they
thought even the pastime there to be an addition of evils. For being outside
and in a private house, they were going to enjoy more liberty; but having been
led into that prison (for they thought all that show to be of a prison, and
not even better than precipices and reefs) they immediately endured a hard
difficulty; For the king commanded them to be sharers of his own table, that
Sybaritic and unclean and profane one, but this was forbidden to them, and it
seemed to be harder than death; and they alone, as lambs among wolves, were
shut up among so many. And it was necessary to melt with hunger, rather even
to be led toward death, or to taste of the forbidden foods. What then do the
young men do, those in orphanhood, the captives, the strangers, the slaves of
those commanding these things? They did not think the necessity enough for
them toward apology, nor the tyranny of the one who held the city, but they
devise and transact all things, so as to flee the sin, though betrayed from
every side. For they could not persuade with money; for how, the captives? not
with friendship and company; for how, the strangers? not to get the better by
power; for how, the slaves? not to prevail by multitude; for how, the three
alone? Having come forward then they persuade that eunuch with words, the one
who had this authority. For since they found him very fearful and trembling
and struggling about his own salvation, and the fear was unendurable, the one


about death shaking his soul. For I fear, he says, my lord the king, lest he
see your faces downcast above the children of your age and you condemn my head
to the king. Having freed him from this dread they persuade him to give the
favor. And since they contributed all the things from themselves, God also
then contributed the things from himself. For the achievement in these things
which they were going to receive was not of God only, but the beginning and
the prefaces were of their mind, which, having furnished noble and manly, they
drew the turn of God and brought to an end what they were eager for.

to harm? see at any rate youth and captivity and orphanhood and leading away

poverty and fewness and the being in the midst of barbarians and the having
enemies as masters and the having been given over into the very royal hands
and the separation from all their own and alienation from priests and prophets
and the others who cared for them and idleness of drink-offerings and
sacrifices and taking away of temple and psalms, and none of these harmed
them; but then they were in better repute more greatly than when they enjoyed
these things upon their own fatherland. And having accomplished this first
contest and having bound on a splendid crown, and having kept the law even in
a foreign land and having trampled a tyrannical command and having conquered
fear of the accursed one and having been harmed in nothing from nowhere; as
sitting at home and enjoying all those things, having so accomplished the work
with license, they were called again to other lists; and again they were the
same. And a contest harder than the former was set for them and a furnace was
kindled and a barbarian army was arrayed against them with the king and all
the Persian force was moved and all things were practiced toward their
deception and force, and various forms of music and varied manners of
punishments and threats and a look fearful from every side and words more
fearful than a look; But nevertheless since they did not betray themselves,
but contributed all the things from themselves, they were never harmed in
anything, but they even bound on crowns more splendid than the former. For
Nebuchadnezzar bound them and threw them into the furnace, yet he did not harm
them, but even benefited them more greatly and made them more glorious. And
having neither a temple (for again I will say the same things) nor an altar,
not a fatherland, not priests, not prophets, in a foreign and barbarian

song, which from that time until now is sung everywhere in the inhabited world
and will also be perceived into the generations after these. Thus then,
whenever no one injures himself, he will be able to be harmed in nothing by
another (for I will not cease continually singing this saying) for if
captivity and slavery and isolation and loss of fatherland and of all one's
own and death and burning and so great an army and a tyrant so cruel, were not
able to ruin three young children, captives, slaves, strangers, being upon a
foreign land, in anything toward their own virtue, but the plot even became a

there is nothing, even if he has the whole inhabited world warring against
him. But God then, he says, stood by them and snatched them out of the flame.

17 Yet I do not admire those children and call them blessed and say they are

they became stronger, but because they were bound for the true doctrines and
were thrown into a furnace and were given over to the fire. For here the whole
trophy was completed for them and, together with their being brought down into
the furnace, a crown was set on; and from those words it began to be plaited
