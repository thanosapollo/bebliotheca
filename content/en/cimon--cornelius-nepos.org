* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Cimon, son of Miltiades, an Athenian, used a quite hard beginning of youth. For
when his father had not been able to pay the people an estimated lawsuit and for
that cause had died in public chains, Cimon was held by the same custody and by
the laws of the Athenians could not be let out unless he had paid the money with
which the father had been fined. And he had in marriage his own full sister,
named Elpinice, led not more by love than by custom: for it is permitted to
Athenians to take as wives those born of the same father. Desirous of this
marriage a certain Callias, not so well-born as moneyed, who had made great
moneys from mines, treated with Cimon that he give her to him as wife: if he
obtained that, he would pay the money for him. When he scorned such a condition,
Elpinice denied she would suffer the progeny of Miltiades to perish in public
chains, since she could prevent it, and that she would marry Callias, if he
furnished what he promised.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Freed from custody in such a way Cimon quickly came to the first place. For he
had enough eloquence, the highest liberality, great prudence both of civil right
and of military affairs, because he had been versed with his father from a boy
in armies. Therefore this man both held the city people in his power and
prevailed most by authority with the army. First as commander at the river
Strymon he put to flight great forces of Thracians, he established the town
Amphipolis and sent there ten thousand Athenians into a colony. The same man
again as commander at Mycalene took a conquered fleet of two hundred ships of
Cyprians and Phoenicians and on the same day used an equal fortune on land. For
the ships of the enemy having been captured he immediately led his forces out of
the fleet and in one running-together laid low a greatest force of barbarians.
Having obtained great prey by which victory, when he was returning home, because
already some islands had defected because of bitterness of command, he confirmed
those well-minded, he forced the alienated to return to duty. Scyros, which at
that time the Dolopes inhabited, because they had borne themselves more
stubbornly, he emptied, he threw the old possessors from city and island, he
divided the fields to citizens. He broke the Thasians, relying on opulence, by
his arrival. From these spoils the citadel of Athens, where it turns toward the
south, was adorned.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
With which things when he alone most flourished in the state, he fell into the
same envy which his father and the rest of the Athenian first men: for by votes
of potsherds, which they call ostracism, he was punished with an exile of ten
years. Of which deed the Athenians repented more quickly than he himself. For
when he with a strong spirit had yielded to the envy of ungrateful citizens and
the Lacedaemonians had declared war on the Athenians, immediately a desire of
his known virtue followed. Therefore after the fifth year from when he had been
expelled, he was recalled into the fatherland. He, because he used the
hospitality of the Lacedaemonians, thinking it better that the cities of Greece
dispute concerning their controversies among themselves by right than contend
with arms, set out of his own will to Lacedaemon and conciliated peace between
the two most powerful cities. After, and not so much, sent as commander to
Cyprus with two hundred ships, when he had conquered the greater part of the
island, entangled in a disease he died in attacking the town Citium.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
This man the Athenians long desired not only in war but also in peace. For he
was of such liberality, although he had estates and gardens in several places,
that he never set a guard in them for the sake of keeping the fruits, lest
anyone be hindered from enjoying those things with which each wished. Always
foot-followers with coins followed him, so that if anyone needed his help he
would have what he would give at once, lest by delaying he seem to deny. Often,
when he saw someone offended by fortune less well clothed, he gave his own
cloak. Daily a dinner was so cooked for him that whom he had seen uninvited in
the forum, he would call all to himself, which he did not pass over doing on any
day. To none his faith, to none his work, to none his household thing was
wanting: he enriched many, several poor dead, who had not left whence they might
be carried out, he carried out at his own expense. By so bearing himself it is
least to be wondered if both his life was secure and his death bitter.
