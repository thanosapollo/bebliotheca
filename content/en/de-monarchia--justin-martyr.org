Of Saint Justin the Philosopher and Martyr
On the Monarchy.
Human nature at the beginning received a union of understanding and salvation
toward knowledge of truth and of the worship due to the one Lord of all; but
envy, having slipped in, turned the excess of human greatness toward the
making of idols, and the superfluous custom, remaining for a long time, hands
down the error to the many as if it were native and true. It is the work of a
lover of man, or rather of a lover of God, to remind those who have neglected
what they ought to know. For the truth was of itself sufficient to show, from
the things contained under the pole, the order of Him who created them; but
forgetfulness, through the long-suffering of God, having taken hold of the
mind of men, acted fraudulently, transferring to mortals the name that befits
the only true God. And through a few the many had a spread of wickedness,
being darkened as to the knowledge of what is firm and unchangeable by popular
custom; for those who at the beginning, in honor of those who excelled,
performed rites and services, threw forgetfulness of the universal glory upon
those after them. But I, as I undertook a little before, using a God-loving
mind, will use a man-loving voice, and I set it before those who have sense,
that it is necessary for all who have made use of the administration of the
universe to keep an unchangeable worship toward the Knower of all. And this I
will not speak adorning it with words, but using throughout the demonstration
from the ancient poetry of Greek history, from the writings given in common to
all; for from those writings from which the very men who handed down the law
of the worship of idols to the many took it, having learned from them, they
will be convicted as ignorant of mind by the poets and lyric writers among
them.
First, then, Aeschylus, having set out the arrangement of the words belonging
to himself, also brought forth the voice concerning the only God, as he says:
Separate God from mortals, and do not think
That He, like yourself, is established in flesh.
You do not know Him; at times He appears as fire,
An unapproachable rush, at times as water, at times as gloom;
And He Himself becomes like to beasts,
To wind, and cloud, and lightning, thunder, rain.
Sea and rocks serve Him
And every spring and gatherings of water;
And mountains tremble, and earth, and the huge
Depth of the sea, and the great height of the hills,
When the master's grim eye looks upon them.
For He can do all things; and this is the glory of God Most High.
And not only was this man initiated into the knowledge concerning God, but
Sophocles also recounts the order of the only Maker of the universe and of one
God thus:
There is one God in truth, there is one God,
Who made heaven and the broad earth,
And the bright swell of the sea and the force of winds.
But we many mortals, wandering in heart,
Have set up consolations of our woes,
Images of gods from stones or bronze,
Or figures of gold-wrought or ivory;
And making sacrifices to these and fair festivals,
We think that we are pious in this way.
But Philemon also, having been well furnished to speak the ancient things,
shares in the knowledge concerning the things that are, as he writes:
And what God, tell me, must we think there is?
The one who sees all things and is Himself not seen.
And Orpheus also will bear witness for me, he who introduced the three hundred
and sixty gods, in the book entitled Testaments, when he appears to repent of
this from the things he writes:
Musaeus! For I will speak true things; and let not the former things
That appeared in your breast rob you of the dear life.
But looking to the divine Word, attend upon it,
Steering the mind's vessel of the heart, and well tread
The path, and look only upon the lord of the cosmos.
He is one, self-begotten; all things have been made as offspring of one;
And He Himself is present among them, and none of mortals
Looks upon Him, but He Himself sees all.
This one from good gives evil to mortals
And chilling war and tearful pains.
And there is no other apart from the great lord.
But I do not see Him; for a cloud is fixed around.
For all mortals have mortal pupils in their eyes,
And they are weak to see Zeus who rules over all.
For He is fixed upon the brazen heaven
On a golden throne, and with His feet He has stepped upon the earth
And His right hand to the bound of ocean
He has stretched out from every side; for around Him tremble the long
mountains
And rivers and the depth of the grey bright sea.
And he speaks these things thus, as having become an eyewitness of the
greatness of God. And Pythagoras also shares with him in the things he writes:
If anyone shall say, I am God, besides the One, this one ought,
Having set up a cosmos equal to this, to say, This is mine;
And not only having set it up to say, Mine, but to dwell
Himself in what he has made; but it has been made by this One.
And concerning this, that He alone is able both to institute judgment of the
deeds accomplished in life and of the ignorance concerning the divine, I have
kindred witnesses to set forth; and first Sophocles, speaking concerning this
also:
For there shall be, there shall be that time of ages,
When the gold-faced aether shall let loose a treasure full of fire;
And the flame, having been fed,
Shall burn all things on earth and in the air,
Raging. And when the whole has failed,
Gone shall be every depth of waves,
And earth empty of seats, nor shall the air still
Bear the winged tribes, being burned.
For we hold that there are two paths in Hades,
One of the just and the other of the unjust.
And then He shall save all that He destroyed before.
But Philemon also again:
Do you think, Nicostratus, that the dead,
Who shared in every luxury in life,
The earth will also cover, as if for all time
They have fled the divine, as having escaped notice?
There is an eye of Justice, which sees all things.
For if the just and the impious shall have one lot,
Go, plunder, steal, defraud, stir up.
Do not be deceived at all; there is judgment even in Hades,
Which God the master of all shall make,
Whose name is fearful, nor would I even name it.
And Euripides:
He gives an ungrudging length of life toward judgment.
And whoever of mortals thinks that, doing some evil
Day by day, he has escaped the notice of God,
Thinks wicked things, and thinking so is caught,
Whenever Justice happens to have leisure.
See, as many of you as think there is no God,
Sinning twice, not reasonably;
For there is, there is. And if anyone does well,
Being wicked by nature, let him gain the time;
For in time this one later will pay the penalty.
And that God is not won over by libation and incense of evildoers, but assigns
the punishments to each in uprightness, Philemon again will bear witness for
me:
If anyone bringing a sacrifice, O Pamphilus,
Some multitude of bulls or of kids or, by Zeus,
Of other such things, or fashioned works,
Having made golden cloaks or purple,
Or figures through ivory or emerald,
Thinks that God has been made well-disposed,
That man is deceived and has light wits.
For the man ought to be useful,
Not corrupting maidens and committing adultery,
Stealing and slaying for the sake of money,
Looking at the things of others, and desiring
Either a costly woman or a house
Or a possession, and simply a boy or a girl,
Horses, oxen, in short, or cattle. Why then?
Do not even desire one stitch of a needle, Pamphilus;
For God sees you, being present near,
Who delights in just deeds and not in unjust;
And He lets the one who toils raise his own life,
Plowing the earth night and day.
But sacrifice to God being just throughout,
Not being splendid in cloaks as in the heart.
Having heard thunder do not flee far at all,
Yourself being conscious of nothing against yourself, master;
For God sees you, being present near.
And again Plato in the Timaeus: But if anyone, examining these things in deed,
should take a test, he would be ignorant of the difference of human and divine
nature, that God mixes all things into one, knowing sufficiently and being
able at once, but no one of men is sufficient for either of these, neither is
now nor shall ever be hereafter.
And concerning those who seem among some to share in the holy and perfect
name, which some have carried off by a vain tradition as gods, Menander in the
Charioteer says:
No god walking outside with an old woman
Pleases me, nor one slipping into houses
Upon the board. The just god ought
To remain at home, saving those who have been established.
The same Menander in the Priestess:
No god through a man saves, woman,
One through another; for if a man draws some god
With cymbals wherever he wishes,
The one doing this is greater than the god.
But these are tools of daring and of livelihood,
Invented for shameless men, Rhode,
Fashioned for a laughing-stock to life.
And in the Hated Man again, setting forth the opinions concerning those taken
as gods, or rather convicting them as not being so, the same Menander:
For if I should see this, I would even take
My soul again; for now; but where is it possible
To find gods so just, O Getas?
And in the Deposit:
There is an unjust judgment, as it seems, even among gods.
And Euripides the tragedian in Orestes:
Phoebus, having commanded me to exact the murder of my mother,
Being more ignorant of the noble and of justice.
We are slaves to gods, whatever gods are.
And do you see Apollo, who, dwelling in the mid-navel seats,
Gives to mortals a mouth most clear?
Whom we obey in all that he may say,
Having obeyed this one I killed her who bore me.
Count him unholy and kill him;
He sinned, not I. What must I do?
Or is the god not worthy, when I refer it to him,
To save me from the pollution.
The same also in Hippolytus:
But the gods do not judge these things rightly.
And in Ion:
But what care have I of the daughter of Erechtheus?
Nothing belongs to me. But with golden
Pitchers, having come to the sprinkling-places,
I will let down the dew. But I must admonish
Phoebus. Why does he do this, marrying maidens by force?
He betrays the children he begets in secret,
He neglects them when they are dying. Do not you; but since you have power,
Pursue virtues. For whoever of mortals
Has been born wicked, the gods punish.
How then is it just that you, having written the laws for mortals,
Yourselves be liable for injustices?
But if you are not present, I will use the word:
You will give penalties to men for forced marriages,
You and Poseidon and Zeus, who rules heaven,
Paying for injustice, you will empty the temples.
For hastening pleasures beyond forethought
You do injustice. It is no longer just to call men wicked,
If we imitate the fair things of the gods,
But rather those who taught these things.
And in Archelaus:
In many things, O child, the gods make men go astray.
And in Bellerophon:
If gods do anything base, they are not gods.
And again in the same:
Does someone say there are gods in heaven then?
They are not, they are not; if any of men says so,
Let him not, being a fool, use the old story.
But look at the things themselves, not having your judgment
Upon my words. I say that tyranny
Kills very many and strips them of possessions
And, breaking oaths, sacks cities;
And those doing these things are more fortunate
Than those living piously in quiet day by day.
And I know small cities that honor the gods,
Which hear greater, more impious ones,
Being mastered by a greater number of spears.
And I think that you, if someone idle should pray to the gods
And not gather a living with his hand,
Would see evil misfortunes make the divine things tower up.
And Menander in Diphilus:
Because one must honor only him who is always lord of all
And father, throughout,
The finder and founder of so many good things.
The same in the Fishermen:
For that which nourishes me, this I judge to be god.
But that which is accustomed to nourish does not need the supply from the one
in need.
The same in the Brothers:
God is always to the good
For the mind, as it seems, O wisest ones.
And in the Flute-Girls:
All things are sacred to the fair word;
For the mind is the god that will speak.
In Phrixus the tragedian:
But if being pious I did the same things
With the most impious, how would this be well?
Or does Zeus the best think nothing just?
In Philoctetes:
And you see how even among gods it is fair to gain,
And he is admired who has the most gold in temples;
What then hinders you too from taking
Gain, when it is possible even to be made like to gods?
In Hecuba:
Zeus, whoever you are, Zeus; for I do not know except by word.
And:
Zeus, whether you are necessity of nature or mind of mortals,
I have prayed to you!
Here then is a proof of virtue and of a mind that loves understanding, to run
back to the communion of the pairing and to attach oneself to understanding
for salvation and to choose the better things according to the free-will laid
upon man, not thinking those with human passions to be lords of the universe,
since they will not even appear to have equal power with men. For in Homer
Demodocus says he is self-taught:
But a god threw the paths of song into me,
being a mortal. Asclepius and Apollo are taught to heal by Chiron the Centaur,
the newest thing, gods by a man. For why must I go through Dionysus, whom the
poet says is mad, or Heracles, whom he also calls wretched? And why must I
proclaim Ares and Aphrodite, the leaders of adultery, and from all these bring
the judgment upon the things set forth? For if someone, not having learned,
should imitate the so-called divine deeds, even among the counterfeit he would
be reckoned a stranger to life and to humanity; but if someone knowing does
so, he will have a reasonable escape from punishments, showing that imitation
of the divine daring is not lawlessness. But if then someone should blame the
deeds, he will also take away the names known from them and will not cover
them with artful and glib speeches. And one must welcome the true and
unchangeable name, proclaimed not through my voice only but also through those
who introduced us to the beginning of education, in order that we may not,
having finished idly the time of living here, render the accounts not only as
ignorant of the heavenly glory but also as ungrateful to the Judge.
