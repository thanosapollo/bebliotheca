Of Proclus, from the same Chaldean philosophy
1 The courts of the divine beings and their dwellings are the everlasting
orders. And the "all-receiving court" of the Father is the paternal order,
which receives and holds together all the souls that have been led up; but
how does the portion of the angels lead a soul up? By shining, he says,
upon the soul with fire, that is, by illuminating it from every side, and
making it full of the unsullied fire which imparts to it an unswerving
order and a power through which it is not whirled out into material
disorder but is joined to the light of the divine beings; and it also holds
it together in its proper place, and makes it unmixed with matter, by
lightening it with the warm spirit and making it aloft through the
uplifting life; for the warm spirit is a communication of life. And
everything that hastens toward the place above is lightened, just as what
is carried toward matter is weighed down. And the end of the ascents is
participation in the divine fruits and the self-shining fulfillment of the
fire, which is the vision of God, as setting the soul before the eyes of
the Father. And the soul is made a hymn-singer of the divine beings,
according to the oracle, by putting forth the ineffable tokens of the
Father and offering them to the Father, which the Father placed in it at
the first procession of its substance. For such are the intellective and
unseen hymns of the soul that is being led up, stirring the memory of the
harmonic reasons which bear ineffable images of the divine powers in it.
2 Of Proclus, from the same philosophy. By "depth of the soul" he means
its triple cognitive powers, intellective, discursive, opinative; and by
"all eyes," their triple cognitive activities. For the eye is a symbol of
knowledge; and life, of appetite; and each is triple. And the earth from
which one must lighten the heart is all that is material and the varied
things carried about in becoming, and every bodily imprint; upon which
follow a vision of the paternal monad, an unsullied gladness toward it, and
stability from this intellective vantage-point; from which it is clear that
our good is mixed, from both motion and the gladness connatural with it.
For every life, having its own activity unimpeded, has received pleasure
yoked together with it. And a hymn of the Father is not composite words,
nor a construction of works; for being alone incorruptible, he does not
receive a corruptible hymn; let us not then hope to persuade the master of
true words by an empty storm of phrases, nor by a show of works beautified
with art; God loves unadorned beauty. Let us therefore offer this hymn to
God: let us leave the flowing substance; let us come to the true goal, the
assimilation to him; let us come to know the master, let us love the
Father; let us obey him who calls; let us run toward the warm, having fled
the cold; let us become fire, let us travel through fire. We have an
unimpeded road for the ascent; the Father guides, having unfolded the paths
of fire; let us not flow as a lowly stream out of forgetfulness.
3 Of Proclus. The root of vice is the body, just as intellect is the root
of virtue. For the latter sprouts from above for souls, while the former
bursts in from the worse things and from below; and to cast it down to
earth is to cut it away from ourselves; and to let it be carried where it
was stationed; and it has been stationed in the whole of becoming. And
since evils are here and "necessarily roam about this place," and our body
too is a part of becoming, it is possible to make a part free from evil,
but the whole of becoming, impossible, unless we should also take away its
being; and into it both jealousy and envy must be cast down, from where it
collected them; for being material they have matter as their nurse; and
"do not quench with the mind" has been said with a view to shutting out,
not to annihilation, just as things quenched in something are wholly
contained in that thing and fill it with their own heat; and instead of
quenching, cast it down, not keeping it shut up within yourself; therefore
he adds: "Do not pollute the spirit," by having it within and hiding it.
And envy is material; for it is a housemate of the privation of goods, and
privation has its being together with barren matter; but the theurgic race
is without envy and stretches itself up toward zeal for the goodness of
God, and is not dragged down into contentions of men and hostilities. And
these passions, when they are shut up in souls, imprint in the spirit some
enmattered vice and fill it with material privation and lifelessness.
4 Of the same, from the same philosophy. When the soul stands according
to its own discursive faculty, it is a knower of beings; and when it has
established itself in the intellective [level] of its own substance, it
thinks all things by simple and undivided apprehensions. And when it has
run back to the One, and has folded together all the multiplicity in
itself, it acts in an inspired way and is joined to the existences above
intellect; for like is everywhere naturally joined to like, and every
knowledge through likeness binds the knower to the known, the perceptive to
the perceptible, the discursive to the discursively thinkable, and the
intellective to the intelligible, so that also the flower of intellect [is
bound] to that which is before intellect. For as in the other things
intellect is not the highest, but that which is above intellect, so also
in souls the first form of activity is not intellective, but more divine
than intellect; and every soul and every intellect has double activities,
some one-like and superior to intellection, and others intellective.
Therefore one must think that intelligible even according to the very thing
present in it and according to existence, having shut the eyes in respect
of all the other lives and powers. For as we approach intellect by becoming
intellect-like, so we run back to union by becoming one-like, standing at
the summit of our own intellect; since the eye too does not otherwise see
the sun than by becoming sun-like, and not by the light from fire; from
which it is also clear that to think that is not to think. "But if," he
says, "you incline your intellect," that is, if you press with intellective
apprehensions toward conjunction with that, and thus "think that"
intelligible "as thinking something," that is, according to some measure of
form and knowledge by way of apprehension, "you will not think that"; for
even if such intellections are simple, they fall short of the unitary
simplicity of the intelligible and are carried to certain secondary
intellective <natures> that have already proceeded into multiplicity. For
nothing knowable is known through a lesser knowledge; therefore neither is
that which is above intellect [known] through intellect; for as soon as
intellect applies itself to something it also says that the object thought
is of such a kind, which is secondary to the intelligible; but if we think
this intelligible in the flower of the intellect in us, established at the
summit of the first intelligible triad, by what then would one still be
joined to the One, which is uncoordinated with all things and
unparticipated? For if the first "Father" is said to "snatch himself" from
intellect and from "power," who is the one who did not even need thus to
snatch himself, but is snatched beyond all things simply and is hymned as
God of all? And if it has also been said elsewhere concerning the very
first Father: "and first power of the sacred word," who is the one above
this, of whom this one, participating, is called sacred? And if that which
reveals what is more ineffable is named word, there must before the word be
the silence that gave subsistence to the word, and before every sacred
thing the deifying cause. As, then, the things after the intelligibles are
words of the intelligibles, which are gathered together, so the word in
those, having received subsistence from another more ineffable henad, is a
word of the silence before the intelligibles, but of the intelligibles when
they are kept silent, a silence. Perhaps, then, the flower of intellect and
the flower of our whole soul are not the same; but the one is the most
one-like [part] of our intellective life, and the other is the one of all
the psychic powers, which are many-formed; for we are not intellect only,
but also discursive thought and opinion and attention and choice, and
before these powers a substance that is both one and many and both
partible and impartible. And the one having appeared as double, and one
being the flower of our first powers, <and the other> the center of the
whole substance and of all the various powers about it, that one alone
joins us to the father of the intelligibles; for it is an intellective one,
and that too is thought by the paternal intellect according to the one in
it; but the one into which all the soul's powers converge is alone
naturally able to lead us to that which is beyond all beings, itself being
the unifier of all that is in us; according to which we have also been
rooted in substance in that, and by being rooted, even if we proceed forth,
we shall not stand apart from our own cause.
5 Of Proclus, from the same philosophy. Philosophy makes forgetfulness
and recollection of the everlasting reasons the causes both of the
departure from the gods and of the turning back toward them; but the
oracles, of the paternal tokens. And both agree; for the soul is composed
of the sacred reasons and of the divine symbols; of which the former are
from the intellective forms, and the latter from the divine henads; and we
are images of the intellective substances, but statues of the unknown
tokens. And just as every soul is a fullness of all the forms, but has its
subsistence wholly according to one cause, so also it participates in all
the tokens through which it is joined to God, but its existence is defined
in one, according to which all the multiplicity in it is gathered into one
summit. For this too must be known, that every soul differs from every
[soul] in form, and as many as the souls are, so many also are the forms of
souls; for first, according to one form, there is a subsistence of many
form-unified individuals both around matter and the composites of beings, a
single underlying nature variously participating in the same form; for if
the being of the soul is a reason and a simple form, either one soul will
differ in no way from another in substance, or it would differ in form; for
it will differ only in what it is, and it is form only. Whence it is clear
that every soul, even if it is full of the same reasons, has nevertheless
received one form that distinguishes it from the others, just as the solar
form characterizes the solar soul, and another another.
