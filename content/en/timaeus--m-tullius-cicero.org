* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I have written much, even in the Academics, against the natural philosophers,
and I have often argued with Publius Nigidius in the manner and method of
Carneades. For that man was furnished with all the other arts that are worthy
of a free man, and was besides a keen and careful inquirer into those things
that nature seems to have wrapped in secrecy. In short I judge that after
those famous Pythagoreans, whose school in a way died out after it had
flourished for some centuries in Italy and Sicily, he arose to renew it.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

When he had waited for me at Ephesus as I was setting out for Cilicia, himself
returning from an embassy to Rome, and Cratippus had come there from Mytilene
to greet and see me, in my judgment easily the first of all the Peripatetics I
have heard, I was very glad both to see Nigidius and to come to know
Cratippus. And at first we spent the time of greeting in inquiry * * *

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

What is that which always is and has no origin, and what is that which comes
to be and never is? Of these the one is grasped by intelligence and reason,
being always one and the same; the other, which sensation without reason
brings to opinion, being wholly a matter of opinion, comes to be and perishes
and can never truly be. Everything that comes to be must come to be from some
cause; for with the cause removed the origin of nothing can be found.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Therefore if he who sets about to accomplish some work looks to that form
which is always the same, and sets that before himself as a pattern, he must
produce a noble work; but if he looks to that which comes to be, he will never
attain the beauty he seeks. All this heaven, then, or world, or whatever other
name it likes, let it now be named by us

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

concerning which let us first consider what must be considered at the outset
in every inquiry: whether it has always been, generated by no origin, or
whether it came to be from some beginning in time. It came to be, since it is
seen and touched and is embodied on every side. All such things move the
senses, and what moves the senses settles in opinion; and we have said that
these have origin and come to be, and that nothing can come to be without
causes.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And to find him who is as it were the parent of this universe is difficult,
and when you have found him, to declare him to the crowd is not lawful. So we
must consider again whether that craftsman of this great work imitated a
pattern that is always one and the same and like itself, or that which we call
generated and originated. But if this world is beautiful and its maker good,
he surely preferred to imitate the form of eternity; if otherwise, which it is
not even lawful to say, he followed a generated model instead of an eternal
one.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

There is no doubt, then, that he preferred to pursue eternity, since nothing
is more beautiful than the world and nothing more excellent than its builder.
Thus it was generated and made toward that which is grasped by reason and
wisdom and is held in unchanging eternity. From which it follows that this
world we see must be an eternal image of something eternal. But in every
search of reason the beginning is the hardest. Of the things we have said,
then, let this be the first distinction.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Every discourse seems to be akin to the things it unfolds. So when it treats
of a thing stable and unchanging, let it be such as cannot be refuted or
convicted. But when it has entered upon imitated and fashioned images, it
thinks it does well if it attains a likeness of the truth. For as eternity
stands to that which has come to be, so truth stands to belief. Therefore if,
discussing the nature of the gods and the origin of the world, we fall short
of what we desire in our mind, so that the whole discourse, clearly and fully
wrought, should be consistent and agree with itself in every part, it will
hardly be surprising, and you ought to be content if probable things are said.
For it is fair to remember that I who discuss am a man, and you who judge are
men, so that if probable things are said you should not ask for anything
beyond.

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Let us then seek the cause that drove him who devised these things to seek an
origin of things and a new construction. Clearly he excelled in goodness; and
the good envies no one; and so he generated all things like himself. This,
surely, is the most just cause of the world's coming to be. For when god had
resolved to fill the world with all goods and to mix in no evil, so far as
nature allowed, whatever there was that fell under the sense of sight he took
to himself, not calm and quiet, but immoderately stirred and flowing, and he
brought it from disorder into order; for he judged this to be better.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

It is not and never was lawful for him who is best to make anything but what
is most beautiful. When he had reasoned, then, he found that of those things
which are perceived by nature, nothing unintelligent is in its whole kind
superior to what is intelligent. Therefore he enclosed intelligence in soul,
and soul in body. So he thought that work would be made most beautiful. For
this reason we must not hesitate to declare, if anything can be tracked by
conjecture, that this world is a living thing, intelligent, and established by
divine providence.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

This granted, we must see what follows: whose likeness among living things god
followed in fashioning the world. None, surely, of the living things known to
us; for they are all partitioned into certain kinds, or begun and in no part
complete; and nothing like what is incomplete and unfinished can be beautiful.
Let us say, then, that the world is like that living thing of which every
living creature is as it were a certain part, whether seen in individuals or
in the whole kind. All living things, then, that are seen by the mind and
understood by reason are comprehended in the embrace of reason and
intelligence, just as men and beasts and all that falls under sight are
comprehended in this world.

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

For since god wished to make a world like that which can be understood as most
beautiful in the nature of things, and which is in every part most complete,
he made one visible living thing in which all living things were contained.
Have we then been right to call the world one, or would it have been better
and truer to speak of more, or of innumerable worlds? One, surely, if it was
made after a pattern. For that which embraces all those living things that are
understood by reason cannot exist together with another. For then there would
have to be another living thing to contain it, whose parts would be the former
living things, and this heaven would be an image of that last one, not of the
nearest. So that this might not happen, and that this world might be most like
the complete living thing by this very fact that it was sole and one, god
created this world unique and only-begotten.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

Everything that has been born must be bodily and visible and likewise
tangible. Further, nothing empty of fire can be seen or looked at, nor can
anything be touched that lacks solidity, and nothing is solid that is without
earth; therefore god, setting about to make the world, first joined earth and
fire. But any two things, to cohere, seek a third something and want as it
were a knot and bond. Of bonds, that is most fitting and most beautiful which
from itself and from the things it binds makes as much as possible one. This
is best achieved by what in Greek is analogia, and in Latin (for one must
dare, since these things are first being made new by us) may be called
comparison or proportion.

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

For when of three numbers or figures or any kinds it happens that as the
first stands to the middle in proportion, so the middle is compared to the
last, and in turn as the last is compared with the middle, so the middle with
the first,
that which is middle then becomes first, then last, and the last and first
become middles; so necessity forces those things which had been separate to be
the same; and when they have been made the same, it follows that all are one.
But if the body of the universe were spread out plane and even, and nothing in
it were † required, one middle placed between would bind both itself and those
between which it was set.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

But since solidity was sought for the world, and all solids are never coupled
by one mean, always by two, it came about that between fire and earth god
placed water and air, and compared them with one another and joined them by
proportion, so that as fire is to air, so air is to water, and as air is to
water, so water is to earth. By this joining the heaven was so fitted that it
falls under sight and touch. And so both for that reason and from these four
things the body of the world was made, bound by the proportion I have spoken
of; from which it embraces itself with a kind of concordant friendship and
love, and coheres so aptly that it cannot be dissolved in any way except by
the same one by whom it was bound.

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Of those four things I mentioned above, all the parts were so placed in the
whole world that no part of this kind should go outside, and that those kinds
in their entirety should be in this universe, for these reasons: first, that
the world as a living thing might be perfect from perfect parts; next, that it
might be one, with no part left from which another might be generated; last,
that no disease or old age might be able to touch it.

* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

For every joining of a body is shaken and broken by the force of heat or cold
or by some violent impulse, and is driven to diseases and old age. This, then,
was the reason of the god who made and built the world: that he might complete
one whole and perfect work from all things whole and perfect, which should be
free of every disease and decay. And he gave it the form most akin and most
becoming. For the living thing by which he wished all the rest of living
things to be contained, he shaped in that form by which alone all other forms
are enclosed, and he fashioned it globular, which the Greeks call
sphairoeides, whose whole extremity is reached by equal radii from the center,
and he turned it so that nothing could be made rounder, that it should have no
roughness, no obstacle, nothing cut with angles, nothing with windings,
nothing projecting, nothing pitted, and all parts most like all, because in
his judgment likeness excelled unlikeness.

* Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

He surrounded the whole figure of the world with smoothness on every side. For
it had no need of eyes, because nothing was left outside that could be seen,
nor of ears, because there was not even anything to be heard, nor was air
poured round the extremities of the world so that it should need breath, nor
did it want food for the body or the removal of finished and consumed food;
for no loss could take place, nor any addition, nor indeed was there anywhere
from which it might come. And so it fed itself by the consumption and decay of
itself, since it both suffered and did all things by itself and from itself.
For so he thought who joined and founded these things, that the world was
content with itself and had no need of another.

* Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

And so he did not attach hands to it, since there was nothing to be taken or
pushed away, nor feet or other limbs by which to support the body's going. For
he gave the heaven that motion which was most fitted to its figure, the one of
the seven motions that most stirs mind and intelligence. And so by one and the
same turning it twists and turns about itself. The other six motions he
separated from it and so freed it from all wandering. For this turning, then,
which did not need feet and stepping, he did not give it limbs for walking.

* Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

This the god who was, thinking of the god sometime to be, made smooth and even
on every side, equal from the middle to the summit, and perfect and complete
from things complete and perfect. And as he placed the soul in the middle of
it, so he stretched it through the whole; then he surrounded it with body and
clothed it from without, and embraced it with a heaven wandering alone,
revolving, driven in a circle, because by its own excellence it could easily
be with itself and would not need another, sufficiently known and familiar to
itself.

* Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

So that eternal god created this perfectly blessed god. But he did not then at
last begin the soul, as we have just said, when he had made the body for it;
for it would not have been right for the greater to obey the lesser; but we
say many things thoughtlessly and at random. God generated the soul older both
in origin and in excellence, and set it as lord and commander over the
obedient body, and he worked it in some such way as this: From that matter
which is undivided and always of one kind and like itself, and from that which
is generated divided in bodies, he mixed a third kind of matter from the two
in the middle, which should be of the nature of the same and of the other, and
he set it between the undivided and that which was divided in body.

* Section 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

When he had taken these three, he tempered them into one form, and he joined
by force that nature which we have called the other with the same, though it
fled and was alien to that coupling; and mixing these with the matter, when
from the three he had made one, he divided that itself into the members that
were fitting. Then he tempered the several parts from the same and from the
other and from the matter. And that division was of this kind: first he took
one part from the whole, then a second double the first, then a third which
should be one and a half times the second and triple the first, then a fourth
which should be double the second, then a fifth which should be triple the
third, then a sixth eight times the first, last a seventh which was
twenty-seven times the first.

* Section 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23
:END:

Then he set about to fill the double and triple intervals, cutting parts again
from the whole; and he placed these in the intervals so that in each there
should be two middles (for I scarcely dare say "means," which the Greeks call
mesotetes; but let it be understood as if I had said so, for it will be
clearer), one of them exceeding the extremes by the same fraction and exceeded
by the same, the other exceeding the extremes by an equal number and exceeded
by an equal number. Then, taking intervals of three to two, four to three, and
nine to eight from these bindings, in the original intervals he filled all the
four-to-three with the nine-to-eight interval, leaving a remainder of each.

* Section 24
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h24
:END:

And of that remaining fraction the number had to the number the same
proportion and comparison in the extremes that two hundred fifty-six has to
two hundred forty-three, and so he had now used up all that mixture from which
he cut these. This whole conjunction, then, he split lengthwise in two and,
fitting middle to middle, crossed it as it were like an X, then twisted it
into a circle, so that they were joined both with themselves and with each
other from the join that was opposite, and by that motion whose circle was
always in the same and was stirred in the same way he embraced them from every
side.

* Section 25
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h25
:END:

And so, when he had embraced one as the outer circle, the other as the inner,
he marked the former of the nature of the same, the latter of the other, and
he twisted that which was of the same from the side toward the right, but the
nearer one he directed from the median line toward the left, and he gave the
primacy to the upper, which alone he left undivided. The inner, when he had
divided it into six parts, he ordered to move as seven unequal circles at
double and triple interval, in courses contrary to one another. Of these he
made three equal in speed, but four both unequal among themselves and unlike
the remaining three.

* Section 26
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h26
:END:

When, then, that god who created the world had generated the soul from his own
mind and will, then at last he laid all that was compacted and bodily under
the soul and made it more inward, and so fitting middle to middle he coupled
them. Thus the soul, setting out from the middle, threw itself in a round
circuit around the extremity of heaven from the highest region, and turning
itself about it brought in the divine beginning of everlasting and wise life.

* Section 27
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h27
:END:

And the body of heaven was made visible, but the soul escapes the gaze of the
eyes. It is, of all things, the one composed of and sharing in the reason and
concord which in Greek is harmonia, of everlasting things that fall under
intelligence, than which nothing better was created by the best and most
excellent parent; for, joined from the same and from the other nature,
compacted with the added matter by a tempering of three parts in proportion,
turning itself about, when it has seized mutable matter and again when it has
seized the undivided and simple, it is moved through all things by itself and
discerns what is of the same kind, what of the other, and judges the rest:
what is most fitted to each thing, what happens in each place or manner or
time, and what distinction there is between those things that come to be and
those that are always the same.

* Section 28
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h28
:END:

But true reason, which is occupied with those things that are always the same
and with those that change, when it is moved in the same and in the other by
itself without voice and without any sound, when it touches that part by which
sense can be stirred, and the circle of that kind of the other, unchanged and
straight, announces all things to soul and mind, then firm and true opinions
and assents are generated; but when it turns among those things which,
remaining always the same, are held not by sense but by intelligence * * *

By reason, then, and divine mind, for the origin of time the course of the sun
and moon was invented

:CUSTOM_ID: h29
:END:

* * * might turn by right of nature, so that the moon's course should circle nearest the earth, and next above the earth should be the circuit of the sun. Then Lucifer and the sacred star of Mercury

keep a course equal in speed to the sun, but a certain contrary force, and by
that meeting which Lucifer, Mercury, and the sun have among themselves, they
by turns outrun and are outrun by one another.

* Section 30
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h31
:END:

The reason for placing the remaining stars, and what their placement is, must
be put off to another discourse, lest a longer speech be set on what had to be
touched than on that for whose sake we touch it. When, then, each of those
stars from which the measure of time was to be sealed had obtained its fitting
course, and living things had arisen from bodies bound with living bonds and
had learned to obey command, then, running from the crosswise motion of the
other nature into the motion of the same nature and sticking there and being
hindered, some traversing a greater circle, some a lesser, those the greater
more slowly, those the lesser more quickly, those which were moved most
swiftly by the motion of one and the same nature seemed to be outrun in speed
by the slower and, when they were overtaking, to be overtaken.

* Section 31
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h32
:END:

For every circle of them was turning as it were in the bend of a helix,
because by going forward in two ways at once contrary they brought it about
that what was slowest became nearest to the swiftest. And that there might be
some evident measure which would declare the speeds and slownesses in the
eight courses, god himself kindled the sun as a light at the second circuit
above the earth, so that the heaven might shine as much as possible for all,
and so that living things who had the right to be taught might from its motion
and from what was like it come to know the nature and power of numbers.

* Section 32
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h33
:END:

Night and day, then, generated in this way and for these reasons, make one
circuit of the circle, the wisest and best; a month, when the moon, having
traversed her course, has overtaken the sun; a year, when the sun has
completed and gone through his whole circle.

* Section 33
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h34
:END:

But the circuits of the other stars men, except a very few, neither call by
name nor measure against one another by number. And so they do not know that
these wanderings of the stars, endowed with infinite multitude and wonderful
variety, are that very thing which is rightly called time. And yet this can be
seen and understood: that the complete and perfect year of the complete and
perfect number of time is then at last fulfilled when the eight circuits,
having finished their courses, have brought themselves back to the same head,
and when that same circle, always like itself, has measured them.

* Section 34
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h35
:END:

For these reasons, then, the stars were born, which, passing through the
heaven, should turn themselves at the turning-back of the solstice and of
midwinter, so that this whole living thing we see might be most like that
living thing we perceive, in imitation of eternity.

And the rest, indeed, up to the origin of time, he had fashioned, stamped from
those things he was imitating; but because he had not yet enclosed every
living thing within the world, from that part the likeness of the image fell
short of the intended pattern. As many, then, and such forms of living things
as the mind, looking upon the form of things, could see, so many and such he
thought to fashion with himself in this world.

* Section 35
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h36
:END:

Now there were four kinds of living things, one divine and heavenly, the
second winged and of the air, the third of the water, the fourth of the earth.
The form of divine animation he made chiefly from fire, so that it might be
most brilliant and most beautiful to see. And since he wished to make it like
the nature of the universe, he rounded it for revolving and made it a
companion of the best wisdom of mind, and distributed it equally around the
whole heaven, so that this, marked with variety, the Greeks might well name
kosmos, and we the shining world.

* Section 36
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h37
:END:

To the divine he gave two kinds of motion, one that should always be in the
same † concerning which, and should conceal the same from all and in one way,
the other that should be driven toward the front from the turning of the same
and the like. The remaining five motions he wished it to be without and free
of, unmoved and standing. Of this kind are those stars which, fixed in the
heaven, are not moved from their place, which are living things, and divine,
and for that reason they cling to their seats and remain forever. Those that
slip in wandering and changeable errancy were generated as we have said above.

* Section 37
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h38
:END:

And the earth, our nurse, which is held up by an axis run through it, maker
and likewise guardian of day and night, he wished to be the most ancient of
all the gods who should be generated within the heaven. But the playings of
the gods and their meetings among themselves, and what turnings happen in
their circles and what advances, and when they almost touch one another, of
those who nearly join, and in the opposite region, and behind whom or before
they slip, and at what times they vanish from our sight and, emerged again,
strike terror into those without reason: if we try to unfold these in words
with no image of those things set before the eyes, the labor would be taken up
in vain. But let this be enough for us, and let what we have prefaced about
the nature of the gods who are seen and who have come to be have this as its
bound.

* Section 38
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h39
:END:

But of the rest, whom the Greeks call daimones, and our people, I think,
Lares, if only this can seem a right rendering, both to know and to declare
their origin is greater than we should dare to profess to write. We must
surely believe the old and, as they say, ancient men, who said they were
offspring of the gods, and so handed down their names to us. They could know
their own begetters best, and it is hard not to give credit to those sprung
from gods, although their speech is confirmed neither by arguments nor by sure
reasons; but because they seem to speak of things known to them, we must obey
the old law and custom.

* Section 39
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h40
:END:

So then, as it has been handed down by them, let the origin of these gods be
held and told, so that we may record that Oceanus and Salacia were generated
and brought forth from the sowing of Heaven and the conceiving of Earth, from
these Saturn and Ops, then Jupiter and Juno, the rest whom we see claimed and
called brothers among themselves and kin, and their, to use the old word,
lineage.

* Section 40
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h41
:END:

When, then, all had been created, both those who move and show themselves
openly, and those who are declared to us only so far as they themselves will,
then that god who generated all things speaks to them: "These things, you who
have sprung from the sowing of gods, attend: of the works of which I am parent
and maker, these are indissoluble against my will. Everything bound can be
dissolved, but it is by no means the part of the good to wish to dissolve what
is bound by reason. But since you have come to be, you cannot indeed be
immortal and indissoluble, yet you shall by no means be dissolved, nor shall
any fates of death destroy you, nor any fraud stronger than my counsel, which
is a greater bond for your lasting than those by which you were bound when you
were generated.

* Section 41
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h42
:END:

Learn, then, what I intend. Three kinds remain for us, and those mortal; if
they are passed over, the completion of heaven will not be perfect. For it
will not hold all kinds of living things in its embrace; but it ought to hold
them, so that nothing may be lacking from the same. If these were made by me
myself, they might equal the life of the gods. So that they may be generated
under a mortal condition, do you undertake to generate them, and imitate my
power, which you remember I used in your origin. Among them, those who shall
be created such that they ought to be as it were of the clan of the immortal
gods, let them be called of the divine kind and hold the first place of all
living things, and let them obey you willingly by right and law; their
beginning and sowing shall be handed to you by me, but you must weave onto
that which will be immortal a mortal part. So living things will arise, whom
you may both nourish while they live and receive into your bosom when they are
spent."

* Section 42
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h43
:END:

This he said. Then he returned to the former tempering, in which he mixed,
tempering the whole soul of universal nature, and pouring the remains of the
former mixture he made them even in nearly the same way, except that they were
not so uncorrupted as those which are always the same, but he took from them a
second and even a third.

* Section 43
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h44
:END:

When the whole had been established, then, he distributed a number of souls
equal to the stars and joined each to each, and so set them as it were in the
chariot of the universe and showed them the fated and necessary laws, and
showed that first there would be one origin for all, moderate and constant and
diminished by none, but that, the souls being sown and as it were scattered,
there would arise at fixed intervals of times a living thing most fitted for
the worship of the gods.

* Section 44
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h45
:END:

But since the nature of the human kind was twofold, the matter stood so that
the more excellent kind would be of those who would be men. But when he had
sown souls into bodies by necessity, and when there was then addition to
bodies, then subtraction, at first there had to arise one sense, common to
all, stirred by a more violent motion and joined to nature, then love mixed
with pleasure and pain, then anger and fear and the remaining motions of the
soul, companions of the former and also contrary and at odds with them;
whoever shall have ruled these by reason will have lived justly, whoever shall
have given himself to them, unjustly.

* Section 45

And he who has completed rightly and honorably the course of living given by
nature will return to that star with which he was joined; but he who has lived
without measure and without temperance, him a second birth will transfer into
a woman's form, and if even then he does not make an end of vices, he will be
thrown still more heavily and will be transferred into the forms of cattle and
wild beasts most like his own manners, and he will not see a bound of evils
before he has begun to follow that turning which he had in himself, inborn and
planted, of the same and the like. That will come about when he has driven out
by reason those turbulent and reasonless things that have settled from fire,
air, water, and earth, and has come to the first and best affection of the
soul.

When he had so arranged these things and had set himself outside all blame and
cause if any fraud or vice should afterward occur, he scattered them as if
sowing, some to earth, some to the moon, some to the remaining parts of the
world that have been established as signs and marks for the spaces of time.
After that sowing he allowed the younger gods, so to speak, to fashion mortal
bodies, and whatever remained of the human soul that had to be added, all
that, and what followed, to polish and complete; then to offer themselves as
chiefs and leaders to this living thing and to rule and govern its life as
beautifully as possible, except insofar as it, not well made itself, should by
its own fault seek some misery for itself.

And he who composed all things remained constantly in his own state; but those
who had been created by him, when they had learned the order of their parent,
followed it. And so, when they had received the immortal principle of a mortal
living thing, imitating their generator and maker they borrowed from the world
particles of fire and earth and water and air, which they should give back
again, and they coupled them among themselves not with the same bonds by which
they themselves were bound, but with such as could not be seen because of
their smallness; with frequent little wedges, as it were, melted in, they made
one body from all, and in it they bound the circuits of the divine soul,
flowing in and flowing out.

But if it has settled in a brightness, then either the same appearance or
sometimes an altered one is returned, when the fire of the eyes has mixed and
joined itself with that fire which is poured before the face. And those things
are seen on the right which are on the left, because they touch opposite parts
with opposite parts of the eyes. But the right answers to the right, the left
to the left, by a turning of the lights, when they do not cohere with each
other. That happens when the smoothness of the mirrors has taken on height on
this side and that, and so has pushed the right into the left part of the eyes
and the left into the right. Faces are also seen upside down by a driving-off
of the lights, which by turning renders what is above as below.

And all these are of that kind which help the causes of things; god uses them
as services when he makes the form of the best, so far as it can be done. But
most people think these are not helpers of causes, but are themselves the
causes of all things, which have the power of cooling, heating, thickening,
melting, but lack all intelligence and reason, which except in the soul are
found in no other nature. But the soul escapes every sense of the eyes; yet
fire, air, water, earth are bodies, and they are seen.

But he who professes himself a lover of intelligence and wisdom must seek
first the first causes of intelligent and wise nature, then the second of
those things which necessarily move others when they themselves are moved by
others. Therefore I see we must do this: that we speak of both kinds of
causes, but separately of those which with intelligence are productive of the
most beautiful and best things, and of those which, empty of prudence, produce
the inconstant and disordered.

I think enough has been said. But let the greatest usefulness given to the
race of men by the gift of the gods be unfolded next. For the eyes have
