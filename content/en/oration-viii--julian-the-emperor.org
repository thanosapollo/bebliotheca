* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

But if I should not also converse with you of as much as I conversed with
myself, when I learned that you must go from us, I shall think I have less for
consolation, O dear companion; or rather I shall consider that I have not even
begun to procure any ease for myself, of which I have not given you a share.
For since we have shared with one another many painful and many pleasant deeds
and words, in private and public affairs, at home and in the camp, a common
healing remedy of the present things, of whatever sort they may be, must be
found. But who would imitate for us the lyre of Orpheus, or sound in answer
the songs of the Sirens, or discover the drug nepenthe? Whether that was a
tale full of Egyptian narrations, or what the poet himself made, weaving into
what follows the Trojan sufferings, Helen having learned this from the
Egyptians: not all that Greeks and Trojans did to one another, but of what
sort the words must be that will take away the griefs of souls and become
causes of cheerfulness and calm. For pleasure and pain somehow seem to hang
from the same peak and to change places with one another in turn. And of the
things that befall, even those that are exceedingly toilsome, the wise say,
bring to the man of sense no less well-being than difficulty; since the bee
also from the bitterest herb that grows about Hymettus draws up sweet dew and
is a maker of honey. And of bodies, those that are healthy and robust are
nourished by whatever foods chance, and things that often seem hard have
become for them not only harmless but even causes of strength; but those whose
body is in a bad state by nature and nurture and habit, being invalids all
their life, to these even the lightest things are accustomed to add the
heaviest harms. Therefore also of the mind, those who have so cared for it
that it is not altogether wicked, but moderately healthy, even if not
according to the vigor of Antisthenes and Socrates, nor the courage of
Callisthenes, nor the impassivity of Polemon, but so as to be able to choose
the mean in such things, they would perhaps be cheered even in more difficult
circumstances.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

I myself, putting myself to the proof as to how I am and shall be toward your
journey, was pained as much as when I first left my own guide at home; for the
memory of all things came upon me at once: of the partnership in labors which
we bore together, of the unfeigned and pure converse, of the guileless and
just association, of the common action in all that is noble, of the equal and
unrepentant zeal and impulse against the wicked, as we often stood together
having equal spirit, like-minded and longed-for friends. And besides these
there came upon me the memory of "Then Odysseus was left alone"; for I am now
like him, since you, like Hector, a god has led out beyond the shafts which
the sycophants have often let fly at you, or rather at me, wishing to wound me
through you, supposing me vulnerable in this alone, if they should deprive me
of the society of a faithful friend and eager fellow-shieldman and
unhesitating partner toward dangers. Yet I do not think you on this account
are less pained than I now am, because you have a smaller share of the labors
and dangers, but even more you fear for me and for my head, lest something
befall it. For I myself also did not set your things second to my own, and I
perceived that you were similarly disposed toward us. Whence reasonably I am
much stung, that to you, who concerning the others can say /I care for
nothing; for my affairs are well,/ I alone am a cause of grief and care. But
of this, as it seems, we share equally, you being pained only for us, and I
always longing for your society and remembering the friendship which, from
virtue first and foremost, and then also through the service which I
continually furnished to you and you to me, we acknowledged, being blended
with one another, not confirming these things by oaths or such necessities, as
Theseus and Peirithous, but from always thinking and choosing the same things:
so far am I from saying that we refrained from giving harm to any of the
citizens, that we never even deliberated any such thing with one another; but
if anything useful has been done or planned in common by us, this it will be
for others to say.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

That I reasonably grieve at the present things, being parted not from a friend
only but also from a faithful fellow-worker (and may the daimon grant it be
even for a little), I think even Socrates, the great herald and teacher of
virtue, will agree with me, judging concerning him from those things by which
we know him, I mean the words of Plato. He says at any rate that "It seemed to
me more difficult to administer political affairs rightly; for neither is it
possible to act without friends and faithful companions, nor is it easy to
have plenty of these with much ease." And yet if this seemed to Plato greater
than digging through Athos, what must we expect concerning it, we who fall
farther short of his understanding and judgment than he of God? And I, not
even for the sake of the service only, which giving in turn to one another in
the state we bore more easily the things done contrary to our purpose by
fortune and by those arrayed against us, but also being about before long to
be in want of the only warmth and delight that is always mine, reasonably I am
stung and have been bitten in my own heart. For to what friend so
well-disposed shall I look hereafter? Whose guileless and pure frankness shall
I endure? Who will advise us prudently, and rebuke with goodwill, and
strengthen us toward the noble without arrogance and conceit, and speak freely
having taken away the bitterness of the words, as those who take away from
drugs what is excessively unpleasant and leave the useful itself? But this
benefit I harvested from your friendship. And being deprived of so many things
at once, of what words could I have plenty, which, when through longing for
you and your counsels and your gentleness I am in danger of giving up my very
soul, will persuade me to be still and to bear nobly whatever the god has
given? For the great emperor, thinking in the same way as he, seems to have
planned these things thus now. What then must one think, and what charms must
one find, to persuade the soul, disturbed by the passion, to be gentle? Are
the words of Zamolxis to be imitated by us, I mean the charms from Thrace,
which Socrates bringing to Athens thought it right to chant over the beautiful
Charmides before healing the pain of the head? Or are these, as being greater
and about greater things, not to be set in motion, like great stage-machines
in a small theatre, but from the former deeds, whose glories we have learned,
as the poet says, as if plucking from a meadow, varied and of many kinds, the
fairest flowers, shall we cheer ourselves with the narrations, adding some
small things from philosophy to them? For as I think those who pour I know not
what drugs into things that are too sweet take away their cloying, so some
things from philosophy added to narrations take away the seeming of
introducing a crowd from ancient history, without need, and superfluous
garrulity.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

/What first? what next? what last shall I recount?/ Whether how that Scipio,
who loved Laelius and was loved in turn by him with equal yoke, as the saying
is, was glad to be with him, and did nothing of which that man had not first
learned and said it was to be done? Whence I think he also furnished a story
to those who from envy reviled Scipio, that Laelius was the poet of the deeds,
and Africanus the actor of them. This same report is attached to us also, and
not only am I not vexed, but I rejoice at it even more. For to be persuaded by
what has been rightly judged by another, Zeno makes a mark of greater virtue
than for someone to know from himself what is needed, adapting the saying of
Hesiod, /This man is altogether best, who obeys one who speaks well,/ saying
it instead of "who thinks out all things for himself." But to me it does not
seem graceful for this reason; for I am persuaded that Hesiod speaks more
truly, and Pythagoras better than both, who also furnished the beginning to
the proverb and gave to life the saying that the things of friends are common,
not meaning money only, surely, but also the sharing of mind and of prudence,
so that whatever you yourself found is no less the persuaded man's, and
whatever of yours I acted, of these very things you reasonably have an equal
share. But these things, of whichever of us they may rather appear, belong
also to the other, and the envious will have nothing more from the words.

But we must go back to Africanus and Laelius. For when Carthage had been
destroyed and all the parts about Libya had become slaves of Rome, Africanus
sends Laelius; and that man put to sea bearing good news to the fatherland;
and Scipio was distressed at being left behind by his friend, yet he did not
think the passion inconsolable for himself. And Laelius too was likely to be
vexed, since he put to sea alone, yet he did not make the misfortune
unbearable. Cato also sailed, leaving his familiars at home, and Pythagoras,
and Plato and Democritus, taking no partner of the journey, though leaving
many of their dearest at home. Pericles also campaigned against Samos not
bringing Anaxagoras, and he reduced Euboea by that man's counsels (for he had
been educated under him), yet not dragging the body along as some other of the
things necessary for the battles. And yet even this man, they say, the
Athenians made, though unwilling, to stand away from association with his
teacher.
But he bore, as being a prudent man, the folly of his own citizens with
self-control and gentleness. For he thought it necessary to yield to
necessity, to the fatherland as to a mother, not justly, yet harshly disposed
toward their association, reasoning these things, as is likely; and one must
hear what follows as of Pericles himself:

To me the cosmos is city and fatherland, and friends are gods and daimons and
all who anywhere are worthy. But one must also honor that in which we have
been born, since this is a divine law, and obey whatever it commands, and not
use force, nor, as the proverb says, kick against the goads; for the so-called
yoke of necessity is inexorable. Yet one must not wail nor lament over the
things it commands more harshly, but one must reason the matter itself. Now it
commands Anaxagoras to be parted from us, and we shall not see the best of our
companions, on whose account I was vexed at the night, because it did not show
me my friend, and I knew gratitude to day and sun, because they furnished me
to see him whom I most loved. But if nature has given you eyes only, O
Pericles, as to the beasts, it is not unreasonable that you should be
especially distressed; but if it has breathed a soul into you and put in a
mind, by which you see many of the things that have come to be, though they
are not present now, through memory, and many of the things that will be,
reasoning discovers and as if seeing with eyes presents to the mind, and of
the things present not only those before the eyes does imagination stamp and
give it to judge and behold, but also things far off and separated by myriads
of stades it shows more clearly than the things that have come to be at our
feet and before the eyes: why must one be so pained and bear it ill? And that
my word is not without witness, /Mind sees and mind hears,/ says the Sicilian,
so sharp a thing and using speed so unmanageable, that whenever Homer wishes
to show some one of the daimons using unbelievable speed of travel, /As when a
man's mind darts,/ he says. Using this, most easily from Athens you will see
him in Ionia, most easily from the Celts him in Illyria and Thrace, and him
among the Celts from Thrace and Illyria. For neither, as it is not possible
for plants to be saved when they change their accustomed country, when the
mixture of the seasons is contrary, does it happen to men changing from place
to place either to be destroyed utterly or to change their manner and alter
concerning the things they had formerly judged rightly. Therefore it is not
likely that goodwill should be duller, if not even more to love and cherish;
for hubris follows satiety, but love follows want. And in this way then we
shall be better off, our goodwill toward one another being stretched, and we
shall hold one another established in our own minds as statues. And now I
shall see Anaxagoras, and again he me; and nothing hinders us from looking at
one another at the same time, not bits of flesh and sinews and the impress of
form, and breasts modeled toward an archetype of body; and yet perhaps nothing
hinders even this from appearing in our minds; but to the virtue and the deeds
and the words and the associations and the meetings which we often made with
one another, not without the Muses hymning education and justice and the mind
that oversees mortal and human things, and going through concerning the state
and laws and manners of virtue and worthy pursuits, as many as occurred to us
when we remembered these things in season. Thinking these things, being
nourished by these images, perhaps we shall not attend to the appearances of
night-dreams, nor will sensation, being badly disposed by the mixture of the
body, cast empty and vain phantasms upon the mind. For we shall not even take
sensation itself to minister to us and to be a servant; but the mind, fleeing
it, will practice these things, being awakened toward the understanding and
habituation of the incorporeal; for we are together with mind and with the
better, and the things that have fled sensation and are separated in place, or
rather that do not even need place, we are by nature able to see and to grasp,
as many of us as have lived worthily of such a vision, thinking it and being
joined to it.

But Pericles, as being a high-minded man and reared freely in a free city,
cheered himself with loftier words; but I, being born of such as men now are,
charm and lead myself with more human words, and I take away the excessive
bitterness of the grief, trying to fit some consolation to each of the
unpleasant and strange phantasms that always fall upon me from the matter, as
a charm to the bite of a beast that bites our very heart within and our wits.
That then is first of the things that appear unpleasant. Now I shall be left
alone, in want of pure association and free converse; for I have not yet
anyone with whom I shall speak with equal confidence. Is it then not easy for
me even to converse with myself? But will someone take away even my thought
and compel me to think other things and to admire things other than I wish?
Or is this already a marvel and like writing on water and boiling a stone and
seeking the tracks of the flight of birds that fly? Therefore since no one
takes these things from us, we shall surely be together somehow with
ourselves, and perhaps the daimon will suggest something useful; for it is not
likely that a man who has entrusted himself to the better should be altogether
neglected and left wholly desolate; but over him the god also held his hand,
and gives courage, and breathes in might, and puts what must be done upon the
mind, and turns him away from what must not be done. A daimonic voice also
followed Socrates, preventing him from doing whatever was not needful; and
Homer also says concerning Achilles: for he put it in his breast, as the god
also awakens our thoughts, whenever the mind, turning back into itself, first
keeps company with itself and with the god through itself alone, hindered by
no one. For the mind does not need hearing in order to learn, nor does the god
need voice in order to teach what must be done; but outside all sensation the
participation comes to the mind from the better; in what manner and how there
is no leisure now to go through, but that it happens is clear, and the
witnesses are plain, not some inglorious men nor worthy to be ranked in the
Megarian portion, but of those who carried off the first prizes in wisdom.

Therefore since one must expect both that a god will be present with us wholly
and that we ourselves shall be together with ourselves, the excessive
unpleasantness of the grief must be taken away. Since even Odysseus, shut up
alone on the island for seven years in all, then lamenting, I praise for the
rest of his endurance, but I do not admire the dirges. For what use is it to
look upon the fishy sea and to pour tears? But not to give up nor to despair
before fortune, but to be a man to the last of labors and dangers, this to me
at least seems greater than according to a man. It is not just then to praise
them and not imitate, nor to think that the god eagerly helped those men, but
will overlook those now, seeing them laying claim to the virtue through which
indeed he rejoiced in those also; for it was not through beauty of body, since
then Nireus ought rather to have been loved, nor through strength, for the
Laestrygonians and Cyclopes were stronger than he by an infinite amount, nor
through wealth, for thus Troy would have remained unsacked. But why must he
have trouble seeking the cause through which the poet says Odysseus was dear
to the gods, when it is possible to hear it from him? /Because you are
courteous and keen-witted and prudent./ It is clear then that if these things
should accrue to us, the better will not fail of what is from itself, but even
according to the oracle given long ago to the Lacedaemonians, both called and
uncalled the god will be present.

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Having cheered myself with these things I go again to that part which in truth
seems small, yet toward reputation is not ignoble. They say Alexander also
needed Homer, not of course being present, but heralding as he did Achilles
and Patroclus and both Ajaxes and Antilochus. But he, always looking down on
the present and reaching after the absent, was not content with the things
according to himself nor satisfied with what was given; and if he had obtained
Homer, perhaps he would have longed for the lyre of Apollo, with which that
god hymned the marriage of Peleus, not thinking this a fiction of Homer's
understanding, but a true deed woven into the verses, as I think /Dawn in
saffron robe was spreading over all the earth/ and /And the sun sprang up/ and
/There is a land, Crete,/ and as many such things as the poets say, some being
and still with us, some coming to be, being clear and evident.

But whether in his case some surpassing greatness of virtue and an
understanding in no way less than the goods that belonged to him led his soul
into so great a desire that he reached after things greater than according to
the others, or some excess of courage and boldness leading into boastfulness
and looking toward arrogance, let it be left to examine in common for those
who wish to praise or to blame him, if anyone even supposes that this portion
belongs to that man. But we, always being content with the present and least
laying claim to the absent, are fond indeed whenever the herald praises,
having been both spectator and fellow-combatant of all things for us, not
having received the words molded at random toward favor and hatred; but it is
enough for us even if he only confesses to love, being in the other things
more silent even than those initiated by Pythagoras.

Here there comes over me also the rumor, that you will go not to the Illyrians
only, but also to the Thracians and the Greeks dwelling about that sea, among
whom, being born and reared, a great love of men and places and cities has
been melted into me. And perhaps no mean love of us has been left behind in
their souls either, to whom I know well that you would be a welcome comer, as
the saying is, giving them a just exchange for those things for which you have
left us here. And this not as praying; since to come to us the same way
quickly is better; but as, if it should happen, I think even toward this not
without consolation nor without cheering, rejoicing with those men that they
will see you from us. For I already class myself with the Celts because of
you, a man ranked among the first of the Greeks both in good order and in the
rest of virtue, and at the peak of rhetoric and not inexperienced in
philosophy, of which the Greeks alone have partaken of the strongest things,
hunting the truth by reason, as it is by nature, having allowed us not to
attend to untrustworthy myths nor to paradoxical wonder-working, as most of
the barbarians do.

But how this also is, let it now be left. But you: for it is already worthy to
send you forth with good omen; may a kindly god lead you wherever you must
journey, and may Xenios receive you and Philios well-disposed, and lead you
safely through land; and if you must sail, let the waves be stilled; and may
you appear to all a friend and honored, pleasant in approaching, painful in
leaving them; and loving us may you least of all long for the partnership of a
companion and faithful friend. And may the god show the emperor also
well-disposed to you and give all the other things according to your mind, and
prepare the journey home to us safe and swift.

These things I pray for you together with the noble and good men, and still
besides these /Health and great joy to you, and may the gods give you
blessings, to return home to your dear fatherland./
