** Ausonius to Pacatus the Proconsul
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2
:END:

I know that others will never give me praise in proportion to my
labour. If you grant it, though, as Afranius says in his Thais: /
I shall find greater praise than labour.
/ What you are about to read
are monosyllables, like so many stops in speech. They leave no room
for eloquence, no forming of a thought, no proposition, reply,
conclusion, or any other sophistic turn that cannot live in a single
verse. Even so they hang together like the separate rings of a chain,
and I have woven a playful little piece at the same time, though I am
used to starting larger things: yet /the labour is on a slight theme,
but the glory is not slight,/ if they win approval. You will make them
into something, for without you they will be monosyllables, or even
less. In them I have played, so far as pleasure goes, and toiled, so
far as trouble goes. I have given the little book the name
Technopaegnion, so that you should not think the toiler lacked play,
or the player lacked art.

** AUSONIUS TO HIS PAULINUS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

I have sent you the Technopaegnion, a useless little work of my idle
leisure. The verses begin with monosyllables and end with them. Nor
did the flinty difficulty stop there. To the misery of joining them
was added this: the same monosyllable that closed the last verse had
to open the next. So say: what delay, what torment! I have spent care
on a vain thing. It is slight, and it is scorned; it is disconnected,
and it is tangled; when it amounts to something, it is found to be
nothing. Still I have toiled to give it some historical or
dialectical matter. The constraint of the rule shut out poetic or
sophistic lightness. In short, there is nothing here to wonder at;
but add a few letters, and there is something to pity and not to
rival. And if you stoop even to this, you will take more trouble from
the loss of talent and eloquence than pleasure from any wish to
imitate.

** VERSES BEGUN AND ENDED WITH MONOSYLLABLES SO THAT FROM THE END OF A VERSE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h1

THEY RUN BACK TO THE BEGINNING

Chance feeds, rules, and destroys the frail affairs of men
Chance, doubtful and forever slipping, whom coaxing Hope fosters
Hope, bounded by no age, whose term is Death
Death the greedy, whom night sinks in hellish gloom
night that shall pass in her turn, when golden light has returned
light granted as a gift of the gods, whose forerunner is the Sun
Sun, from whom not even Mars, strong in arms, hides in Venus's stealth
Mars, sprung from no father, whom the Thracian race worships
race of unbridled men, with whom right rushes into every crime
right to sacrifice a man in the rites: that is the wild custom of the place
wild custom of a bold people, whom no law holds
law which justice founded by natural command
justice born of men's piety, justice the sure mind of God
mind, which with heavenly sense waters the deserving HEART
HEART, lively, having the likeness of the world, the soul's vigour and
force:
yet here there is no force: it is only a jest and a thing of nothing.

** PREFACE TO MONOSYLLABLES PLACED ONLY AT THE END
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

As in the old proverb /the bandy-legged woman follows Vibia,/ I weave
on the worthlessness of like trifles, and these verses too end in
monosyllables, though they begin freely, even if they close by the
rule. But I have toiled that, so far as they could before the most
indulgent ears, the absurd might sound in tune, the tasteless have
flavour, the gaping fit together; in short, that they should have a
bitter sweetness, a clumsy charm, and a rough smoothness, all of
which, since the unlovely matter strips them of grace, a kindly
reading must win over. You too, more confidently, entrust me your
own, for they are better, so that, as we began with an adage, we may
end with a proverb and /mules may scratch each other./

** ON THE LIMBS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

A new tooth marks in boys the first seven years,
a stronger voice forestalls the years of ripening.
Man's brow is unconquered by winds and suns,
and sinews join with flesh a hard bone.
The heart beats, restless, lively, round, keen, and warm,

whence the senses thrive, which the ruling mind quickens,
and the speaking mouth turns them into words by measured law.
How strong is the dark bile, yet how small a part of a man!
How slender the leg, and what a bodily mass it bears!
Under how great a weight does the foot govern our way!

** ON UNCONNECTED MATTERS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1
:END:

Often in marriages a dowry becomes a harm, if it is too great.

Either sex has power, but the male prevails in command.
He who shall do right, not he who lords it, will be king.
Strife vexes friendships and unbinds treaties.
Begin whatever you do: the first part of the work stands for the whole.

Praise winds its way to heaven and sets the deserving among the gods.
A city is packed both with learning and with vices.
In safe cities a citadel is more fortified than cities.
Great honour belongs to gold, yet the price of gold is bronze.
The long day is toilsome for a man, but night is the tempering,
which the region of the Ethiopians lacks, a sleepless, restless race,
where always in eternal wheeling a bright light remains.

** ON THE GODS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h1
:END:

There are monosyllables of the heaven-dwellers too. First of the gods
is Right,
who is Themis to the Greeks; after her Rhea, who is Ops to the Latins;

then the brother of Jupiter and Consus, Tartarean Dis,
and the sister and wife of her brother, queen of the gods, Force,
and he who is found as father in a four-horse chariot, the Sun,
and he who stirs the fierce motions of war, Mars strong in arms,
whom piety never, never good Peace, disturbs.
Nor shall you be passed over in silence, Pan of Maenalus, dweller in
the groves,
nor the genius of homes, Lar, born of Larunda,
and Nar, sulphurous, foremost among Italian rivers,

and night-coloured Styx, who holds the gods' solemn perjuries,
and Libs, the white south-west, who spreads the sail-winged sea,
and good Hope, who never in doubts abandons a man.

** ON FOODS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

Nor will I pass over our foods, which ancient custom holds,
to which sea-salt is added as a relish.
Once the acorn was food common to cattle and to man,
before the eared crop was at hand in the fields.
Soon spelt, and from spelt's flour porridge-making meal,
with which once the commons of Romulus' sons furnished their tables,
hence food, hence drink, when porridge was thinned with water,
among fruits there is black cumin, matching pepper in bite,
and the flat, round lentil from Pelusian seed,
and the five-kind nut, guarded by a double shell,

and what is pleasing as food and drink, the bees' keen labour, honey:
that liquor of a strange nature, on which the dregs float at the top.

** ON HISTORIES
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h1
:END:

Phoebus, the Oebalian flower gave you a new comfort.
Another flower: the sacred spring is guilty of Narcissus' death.
The lightning-swift boar has the ill glory of Adonis' slaughter.
Juno's cloud mocks the perjured Lapith.
The Parnassian lot of the Delphic god also mocks the Aeacid.
The cow cuts the Thracian, Libyan, and Cimmerian straits.
Not without a Hamadryad's fate does a tree-beam fall.
The Saturnian sickle cut that from which Venus was born.
A dry cross for birds stood among the Scythian rocks,
whence a bloody dew from Prometheus' body
sprinkles the crags, and the whetstone begets dread aconite.
When Ibycus perished, a high-flying crane was the informer.
At the Aeacid's tomb Andromache's sister-in-law was sacrificed.
In an Argive prison a slow death came upon Philopoemen.
The stripped Armorican Lars gave the third rich spoils.

A late violent death by poisoned drink carried Hannibal off.
How great the affairs of Asia that undeserved fraud gave to death!

An avenging torch blazed from the Euboean cliffs.
He stands at Jove's cup, whom Dardanian Tros begets.
A Cretan man was borne above the air on swift wings.
A savage Thracian brought unchaste force upon you, Philomela.
The Lydian is a barbarian, the Geta deceitful, the Phrygian womanish,

the Ligurians false, the Carian held in no esteem.
The robe-flowing Ser combs woodland fleeces.

Known among portents is the Theban Sphinx of three-bodied form.
Known to Caledonian daughters-in-law is the Strix, of female sex,

** BY QUESTION AND ANSWER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h1
:END:

Who undergoes the penalty in a capital trial? A surety.
What if the suit is about money, who will be given? A bondsman.
Who is matched against the murmillo? The ambidextrous Thracian.
Among virtues, what name has Mercury? Thief.
Censers and bowls: what third vessels of the gods? A platter.
What land girt by the sea begets Hippocrates? Cos.
Does the herd or the kingdom more trouble Minos' daughter? The herd.
What besides a cloud was set upon the Phaeacians? A mountain.

Say, when food fails, who is fatter in sleep? The dormouse.

Say, what makes hides fit for shields? Glue.
What will be the nominative of the ablative sponte? Spons.
What four-footed beast is joined with singing-birds in auspices? The
mouse.

What floats on the sea that does not swim in a river? Pitch.

What contains twelve equal parts? The as.
If a third portion is missing, what is left? The bes.

** ON MONOSYLLABIC GREEK AND LATIN LETTERS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h1
:END:

A, leader of the elements, thrives in Latin studies
and fi is written last among Argive signs.

What eta is worth among the Aeolians, and what epsilon is worth, that
is Latin E.
What Latin E always furnishes as short, the Doric voice Ε.
This with a smooth and clear sound the Attic race denies to O.

What omega and Greek omicron make up, the Roman voice O balances.
I am a letter, a full sound like Iota, commanding I.
Unknown to Cecropian signs, sounding funereal, V.
The two-way of Pythagoras, I lie open with ambiguous branches, Y.

In Greek words I am never seen last, M.
Zeta lying down, if it rise, will be the sign that is read N.
Imitating Maeander and wandering bends, I wander, Ξ.
The divided monosyllable of Beta is Italian B.
I bear not the form but the sound of Delta, Roman D.
What is the form of an enemy's ridge, this Pi will make.
If you write Ausonian Pe, I shall be Cecropian P,
and what is Rho in Greek will be changed into Latin P.
As a mast bears a yard at its top, so I am T.
This is a breathing, quickening the thinnest things with a blast, H.
This is added in Latium to three names only, K;
after C prevailed, which earlier served in Gamma's place,

and, doubled back, gave another title in its stead, G.
When washed and girt with two handles, you will read Ϡ.

In Latium the number ten, Argive Χ.
This crane's likeness of Palamedes is stretched out, Φ.
I was once Boeotian Koppa, now Latin Q.
A three-horned fork in shape, I am almost last, Ψ.

** THE GRAMMARIANS' SCOURGE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14-h1
:END:

And word-craft too? Only hiss, you who too fiercely
condemn trifles: even worthless wares have their price!
As Ennius records, let joy-bringing gau fill you.

Let the jealous mind of men cook pus thickened with gall.
Say, what do Maro's Catalepta mean? In them he set the Celts' al
there follows, no more clearly, tau:

is sil the sound of a foreign name or of a Latin one?
And what is min, death-dealing, badly mixed with a brother?

Why does one thing mark empire, a lawsuit, and love?
Laid with twin boats on the sea, is it a pontoon or a bridge?
In the Bucolic Maro said saepes: why did Cicero say saeps?
The usual word, best known to all, if you recall, is lac
why is it condemned, so that reason may rather make lact?
Or is seps, Libya's deadly plague, a Roman word?
If the good man is insons, is the opposite, the accused, sons?
Why does the rich in wealth have a name from Stygian Jove, Dis?
From where does the man of Rudiae say /the gods' house, high-sounding
cael/?

And after whose manner did he add /into his own do/?

Or, speaking of foliage, why does he say /poplar fros/?

But where am I going? What end, what limit and finishing-line?
Be kind, Pacatus, a good, learned, easy man;
this whole work is scattered, like Antiphilae's hair: peace.
