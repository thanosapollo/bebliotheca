PREFACES TO THE PSALMS.
=55.531=
After Moses and his successor Jesus the son of Nave, and those called
judges in Israel, when Saul had taken up the kingdom of Israel, and had
been rejected, God raised up for them a king of virtue, both just and a
prophet, who composed the book of one hundred and fifty Psalms, moved
=55.532= by the Holy Spirit, in meter according to the measure proper to
his own tongue, and melodiously with rhythm and various instruments and
dances and songs singing them. For this man held a harp, and he had also
various choruses of young prophets under himself; for so he called those
who remained with the prophets; and often he named them also sons of
prophets, having various instruments, this one cymbals, that one pipes,
this =55.533= one drums, that one trumpets, this one the psaltery and the
harp, that one the songs called bucolic. And each chorus had a leader,
of whom one was called Asaph, another Idithun, another the sons of Core,
another Ethan the Israelite, another Moses the man of God. When therefore
he was moved by the Spirit to say something in advance, either concerning
the captivity of the people, or concerning their return, or concerning
moral teachings, or concerning providence, or concerning the Master
Christ, he composed each psalm in meter, converging into one subject (for
this reason also there are small and great psalms), and handed it over to
one chorus. And if again he judged to hand over in the middle of the
psalm the remainder of the psalm to another chorus, then this very
alternation of the psalm was called Diapsalma. So that also whenever
they, wishing (sic) in the middle of the psalm to hand it over to those
called singers, that is the bucolics, then it was called Ode of
diapsalma; since the singers received in alternation the remainder of the
psalm to sing.

One can learn about this, if one wishes, from what is written in the
Paralipomena of the kingdoms thus: And he sang this song by the hand of
Asaph the prophet. And after he first handed over the psalm, thereafter
each chorus, both by itself and with one accord, with delight and
rhythm, some with these instruments, some with those, sang the psalm
antiphonally with dance, to the glory and praise of God. And one can
learn again also about this, that =55.534= David himself, taking up the
ark from the Philistines, danced before it, and being reproached by his
own wife Michal, said: I will play and laugh before the Lord. For he not
only did not cease, but he also promised to intensify such things. And
some, not having attended to the order and the exactness, nor being
willing to be taught by those who know these things, turned to
allegories, declaring that not all the psalms are of David, but taking
from after him those that are clear, saying that which at any time the
Lord or an apostle concerning some psalm of another did not mention,
except of David. We sketch therefore him sitting, and the choruses before
him on the right hand and on the left, so that one may know also the
spectacle of this order. And after these things, saying also those psalms
which he himself was deemed worthy to foretell concerning the economy of
the Master, having varied them diversely in the composition and having
interchanged them variously in form concerning the passion and the
resurrection of his manhood. Likewise also that by his divinity he is
eternal and maker of all things and renewer of the whole, toward which
every aim of the God-inspired Scripture looks. Chorus of Idithun, chorus
of the sons of Core, chorus of Ethan the Israelite, chorus of Asaph,
chorus of Moses the man of God. These choruses are of the prophets under
him, singing with various instruments and psalmodizing and dancing to the
glory of God the one hundred and fifty Psalms which came to be from the
Holy Spirit.

=55.533= INTRODUCTION TO THE PSALMS.
αʹ. All the divine Scriptures are holy; but the Psalms have something
more; for instance, concerning temperateness at once, rather indeed
before all things, concerning not keeping company with the wicked, from
the very beginning of the book. For this reason the blessed =55.534=
David began from here, saying: Blessed is the man who did not walk in the
counsel of the ungodly. And when he says blessedness, he means the summit
of all the goods. And concerning keeping company with the good, and
concerning controlling the belly, and concerning =55.535= holding the
hands, and concerning intemperance, and concerning not taking advantage,
that riches are nothing, nor glory, and suchlike things. And concerning
being temperate you will hear him saying: My sores were filled with
mockings; and again, You utterly destroyed everyone who fornicates away
from you. And concerning the need to control the belly: And he killed, he
says, in the most of them, while their food was yet in their mouth. And
that one must abstain from gifts: If wealth flows, do not set your heart
upon it. And not to desire glory: Neither shall his glory go down after
him. Not to be emulous of the wicked: Do not be emulous of evildoers. To
hold dominions as nothing: I saw the wicked highly exalted and lifting
himself up as the cedars of Lebanon; and I passed by, and behold, he was
not. To hold the present things as nothing: They counted happy the people
to whom these things are; blessed is the people whose Lord their God is.
That we do not sin simply, but there is a requital, he says: For you will
render to each according to his works. And why does he not requite day by
day? God is a just judge and strong and longsuffering. That humility is
fair: Lord, my heart was not lifted up, he says. And that pride is evil:
Because of this pride prevailed over them to the end. And again, The Lord
resists the proud. That almsgiving is fair: He scattered, he gave to the
poor; his righteousness abides unto the age of the age. That to show
mercy is praiseworthy: Kind is the man who pities and lends. That one
must not speak evil: Him who speaks secretly against his neighbor I drove
out. And many things more philosophical than these you will find from
here. For he speaks not only concerning the things present, but also
concerning the things to come, and concerning the things seen, and
concerning the invisible creation. And concerning heaven do you wish to
learn, whether it remains such, or changes? He answers you clearly and
will say: The heavens shall be worn out as a garment, and as a vesture
you shall fold them, O God, and they shall be changed. And concerning its
form, if you wish to hear, you will hear again: Who stretches out the
heaven as a curtain. And if you wish to know something more concerning
his back, he will say to you again: Who covers his upper chambers in
waters. And not even here does he stand, but he speaks with you also
concerning the breadth and the height, showing these to be of equal
measure: For as far, he says, as the east is from the west, so far did he
remove our transgressions from us. According to the height of the heaven
from the earth the Lord made strong his mercy upon those who fear him.
And if you should search out the foundations of the earth, neither will
he hide these from you, but you will hear him saying: Upon the seas he
founded it. And if you wish to learn concerning earthquakes whence they
come, he will free you from all perplexity, speaking thus: Who looks upon
the earth, and makes it tremble. And if you seek the use of the night,
these things too you will know to learn from him: In it all the beasts of
the forest will go. And wherefore the mountains are useful, he will say
to you: The high mountains are for the deer. And wherefore the rocks, he
says to you: The rock is a refuge for the hares. Wherefore the fruitless
trees, learn: There sparrows will nest; wherefore the springs in the
deserts: Upon them the birds of the heaven shall dwell, and the beasts of
the field. Wherefore wine? Not that you may drink only, since the nature
of water sufficed, but that you may be glad and rejoice: For wine makes
glad the heart of man. And having learned this, you will know also unto
what point one must use wine. Whence the birds and the wild beasts are
fed, you will hear him saying: All these look toward you, to give them
their food in due season. And if you should say, wherefore the cattle? he
answers you, that these also are for you: For he who causes to spring up,
he says, grass for the cattle, and green herb for the service of men.
What is the use of the moon, hear him saying: He made the moon for
seasons. That all things visible and invisible are made, this too he
taught clearly: He himself, he says, spoke, and they were made; he
himself commanded, and they were created.

And that there shall be a loosing of death, he teaches, saying: My God
shall redeem my soul from the hand of Hades, when he receives me. Whence
our body came to be, and where again it departs, he says: He remembered
that we are =55.536= dust, and he shall return to his dust. Wherefore all
these things? For you: For with glory and honor did you crown him, and
you set him over the works of your hands. Have men something common with
the angels, and this he says: You diminished him a little below the
angels. And concerning the love of God he says: As a father pities sons,
the Lord pitied those who fear him. Concerning those who will succeed us
after these things, and that unruffled end: Return, my soul, into your
rest. Wherefore the heaven is so great, he will say: The heavens declare
the glory of God. Wherefore night and day? Not that they may shine and
give rest only, but that they may also instruct. For they are not
speeches, nor words, whose voices are not heard. How the sea lies around
the earth: The abyss as a garment, its vesture. For so the Hebrew has it.
Taking a beginning from the things said, you will know also all the
rest: the things concerning Christ, the things concerning the
resurrection, the things concerning the life to come, the things
concerning rest, the things concerning punishment, those according to the
moral word, all the things concerning the doctrines, and you will find
the book filled with ten thousand goods.

And if you fall into temptations, you will take from here much comfort;
and if into sin, you will find myriad medicines laid up here; if into
poverty, if into affliction, you will see many harbors. And if you are
just, you will reap from there much profit; and if a sinner, much the
comfort. For whether you are just and suffer dreadful things, you will
hear: For your sake we are put to death all the day; we were counted as
sheep of slaughter. All these things came upon us, and we did not forget
you. And if your achievements lift you up, he says to you: Enter not into
judgment with your servant, for before you shall be justified every
living thing; and you will straightway be humbled. And if you are a
sinner and have despaired of yourself, you will hear him continually
singing: Today if you hear his voice, harden not your hearts, as in the
provocation; and you will rise up quickly. And though you have a diadem
on your head, you will learn that A king is not saved by much strength,
and a giant shall not be saved in the multitude of his might; and you
will be able to keep measure. And if you are rich and in high estate, you
will hear again: Woe to those who trust in their strength, and glory in
the multitude of their riches! and that Man, as grass are his days; as
the flower of the field, so shall he flourish; and you will hold nothing
of the things on the earth to be great. For the most brilliant of all,
glory and dominion, when it is so cheap, what other thing of the things
on the earth would be worth a word? But have you fallen into despondency?
Say: Why are you downcast, my soul, and why do you trouble me? Hope in
God, for I shall give thanks to him. But do you see some flourishing
beyond their worth? Hear him saying: As grass they shall quickly be dried
up, and as green herbs they shall quickly fall. Do you see the just and
sinners chastened? Behold that the cause is not the same: For many, he
says, are the stripes of the sinner; but upon the just not so, but Many
are the afflictions of the just, and out of them all the Lord will
deliver them; and again: The death of sinners is evil; and, Precious
before the Lord is the death of his holy ones. Say these things
continually, be instructed from here. For each of these words is a
boundless sea of meanings; for we have run through them simply; but if
you should wish to search out the things said with exactness, you will
see much the wealth both from these and from the other Scriptures
likewise.

βʹ. But since reading has pain and much burdensomeness, let us lodge in
the things said before, that you may at once both delight your soul with
songs and steal away the pain. Let the mouth then sing, and let the mind
be instructed; not even is this itself a small thing. For when it allows
not envying, nor being grieved and desponding unseasonably, nor holding
wealth as anything, nor affliction, nor poverty, nor even life itself to
be anything, it frees you from all the passions. =55.537= And especially,
if we instruct the tongue to sing, the soul will be ashamed, willing to
sing the contrary things of this one. We need therefore the harp of
David, that we may sing to the soul the divine incantations, both those
from here and those of the good deeds. Otherwise, if we do the one only,
and hearing the song war against the one singing these things through the
deeds, the medicine will be for judgment to us, and the madness of the
devil will become more wild. For before hearing the wicked demon fears,
lest perhaps hearing we should set ourselves right; but when, having
heard, we remain the same, even this is taken away. Let us sing then the
song from the deeds, that we may cast out the harder sin of the demon,
and draw to ourselves the grace of the Spirit. For nothing, nothing so
raises up a soul, and wings it, and frees it from the earth, and looses
the bonds of the body, and makes it philosophize, and laugh at all the
things of life, as the melodiousness from the psalms, and a song composed
with rhythm. For since God saw the most of men to be more slothful, and
not gladly enduring the labor from there, wishing to make the pain more
desirable, and the sensation of the labor to be cut short, he stirred the
tongue of the blessed David to set forth the psalms distinctly, and to
mix melody with the prophecy, that all being delighted by the rhythm of
the melody, with much readiness they might send up to him the holy
hymns. For so our nature is suited to songs and melodies gladly and
sweetly, so that even infants, wailing and being vexed, are so lulled to
sleep. For certainly the nurses, bearing them in their arms, often going
out, and singing over them some childish songs, so lull their eyelids to
sleep. For this reason also travelers, often driving the beasts of burden
at midday, singing, do this, comforting the toil of the journey with
those songs. And not travelers only, but also husbandmen treading the
winepresses, and gathering the vintage, and tending the vines, and
working at something else, often sing; and sailors rowing do this. And
now also women weaving, and separating the threads entangled with the
shuttle, often indeed each by herself, and often again all with one
accord singing some one melody. Women do this, and husbandmen, and
sailors, striving to comfort with the song the pain from the works; since
the soul, if it should hear melody and song, can bear all things the
vexatious and painful more easily. Since therefore our soul is suited to
this form of delight, that the demons bringing in fornicating songs may
not overturn all, God fortified against them the psalms, so that the
thing should be at once both pleasure and profit. For from the songs from
without are harm and destruction, and many dreadful things would be
brought in, the soul become by these songs more wanton and more lawless,
and softer and weaker; but upon the spiritual ones, much the gain, great
the profit, great the sanctification, and it would become the subject
matter of all philosophy, the words cleansing the soul, and the Holy
Spirit hovering over the soul singing such things. Let us sing then to
the soul the psalms full of philosophy, that we may be led little by
little also to the higher things. For when in the psalms you have
learned, then also you will know hymns, a diviner thing. For the psalms
have all things, but the hymns again nothing human. For the powers above
hymn, they do not psalm: For a fair hymn is not, he says, in the mouth of
a sinner. =55.538= For which reason Paul also counsels, saying: Singing
in our hearts to the Lord, not simply with the mouth, but with attention.
For this is to sing to God; that other, to the air; for the voice is
simply dispersed. There is no need of place, no need of time, but in
every place, and at every season, it is permitted to sing in the mind.
And whether you walk in the marketplace, or are on the roads, or sit with
friends, it is permitted to rouse the soul, it is permitted to cry out
while silent. So Moses also cried, and God heard. And if you are a
craftsman, sitting in the workshop and working, you will be able to sing.
And if you are a soldier, attending at the judgment seat, you will be
able to do this same thing. For it is permitted to sing also without
voice, the mind resounding within. For we do not sing to man, but to God,
who is able both to hear hearts, and to enter into the secret things of
our mind. But since the spiritual men, those having the gifts of the
Spirit, pray always for their neighbors, and offer the supplications to
God with compunctions and groanings, let us do this also, and entreat God
each day, both for ourselves, and for our brethren. And how is it
possible to entreat God? From having the life straight, and a conscience
purified. And these it is nowhere else possible to set right, but in
meditating in the law of the Lord day and night. And let no one say to
me: Before the interpretation I did not know the power of the psalms. For
even before the interpretation, one verse suffices to plant much
philosophy in anyone soever wishing to attend, and to correct into
doctrine, and to benefit unto life in the greatest things. For whenever
you say, Blessed is the man who fears the Lord, and being able to know
what it is that you say, you will emulate not the one in dominion, nor
the one being rich, nor the one having bright houses, nor the one turning
about in palaces, nor the very one having the diadem encircling his head,
nor any other, but the one in reverence, the one in philosophy, the one
being in the fear of God, not only for the things to come, but also for
the present things themselves. For even here this one is stronger than
that one.

Let us meditate then also to entreat God; let us learn how one must make
the supplication. For God seeks not beauty of tongue, nor compact of
words, but the beauty of the soul. And if it utters the things that seem
good to him, having accomplished all, it departs. For if one keeping
company with a wonderful man bears much fruit from the association, much
more with God continually through stretched-out prayer. For even if one
being a sinner should pray with humility, which is a part of virtue, he
will be able to accomplish great things. Just as therefore also the
publican (for even from a bare word he accomplished all), since his mind
was well prepared. And how was it prepared? He was distraught, he smote
his breast, nor did he endure to look up into heaven. And if you too pray
thus, you will make the soul lighter than wings. Let the prayer then be
tame, calm, having a fair and soft countenance with meekness, and not
made against enemies. This prayer is worthy of the heavens, this tongue
worthy of the angels. For thus also we shall be heard for ourselves, when
for those doing injustice and behaving insolently we offer the
supplication to God. And if we thus tune the soul, we shall be able with
boldness to say also we to God: Have mercy on me, and hear my prayer; and
he will readily nod assent, and will provide for us the kingdom of the
heavens; which may we all be counted worthy to attain, by the grace and
philanthropy of our Lord and God and Savior Jesus Christ, to whom is the
glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
