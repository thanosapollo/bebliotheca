* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

After the elder Gordian had been killed in Africa together with his son, as
Maximinus was coming raging toward the city to avenge their having been named
Augusti, the senate, badly frightened, rushed to the Temple of Concord on the
seventh day before the Ides of July, during the Apollinarian Games, looking
for some remedy against the fury of that most wicked man.

When two men of consular rank, and distinguished men besides, Maximus and
Balbinus, had entered the Curia (most writers omit Maximus in the history and
put the name of Pupienus in his place, though both Dexippus and Arrianus say
that Maximus and Balbinus were chosen against Maximinus after the Gordians),
of whom one was famed for kindness, the other for courage and severity, and
they showed especially on their faces their fear at Maximinus' coming, while
the consul was reporting on other business, the man who was to give the first
opinion began as follows:

/Petty things occupy you, and at this most burning hour we are treating almost
old wives' business in the Curia. What use is it to deal with the restoration
of temples, the ornament of a basilica, the Baths of Titus, or finishing the
Amphitheatre, when Maximinus hangs over us, the man you named an enemy along
with me before? The two Gordians, who had been our defence, have been killed,
and at present there is no help by which we may breathe again. Come then,
conscript fathers, name emperors. Why delay? Do not, while each of you is
afraid on his own, be crushed in fear rather than in courage./

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

By these shouts and others like them Maximus and Balbinus were made emperors.

They left the senate, climbed the Capitol first, and offered sacrifice. Then
they called the people to the Rostra. After they had spoken there of the
senate's decision and of their own election, the Roman people, together with
soldiers who had happened to gather, shouted,

/We all ask for Gordian as Caesar/

This was Gordian's grandson by his daughter, fourteen years old, as most say.
He was seized at once and, by a new kind of senatorial decree (a decree of the
senate had been passed that same day), he was brought into the Curia and named
Caesar.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

The emperors' first motion was that the two Gordians should be named gods.
Some think only one was named, the elder. I remember reading in the books
Junius Cordus wrote at such length that both were enrolled among the gods. The
elder ended his life by a noose; the younger was consumed in war, and he
certainly deserves greater reverence, because war snatched him away. After
these motions the city prefecture was conferred on Sabinus, a man of weight
and of a character matching Maximus, and the praetorian prefecture on Pinarius
Valens.

But before I speak of their deeds I want to say something of their characters
and birth, not as Junius Cordus chased down every detail, but as Suetonius
Tranquillus and Valerius Marcellinus did. Curius Fortunatianus, who wrote out
this whole history, touched on few things. Cordus wrote so much that he even
set down many things that were less honourable.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Maximus' father was also Maximus, one of the commons. Some say a blacksmith,
others a maker of coaches and wagons. He had this son by a wife named Prima.
The boy had four brothers and four sisters, all of whom died before puberty.

When Maximus was born, an eagle is said to have thrown down beef, and a great
deal of it, into their chamber, which opened by a narrow impluvium. It lay
there, and no one dared touch it for fear of religion. Then the eagle took it
up again and carried it to the nearby shrine of Jupiter Praestes. At the time
it seemed to hold no omen, but empire later proved it had not been done
without cause.

He spent all his boyhood in the house of his kinsman Pinarius, whom he raised
to the praetorian prefecture as soon as he became emperor. He gave little time
to the grammarian and the rhetor, because he always studied military valour
and severity. At last he was a military tribune and commanded many units.
Afterwards he held the praetorship at the expense of Pescennia Marcellina, who
took him up and raised him in place of a son. Then he held the proconsulship
of Bithynia, next of Greece, and third of Narbo. Sent out besides as legate,
he crushed the Sarmatians in Illyricum. Transferred from there to the Rhine,
he waged a campaign against the Germans with fair success. After this, as city
prefect, he was approved as most prudent, most able, and most severe. So the
senate conferred empire on this man as if he were a noble, which was not
lawful for a new family. They conferred it all the same. Everyone in the
senate then confessed that no one fitter ought to receive the name of emperor.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And since most people want even the smaller things: he ate greedily, drank
very little, and was extremely sparing in sexual affairs. At home and abroad
he was always so severe that he even got the nickname the Stern. His face was
most grave and parched. He was tall, of the healthiest constitution,
forbidding in manners, and yet just, and never, through to the end of his
dealings, either inhuman or unmerciful. When asked he always pardoned, and he
was not angry except where it was proper for him to be angry. He never lent
himself to factions. He held fast to his judgement and trusted himself rather
than others. The senate therefore loved him much, and the people held him in
fear, because they knew his censor-like prefecture and saw that it could grow
more violent once he held empire.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Balbinus was of the highest nobility and twice consul, governor of countless
provinces. He had ruled Asia, Africa, Bithynia, Galatia, Pontus, the Thracian
lands, and the Gauls in civil administrations, and he had sometimes led an
army, but he had been lesser in war than in civil affairs. Yet by kindness, by
too great holiness and modesty, he had won himself enormous love.

He came of a most ancient family, as he himself used to say, tracing his
origin from Cornelius Balbus Theophanes, who had earned citizenship through
Gnaeus Pompeius. That Balbus was the most noble man of his own country and
likewise a writer of history.

He was equally tall, conspicuous in quality of body, and excessive in
pleasures, which the abundance of his riches even helped, for he was rich from
his ancestors and had gathered much himself by inheritances.

He was famous in eloquence, outstanding in poetry among the poets of his time,
greedy of wine, food, and sexual affairs, elegant in dress, and nothing was
lacking to make him commendable to the people. He was lovable even to the
senate.

This is what we have learned of both men's lives. Some have thought they too
should be compared as Sallust compares Cato and Caesar, so as to call the one
severe, the other merciful, that one good, this one constant, that one giving
nothing, this one overflowing

:PROPERTIES:

:END:

with every kind of wealth, they would say. So much for their characters and
birth. When all imperial honours and insignia had been decreed, and they had
received tribunician power, proconsular right, the chief pontificate, and even
the name of Father of the Fatherland, they entered on empire. But while they
were performing the rites on the Capitol, the Roman people spoke against
Maximus' rule. Ordinary people feared his severity, which they believed was
most welcome to the senate and most hostile to themselves. So it came about,
as we have said, that they asked for the young Gordian as emperor, and he was
made so at once. They were not allowed to go to the Palatine packed with armed
men until they named Gordian's grandson with the title of Caesar. When this
was done and the rites celebrated, stage-plays given and circus games and even
a gladiatorial show, Maximus took vows on the Capitol and was sent to the war
against Maximinus with a huge army. The praetorians remained at Rome.

It remains to tell briefly where the custom came from, that emperors setting
out to war should give a gladiatorial show and hunts. Many say that among the
ancients this was a vow against the enemy, so that Nemesis (that is, a certain
force of Fortune) might sate herself on the blood of citizens offered under
the guise of fights. Others hand this down in writing, and I think it nearer
the truth: Romans about to go to war ought to have seen fights and wounds and
steel and naked men closing with one another, so that in war they would not
fear armed enemies or shudder at wounds and blood.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

When Maximus had set out to war, the praetorians remained at Rome. Between
them and the people there was so great a riot that it came to civil war. The
greatest part of the city of Rome was set on fire, temples were defiled, and
all the streets were stained with blood, since Balbinus, a gentler man, could
not quiet the riot.

When he came out in public he stretched out his hands to each person in turn
and almost suffered a blow from a stone. Others say he was even struck with a
club. He would not have quieted the tumult if he had not brought the child
Gordian, clothed in purple, before the people, set on the neck of a very tall
man. At the sight of him the people and the soldiers were so far calmed that
for love of him they returned to concord. Never was anyone at that age so
loved by the merit of his grandfather and uncle, who had ended their lives in
Africa for the Roman people against Maximinus. So much does the memory of good
deeds avail among Romans.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

When Maximus had set out to war, the senate sent through all the regions men
of consular, praetorian, quaestorian, aedilician, and even tribunician rank,
so that each city should prepare grain, arms, ramparts, and walls, and
Maximinus might be worn down through the several towns. It was then ordered
that everything from the fields should be gathered into the cities, so that
the public enemy should find nothing. Letters were sent besides to all the
provinces by the dispatch riders, and whoever had helped Maximinus was to be
counted among the enemies.

Meanwhile at Rome riots arose again between the people and the soldiers.
Balbinus posted a thousand edicts and was not heard. The veterans withdrew
into the Praetorian Camp with the praetorians themselves, and the people began
to besiege them. They would never have been brought back to friendship if the
people had not cut the water-pipes. In the city, however, before it was said
that the soldiers were coming in peace, tiles were thrown from the roofs and
all the vessels in the houses were hurled down. The greater part of the city
therefore perished, and the wealth of many, for brigands mixed themselves with
the soldiers to plunder what they knew where to find.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

While this was going on at Rome, Maximus, or Pupienus, was preparing war at
Ravenna with huge equipment. He feared Maximinus very violently, and he used
to say very often that he was waging war not against a man but against a
Cyclops. Maximinus was so defeated at Aquileia that he was killed by his own
men. His head and his son's were brought to Ravenna, and Maximus sent them on
to Rome. The devotion of the people of Aquileia for the Romans must not be
passed over here. They are even said to have shorn the hair of their women for
bowstrings to shoot arrows.

Such was Balbinus' joy (and he had feared more) that he made a hecatomb as
soon as Maximinus' head was brought. A hecatomb is a sacrifice of this kind: a
hundred turf altars are built in one place, and at them a hundred swine and a
hundred sheep are slaughtered. Then, if it is an imperial sacrifice, a hundred
lions, a hundred eagles, and other animals of this kind are struck down by the
hundred. The Greeks also are said to have done this once when they were
suffering from a plague, and it is established that many emperors celebrated
it.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

When this had been done, Balbinus, with the highest congratulation, was
awaiting Maximus as he returned from the Ravenna district with his army intact
and his supplies. For Maximinus had been defeated by the townsmen of Aquileia,
by the few soldiers who were there, and by the consulars Crispinus and
Menophilus, who had been sent by the senate. Maximus himself had gone to
Aquileia only so that he might leave everything safe and intact as far as the
Alps, and check any remnants of barbarians who had favoured Maximinus.

Finally twenty senatorial envoys were sent to him, whose names are in Cordus
(among them four consulars, eight praetorians, eight quaestorians), with
crowns and a senatorial decree in which gilded equestrian statues were voted
him. At this Balbinus was somewhat angry. He said that Maximus had laboured
less than he, since he himself had crushed such great wars at home, while the
other had sat idle at Ravenna. But wishing carried so much force that Maximus,
because he had set out against Maximinus, was even voted a victory he did not
know had been won. Taking over Maximinus' army, Maximus came to the city with
huge pomp and a crowd. The soldiers grieved that they had lost the emperor
whom they themselves had made and now had those whom the senate had selected.
The grief could not be hidden. It appeared on each man's brow, and already
they did not even abstain from words, although Maximus had often said among
the soldiers that there ought to be forgetfulness of the past, had given large
pay, and had dismissed the auxiliaries to the places they had chosen. But
soldiers' minds once steeped in hatred cannot be reined in. When they had
heard the senate's acclamations, which touched the soldiers, they became
sharper against Maximus and Balbinus, thinking every day among themselves whom
they ought to make emperors.

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

This was the end the good emperors had, unworthy of their life and character.
There was nothing braver than Maximus, or Pupienus, nor anything kinder than
Balbinus, which can be understood from the thing itself, for the senate would
not, when it had the power, choose bad men.

To this is added that they were tested by many honours and offices. One, twice
consul and prefect, the other consul and prefect, had come to empire in old
age, lovable to the senate and even to the people, who already somewhat feared
Maximus. These are the things which we have in large part gathered from
Herodian, a Greek writer, concerning Maximus.

But many have said that Maximinus was defeated at Aquileia not by Maximus but
by the emperor Pupienus, and that he himself was killed with Balbinus, so that
they pass over the name of Maximus. Such is the ignorance, or the usurpation,
of historians competing among themselves, that many want the same Maximus to
be called Pupienus, although Herodian, a writer of the times of his own life,
says Maximus, not Pupienus, and although Dexippus too, a writer of the Greeks,
says that Maximus and Balbinus were made emperors against Maximinus after the
two Gordians, and that Maximinus was defeated by Maximus, not by Pupienus.

To this is added the ignorance of writers who say that the little Gordian was
praetorian prefect, many not knowing that he was often carried on a neck to be
shown to the soldiers.

Maximus and Balbinus, however, reigned one year, while Maximinus had reigned
with his son, as some say, for three years, as others for two.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Balbinus' house is shown even now at Rome in the Carinae, large and powerful
and possessed by his family down to this time. Maximus, whom most think
Pupienus, was of the utmost poverty but of the fullest valour.

Under them the Carpi fought against the Moesians. There was also the beginning
of a Scythian war, and there was also the destruction of Istria at that time,
or, as Dexippus says, of the Istrican city.

Dexippus praises Balbinus enough and says that he met the soldiers with a
brave spirit and was killed without fearing death, and that he was trained in
all disciplines. Maximus, however, he denies was a man of the kind that most
Greeks have said. He adds besides that the hatred of the people of Aquileia
against Maximinus was so great that they made bowstrings from their women's
hair and so shot arrows. Dexippus and Herodian, who have pursued this history
of the emperors, say that Maximus and Balbinus were emperors, chosen by the
senate against Maximinus after the death of the two Gordians in Africa, with
whom the third Gordian, a boy, was also chosen. But among most Latin writers I
do not find the name of Maximus, and I find Pupienus as emperor with Balbinus,
to such a degree that the same Pupienus is said to have fought with Maximinus
at Aquileia, although, as the historians named above assert, not even Maximus
is shown to have fought against Maximinus, but to have stayed at Ravenna and
heard there that the victory had been accomplished. So it seems to me that
Pupienus is the same man who is called Maximus.

This letter proves that Pupienus is the same man who is called Maximus by
most. In these times Pupienus is not easily found among the Greeks, and
Maximus is not easily found among the Latins, and the things that were done
against Maximinus are said to have been done now by Pupienus, now by Maximus.
