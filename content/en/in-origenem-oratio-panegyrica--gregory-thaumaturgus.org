Let this speech of mine then be a thanksgiving; I would not wish to say it to
God who is of the universe; and yet from there all the beginnings of goods
come to us, and from there we must also begin our thanksgivings or hymns and
praises. But even if I should offer myself entire, not such as I now am,
profane and unclean, mixed and stained with all-accursed and unclean evil, but

4 But the praises and hymns toward the king and guardian of all, the
sufficient fountain of all goods, we will leave to the one who even in this
heals our weakness and who alone is able to fill up what is lacking, the
protector of our souls and savior, his firstborn Word, the craftsman and
governor of all; it being possible for him alone, both for himself and for
all, for each privately and for all together at once, to send up to the Father
continual and unceasing thanksgivings. Because he, being the truth and the
wisdom


and power of the Father of the universe himself, and besides being in him and
simply united to him, there is no way that either through forgetfulness or
unwisely or by some weakness, as if somehow alienated from him, he will either
fail to reach the power of the praise, or will reach it and yet willingly
(which it is not lawful to say) leave the Father unpraised; it being possible
for this one alone to fulfill most perfectly all the desert of the praises
that belong to him; whom the Father of the universe himself, having made one
toward himself, through him all but going around himself, would honor and be
honored, in a manner, by the power wholly equal to his own; which first and
only among all that exist the only-begotten of his has obtained, the God Word
who is in him; all the others being able to be thankful and pious only in this
way, if we, bringing upon him alone, in return for all the goods from the
Father to us, dedicate the power of worthy thanksgiving, having confessed that
this is the one road of piety, all remembrance of the cause of the universe
through him. Therefore of the sufficient providence over all, which cares for
us in the greatest things and in the least and is even brought forward to
this, let that one be confessed worthy, a sufficient Word for thanksgivings
and hymns, being most perfect and living, and being the living Word of the
first mind itself. But let this ours be a thanksgiving most of all among all
men to this sacred man; and if I should also wish to speak still further, even
of those who are not seen, yet are more divine and who care for men, to this
one who from childhood by some great judgment was allotted to manage and nurse
and oversee me, a sacred angel of God, he who nourishes me from my youth, says
that man dear to God, speaking of his own, clearly. But he, being great,
analogously having been allotted some greatest one, either another whoever he
might be, or perhaps even the angel of great counsel himself, the common
savior of all, by perfection already as sole guardian for himself, I do not
know this clearly, except that he both knows and praises his own great one,
whoever he may be; and we, besides the common governor of all men, also this
one, whoever

the most principal of all) by his management, one belonging to me neither by
race and some human blood, nor otherwise dwelling near or being one of the
neighbors, nor even of the same nation at all, these being what becomes a
pretext of friendship and acquaintance for most men; but, to speak concisely,
unknown, alien, estranged, standing very far apart from one another, as much
as nations in between and mountains and rivers keep us apart, having brought
us to the same place by the truly divine and wise foresight, he contrived this
saving meeting for me; providing for this from above, I think, from first
birth and upbringing. But how, it would be long to go through, not only for
one being exact and trying to leave nothing out; but even if, omitting most
things, I should wish to mention a few of the more principal all at once.

5 For the first upbringings from birth were under parents, and the ancestral
customs that had gone astray; from which neither another, I think, expected we
would be freed, nor was there any hope for me either, being a child and
without reason, and under a superstitious father. Then loss of a father and
orphanhood, which perhaps was even the beginning of the knowledge of the true
for me. For then first I was transferred to the saving and true Word, I do not
know how, compelled rather than willing. For what judgment was there for me,
being fourteen years old? Except from that time this sacred Word somehow to
sojourn with me


began at once, as indeed the common reason of all men was just then being
fulfilled, yet it sojourned then first. Which even is no small thing, if not
of old, at least now as I reckon it I set down as a sign of the sacred and
wonderful providence about me, this concurrence thus numbered with the years;
so that all that preceded this age, as many as were works of error, might be
given over to infancy and unreason, and the sacred Word might not be given in
vain to a soul not yet rational, but to one already become rational, if not of
a divine and pure word, at least not deserted of the fear according to this
Word, but together both the human and the divine Word might begin in me, the
one helping with a power unspoken by me but proper to itself, the other being
helped. Which, as I reckon it, fills me at once with gladness and with fear,
being magnified by the advancement, yet fearing lest somehow, having been
deemed worthy of such things, I may likewise fail of the end. But I do not
know how the speech has lingered for me in this part, wishing to recount in
order the wonderful management toward this man, yet hurrying nonetheless first
and speaking briefly to these things that follow, not as rendering a praise
owed to the one who thus managed, or a thanksgiving and piety (let us not be
burdensome, naming them thus, yet saying nothing worthy); but as making a
narrative and a confession or some of the more decent of these names. It
seemed good to the mother left alone from the parents to care for us that we,
being educated in other things, as children supposedly not ignobly both born
and reared, should also attend a rhetor, as being about to become rhetors. And
indeed we attended, and those then judging said we would not be long in
becoming rhetors; I myself neither know how to say this, nor would I wish to;
but there was no speech of these things, nor yet any laying down of the causes
able to bring us here. But the divine pedagogue and true guardian, being
sleepless, neither when my own were intending it nor when I myself was eager,
was upon it, having put it into the mind of one of my teachers, who had
otherwise been entrusted to educate me in the Roman tongue (not as one who
would come to the peak, but so that I might not be altogether inexperienced
even of this tongue; and he happened not to be inexperienced of laws); having
put this into his mind, he urged me through him to learn thoroughly the laws
of the Romans. And that man did this even persistently; and I for my part was
persuaded, gratifying the man rather than being a lover of the art. And he,
having taken me as a hearer, began to teach with ambition; and he uttered
something which has turned out for me the truest of all things: that the
learning of the laws would be a very great provision for the journey for me
(for that is the name he named), whether I should wish to be some rhetor of
those who contend in the lawcourts, or some other as well. He uttered it thus,
stretching the speech toward human things; but to me he seems simply to have
prophesied under some more divine inspiration, beyond his own conjecture. For
since I was being educated, willing and unwilling, in these laws, bonds of a
sort had already been laid down, and the cause and starting-point of the road
here was the city of the Berytians; and it is a city not far distant from
those here, somewhat more Roman, and trusted to be a school of these laws. And
this sacred man too, from Egypt, from the city of the Alexandrians, where he
happened to have his hearth before, other affairs were moving and transferring
even him to this place, as if to meet us. I myself neither know how to give
the causes of these things either, and I will willingly pass them by.
Nevertheless, not yet at least was anything so necessary for my arrival here
and the intertwining with the man, as far as our laws went, it being possible
even to go abroad to the city of the Romans. How then was this too procured? A
kinsman of mine by marriage, the husband of my sister, the then ruler of the
Palestinians, having taken this man suddenly unwilling and alone, separated
from his bedfellow, brought him here, to help together and share the labors of
the ruler of the nation; for he was some man of law, and perhaps still is;
who, having come together with him, was about before long to receive his wife
sent for, being grievously


and unwillingly separated from her, and drawing us too along together with
her. Suddenly then, I do not know how, when we were intending to go abroad,
but rather to go abroad somewhere else than here, a soldier stood over us
bearing a command, to escort and keep safe our sister as she came to take her
husband, and to bring us too as fellow-travelers together with her; both to
gratify the kinsman by marriage, and especially the sister, so that she might
not be unseemly or more hesitant toward the journey, having honored her with
the household servants themselves and the relatives, and having accomplished
some other not small thing of what was serviceable, if we should come to the
city of the Berytians, working out there the lesson of the laws. Everything
therefore was moving us, the reasonable toward the sister, our own lesson, and
besides even the soldier (since this too must be mentioned), bearing authority
for the use of more of the public vehicles, and tokens of a greater number for
our sake rather than for the sister alone. These things appearing; but the
things not appearing yet more true, the communion with this man, the true
lessons through him about the things of the Word, the benefit of our souls for
salvation, were leading us here, we being blind and not knowing, yet savingly
for us. Therefore it was not the soldier, but some divine fellow-traveler and
good escort and guardian, who through all this life as through a long journey
keeps us safe, who, having passed by the other things and Berytus, for the
sake of which we most thought we were setting out here, brought us and set us
here; doing and moving everything, until by every device he should bind us to
this one who is the cause of many goods for us. And he, having come through so
much, handing over the management to this man, the divine angel somewhere here
perhaps even rested, not from some toil or labor (for the race of divine
ministers is untiring), but because he had handed us over to a man who would
fulfill, if possible, all providence and care.

6 And he, having received us from the first day, the day truly first for me,
the most honorable of all days, if one must say it, when first the true sun
began to rise for me, first, like some wild beasts or fishes or some birds,
that have fallen into the nets or into the seines, yet try to slip out and run
away, and wish to withdraw from him to Berytus or to the fatherland, he
contrived to bind us in every way; turning all speeches and moving every rope
(this indeed the saying) and putting forth all his powers; praising philosophy
and the lovers of philosophy with long praises and with many that were
fitting, saying that these alone truly live the life that belongs to rational
beings, those who practice living rightly, both knowing themselves first who
they are, and then the things that are truly goods, which a man must pursue,
and the things truly evils, from which one must run away; and blaming
ignorance and all the ignorant; and these are many, as many as, like cattle,
being blind in mind, not even having recognized this very thing which they
are, wandering like the irrational, neither themselves knowing at all what
good or evil ever is, nor wishing to learn, as they rush upon it as upon a
good and are fluttered about money and reputations and honors from the many
and the good condition of the body, setting these both at much and at
everything, and the arts, as many as can procure these, and the lives, as many
as will furnish these, armies and the forensic art and the thorough learning
of the laws; these things which most of all were shaking us, speaking even
very artfully, of the most sovereign, he says, of the things in us, reason,
having neglected it. I am not able now to say how many such voices he sounded
forth, urging us to do philosophy, not of one day only, but of as many more of
the first as we were coming to him, struck as by some dart by the speech from
him even from first youth (for it was somehow mixed even with some sweet
grace and persuasion and some necessity), yet still somehow turning and
reckoning, and having persisted in doing philosophy,


not yet wholly persuaded, yet somehow not able to withdraw again, and always
being drawn toward him as by some greater necessities, his speeches. For
wholly he used to say that it is not even possible to be pious toward the
master of the universe (this which alone of all animals on earth man has been
honored and deemed worthy to have, and reasonably every one whatsoever, both
wise and ignorant, clings to this, whoever has not utterly lost the notions
under some madness of mind), so then that it is not even possible to be pious
at all, speaking rightly, for one who has not done philosophy; until, pouring
over us many such other speeches upon others, like some enchanted, at the end
by his arts he simply set us unmoved, bringing us and settling us by his
speeches, I do not know how, with some divine power.

For he also struck into us a goad of friendship, not something easy to fight
down, but sharp and most effective, of courtesy and of the good disposition,
as much as some well-wishing showed itself to us in the very voices of him
addressing and conversing; not trying to go around us otherwise with speeches,
but with a right and humane and most kind purpose both to save us and to make
us partners of the goods from philosophy, and of the others especially, as
many as the divine, beyond the many, or perhaps even beyond all men now, has
granted to him alone, the teacher of piety, the saving Word, who visits many
and works upon all, as many as he may meet (for there is not anything that
will stand against him, being and going to be king of all), yet hidden and not
known either easily or even with difficulty to the many, so as for those asked
to have something clear to say about him. So then some spark, having struck in
the midst of our soul, was kindled and blazed up, both the love toward the
sacred Word himself, most attractive of all by unspeakable beauty, the most
lovely, and the love toward this man his friend and advocate; by which
especially, wounded, I was persuaded to neglect all the affairs or lessons
that seemed to belong to us, both the others and even my beautiful laws
themselves, and fatherland and household, both those present here and those to
whom we had gone abroad. And one thing was dear to me and beloved, philosophy
and this divine man her leader; and the soul of Jonathan was knit to David.
This I read later in the sacred writings, but I suffered it first no less
clearly than it is said, although it has been most clearly oracled. For
Jonathan was not simply knit to David, but the most sovereign things
themselves, soul, these which not even when the appearing and seen men have
been separated will themselves be forced by some device to be separated;
unwillingly, in no way. For a soul is free and not shut in in any manner, not
even if you keep it locked in a little house. For it is by nature, at least in
the first account, wherever the mind may be; and if it also seems to you to be
in the little house, by some second account it appears there to you; in no way
prevented from being there on this account, wherever it may wish to be, or
rather being altogether only there both able and reasonably trusted to be,
wherever and toward whatever the works proper to it alone according to itself
may be. Did he not then make clear this most evident thing which I suffered in
very brief words, that the soul of Jonathan had been knit to the soul of
David? These things which unwillingly, as I said, will in no way be overcome
to be separated, and willingly will not easily wish it. For I do not think the
authority of the dissolving of these sacred bonds of friends is upon the
worse, which is of many turns and easier to change purpose, upon which not
even the binding down happened at the beginning to it alone; but upon the
better, which is lasting and not easily shaken, upon which it rather was both
to work the bonds and this sacred knot. At any rate it was not the soul of
David that was knit by the divine Word to the soul of Jonathan; but conversely
the soul of the worse, having suffered this, is said to be knit to the soul of
David. For the better, being self-sufficient, would not choose to be knit to
its own worse, but the worse, needing help from the better, being knit to the
better had to be fastened on; so that


the one remaining in itself may enjoy no harm from the communion toward the
worse, and the unordered from itself, bound down and fitted to the better,
having harmed nothing, may be overcome by the necessities of the bonds toward
the better. Therefore it belonged to the superior to work the bonds, but not
to the lesser; and to be bound belonged to the worse, so as not even to have
authority somehow to be released from the bonds. With some such necessities
this David, having bound us tight, holds us both now and from that time, we
being unable, even if we should wish, to be released from his bonds. So then
even if we should go abroad, he will not let our souls go, having them thus
knit according to the divine writing.

7 Except that, having taken us thus from the beginning and gone around us in
every way, when the greater part had been accomplished for him and we seemed
to remain, from there, as if some good farmer some idle land, either not
well-soiled at all, but some salty and burnt, rocky underneath and crumbling,
or not wholly barren nor even ungifted, but even much-natured, yet fallow
nonetheless and neglected, made coarse with thorns and wild bushes and hard to
work; or as some planter a plant, either wild and barren of tame fruits, yet
not wholly useless, if someone by the planting art should bring and graft a
tame shoot, having split the middle, then joining and binding, until the two,
having grown together as one, are nourished (for thus you may see some mixed
tree, bastard yet fruitful from unfruitful, bringing up fruits of the fair
olive upon wild roots); or wild, yet not useless to a craftsman planter, or
even tame, otherwise fruitful, yet again by lack of art unpruned and unwatered
and parched, choked by the many superfluous shoots growing at random, hindered
from being perfected in the growth and bearing the fruit by one another;
having taken some such as we, and going around by his own farming art and
observing not only the things seen by all and looked at on the surface,
digging up and making trial of the inmost, asking and proposing and hearing
those who answered, when he observed something not useless and unprofitable
and ineffectual in us, he hoed, he turned, he watered, he moved everything, he
applied all the art and care from himself, and he worked us; thorns and burs
and all the race of wild herbs or plants, as much as our soul, running to
wood, sent up and gave forth in agitation, as being unordered and rash,
cutting out and taking away all by the refutations and by the preventing;
laying hold of us even very Socratically at times and tripping us with the
speech, if somewhere he saw us wholly bolting like some of the wild horses,
leaping outside the road and running around much at random, until by some
persuasion and necessity, as by a bit, by the speech from our mouth, he set us
quiet for him; with difficulty for us and not without pain at first, as being
unused and not yet practiced at following the speech, applying the speeches
from himself, but cleansing out nonetheless. Where

he had made us fit and well prepared for reception of the speeches of the
truth, then indeed, as to earth well worked and soft and ready to grow the
seeds brought upon it, he brought them unsparingly; making both the sowing of
the seeds timely, and all the remaining care timely, working each thing
suitably and with the things proper to the speech; whatever of the soul was
blunt and bastard, either having grown thus or even being thickened by
superfluous nourishments of the body, sharpening and thinning it by the fine
speeches and manners of the rational affections, which, being unrolled from
the simplest first things, successive and turning in many ways, go forward
into some unmanageable and hard-to-unwind entanglement, raising us as if
sleeping, and we being taught to hold always to the things set forth, in no
way slipping down either by length or by fineness. And as much as was


indiscriminate and rash, both of those assenting to whatever came to hand, of
whatever sort they might be, even if they happened to be false, and often
contradicting, even if some true things had been said, educating this too by
these aforementioned and by other varied speeches; for this part of philosophy
is of many forms, accustoming us not at random nor as it chanced both to cast
the testimonies and again to refuse, but examining accurately not the obvious
only (for many things reputable of themselves and of solemn look, having run
into our hearings under seemly voices as true, being unsound and false, having
seized and taken a vote of truth from us, not long after were detected as
rotten and not trustworthy, feigning the truth in vain; and easily showed us
laughably deceived, and having testified at random to those to whom least we
ought; and again other things solemn and not boastful, or lying in voices not
trustworthy, seeming paradoxical and most unbelievable of all, having been
rejected of themselves as false and insulted unworthily, then later, for those
who tracked and observed accurately, were observed to be most true of all and
simply unconquerable, the things till then thought rejectable and unapproved),
not then the obvious only and what juts out, and is sometimes deceitful and
sophistical, but searching the inward and tapping each, lest somewhere
something rotten sound, and first confirming ourselves in themselves, thus
also to agree with those outside and to declare about each he taught. Thus the
critical part of our soul about expressions and speeches was educated
rationally; not according to the judgments of fine rhetors, whether something
is Greek or barbarian in voice; this small and not necessary lesson; but this
most necessary for all, Greeks and barbarians, and wise and private, and
wholly (lest my speech be long, going through each art and pursuit) for all
men who have chosen any life whatsoever; if at least it is a care and a
serious thing for all who converse about anything whatsoever not to have been
deceived.

8 And let this not be what the form dialectic alone has been allotted to
accomplish; and as for the low part of our soul again, amazed at the
great-working and wonder-working and varied and all-wise crafting of the
world, and having wondered irrationally and cowered under astonishment, yet
knowing not even anything to reason out, in the manner of irrational animals,
raising and setting it upright by other lessons, the natural ones, making each
of the things that are clear, and distinguishing even very wisely into the
very first elements, then weaving by speech and going over both the nature of
the wholes and that of each in part, and the many-formed turning and change of
the things in the world; until, bringing us under the clear teaching from him
and speeches, both of what he learned and of what he found out about the
sacred management of the universe and the blameless nature, he set down in our
souls a rational wonder instead of an irrational. This lesson which, being
high and inspired, the study of nature most lovely to all teaches. What need
to speak of the things of the sacred lessons, geometry dear to all and
undisputed, and astronomy that travels the heights? Which each he was
imprinting on our souls, teaching or reminding or I do not know what one must
say, making the one as a base of all simply, unshaken, geometry, and some safe
foundation; and leading up even as far as the highest through astronomy, as
through some ladder heaven-high of each lesson, having prepared heaven
walkable for us.

9 And the things that are most crowning of all, and for the sake of which
especially all the philosophic race labors, as from a varied planting, of all
the other lessons and of long philosophy, receiving good fruits, the divine
virtues about character, from which the undisturbed and stable condition of
the impulses of the soul comes; and painless and unaffected by all evils,
well-ordered and stable


and godlike he was going to make us truly and blessed. And these too he worked
out with speeches of his own, soothing and wise, and not least also most
necessary, about our characters and manners. And not with speeches only, but
already even with deeds in a manner our impulses were steered, by the very
theory and observation of the impulses and affections of the soul, from which
especially, when it has been well known, our soul is by nature set right from
disharmony, and transferred from confused to the judged and well-ordered; so
that, as in a mirror having seen itself, the very beginnings and roots of the
evils, all its irrational, from which the out-of-place affections rise upon
us; and again as much as is its best part, rational, under which when it rules
it remains by itself unharmed and unaffected; then having observed these
accurately in itself, all that grow out from the worse, pouring us out by
incontinence or contracting and choking us by lowliness, such as pleasures and
desires or griefs and fears, and as much a rank of evils follows these kinds,
these it would cast out and put out of the way, rising upon them still
beginning and just growing and not allowing them even a little to increase,
but destroying and making them vanish; and as many goods as rise for us from
the better, these it would nourish and keep safe, both nursing them beginning
and guarding them together until perfection. For thus the divine virtues would
someday come to be present to a soul, both prudence, able first to judge these
very movements of the soul, from themselves and from the knowledge that has
come about of the goods and evils outside us, if there are any; and
temperance, the power choosing these rightly in the beginnings; and justice,
which assigns the worthy things to each; and the salvation of all these,
courage. He did not then accustom us with speeches announced, as if prudence
were knowledge of goods and evils or of things to be done and not to be done;
this empty and unprofitable lesson, if the speech should be without the deeds,
and prudence not doing the things to be done and turning away from the things
not to be done, but furnishing those who have it to know these, such as we see
many. And temperance again, that it would be some knowledge of things to be
chosen and not to be chosen, the other philosophers not teaching it very much,
and especially the newer, being vigorous and strong in speeches (such as I
have often wondered at, when they prove the same virtue of God and of men, and
that the wise man on earth is equal to the first God), yet able to hand down
neither prudence, so that one might also do the things of prudence, nor
temperance, so that one might also choose these things which he learned; and
likewise also upon justice, and still upon courage. Not thus did this man go
through the things about virtues for us with speeches, but he rather called us
to the deeds; and he called more by the deeds than by what he said.

10 And I ask of the philosophers now living, as many as I myself knew and as
many as I heard others narrating, and of the other men as well, to be without
indignation toward the things now said by us. And let no one either by
friendship toward the man or even still being at enmity with the remaining
philosophers. (of whom, if any other, I myself also wish to be a lover because
of the speeches, and I wish to praise them myself and to hear others saying
the fairest things about them; but such they are, that the very name of
philosophy is outraged to the extreme by almost all, and I would almost choose
to be altogether a private man, rather than learn something of what these
announce, to whom through the remaining life I thought it not even worthy to
approach, perhaps not thinking this rightly), except let no one suspect me to
say these things by some ambition either of the praises toward the man or by
this other ambition toward the philosophers outside; but let him be persuaded
that we say even lesser things than his deeds, lest we seem to flatter, not
procuring for ourselves words and names and artful starting points of encomia;
who not even when I was a lad and from a rhetor


I was being taught the popular rhetoric, willingly endured to praise and to
speak an encomium about anyone, that was not true. So then neither now, having
set out to praise, do I think I must simply exalt this man by the blames of
the others; or I would speak ill of the man, having compared his blessed life
to the stumbles of others, in order that I might have something greater indeed
to say about him; we are not so foolish; but I will confess this very thing
which I have suffered, without some comparison and knavery in speeches.

11 This man first and alone urged me to do philosophy even the philosophy of
the Greeks, having persuaded me by his own manners both to hear and to bear
the speech about characters, I who would not have been persuaded, as far as
the other philosophers went (again I confess), not rightly, yet almost
unhappily for us. Indeed I did not even meet more at first, but some few who
professed to teach, but who had set doing philosophy at words for all. But
this man first urged me to do philosophy even with the speeches, having
anticipated the urging through speeches by the deeds; not announcing only
practiced words, but not even thinking it right to speak, unless he should do
this with a sincere purpose even struggling to practice the things said, or
trying to present himself such as he goes through in speeches the one who will
live well, and a pattern, I wished to say, having set forth of a wise man; but
since the speech promised us truth from above, not elegance, I do not yet say
him a pattern of a wise man; although wishing to say it and it being true; but
I let this go now. Not an exact pattern then, but one wishing even exceedingly
to assimilate, forcing with all zeal and eagerness, if one must say it, even
beyond the power of men; and indeed also to mold us others such, not masters
of speeches and knowers of the things about impulses, but of the impulses
themselves; pressing toward the deeds and the speeches and bringing on no
small portion of each virtue, perhaps even the whole, if we had room, in the
very theory; both compelling us to do just things, if one must say it, through
the soul minding its own, which he persuaded us to add ourselves to; leading
us away from the meddlesomeness according to life and from the trouble of the
marketplace, and having raised us to look around ourselves and to do the
things truly our own. And this is to do just things, and this justice the
true, some of the ancient philosophers also have said, saying the minding of
one's own, as it seems to me, even more effective toward blessedness both for
themselves and for those who come to them; if at least it belongs to this
virtue to assign according to desert and the proper things to each. For what
else would be proper to a soul, and what so worthy, than to care for itself,
not looking outside nor doing others' business nor, to speak concisely, doing
injustice to itself the worst injustice, but turned inward toward itself,
rendering itself to itself and doing just things? Thus he educated, compelling
us to do just things, if one must say it; and to be prudent again no less by
being toward itself and wishing and trying to know ourselves; this the best
work of philosophy, which is even attributed to the most mantic of daemons as
an all-wise command: Know yourself. And that this is truly a work of
prudence, and that this is the divine prudence, is well said by the ancients;
being truly the same virtue of God and of man, of the soul itself practicing
to see itself as in a mirror and mirroring the divine mind, if it should
become worthy of this communion, in itself, and tracking some unspeakable road
of this deification. And followingly also to be temperate and to be
courageous; to be temperate, being kept safe in this prudence of the soul
knowing itself, if ever this should come to be for it; for this again is
temperance, being some sound prudence; and to be courageous upon all,
remaining in the said practices, and not falling away either willingly or
under some necessity, but guarding and being masters of the things said; and
this is, being some savior and guardian of doctrines, this virtue.




12 And indeed just and prudent and temperate or courageous, by our own
slowness and sluggishness, and this although he is very much hurrying, he is
still going to make us; we neither having nor yet having come near any virtue
whatsoever of men or divine, far from it; for these are greatest and high, and
neither is to be taken nor for anyone to obtain, unless God at least should
breathe power; and we have neither been born so fit, nor do we yet confess
ourselves worthy to obtain; not having done, from slackness and weakness, all
that those who aim at the best and seek the perfect must. Just or temperate or
to have some of the other virtues we are still going to be; but lovers he long
ago also made us, loving a most sharp love, which perhaps upon him alone, this
wonderful man and friend of the virtues and advocate, having implanted a love
for his own virtue and for the beauty of justice, whose golden face he truly
showed us, and of prudence desirable to all, and of the most lovely true
wisdom, and of godlike temperance, which is stability of soul and peace to all
who have acquired it, and of most wonderful courage, our endurance, and upon
all piety, which they say is mother of the virtues, speaking rightly. For this
is beginning and end of all the virtues; for setting out from this the
remaining virtues too would most easily come upon us; if this which every man
must, at least who is not godless or pleasure-loving, to become a friend and
advocate to God, desiring and hurrying to acquire for themselves, we should
care for the remaining virtues; so that we may not, unworthy and filthy, but
with all virtue and wisdom as with some good escort and most wise priest,
approach God. The end of all I think is no other than, having been made like
with a pure mind, to come to God and remain in him.

13 Upon all the other love of labor and zeal, the teaching about theology and
the reverence, how would I go through it in speech, having entered into the
very disposition of the man, with what purpose and preparation he wished us to
learn thoroughly all the speeches about the divine, guarding lest somehow we
should run risk about the most necessary of all, the knowledge of the cause of
all. For he thought it right to do philosophy, collecting of the ancients all
as many writings as there are both of philosophers and of hymn-singers, with
all power, putting nothing aside nor rejecting (for we did not yet even have
the judgment); except as many as would be of the godless, as many as, having
rolled out together even from the human notions, say there is no god or
providence (for these it is not even worthy to read, lest even in the chance
thing our soul be stained, being about to be pious yet hearing speeches
contrary to the service of God; for neither do those approaching the temples
of piety, as they think, touch at all any of the profane); the things of these
then are not even at all even worthy to be numbered among men who have taken
up being pious; but to meet and converse with all the rest, having preferred
no one race nor philosophic speech, nor again having rejected, neither Greek
nor barbarian, but hearing all. Wisely this and very artfully; lest somehow
one single speech by itself of these or of those, having been heard and
honored alone, even if it happen not to be true, as being alone true, having
entered into our soul may deceive and disposing us by itself may make us its
own, no longer able to withdraw nor to wash it off, like wools dyed with some
fast dye. For a terrible and versatile thing is the speech of men, varied in
sophisms, and sharp, having run into the hearings, both to stamp the mind and
to set it forth, and having persuaded those once seized to be loved as true,
and to remain within, even if it is false and deceitful, like some wizard
ruling, having as champion the deceived himself. And again easy to deceive by
speech and easy toward assent is the soul of man, ready, before it distinguish
and examine in every way, by its own bluntness and weakness, or by


the subtlety of the speech toward the accuracy of the examination, giving
itself over more idly often to false speeches and doctrines, themselves having
gone astray, and leading astray also those who have held them; and not this
only, but even if another speech should wish to set it right, no longer
admitting nor being persuaded otherwise, but held by that in itself, as by
some inexorable tyrant that has overpowered it.

14 For did these things not introduce both the doctrines that fight and oppose
one another and the factions of philosophers, some standing against the
doctrines of others, and some ruling other things and others attaching
themselves to others; and all wishing and professing to do philosophy, from
the time they were first urged, and saying they wish it no less when they have
come to be in the speeches than as they were beginning, or rather even now
having more the love of philosophy, when it has been possible for them (as one
might say) both to taste it and to spend time besides with the speeches, than
when first being inexperienced they were then raised by some impulse to do
philosophy; saying these things, yet no longer having hearkened to any
speeches of the other-doctrined? Neither then did any one of the ancients urge
any of the newer, or of the Peripatos, to turn toward him and to do philosophy
his philosophy, nor conversely, and wholly no one anyone. For one would not
easily be persuaded to attach himself, changing from his own, to others, and
these perhaps to these, which if he had been persuaded before coming to do
philosophy, he would first also have loved; [he was easily persuaded] as
indeed, the soul not yet having been preoccupied, he would not yet have held
to speeches and would have loved, similarly opposing because of those things
these which he now has. Such things have our fair and most learned and most
examining Greeks philosophized, each saying that those things alone to which
he happened from the beginning, driven by some impulse, are true, and all the
remaining of the other philosophers deceit and nonsense; himself by speech
confirming his own no more than each of the others stand for their own, of the
not needing to change and to change purpose by necessity or persuasion; having
no other (if one must speak the truth) than the irrational impulse from
philosophy toward these doctrines, and a judgment of what he thinks true (lest
it be paradoxical to say) no other than the undiscerning chance; each loving
these to which he happened first, by which as if bound, no longer able to
attend to others; if he should even have something to say with a demonstration
of truth of his own about all, and that the things of the opponents would be
false, helped even by the speech, since even unhelped, having given himself
and receiving at random as a windfall the speeches that preoccupied him; which
both in the other things tripped those who have them, and indeed also of the
greatest and most necessary of all, the knowledge about the divine and piety.
And they remain nonetheless bound in them in a manner, and no one would still
easily rescue them, as from a shoal in a very broad plain hard to cross, no
longer allowing those who have once fallen either running back or even having
crossed to be saved, but holding them in itself until the end; or as from a
deep and thick and high wood, into which some traveler entered, as indeed
perhaps even about to set out and set himself again in the clear by the
enclosure, yet by length and thickness is not able, turning very much in it,
and finding some continuous roads inside he travels variously, as indeed
perhaps about to set out through some of them; but they lead only to the
inward, and an exit in no way, being some roads of the wood itself alone; and
at last the traveler, having grown weary and given up, as if all things have
become wood and there is no longer any dwelling on earth, wishes to remain
there having set his hearth, and procuring breadth for himself as he can in
the wood; and as from some labyrinth, into which, an entrance of one
appearing, someone having suspected nothing intricate from the outside having
entered through the appearing door of one, then having gone forward as far as
the inmost, having seen an intricate spectacle and


a most wise construction, many-pathed and made artful with continuous
entrances and exits, wishing indeed even to go out would no longer be able,
caught inside by a construction seeming wise to him. And no labyrinth is so
hard to unwind and intricate, nor a wood thick and intricate, nor a plain thus
or a shoal, terrible to overpower those who have come near, as speech, if
there should be one according to these very philosophers. In order then that
we might not suffer the same as the many, he did not lead toward one some
speech of the philosophers, nor think it right to go off according to them,
but he led toward all, wishing us to be inexperienced of no Greek doctrine.
And he himself also went in together, leading and taking by the hand as in a
journey, if somewhere even something crooked and unsound and sophistical
should meet; as a craftsman, from the long spending of time with the speeches
not unused to nor inexperienced of anything, himself remaining aloft in
safety, and stretching a hand to others would keep them safe, drawing up those
as if being baptized; collecting and setting before us all that was useful of
each of the philosophers and true; and sorting out as many as were false, both
the others and especially as many as were proper against the piety of men.

15 About these advising us to attend to nothing, not even if some all-wise man
should be witnessed by all men, but to attend to God alone and to his
prophets; himself interpreting and making clear whatever might be dark and
enigmatic, as many things are in the sacred voices (either it being thus dear
to God to converse with men, so that the divine Word may not, naked and
uncovered, enter even an unworthy soul, such as the many, or even every divine
oracle being by nature most clear and simplest, yet to us, having stood away
from God and having unlearned to hear by time and antiquity, appearing unclear
and dark, I am not able to say); except making clear and bringing into light,
whether they happen to be enigmas, being a terrible hearer of God and most
intelligent; or even having nothing crooked by nature nor unintelligible to
him, he thus being the only one of men now, of whom I myself both knew and
heard others speaking about some, having practiced to receive the pure of the
oracles, bright, in his soul and to teach others; because the leader of them
all, who sounds under and suggests to the prophets dear to God all prophecy
and mystic and divine speech, having thus honored him has set him as a friend
advocate; making the teaching of those things which through others he only
hinted, through this man, and having granted to this man both to search out
and to find the speeches of as many things as, being most trustworthy, he
either commanded kingly or even declared; so that if someone should happen to
be hard in soul and unbelieving or even a lover of learning, having learned
from this man he would be compelled in a manner both to choose to understand
and to believe and to follow God. And he says these things not otherwise I
think than by communion of the divine spirit; for there is need of the same
power both for those prophesying and for those hearing prophets; and one would
not hear a prophet, to whom the very spirit that prophesied has not granted
the understanding of its speeches. Such an oracle also lies in the sacred
writings, saying that the one who shuts opens alone, and another not anyone;
and the divine Word opens the things shut, making the enigmas clear. This
greatest gift this man has, having received it from God, and a most fair
portion from heaven, to be an interpreter of the speeches of God toward men,
to understand the things of God as God speaking, and to narrate to men as men
hear. Therefore nothing was unspeakable to us, for neither was it hidden and
untrodden; but it was possible to learn every speech, both barbarian and
Greek, and more mystic and more civic, and divine and human, going around all
with all abundance and searching, and being filled with all and enjoying the
goods of the soul; whether some ancient lesson of truth, or even another such
that someone might name, in him we were, having the wonderful and full of the
fairest spectacles


preparation and authority. And to speak concisely this was truly a paradise
for us, an imitator of the great paradise of God, in which it was not to work
the earth below nor to nourish the body growing thick, but only to increase
the advantages of the soul, having planted ourselves as some seasonable plants
or having been grafted in us by the cause of all, being gladdened and
luxuriating.

16 This is truly a paradise of luxury, this true gladness and luxury, which we
luxuriated in in this time that has been gone through, and not already little
and altogether little, if it will stop up to this, we having already gone away
and withdrawn from here. For I do not know what I have suffered or again
sinned that I go out, I am driven out; what one must say, I do not know, but
that I am a second Adam out of paradise, and I have begun to speak. How well I
was living, hearing a teacher speaking and being silent; would that now too I
were learning to keep quiet, being silent, and not (this new spectacle) make
the teacher a hearer. For what need had I of these speeches? And why even
address such things, when I ought not to be absent but to persist? But these
seem to be faults of the old deceit, and these just penalties of the ancient
still await me; or again I seem to myself to disobey, daring to overstep the
speeches of God, when I ought to remain in them and at them. And I go away,
fleeing from this blessed life no less this I, than that ancient man from the
face of God, and turning back to the earth from which I was taken; therefore I
shall eat earth all the days of my life there, and I shall work earth, and
this sending up thorns and burs for me, my griefs and the shameful cares,
having been let go from the fair and good cares; and to what I have left I
turn again, the earth from which I went out, and my kindred below, and into
the house of my father; having left the good land, where the good fatherland
being mine was unknown of old, and kindred, whom having as proper to my soul I
later began to know, and the house of our truly father, in which remaining he
is solemnly honored and revered by the true sons wishing to remain in it, the
Father. And I, unsolemn and unworthy, go out from these, turned to the things
behind and running back. A certain son is said, having received from a father
the portion falling to him toward another brother of his, to have wished this,
to go abroad from the father into a far country; and living prodigally to have
squandered the paternal substance and consumed it; and at last in want having
hired himself to feed swine, and being compelled by hunger even to share the
foods with the pigs, to desire yet not even obtain this. He paid then the
penalty of the prodigality, having exchanged, instead of the paternal table
that was kingly, what he did not foresee, the swine-foods and those in hired
service. Something such we seem about to suffer having gone away, and not even
with all the portion that falls; for we have not even taken what we ought, yet
we go away nonetheless, having left the fair and dear things with you and
beside you, and having exchanged the worse. For all gloomy things will succeed
us, noise and disturbance out of peace, and out of a quiet and well-ordered an
unordered life, and out of this freedom a hard slavery, marketplaces and
lawsuits and crowds and luxury; and leisure for us no longer toward the better
not even any, nor will we speak the divine oracles, but we will speak the
works of men (this indeed even a simple curse has been thought to be for a
prophet man), and we even of wicked men. Night truly out of day, and out of
bright light darkness, and out of a festival mourning, and out of a fatherland
a hostile country will succeed us, in which it is not possible for me to sing
a sacred song (for how in a land alien to my soul, where remaining it is not
possible to be with God?) but only to weep and groan, being reminded of the
things here, if even this someone will grant me. Enemies once are said, having
come upon a great and sacred city, in which the divine was served, to have
dragged captive the inhabitants and hymn-singers and theologians into the same
country, and it was Babylonian; and those brought into it,

17 But why do I lament these things? There is the savior of all, and guardian
and physician of all the half-dead and the robbed, Word, the sleepless
guardian of all men; there are for us also seeds, both those you showed us
having and as many as we have received from you, the fair injunctions; with
which we go away, weeping as we travel, yet carrying with ourselves
nonetheless these seeds. Perhaps then the guardian will keep us safe, having
stood over us; perhaps we shall also turn back to you again, bringing from the
seeds both the fruits and the little sheaves, not perfect (for how could we?),
but such as possible for us from the actions in civic life, corrupted in the
power either by some unfruitful or evil-fruited, yet not also going to be
corrupted further beside us, if God should nod.

18 For me then let the speech stop here, being bold upon what least it ought,
yet well-mindedly and having given thanks somewhere I think according to our
power, saying nothing worthy, yet not having been wholly silent; and still
also having wept, as those going abroad from friends are accustomed, boyish,
nothing holding of flattery nor more ancient or more over-busy, I do not know;
yet there is also with it the not fabricated, this I know clearly, and true
altogether, with a healthy purpose and a sincere and whole choice.

19 But you, rising, O dear head, and having prayed already send us, having
saved us present by your sacred lessons, and saving us by the prayers even
when we have gone abroad; and indeed hand over and set before, or rather hand
over to the God who brought us to you; giving thanks for the things that have
gone before on our behalf, and asking to lead by the hand even in the things
to come, standing over through all, sounding his commands under to our mind,
casting into us his divine fear, which will be a best pedagogue; for we shall
not, once we have gone away, obey him as in the freedom with you. Ask also
that some consolation come to us from him of your leaving, to send out a good
escort, a fellow-traveling angel. And ask also that having turned us he may
bring us to you again; and this at least most of all alone will console us.
