Discourse of Amphilochius, bishop of Iconium, on Zacchaeus. Nothing so raises
the soul toward gladness as fear of God and abstinence from evils and the
course of repentance and the manner of confession. Hence also today David was
blessing those who have obtained forgiveness of sins, at once displaying the
lovingkindness of Christ, and at once preparing sinners to run to repentance
... blessed, he says, are they whose iniquities have been forgiven and whose
sins have been covered ... let whoever then, fornicator or publican, run to
the inexhaustible springs of Christ's salvation.
Without it there is no receiving a release from evils nor obtaining the
beatitude, even if you name prophets or apostles or evangelists. For all drew
from the same spring. Among the prophets David himself, after adultery, is
still even now a prophet through the grace of the one who pardoned. And among
the apostles Peter and Paul: the one after the denial has the keys of the
kingdom, the other after the persecution was appointed apostle of the
Gentiles, having changed Jewish zeal into an evangelical manner. For in the
Gospels I know not only Matthew being saved from among publicans, but also two
others with him: of whom one, praying, from beating his breast, the treasury
of evils, from not daring to lift his hands to the holy things, from bowing
downward, was not only justified but also crowned against a Pharisee; and
Zacchaeus, having gone up onto the tree on which he had often stood watching
lest some merchant pass by unnoticed, so that he might not remain unfined, was
keeping watch lest the merchant of heaven and earth run past him unnoticed,
the one carrying the inviolable treasure of the kingdom. But that we may not
altogether confuse the histories of the publicans together, if it seems good,
taking Zacchaeus alone today as the subject of our treatment let us broaden
the discourse.
Jesus, he says, going out, was passing through Jericho, and behold a man
called by name Zacchaeus, and this man was a chief publican. The evangelist

The spring of Jericho was formerly a mother of barrenness, a nurse of
unfruitfulness, having incomplete water. For it had a gaping stream, flowing
noiselessly like oil, but the sight of the waters sent the beholders away
thirsty; for the drinking was not without danger for those in need; for it had
a deadly flow. For the beauty of the spring moved the passers-by to a run, but
the fear of harm checked the eagerness.
Hence since the water flowed in vain at the spring, often the neighbors
standing by groaned, not from want of water but dragged into thirst by the
sight of the useless waters. They could not heal the suffering of thirst, so
that they complained to it saying: "Why do you flow in vain, O spring? You
were better not appearing, but flowing in mountains and sands and in deserts
where you did not have many witnesses of the evil." And why was the water
undrinkable? Because it made dead the bodies of those who drank; for if a man
drank of it, he did not become a father, nor did a woman become a mother,
having lost the grace of mothers. And not only this, but also earth receiving
the stream denied the accustomed yield of ears of grain, and the well-shaded
graces of palms stripped off the robe of the leaves, and to speak in sum all
things became desolate as having fallen unjustly upon the flow. Such was the
spring that of old went about Jericho, until Elisha the prophet came and
taking salt and casting it on the spring made the streams live. For thus, he
says, says the Lord: I have healed these waters. Thus says the one who always
speaks and brings the word into deed: Let there no longer be from you barren
and sterile. He spoke and the water was changed and wombs were in labor and
the earth gave forth ears and the clusters blossomed again and the olive
displayed its own grace. And the neighbors were reconciled to the accustomed
spring, of old making war and being warred upon, but later longing and living
nearby.
It is necessary to say what the riddle of the spring means. This spring
flowing abundantly, but pouring forth of old a useless and barren stream,
preserves the account of the church. For before Christ it was so impious that
even a man drinking from the spring of the stream lost even being a human,
denying the nature of the rational soul by the entanglement of idols, and a
woman did not become a mother of virtues nor give birth to shoots of
temperance or gush the white milk of piety. But when she was in this state the
Lord came, casting in the apostles as salt, seasoned her and made her
drinkable. And that the apostles were salt, hear Christ saying to them: You
are the salt of the earth; and that the barren and unfruitful became
many-bearing, having partaken of the apostolic salts, the prophet says:
Rejoice, he says, barren one who does not bear, break forth and cry out, you
who do not travail, because many are the children of the desolate more than of
her who has the husband. So Jesus came to Jericho, the spring to those richly
nourished by the spring, the many-powered grace to the many-treed and
many-streamed city. And behold a man called by name Zacchaeus, and he himself
was a chief publican. The evil was double, because he both pursued an unjust
craft and ruled those who pursued it badly; not only sinning but also
receiving the evils of others. For he was cutting a strange road of injustice,
walling off the highways against travelers; for neither imitating robbers did
he lie in wait for passers-by, nor in zeal of hospitality did he wait for
travelers, but having injustice as a law he taxed others' labors, imitating
the pitiless gluttony of drones. For just as drones harvest the toil of bees
without sharing in the labors, so also the publicans plunder the labors of
foreign travelers, occupying themselves with idleness and sitting beside the
crossroads.
Someone was sailing the sea, fighting with great waves, warring with winds,
cutting a long and unmanageable sea, but Zacchaeus was taking away the profit
by the tax. A shepherd in drought and burning heat, living with rain and snow
and frost, having as hut the juttings of the rocks, food cheese and milk, as
garment the unworked hide of the sheep, but this rustic, the poor man,
conversing with mountains, fell in with Zacchaeus, being tithed of his flocks,
being robbed lawfully, being slain without a sword. For he who is struck with
iron and receiving a flowing wound goes away, having fled the hardship of the
suffering by insensibility, but he who is shot with involuntary poverty has
the pain as death and has died; not being consumed once, but he dies always
little by little.
And to speak briefly, as impregnable forts to soldiers and submerged rocks to
pilots and suspected ambushes to those at war, so to merchants and travelers,
herdsmen and shepherds Zacchaeus was to be fled. But this man, so mad about
the irrational gathering of money, seeking to see Jesus was not able; for both
smallness of stature and heaviness of injustice held him. Hence now healing
the paltriness of his stature by a device of intelligence, running ahead in
front he went up onto a sycamore and hides under the spreading of the leaves,
thinking to see and not be seen, thinking to escape the notice of the one who
knows all; which indeed the woman with the issue of blood also underwent,
thinking to steal Jesus, who is gladly stolen in such things. But she, having
come near, touched the hem; this man, being far off, laid hold of Christ by
faith. Hence he goes up onto the wood, healing the evils of Adam; for the one
is led astray by the wood, fleeing God, but the other is saved from a wood,
desiring to see God. For hearing that he works many and strange wonders, that
besides the bodies he also heals the souls, taking away the sins of the souls,
granting the bodies freedom from suffering, he desired to see the one who
grants all things to all, reasoning with himself: "Who then is this Jesus who
cleanses lepers, who heals the blind, who forgives sins to those who ask? Of
what sort in appearance, of what kind in form? Does he know all things? Does
he also test the thoughts of those who are absent? Or does he examine only the
reasonings of those present? Does he as God track out the thoughts of each
one's heart? From where then shall I know these things? Whom shall I take as
teacher of these? Whom? Experience, the teacher of all. Having gone up onto
the tree I hide under the spreading of the leaves. I escape notice and I learn
whether I can be saved. If he knows the movement of my soul, I know that he
wipes out the sin of my soul; this one thing then I shall know, that he knows
the hidden things of reasonings.
If, being pressed by the crowd, he sees me who am hiding, and not only sees
but also uncovers the desire of my soul, I choose to throw away all and find
one. I want to imitate Matthew; for he too was a publican as I also am, but
Matthew did not run forward of his own choosing, but being called he obeyed.
And perhaps seeing him and thinking him one of the travelers he hollowed his
hands and spread a deep fold, being carried by custom toward taking, and
wanting to tax Christ he was taxed in return by him, not even in appearance,
but giving his whole self. For as soon as he heard: Come follow me, he
overshot the call by eagerness, running past with sharper speed than the one
drawing him. If then he calls publicans and not only calls but also justifies,
the swarm of former evils does not harm me; for if Elisha, casting salt on the
spring that nourished, made its barrenness fruitful, surely he too, seasoning
my soul with grace as with salt, will awaken fruit-bearing of virtue." And
while he was considering these things Jesus came to the place; looking up he
