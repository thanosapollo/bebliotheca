On Lazarus and the Rich Man, Homily 1.
=59.591=
1. The stadium of the Church is a very great school, a training-place of the
rich, a refuge of the poor. Come then, beloved, since we have stood in the
wrestling-pit of teaching, following the lessons of the one who went before,
taking in hand what has just been said, let us sprinkle upon you all the drops
of the word owed from us =59.592= as a dew. Now the reading was relating to us
the manner of life of a rich man and a poor man: the rich man's life plunged
in luxury, the poor man's life marked with beggary. There was a certain rich
man, and he was clothed in purple and fine linen, making merry =59.593= every
day splendidly. And there was a certain poor man named Lazarus, who had been
laid at the rich man's gate, covered with sores, and desiring to partake of
the crumbs that fell from the rich man's table; and no one gave to him, but
even the dogs coming licked his sores. It is necessary then, beloved, to
recount the particulars, so that, like sailors, from the experience of those
who have sailed before, we too may flee the submerged rocks of shipwrecks; so
that the rich, from the punishment of the rich man who went before, may bring
themselves to sobriety, and the poor, seeing their hopes shining beforehand in
Lazarus, may run through the course of affliction with eagerness. There was a
certain rich man. A certain man. He has not been honored with the title of a
name, as the righteous Job was. There was a certain man in the land of
Ausitis, whose name was Job. But this man's name has been passed over in
silence; for the fruitless man is deprived even of the name, since the names
of the righteous also are in the book of life, but those of sinners shall be
blotted out, having only the common name of nature, "man"; for this name is
common to all. But he had the name in common, yet a cruelty of wickedness that
was his own. There was a certain rich man. Kinsman of the poor Lazarus
according to the nature of the body, yet a stranger to the inheritance in
Christ because of the baseness of his choice. There was a certain man. He was
a man in form, but in mind a beast that feeds alone. There was a certain rich
man. Rich in possessions, rich in accusations, rich in bronze, rich in the
rust of sin; shining with silver, but darkened with sins; rich in gold, but
poor in Christ; having much clothing, but having the soul naked; keeping
costly garments, and feeding many moths. There was a certain rich man. Pluming
himself on wealth, having no flower of righteousness; an autumn tree,
fruitless, twice dead. There was a certain rich man, and he was clothed in
purple and fine linen. He had purple as a garment, yet he had been cast away
from the kingdom of God; he had purple, not sprinkled with the blood of
Christ, but dyed from the blood of a sea-snail; not betrothing to him the
kingdom of heaven, but flashing before him the fearful flame of Gehenna. There
was a certain rich man, and he was clothed in fine linen and purple, making
merry every day splendidly. Making merry, not as the righteous do in the Lord
(for the gladness of the righteous is the remembrance of God, as David bears
me witness, saying: I remembered God, and I was glad), but he was making merry
in drunkenness, in prodigality, in surfeits, in disorders, in nothing unlike
swine rolling in the mire. But there was a certain poor man, Lazarus. He named
the poor man, honoring the beggar by the title of the name. But there was a
certain poor man, Lazarus, who had been laid at his gate, tossed violently by
the triple wave of poverty, yet not long after escorted by angels into the
calm bosom of Abraham. But there was a certain poor man, Lazarus, who had been
laid at the rich man's gate, covered with sores. O with how many evils was the
poor man pressed! By the heaviest poverty he was battered as by a violent
hail; by the seething blisters he was eaten as by sparking coals; from every
side torrents of affliction dashed against him, dissolving the body, crushing
the heart; no relief of calm was found for him. Without were fightings, within
were fears. For without, the body was being subdued by the blisters, and
within, the heart was being eaten by cares that could not be put off. No
grain-bearing land offered him seed of bread, no vine brought him a cluster of
sweet juice, no tree farmed for him the sweetest fruit of produce, nor was
there anything else of the earth toward the consolation of his poverty. How
should there be, for whom there was not even a span of arable ground, nor a
cubit's breadth of floor for dwelling, but he had been laid in the open air
upon the dunghill? Dung was his covering both by night and by day, warming him
in part in winter, and in summer burning him terribly. He had a body spotted
with wounds, feeble for farming, useless for travel and trade, and unfit for
the other =59.594= occasions of need. From every side want for him, from every
side constraint; groans grew upon his heart before tears, like thistles;
channels of tears were borne upon his cheeks, furrowed by the continual rush
of weeping; he rehearsed death in his sleep, he looked about for death after
sleep, and nowhere did he find it. For as a release from long pains and cares
he sought death, in nothing unlike the much-afflicted Job. For that man too,
having lost his money, his herds, his possessions, at last naked in body,
seized by worms, had been laid upon the dunghill, and desired death. For what
did he say? Why is light given to those in bitterness, and life to souls in
pain? Who long for death and do not obtain it, digging for it as for treasure;
and they become overjoyed if they attain it; for death is rest to a man. But
he also desired, it says, to be filled from the crumbs that fell from the rich
man's table, and no one gave to him. O strange life! The rich man, watered in
the heavily laden stream of luxury, was drunk, while the poor man, having no
moisture of plenty, was withering in the burning heat of poverty.

Why then did the well-tempered voyage of life not come about for them in their
sharing with one another? Why? So that the poor man might receive the bright
crowns of endurance, and the rich man might chew with his own teeth the unripe
grapes of a soul that had become a beast. But even the dogs, it says, coming
licked off his sores. The dogs were more noble than the rich man, better than
that man's inhumanity. While that man never sprinkled a drop of oil as dew
upon the poor man, the dogs, having softened the sharpness of their teeth by
kindness to man, treated him with a gentle tongue, as if wiping away all the
filth and all the discharge of his wounds; and taming the wild sores by the
smoothness of the tongue, they quietly stole away the pains of his wounds. But
the rich man never even granted the poor man a cheerful eye, nor a word, even
a fruitless one; he did not throw him a rag, not a leftover of foods, not one
obol eaten away by rust, not bread, not a fragment of bread spoiled by mildew,
but he threw everything into his own belly, as into an inn, through the funnel
of the throat.
2. But what? As were each man's rations, such also are the repayments. And it
came to pass, it says, that the poor man died, and was carried away by the
angels into the bosom of Abraham. At last the poor man, delivered from stormy
poverty, sailed into the calm harbor of Abraham. For it came to pass, it says,
that Lazarus died, and was carried away by the angels into the bosom of
Abraham. Do you see how poverty, after the departure from here, has angels as
escorts? And it came to pass that the rich man also died and was buried. At
once burial is set down upon the rich man, according to what is said by David:
And their graves are their houses forever. For this reason it is said to you:
Poor man, Do not fear when a man grows rich, because when he dies he will not
take all things, nor will his glory go down with him. For here he leaves all
things behind, the slaves, the shield-bearers, the attendants, the parasites,
the flatterers, the chariots, the horses with golden bits, the baths, the
estates, the gold-roofed houses, the mosaic floors, the kingship, the
authority, the lordship; he leaves all things here, and goes away naked. And
being in Hades, Lifting up his eyes. Consider in how great a depth the rich
man was, and in what a height the poor man. And lifting up, it says, his eyes,
the rich man saw Abraham, and Lazarus in his bosom. Why then did Lazarus not
see the rich man? Because the one who is in the light does not see the one who
is in the darkness, but the one who is in the darkness sees the one who is in
the light. And he said: Father Abraham, have mercy on me. Now you say, Have
mercy, when it is not a time of mercy. Did you not hear the one who says,
Judgment is without mercy to him who has not done mercy? Now you repent, when
there is no confession. Did you not hear =59.595= David saying, And in Hades
who will confess to you? Have mercy on me, and send Lazarus. Do you recognize
Lazarus now? Do you now come to know the one who was upon the dunghill, whom
once you were not willing to honor even as equal to the dogs? You shut your
bowels to him in the life of plenty; I too now shut the bosom of rest in the
time of punishment. Did you not hear God crying out to you through all the
prophets and saying: Keep mercy and judgment, and draw near to your God,
because I desire mercy rather than sacrifice? and, Blessed are the merciful,
for they shall obtain mercy? and again, He who has mercy on a poor man lends
to God? Where then you sowed, there seek also to reap; where you lent, there
also demand repayment.

But you never threw the seed of almsgiving, nor did you lend to God through
almsgiving toward the poor man, but you stored all things in your own belly.
Where you had your treasure, there have your heart also. Have mercy on me, and
send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water and cool my
tongue, because I am in anguish terribly in this flame. He is punished in the
tongue, through which he received his food; he sought a drop of water, because
he never even shared a cup of cold water in the name =59.596= of a disciple
with one who was thirsty. If you had gained Lazarus as a partner in your good
things in this life, you would now have shared the kingdom with him; if you
had considered the poor man, you would now have been delivered from the evil
judgment. For blessed is he who considers the poor and needy; in an evil day
the Lord will deliver him. Smooth out that many-flamed chasm by the rich
laying-down of your money upon the poor; dew mercy now, and you have quenched
the furnace; feed orphans, and you have killed the sleepless worms; check a
widow's tear, and you have wiped your soul of the mourning of sins.

For this reason these things have been read to you, so that you may not,
stepping in the same tracks of the unmerciful rich man, fall into the same
punishment of Gehenna. And you, the poor man, do not grow faint, nor weary,
being tossed in the triple wave of poverty. Look toward Lazarus the most
splendid; keep your eye stretched there both by night and by day; so that,
steering your life with the rudder of endurance, you may reach the same harbor
of rest, in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the glory unto the ages of ages.
Amen.
