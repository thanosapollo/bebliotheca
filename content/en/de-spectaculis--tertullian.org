** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

Servants of God, learn what standing of faith, what reason of truth, what rule
of discipline, among the other errors of the world, takes away even the
pleasures of the spectacles; you who are now coming to God, recognize it, you
who have already testified and confessed that you have come, lest anyone sin
either by not knowing or by pretending not to know. For so great is the force
of pleasures that it both stretches ignorance into an occasion and corrupts
conscience into pretence. Perhaps the opinions of the heathen still soothe
someone toward both, who in this cause have been accustomed to argue against
us thus: that such outward comforts of eyes or ears in no way clash with
religion in mind and in conscience, and that God is not offended by a man's
delight, which, with fear toward God and his honor kept safe, it is no crime
to enjoy in its own time and in its own place. Yet this is just what we are
preparing to show, how these things do not suit true religion and true
obedience toward the true God.

There are those who think that Christians, a race made ready for death, are
trained to this stubbornness by the surrender of pleasures, so that they may
more easily despise life once its tethers, as it were, have been cut, and may
not long for what they have already made superfluous for themselves; so that
this may be thought fixed by human counsel and foresight rather than by divine
rule. Of course it would weary even those who persist in such great pleasures
to die for the Lord. Yet even if it were so, so fitting a counsel was owed so
much obedience by the stubbornness of discipline.

Now indeed there is no one who does not put this forward as well: that all
things were instituted by God and assigned to man, as we preach, and are
certainly good, as all things of a good author; that among these are counted
all those things from which the spectacles are equipped, the horse for example
and the lion and the strengths of the body and the sweetness of the voice;
therefore what consists of his own creation can seem neither foreign nor
hostile to God, nor should what is not hostile to him, because it is not
foreign, be charged against the worshippers of God. Plainly even the very
buildings of the places, because stones, because rubble, because marbles,
because columns are God's things, who gave them for the furnishing of the
earth; and even the acts themselves are carried out under God's heaven. How
wise an advocate human ignorance thinks itself, especially when it fears to
lose something of this kind from the joys and fruits of the world. In short
you will find more whom the danger of pleasure rather than of life calls away
from this sect. For even a fool does not dread death as due, even a wise man
does not despise pleasure as desired, since there is no other charm of life
for fool and wise man alike except pleasure. No one denies, because no one is
unaware, what nature of itself suggests, that God is the founder of the
universe and that this universe is as good as it is made over to man. But
because they do not know God inwardly except by natural right, not also by
familiar right, from afar, not from near, they must be ignorant how he
commands or forbids the things he instituted to be administered, and at the
same time what rival power there is from the opposite side for adulterating
the uses of the divine creation, because you would know neither the will nor
the adversary of him whom you know too little. Therefore we must look not only
at this, by whom all things were instituted, but by whom they were turned. For
it will appear for what use they were instituted, if it appears for what they
were not. There is much difference between corruption and integrity, because
there is much between the institutor and the interpolator.

For the rest, all kinds of evils, which even the heathen both forbid and
punish as undoubted, consist of the works of God. You see murder carried out
by iron, by poison, by magic bindings: iron is as much God's thing as herbs,
as angels. Yet did the author provide these for the slaughter of man? On the
contrary, he destroys every kind of murder by one principal precept: You shall
not kill. Likewise gold, bronze, silver, ivory, wood, and whatever material is
seized for the making of idols, who placed these in the world except God, the
author of the world? Yet was it that they might be worshipped against himself?
On the contrary, the chief offence with him is idolatry. What is there that is
not God's which offends God? But when it offends, it has ceased to be God's,
and when it has ceased, it offends. Man himself, the author of all outrages,
is not only the work of God but even his image; and yet both in body and in
spirit he has fallen away from his institutor. For we did not take eyes for
lust and tongue for evil-speaking and ears for a receptacle of evil-speaking
and gullet for the crime of the gullet and belly for partnership with the
gullet and genitals for the excesses of unchastity and hands for violence and
steps for a wandering life, nor was the spirit planted in the body so that it
might become a thinking-shop of snares, of frauds, of iniquities. I do not
think so. For if God, the exactor of innocence, hates all malignity and even
malice thought out, it is beyond doubt that whatever he founded he did not
found for the issue of the works he condemns, although the same works are
administered through the things he founded, since this is the whole reason of
condemnation: the perverse administration of creation by the created.

We therefore, who having known the Lord have also looked upon his rival, who
having found the institutor have at the same time caught the interpolator,
ought neither to wonder nor to doubt: since that power of the interpolating
and rival angel from the beginning cast down from integrity man himself, the
work and image of God, possessor of the whole universe, it likewise changed
his whole substance, instituted for integrity together with himself, into
perversity together with himself against the institutor, so that, in that very
thing which he had grieved was granted to man, not to himself, he might both
make man guilty before God and set up his own domination.

Armed with this knowledge against the opinion of the heathen, let us turn
rather to the cavils of our own people. For the faith of some, either simpler
or more scrupulous, demands from the scriptures an authority for this
surrender of the spectacles, and sets itself in uncertainty, because
abstinence of this kind is not forbidden to the servants of God expressly or
by name. Plainly we nowhere find it laid down as openly as, You shall not
kill, you shall not worship an idol, you shall not commit adultery, you shall
not admit fraud, so expressly defined: you shall not go into the circus, not
into the theatre; you shall not watch a contest, a show. But we find that that
first voice of David also belongs to this kind: Blessed is the man, he says,
who has not gone into the council of the impious and has not stood in the way
of sinners nor sat in the chair of pestilences. For although he seems to have
proclaimed that just man, because he did not take part in the council and
sitting of the Jews when they were to consult on killing the Lord, yet divine
scripture is always widely divided, wherever according to the sense of the
present matter discipline also is fortified, so that here too the voice is not
foreign to the interdiction of the spectacles. For if he then called a few
Jews the council of the impious, how much more so great a gathering of a
heathen people? Are the heathen less impious, less sinners, less enemies of
Christ than the Jews then were? What of this, that the rest also agrees? For
at the spectacles there is sitting in a chair and standing in a way; for they
call ways and hinges the paths of the belts around the circuit and the
divisions of the commons down the slope; the chair too is the name of the very
place in the curve for sitting together. Therefore on the contrary, Unhappy is
he who has gone into any council of the impious whatever and has stood in any
way of sinners whatever and has sat in any chair of pestilences whatever. Let
us understand it said generally, when something1 else can also be interpreted
specially. For some things pronounced specially also have a general sense.
When God warns Israel of discipline or rebukes them, he certainly has it for
all; when he threatens Egypt and Ethiopia with destruction, he certainly
prejudges every sinful nation. So every sinful nation is Egypt and Ethiopia
from the species to the genus, just as every spectacle also is a council of
the impious from the genus to the species.

Lest anyone think we are being subtle, I will turn to the principal authority
of our own seal itself. When we have entered the water and profess the
Christian faith in the words of its law, we bear witness with our own mouth
that we have renounced the devil and his pomp and his angels. What will be the
highest and foremost thing in which the devil and his pomp and his angels are
held to be counted, if not idolatry? From which every unclean and wicked
spirit, so to speak, for I will not linger longer on this. Therefore if it is
established that the whole equipment of the spectacles consists of idolatry,
it will beyond doubt have been already judged that the testimony of our
renunciation in the bath, which is made over to the devil and his pomp and his
angels, namely through idolatry, also belongs to the spectacles. We will
recall the origins of each, in what cradles they grew up in the world, then
the titles of some, by what names they are called, then the equipment, with
what superstitions they are furnished, then the places, to what presiding
powers they are dedicated, then the arts, to what authors they are assigned.
If anything from these has not belonged to an idol, that will belong neither
to idolatry nor to our abjuration.

As to origins, being more hidden and unknown among most of our people, it was
not necessary to investigate more deeply1 nor abundantly than from the records
of heathen literature. There are many authors extant who have published
commentaries on this matter. From these the origin of the games is handed down
thus: Timaeus reports that Lydians, migrants from Asia, settled in Etruria
under the leadership of Tyrrhenus, who had yielded the kingdom to his brother
in a contest. Therefore in Etruria, among the other rites of their
superstitions, they also institute spectacles in the name of religion. From
there the Romans borrow the summoned craftsmen, the time, the name, so that
the players were called ludii from the Lydians. But even if Varro interprets
ludii from ludus, that is from play, just as they also called the Luperci
ludii because they run about playing, still he counts that play of the young
both to feast days and to temples and to religions. Nothing now about the
cause of the word, when the cause of the thing is idolatry. For even when the
games were called Liberalia indiscriminately, they plainly sounded the honor
of Father Liber. For they were first made to Liber by rustics for the benefit
they ascribe to him of the showing of wine. Then the games called Consualia,
which at first honored Neptune. For they call the same one also Consus. Then
the Equirria, from the horses, Romulus named for Mars; although they also
claim the Consualia for Romulus, because he dedicated them to Consus, the god,
as they will have it, of counsel, namely of that counsel by which he then
devised the rape of the Sabine virgins for his soldiers into marriages. A fine
counsel indeed, and even now among the Romans themselves just and lawful, not
to say before God. For this too makes for the stain of the origin, lest you
think that good which took its beginning from evil, from shamelessness, from
violence, from hatred, from a fratricide founder, from a son of Mars. And now
an altar to that Consus is sunk in the circus at the first turning-posts under
the ground with an inscription of this kind: CONSUS CONSILIO MARS DUELLO LARES
† COILLO POTENTES. They sacrifice at it on the Nones of July, the public
priests; on the twelfth day before the Kalends of September the flamen of
Quirinus and the virgins. Then the same Romulus instituted games to Jupiter
Feretrius on the Tarpeian, which Piso reports were called Tarpeian and
Capitoline; after him Numa Pompilius made them for Mars and Robigo (for they
have also invented a goddess of mildew); then Tullus Hostilius, then Ancus
Marcius and the rest. Who instituted games, which, for whom, in what order and
to what idols, is set down in Suetonius Tranquillus or in those from whom
Tranquillus received it. But these will be enough for the guilt of the origin
from idolatry.

There is added to the testimony of antiquity the following posterity, carrying
forward the form of the origin from the titles of this time also, by which it
has been marked to what idol and to what superstition the games of either kind
were noted. For the Megalensian and the Apollinarian, likewise the Cerealian
and Neptunalian and Latian and Floralian are celebrated in common; the rest of
the games have causes of superstition from the birthdays and solemnities of
kings and from public prosperities and municipal feasts. Among these even
bequest-editions for the memories of private persons make offerings to the
dead, that too according to the antiquity of the institution. For from the
beginning the games were counted in two ways, sacred and funeral, that is to
the gods of the nations and to the dead. But as to idolatry it makes no
difference with us under what name and title, so long as it comes to the same
spirits whom we renounce. Let them do it for the dead, let them do it for
their gods; they do it for their dead just as for gods; there is one condition
of both parts, one idolatry, one renunciation of ours against idolatry.

The origin of the games of either kind is therefore common, and the titles
common, as from common causes. Likewise they must have common equipment from
the general guilt of idolatry their foundress. But the array of the circus
games is a little more processional, to which this name properly belongs: the
pomp going before, proving whose it is in itself from the series of images,
from the troop of likenesses, from the chariots, from the tensae, from the
carriages, from the seats, from the crowns, from the spoils. How many rites
besides, how many sacrifices go before, come between, follow after, how many
colleges, how many priesthoods, how many offices are moved, the men of that
city know, in which the conventicle of demons has sat down. If these things
are administered with lesser care through the provinces according to lesser
means, still all circus games everywhere are to be assigned there from which
they are also sought; let them be inquired into1 from where they are taken.
For even a thin rivulet from its own spring and a modest shoot from its own
foliage holds the quality of its origin; let its display or its frugality see
to it * * be.2 Any pomp of the circus whatever offends God: even if it carries
about few images, there is idolatry in one; even if it draws one tensa, it is
still the wagon of Jupiter; any idolatry, sordidly furnished or moderately
rich, is splendid by the rating of its own crime.

To follow out the places also according to the plan, the circus is principally
consecrated to the Sun. His temple is in the middle of the space and his image
flashes from the pediment of the temple, because they have not thought that he
should be consecrated under a roof whom they have in the open. Those who
affirm that the first spectacle was given by Circe [have] to the Sun her
father, as they will, also argue the name of the circus from her. Plainly the
sorceress did them a service under this name, whose priestess she was, namely
for demons and angels. How many idolatries then do you recognize in the dress
of that place? The several ornaments of the circus are several temples. They
ascribe the eggs to the honor of the Castors, who are not ashamed to believe
those born from an egg of swan-Jupiter. The dolphins spout for Neptune; the
columns support Sessiae from sowings, Messiae from harvests, Tutulinae from
the guarding of fruits; before them three altars obey three gods, the Great,
the Powerful, the Valiant. They think these the same as the Samothracian gods.
The bulk of the obelisk, as Hermateles affirms, is prostituted to the Sun. Its
writing, whence also its origin and rating: the superstition is from Egypt.
The council of demons was cold without its Great Mother; she therefore
presides there over the euripus. Consus, as we have said, lurks at the
turning-posts under the earth of the Murcian. Those too he has made an idol:
for they will have Murcia a goddess of love, to whom they have vowed a temple
in that part.

Mark, Christian, how many unclean names have possessed the circus. Foreign to
you is the religion which so many spirits of the devil have occupied.

As to places indeed there is room for a further treatment, to forestall the
question of some. What then, you say, if I go to the circus at another time,
shall I be in danger of defilement? There is no rule about places. For not
only these meeting-places of the spectacles, but even the temples themselves a
servant of God can approach without danger to discipline, when a simple cause
urges, provided only it does not belong to the proper business or office of
that place. For the rest, even the streets and the forum and the baths and the
stables and our own houses themselves are not wholly without idols: the whole
world Satan and his angels have filled. Yet it is not because we are in the
world that we fall away from God, but if we have touched anything of the
world's crimes. So if I enter the Capitol or the Serapeum as a sacrificer or
worshipper, I shall fall away from God, just as I shall as a spectator of the
circus or the theatre. Places do not contaminate us by themselves, but the
things that are done in the places, by which we have argued that the places
themselves also are contaminated: from the contaminated we are contaminated.
For this reason we recall to whom places of this kind are dedicated, that we
may show that the things done in those places belong to those to whom the
places themselves are dedicated.

Now of the art by which the circus games are exhibited. The equestrian matter
was once simple: it was done from the back, and certainly common use was not
guilty. But when it was forced to the games, it passed from the gift of God to
the offices of demons. Therefore this kind is assigned to Castor and Pollux,
to whom Stesichorus teaches that horses were distributed by Mercury. But
Neptune too is equestrian, whom the Greeks call ἵππιον. They have ordained
six-horse teams for Jupiter, four-horse for the Sun, two-horse for the Moon.
But also first Erichthonius dared to join a chariot and four horses and,
swift, to stand upon the wheels, a victor. Erichthonius, son of Minerva and
Vulcan, and indeed from lust fallen to the earth, is a demonic portent, or
rather the devil himself, not a snake. If however Trochilus the Argive is the
author of the chariot, he dedicated that work of his first to Juno. If at Rome
Romulus first showed the four-horse team, I think he too is enrolled among the
idols, if he is the same as Quirinus. With such authors the four-horse cars
were brought out, and deservedly they also clothed the charioteers with the
colors of idolatry. For at first there were only two, white and red. White had
been vowed to winter for the white snows, red to summer for the redness of the
sun. But afterward, as pleasure and superstition advanced, some consecrated
the red to Mars, others the white to the Zephyrs, the green to Mother Earth or
to spring, the blue to Sky and Sea or to autumn. But since every kind of
idolatry has been condemned by God, that too is certainly condemned which is
profaned to the elements of the world.

Let us pass to the stage matters, whose origin we have already shown to be
common and whose titles equal, according to the very name of the games from
the beginning, and whose administration is joined with the equestrian matter.
The equipment too is partner from that part by which they go to the stage from
temples and altars and that unhappy business of incense and blood, among pipes
and trumpets, with two most filthy overseers of funerals and of rites, the
arranger and the soothsayer. So when we have passed from the origins of the
games to the circus games, from there we will now direct ourselves to the
stage games from the vice of the place.1 The theatre is properly a shrine of
Venus. For this is the way that kind of work has come out in the world. For
the censors often, just as theatres were arising, used to destroy them,
consulting the morals, of which they plainly foresaw a huge danger from
wantonness, so that already here the judgment of the heathen themselves may go
over to testimony, making with us, and even a human prerogative may go to the
heightening of our discipline. So Pompey the Great, lesser only than his own
theatre, when he had built that citadel of all baseness, fearing someday a
censorial penalty upon his memory, set a temple of Venus on top and, calling
the people by edict to the dedication, named it not a theatre but a temple of
Venus, to which, he said, we have subjected the steps of the spectacles. Thus
he veiled a condemned and to-be-condemned work with the title of a temple and
cheated discipline by superstition. But Venus and Liber agree. Those two
demons are leagued and sworn together, of drunkenness and of lust. Therefore
the theatre of Venus is also the house of Liber. For they also properly called
other stage games Liberalia, besides those devoted to Liber, which are the
Dionysia among the Greeks, instituted even by Liber. And there is plainly in
the stage arts also the patronage of Liber and of Venus. What is private and
proper to the stage they sacrifice from gesture and from the bending of the
body to the softness of Venus and of Liber, to the one by sex, to the other by
luxury, with bodies loosened. But what is carried out by voice and measures
and instruments and letters1 they have Apollos and Muses and Minervas and
Mercuries as contractors. You should hate, Christian, those whose authors you
cannot but hate.

Now we wish to suggest concerning the arts and concerning those whose authors
we execrate in their names. We know that the names of the dead are nothing,
just as their images themselves are nothing; but we are not unaware who work
and rejoice and feign divinity under those names by the instituted images,
namely wicked spirits, demons. We see therefore that their arts also have been
dedicated to the honors of those who inhabit the names of their authors, and
that they are not free from idolatry, whose institutors are even for that
reason held as gods. Nay, as to what belongs to the arts, we ought to have
prescribed more deeply2 that the demons from the beginning, looking out for
themselves among the other defilements of idolatry even the defilements of the
spectacles, by which they might call man away from the Lord and bind him to
their own honor, also inspired the talents of arts of this kind. For it would
not have been procured by others, that which was going to come through to
them, nor would they then have put it forth through other men than through
those in whose names and images and stories they set up for themselves the
fraud of consecration as a business they were going to transact. That the
order may be carried through, let us enter also upon the treatment of the
contests.

Their origin is from the neighborhood of the games. Hence they themselves too
were instituted as sacred or funeral, and are made either to the gods of the
nations or to the dead. Likewise the titles: the Olympia to Jupiter, which at
Rome are the Capitoline, likewise the Nemea to Hercules, the Isthmia to
Neptune, the rest various contests of the dead. What wonder then if even the
equipment of the contests is defiled by idolatry from profane crowns, from
priestly presidents, from collegiate ministers, from the very blood of oxen at
last? To complete the matter of place also, and of the common place, for the
college of the musical and Minerval and Apollinarian arts, even the Martial by
duel, by the trumpet they rival the circus in the stadium, which is certainly
a temple itself too of that idol whose solemnities it conducts. But the
gymnastic arts also the disciplines of the Castors and Herculeses and
Mercuries have handed down.

There remains the review of that most notable and most received spectacle. It
was called munus from office, since office is also the name of a munus. But
the ancients thought they were doing this office to the dead by this
spectacle, after they had tempered it with a more humane atrocity. For once,
since it was believed that the souls of the departed were propitiated by human
blood, they used to buy captives or slaves of bad condition and sacrifice them
at the funerals. Afterward it pleased them to cloak impiety with pleasure. So
those whom they had prepared, trained with such arms as they then could and in
such a way as they could, only that they might learn to be killed, they soon
on the appointed day of the offerings spent at the tombs. Thus they consoled
death with murders. This is the origin of the munus. But little by little they
were carried forward to as much favor as also to cruelty, because it was not
enough for the pleasure of the holidays unless human bodies were also torn
apart by beasts. What therefore was offered to the dead was certainly assigned
to the funeral offering; which kind is likewise idolatry, since idolatry also
is a kind of funeral offering: this as well as that ministers to the dead. But
in the idols of the dead demons take their seat.

To consider the titles also, although this kind of edition has passed from the
honors of the dead to the honors of the living, I mean the quaestorships and
magistracies and flamenships and priesthoods, yet since the dignity of the
name is held by the crime of idolatry, whatever is administered in the name of
the dignity must also share the stains of that from which it has its causes.
We will interpret the same of the equipment, to be assigned to the array of
the honors themselves, because the purples, because the fasces, because the
fillets, because the crowns, because lastly the assemblies and edicts and the
porridge of the day before, are not without the pomp of the devil, without the
imitation of demons. Why should I make a speech about the horrifying place,
which not even perjuries endure? For the amphitheatre is consecrated with more
and harsher names than the Capitol: it is the temple of all demons. As many
unclean spirits sit down there as it holds men. To conclude also concerning
the arts, we know Mars and Diana as the presidents of either game.

Enough, I think, we have filled out the order, in how many and in what ways
the spectacles commit idolatry, from origins, from titles, from equipment,
from places, from arts, so that we may be sure they in no part suit us, who
twice renounce idols. Not that an idol is anything, as the apostle says, but
because what they do they do to demons seated, that is, in the consecrations
of idols, whether of the dead or, as they think, of gods. Therefore, since
both kinds of idols are of one condition, while the dead and gods are one, we
abstain from both idolatries. No less do we spit out temples than monuments;
we know neither altar, we adore neither image, we do not sacrifice, we do not
make offerings to the dead. But neither do we eat of the sacrifice and of what
has been offered to the dead, because we cannot eat the supper of God and the
supper of demons. If then we free gullet and belly from defilements, how much
more do we keep our more august parts, both ears and eyes, from pleasures of
idol-sacrifices and dead-sacrifices, which are not transacted in the
intestines but are digested in the spirit itself and in the soul, whose
cleanness belongs more to God than that of the intestines.
