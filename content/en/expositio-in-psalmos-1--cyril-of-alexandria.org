Of Cyril, Archbishop of Alexandria, on the Psalms.
Sacred words and a gathering of divine lessons procure a glorious
benefit for those who have spoken them. For wisdom is better than
precious stones, and every precious thing is not worthy of her. And I
think one must labor, and very eagerly, in things so profitable, and
that the lover of things worthy of admiration must be stronger than
hesitation and sloth; for the choicest of achievements would not be
capturable by those who are accustomed to sink into sloth, but by those
who are willing to sweat over them. Hence I too have been spurred even
now to the need of expounding, according to my power, a few things also
upon the book of the Psalms, for I hold it in great admiration. Some
have already written before us, yet it is still a god-loving exercise
for us also to put forward the composition.
All Scripture, then, being God-inspired, is rich in what is profitable,
for it directs toward what is pleasing to God, and works an exact
knowledge of the excellent life; yet especially the writing of melody
almost even intoxicates with the narratives concerning Christ, the
Savior of us all, and it proclaims beforehand the mystery of the
Incarnation, finely and as if filed down, omitting nothing at all of the
things that have come to pass. For it narrates all things clearly: both
the teachings of the Savior and the very signs of God, and besides these
the drunken outrages of the Jews against him, the cross and the things
upon it, the reproaches and the sneering, the descent into Hades, the
redemption of the spirits below, the resurrection from the dead, the
ascent into the heavens, the falling away of those from Israel, the
calamities, the capture of Jerusalem, the turning away that came upon
the whole nation, the remnant that was saved, the proclamation of the
holy apostles, the spiritual liturgy, the calling of the nations, the
turning of Israel through faith that followed after this, the honors
that will later belong to the saints, the city above, the running up
into incorruption of the bodies from the earth; and, in a word,
everything that tends to benefit and salvation for those who will come
upon it: this it is her purpose to narrate clearly and without error
and as from the Holy Spirit.
And it must be known that the inspired David, being a prophet and
knowing that God swore to him with an oath to set upon his throne one
from the fruit of his belly, as it is written, loves to dwell in the
narratives concerning Christ. From this I think he also came to the
need of composing odes and songs of doxology. And the matter was not
done out of tune with the Law. For it commanded this also among the
rest, and at any rate it legislated the manner for each sacrifice. And
it also makes mention of a sacrifice of praise, which it also names a
sacrifice of salvation, for it is written: I will take the cup of
salvation and call upon the name of the Lord. And God spoke thus to the
hierophant Moses: This is the law of the sacrifice of salvation, which
they shall offer to the Lord. If he offer it for praise, he shall also
offer for the sacrifice of praise loaves of fine flour made up in oil,
and unleavened cakes anointed with oil, and fine flour mixed with oil;
upon leavened loaves he shall offer his gifts upon a sacrifice of praise
of salvation. And yet it has clearly commanded in other places that
what is leavened is not to be brought to God; for it said thus: You
shall not offer to God any leaven and any honey. You shall offer them
as a gift of firstfruits, but they shall not go up upon the altar for a
savor of fragrance to the Lord. Then how is the sacrifice of salvation
and of praise accomplished upon leavened loaves? It has a mystical
meaning.
For the Law proclaimed beforehand that those from Israel who believed
were going to bring the sacrifices of praise to God mixed together with
those from the nations.
The matter, then, was found from the Law, I mean the glorifying of the
God of all in the holy temple; and for this there had been appointed
choirs and choir-leaders, singers and sacred psalmists from the sons of
Levi, and they offered up the hymns to God both in the morning and in
the evening, when the lambs were being sacrificed and the fine and
compounded incense was being burned. For it is written thus in Exodus:
And these are the things you shall do upon the altar: two yearling
lambs without blemish each day upon the altar continually, a perpetual
offering; you shall offer the one lamb in the morning, and you shall
offer the second lamb toward evening. And concerning the incense it
said thus: And you shall make an altar of incense of incorruptible
wood. And it adds to these: And he shall burn upon it fine incense
morning by morning, when he dresses the lamps; at evening he shall burn
upon it incense of a perpetual offering continually before the Lord
throughout their generations. And the sacrifice of the lambs would
signify that Christ has been sacrificed for us and on our behalf, and
has been offered up as a savor of fragrance to God and Father; but also
the offering of the fine and compounded incense would altogether hint
at this, for the sacrifice is perpetual and unceasing; for Emmanuel is
always fragrant in a noetic sense, being the odor of the knowledge of
God and Father, and making his holy tabernacle, that is the Church,
full of him.
They sang, therefore, as I said, while the sacrifice of the lambs was
being performed, and also at all the other sacrifices, fittingly for
each as was reasonable; for it is written thus in the first of
Chronicles: And the Levites were those who carried the tabernacle and
all its vessels for its ministry; for also in the last words of David
is the number of the sons of Levi from twenty years old and upward,
because he set them under the hand of Aaron to minister in the house of
the Lord, over the courts and over the chambers, and over the purifying
of all the holy things, and over the works of the ministry of the house
of God, and for the loaves of the presentation, for the fine flour of
the sacrifice, and for the unleavened cakes, and for the frying-pan,
and for that which is mixed, and for every measure; and to stand in the
morning to praise and give thanks to the Lord, and likewise in the
evening; and over all the whole-burnt offerings offered to the Lord on
the sabbaths and on the new moons and on the feasts, according to
number, according to the ordinance concerning them, continually to the
Lord.
And that certain chosen ones of those from the blood of Levi were
appointed for the odes, the sacred writing will again confirm, for it
has thus in Chronicles: And David the king and the rulers of the host
set apart for the works the sons of Asaph and Heman and Jeduthun, those
who uttered in harps and in nablas and in timbrels. Those of whom the
account speaks, then, sang according to a rhythm, I suppose, that was
native and familiar to the peoples of the Hebrews, the psalms having
been composed for them. And they say that the inspired David composed
and sang some of them himself, having received the divine temple, and
that others again the psalm-singers, being priests and holy and having
a spirit of prophetic grace. For this reason also it has been clearly
written, some of David, and some to David, the writers of them perhaps
dedicating them to the blessed David as inventor and first of psalmody,
or also (which, as I think, also appears more fitting to understand)
referring the power of the ode to Christ, for he has been named David
also in the sacred writings, because according to the flesh he came
from the blood of the blessed David. At any rate it is written
concerning the sons of Israel, that in the last times they too will
change their mind, and will be called through faith into the knowledge
of Christ. For it says: And after these things the sons of Israel shall
return and shall seek the Lord their God and David their king.
And it must be known that some of the psalms, as I have already said
before, speak clearly and plainly the mystery of Christ the Savior of
us all, while others have the force of speaking in a person. For one is
introduced, perhaps, offering thank-offerings for the things in which
he has been well treated, and fulfilling the sacrifice of praise;
another suffering persecutions for piety, and enduring afflictions and
trials, and longing for help from above; and another having played the
man and conquered and prevailed over the strength of those who stood
against him; and another held fast in the storms of sin and of the
passions, and asking mercy from him who knows how to save. And there
are also those that have a narration and teaching of a most lawful and
decent way of life, and how one might live splendidly, and they also
make remembrance of more ancient deeds that have come to pass to the
glory of God. And all these things will work a very great benefit, and
will show as craftsmen for every good work those who wish, with an
accurate eye of the mind, to survey the way of the holy life.
