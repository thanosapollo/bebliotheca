#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Fragments edited by J. M. Edmonds
#+END_QUOTE

When the wind strikes the gray sea gently,
I am stirred in the coward mind, nor is earth any longer
dear to me, and the great sea longs for me much more.
But when the hoary deep sounds, and the sea
foams in a hump, and the long waves rage,
I look toward land and trees, and I flee the sea,
and earth is welcome to me, and the thick wood has pleased,
where even if a great wind blows, the pine sings.
Truly the fisher lives a bad life, whose house is the ship,
and toil is the sea, and fishes the wandering hunt.
But for me sweet sleep under a deep-leaved plane,
and I would love to hear the nearby sound of a spring,
which delights the rustic as it sounds, it does not trouble.

Pan loved Echo the neighbor, and Echo loved
the leaping Satyr, and the Satyr raged for Lyda.
As Echo the Pan, so much the Satyr burned Echo,
and Lyda the little Satyr; and Eros smoldered by exchange.

For as much as one of them hated the one who loved,
so much likewise the one who loved was hated, and he suffered what he did.
These teachings I say to all the unloving:
cherish those who love, that if you love you may be loved.

Alpheus, when he travels after Pisa upon the sea,
comes to Arethusa bringing wild-olive-bearing water,
bringing as wedding-gifts fair leaves and flowers and holy dust,
and he goes deep into the waves, and the sea
he runs under from beneath, and water mixes not with waters,
and the sea does not know of the river going through.
A boy, flax-setter, badly-devising, teaching terrible things,
Eros even taught a river through a love-charm to dive.

Having set down torch and bows he took a cattle-driving rod,
curly Eros, and he had a pouch from the shoulder,
and having yoked a toiling neck of bulls under the yoke
he sowed a wheat-bearing furrow of Deo.
And looking up he said to Zeus himself: /fill the fields,/
/lest I put you, the ox of Europa, under the ploughs/.
