* Dialectics, or Philosophical Chapters
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Another chapter.
Genus is divided into species. The genus is above, and the species are
below. It is impossible for the genus not to have species beneath it, and
impossible for the species not to have a genus above them, just as it is
impossible for a father not to have a son and impossible for a son not to
have a father. The genus holds the rank of a father, and the species that
of a son. When the genus is divided into species, the species must receive
both the name and the complete definition of their genus. But the genus
does not in every case admit the definition of the species, nor do the
species admit one another's definition. The common name of all beings is
being. Being is either a self-subsistent thing that needs nothing else
for its constitution but has its being in itself, such as a body, or a
thing that cannot be in itself but has its existence in another, such as
color. Being is divided into substance and accident. Substance is a
self-subsistent thing that needs nothing else for its constitution but
has its being in itself, such as the body. Accident is a thing that
cannot have its being in itself but has its existence in another, such as
color. For the body is not in the color, but the color is in the body,
nor is the body said to be of the color, but the color of the body.
Substance does not change, but accident changes. For the body does not
change, but the color changes. Therefore neither substance nor accident
receives the complete definition of being, but substance receives the
half and accident the half. For this reason being is not a genus, nor
are substance and accident species. The most generic genus is the first
genus, which, being a genus, is not a species, because it has no other
genus above it.
The most specific species is the last species, which, being a species, is
not a genus, because it has no other species beneath it. Those between
the most generic genus and the most specific species are called subaltern
genera and species, because they have a genus above them and species
beneath them. The subaltern genera and species are the constitutive and
essential and natural differences and qualities. For these constitute
the most specific species: the most specific species are constituted from
the genus and from them. Those below receive all of those above them;
those above do not in every case receive those below them. The Fathers
called the most specific species substance and nature and form, and those
divided from the most specific species, that is those beneath it,
individual and hypostasis and person. The genus is one, and under one
genus there are two species divided against each other: for example,
substance is a genus. It is divided into two species, into body and
incorporeal. These are called species divided against each other. The
vainly wise of those outside call hypostasis a first, particular
substance; the most specific species, that is nature, a second, common
substance; and the most generic genus a third, more universal substance.
Those who follow them are therefore forced, in the case of the Holy
Trinity, to speak of four substances, three particular and one common.
When in the same nature a thing and its contrary are observed, these are
accidents: for example, in the nature of man, whiteness and blackness,
grey-eyed and black-eyed, tall and short, sickness and health, and the
like. But when a thing is observed, and it is impossible for its contrary
to be observed, this is natural: for example, the rational is observed in
man, but the irrational cannot be observed in man. The natural is called
an essential property; the accident is called a non-essential property.
Natural properties are called constitutive; hypostatic properties, that
is accidents, are called characterizing properties. According to natural
properties nature differs from nature and species from species; according
to accidents a hypostasis differs from a hypostasis of the same species
and of the same substance. For example, man differs from horse according
to the rational and the irrational: this is a natural difference. Peter
differs from Paul, in that the one is bald and the other thick-haired.
See: baldness and hairiness are accidents. 50. Being (it is not a genus;
substance is the first and most generic genus) is divided into substance
and accident, into body and incorporeal, into animate and inanimate, into
sensitive and insensitive (which is animal, zoophyte, and plant), into
rational and irrational, into mortal and immortal (which is man), into
Peter, Paul, John, and the rest of men. Peter and Paul and John and the
rest of men are called hypostases and persons and individuals. And those
between substance and man are called subaltern genera and species. And
these are essential and natural divisions, constitutive and specifying,
and differences and qualities: dividing of those above and constitutive
of those below, species of those above and genera of those below, and
specifying because they constitute the most specific species. Man is a
most specific species. Substance is a self-subsistent thing that needs
nothing else for its constitution. Substance is everything that is
self-hypostatic and does not have its existence in another. The accident
is in the substance. Substance is so called from being in the proper
sense; accident is so called from happening, and from sometimes being and
sometimes not being, and from its being possible in the same thing both
for the same accident to be and not to be, but rather its contrary.
Nature is the principle of the motion and rest of each of the beings: for
example, the earth is moved with respect to sprouting, but is at rest
with respect to passage from place to place, for it is not moved from
place to place. Nature, then, is the principle and cause of its motion
and rest, according to which it is essentially and naturally disposed to
be moved and to be at rest. Nature is so called from being naturally
disposed, which signifies substance. For substance is the cause of its
motion and rest. Nature and substance are therefore the same. And it is
called nature from being naturally disposed. Form is substance shaped
by body, by the animate, and by the sensitive, and completing the animal,
and again taking on the rational and the mortal, it completes the species
of man, which most specific species is called form, as being shaped
substance. Substance and nature and form are therefore the same. The
individuals under the same most specific species are therefore
consubstantial, of the same nature, of the same species, and of the same
form. The most specific species, and the individuals under one species
and another, are called of different substance, of different nature, of
different species, and of different form. Those under the same genus are
of the same genus; those under one genus and another are of different
genera. It is impossible for one composite nature to come to be from two
natures. The name hypostasis sometimes signifies existence simply, that
is substance, and sometimes existence in itself and self-constituted,
that is the individual, such as Peter, Paul. The holy Fathers called
hypostasis and individual and person the same.
Hypostasis is that which, from substance and accidents, exists in itself
in a self-constituted way and differs in number. For Peter and Paul are
not one but two. And it is called hypostasis from subsisting. "One" as
a thing is said of the one nature, and "one" as a person of the one
hypostasis. For example, Peter and Paul are not called one person,
because they are one hypostasis and another, but they are called one
thing, because they are of one nature. "Another thing" is said of
difference of nature, and "another person" of hypostasis. For example,
we cannot say "Peter is another thing and Paul is another thing," because
they are one according to nature; but we say "Peter is another person and
Paul another," because they are not one man but two. The enhypostatic
sometimes signifies being, and sometimes the individual. Properly, the
enhypostatic is either the most specific species, because it is not
observed in itself but in the hypostases, or that which is composed with
another different in substance toward the coming-to-be of some whole: for
example, man is from soul and body. Neither the soul is called a
hypostasis nor the body, but they are enhypostatic, because the one never
existed before the other nor apart from the other, and that which is
completed from both is the hypostasis of both. Hence also the flesh of
the Lord is not called a hypostasis but enhypostatic, because it never
existed in itself but in the hypostasis of the Word, and it had and has
that as its hypostasis. And the anhypostatic sometimes signifies the
non-existent, and sometimes the accident.
