THE LORD NIKEPHOROS THE EMPEROR προλ.1 Wishing to hand down the method of
skirmishing, even if perhaps at the present time it is not needed in the parts
of the east, inasmuch as Christ our true God has blunted the greater part of
the power and strength that the descendants of Ishmael had against us and has
checked their assaults, still, so that time, bringing in forgetfulness and
ignorance, may not destroy what is useful in it and wipe it out, we thought it
necessary to hand this method down in writing, so that, if ever Christians
should have need of it when some future season calls, it may be a ready and
good helper both for the whole commonwealth and for those who handle it.
προλ.2 We, then, have not received it by hearing only, but have also been
taught some experience from it, having had as teachers and instructors those
who, so to speak, invented the method, and having ourselves also used it alone
and taken some experience of it into our bosom, as far as was possible for us.
προλ.3 This method, then, has its usefulness in this, that those who practiced
it with a small fighting force accomplished very great and memorable deeds;
for things which the whole Roman army was not able or did not dare to achieve,
when the affairs of the Cilicians and of Chambdas were at their height, one of
the best generals at times, with only the people of the theme under him,
handling the enemy prudently and with experience and disposing and generaling
intelligently, accomplished by himself. προλ.4 We say this, not because we
prefer the little


fighting force to the greater, nor because we exalt this method above the rest
of the devices and practices of tactics, but because for the best generals it
has been established as the best helper, so that, when they could not array
themselves against the enemy face to face, using this method they might keep
both themselves unharmed and their own country. προλ.5 Bardas the late Caesar,
so far as I know, brought this method to the height of success and renewed it
when it had vanished altogether, that we may not number the ancient generals
one by one, but only those known to all and of our own time, who, generaling
in the themes neighboring Tarsus, Cappadocia and the Anatolikon, ten thousand
times wounded the forces of the Tarsites and the rest of the Cilicians and set
up very great trophies against them; from him we too, having been trained in
this method, have received at least some experience of it. προλ.6 In addition,
Constantine the patrician, whose surname is Maleinos, having generaled the
Cappadocians for very many years and having this as a helper, accomplished
achievements that were not the least. προλ.7 With these also the lord
Nikephoros, the celebrated emperor, practiced it to the height, who indeed,
while generaling and using it, did very many and admirable deeds, routing the
enemy ten thousand times, so to speak, and cutting down very great armies of
them; whose acts of prowess, in the time when he was general, it will be
wearisome for readers to write or to number, both because of the exceeding
multitude and because they are known by many. προλ.8 He, having learned by
deed that it is useful, set out in writing for the common benefit how one
ought to use it, and he charged me to write about it as accurately as possible
and to hand it down to those after us; and not only about this method that
belongs to the east, but also about the one that has been established as
useful for the west, because he generaled very much in it and gathered
experience, as much as was possible for our nature. But concerning that method
it will be said separately and in other writings; for the present our aim is
to treat of the other. Wherefore, keeping his command, and using the grace of
God as a helper, which takes part in all good things, I begin the subject
somewhat in this way. 1.τ Concerning watches and how great a distance each of
them ought to stand apart from the others 1.1 Those who take up the care of
the great frontier themes and have the passes under their authority ought with
every device and purpose and sleepless attention to be eager and to strive to
guard the lands of the Romans unharmed and unmolested from the enemy's raid,
setting watchmen who are stout and fit and who know the roads to the height.
1.2 And if there are high and hard-to-cross mountains bounding the enemy
country, the watches are to stand on these; and the stations of the watchmen
are to be distant as far as 3 or 4 miles. And when they see the enemies going
out, they are to go at a run to the other post and report what they saw, and
those again to the next post with much haste; and thus in order the posts of
the horsemen that stand in the level places are to be informed, and through
them the general is to learn the assault of the nations. 1.3 They are to hold
out fifteen days in the guarding of the roads, carrying also the food
sufficient for them for those days; then others are to go to their relief,
being well mustered and made ready by the officers, and being sent sound and
not short of the number that has been appointed, and they are not to be
allowed by their officers to sit at home for shameful gain. 1.4 It is possible
for the watchmen to look toward the places in which it is fitting that the
camps of the enemy be made; for there the camps are accustomed as a rule to be
made, where there is sufficient water and level ground. Others are to look
toward the places where the road makes a defile, and others where there is a
river hard to cross. And if these keep watch so securely, the enemy will not
be able to make their


going-out in secret. 1.5 And they are to send faithful and most experienced
men to look upon them, whether they are guarding their stations well and
sleeplessly. 1.6 And let the appointed watchmen go sound in number and not
short, and let them not abandon the stations in which they were ordered to
look and to guard the roads, nor linger at the same posts, but change the
places and move elsewhere, so that, lingering long in the same places, they
may not be recognized and become easy to catch for the enemies. 2.τ Concerning
beacon-watches and spies 2.1 Necessary also is the benefit of the
beacon-watchmen; and the general must take much care of these too and set the
beacon-watches in fit places, so that, when a movement of the enemies takes
place, both the watchmen may perceive it through the beacon-watches, and the
general may know beforehand the going-out of the enemies and by what road they
are about to go out, and the countries, being informed through these and
through those who send the people into the strongholds, may flee to the
strongholds, and their flocks as well. 2.2 And on the edges of the Armenian
themes, since the Armenians do not perform the service of the watches well or
securely, it is owed that the type that has prevailed from of old in the
watches of the Armenians be kept, and that fit men be chosen and enrolled,
taking pay collected from the army, in return for which such an army ought to
serve the liturgy of the watches, and likewise the appointed ration each
month, and that they be relieved each month and through them the roads by
which the enemy go out be guarded securely. 2.3 But since not even these who
are with pay and ration, being Armenians, serve the duty of the watches well,
it is necessary to learn the movements of the hosts from the spies who are
sent, and to choose trapezitai who are noble and brave, whom the Armenians
call tasinarioi, and to enroll them in the registers and set leaders over
them, who besides bravery have also much experience of the roads and of the
countries of Syria, and to send these continually to go down into the
countries of the enemy and to raid them and do them harm, and if anywhere they
are able to seize some of the enemy and bring them to the general who holds
the command, so that through them he may learn the movements and plans of the
enemies. 3.τ Concerning movement of enemies and occupying the difficult ground
beforehand 3.1 And the general, being taught the movements of the enemies,
ought himself also to gather his own people and arm them, and to come to the
edges, and to gather all the infantry toward the road by which the enemy will
set out to go forth. And if they learn that the enemies are going out with a
small force, let him hasten to meet them and fight them down with the infantry
people and the cavalry, if indeed he has been able to gather and set in order
the army of the infantry. 3.2 And since fighting with infantry is more fit in
the defiles and the difficult ground, it is necessary to occupy beforehand and
hold the higher parts of the mountains with them, and from both sides, if the
situation of the place is fit, to station the infantry arrays, and in places
where it is fitting that a fight of cavalry take place, let cavalry also be
with the infantry, so that the enemy, hearing of such a preparation and
occupying of the defiles, may either hold back the rush of their onset or be
overcome with the help of God. And where the situation of the place does not
fit an attack of war from both sides, but from one, likewise that higher side
too must be occupied beforehand. 3.3 And in places where the unfitness of the
ground prevents the attack of the fight from being made from a high hill, but
the road is a little uphill and rough, having also gullies, and from these is
found itself also most narrow, one must likewise prepare the array of the
infantry from the higher places and


hold the road and block it with shield-bearing hoplites and javelineers, and
behind these those who throw stones from the hands, and with them also archers
and slingers, and after the first array prepare a second following it, and on
either side of the array that guards the middle station javelineers, both
light-armed and slingers. 3.4 And if other roads are said to be on the right
and left of the array that guards the public road, not nearby but far off,
which the frontiersmen call paths, these too must be held with infantry and
guarded securely, lest ever the enemy, learning that the public road is held
in security with a multitude of people, set out upon another of the side roads
and, if it is not well and securely guarded, find a passage from there and
come to be on the flank or even at the back of the arrays and cause
distraction and fear to the Roman army. 3.5 And if both are guarded securely,
either the enemy, setting out to battle, will be put to shame with the help of
God, or, seized by dread, they will make their onset by another road distant
by an interval of many days. And from this two evils come upon the enemies:
one, to be worn out by the interval of many days, having left their camps and
the straight road; another, which also brings destruction upon them, that they
themselves grow cowardly and are cast into faintheartedness, while the Romans
become more eager and more daring for the war against them. 3.6 Such a thing
also happened in former times to Ali the son of Chambdas for a third time:
twice in the times of the celebrated and thrice-blessed Constantine the
Porphyrogennetos the emperor, and once under the good emperor Romanos, his
late son. And almost all know accurately the utter destruction of the deniers
of Christ that took place then; but the people of the Tarsites also and of the
country of the Cilicians were trodden down in various places of the defiles by
the generals then caring for the frontier. 4.τ Concerning making the attacks
against the enemies in secret, and concerning meeting the enemy as they turn
back toward their own country 4.1 The general must have this too as an aim and
a study and set all eagerness upon it, to make the attacks of the enemies
secretly and unexpectedly, if possible; for if the general succeeds in such a
practice, even with a very small people he will tread down many of the enemy.
4.2 And if, as has been shown above, he also finds a place in the defiles that
has fitness for the war against the enemies from two sides, let him take hold
of the fight without hesitation; and, arraying well the people under him, with
the help of God he will rout the enemy by force. 4.3 Yet it is by far more
profitable and more fitting, rather than to meet the enemy when they are about
to go out against Romania, when rather they are turning back from our
countries toward their own; for then, because they have spent much time in the
Roman countries, they are greatly worn and distressed. And if they happen also
to be carrying a multitude of goods and captives and cattle, and they
themselves and their horses being exhausted are found slack in a time of war,
hastening and desiring to reach their own country more quickly. 4.4 And
otherwise also the armies of the Romans, in the stretching out of the days,
not only those near the passes are gathered more quickly, but also those that
happen to be far off; and they are assembled into a sufficient multitude, and
they are well fitted and prepared for war. And then rather, as we said before,
both by day and by night the war against them has the victory without
hesitation. 4.5 Wherefore the general must not yet leave them on the return.
But to prepare, at the going-out of the enemy, to meet them for war in the
defiles


is less fit, and perhaps the labor happens to be in vain, because the infantry
army cannot so quickly in a few days be gathered and well prepared. And
otherwise also the enemy, being fresh and not yet disarmed by toil, will be
hard to overcome. 4.6 Yet in this it has benefit and use, that they become
afraid, as often as they wish to go out, by the occupying of the defiles, and
that their continual going-out against the Roman themes is shortly cut off.
5.τ Concerning occupying beforehand the waters that are in the defiles 5.1 In
the places of the defiles and passes where the general prepares for war, if
there is in them a spring gushing water, he will be eager that this be
occupied by his own people, so that the people may draw the water from there.
And if it happens to be scanty, he will be eager that it not be muddied, and
no small grief and necessity will come upon the people from this. And if there
is no spring very near, from which the people find drink and refreshment, and
especially in time of summer, let him appoint that ten skins be carried in
each century and that the water be brought by them and in the hour of battle
be supplied for drink and refreshment to those fighting in the war. 5.2 But
concerning those who are about to be fitted for war in the defiles let enough
have been said. And if there will also be need of night fighting in such
difficult ground, this too is very useful; but concerning these things I will
show in another account, and I will begin the instruction of skirmishing,
which, being beneficial and necessary, with a small and easily numbered people
prepares many multitudes of the enemy to accomplish nothing, and by the nod of
God for the most part also overcomes them. 6.τ Concerning skirmishing against
independent raids and estimating the number of their people 6.1 The raids of
the enemy that are called independent raids, going out from their own country
without infantry, have a custom of making the march in haste and of not
spending the whole night camping anywhere, but of resting the horses a little
only so as to give them the feeds and nothing more; for as a rule the
independent raids make the going-out with a small and chosen people, and they
hasten to reach as quickly as possible the villages in which they have planned
to make the attack and the raid. 6.2 When therefore the general learns their
going-out from the beacon-watches and the watchmen, let him with much haste
occupy the villages on the edges, sending ahead a tourmarch or another officer
of the most experienced and useful, with chosen officers and well-mounted men,
to meet them and with experience to see them and to shadow them and to report
to the general in what place they have arrived, and if possible to observe
also the number of their people. 6.3 For it is possible not only from the
sight to count them and estimate them, but also from their hoof-print; for a
mass of grass being in the wastes and being trampled by the feet of the
animals, the most experienced of the men can from this estimate the number of
the people, even if not accurately; and likewise also at the fords of the
rivers, and more accurately also from their camps, when they set out from
there. 6.4 And the general, learning the arrival of the enemy's raid and
estimating the villages in which they are about to make the raids, let him
come near these and, hiding himself with his people in a fit place, and
sending watches of horsemen everywhere, when he is informed about them, and
they are seen riding out and scattering, let him go out upon the scattering,
and meeting them he will overcome them without toil. 6.5 And if it happens
that some of the villagers are seized by them before they ride out and learn
both the presence of the general and the sending of the villages into the
strongholds, and they will not dare to ride out, they will turn back without
result in haste toward their own country, only the labor and the wearing and
the distress


having undergone. 7.τ Concerning the gathering and movement of a host and
concerning allowing merchants to enter and spy 7.1 Hearing of the preparation
and movement of a great host, in the season especially when the great hosts
were accustomed to be gathered, that is in the month of August (for in such a
season from Egypt and Palestine and Phoenicia and Coele Syria multitudes used
to come up into Cilicia and into the countries of Antioch, of Chalepe, and
taking Arabs also they used to make the going-out against the Romans in the
month of September), in such a season then, when the gathering of the enemy's
host is about to take place, send more continually the trapezitai, that is the
tasinakia, and very many spies of the useful and faithful. 7.2 And allow
merchants also to enter, and pretend also friendship toward the ruling emirs
of the fortresses lying beside our edges, and write to them and send men with
gifts, so that through this many-formed busyness he may be able to be taught
clearly the plans and studies of the enemy and the multitudes of the hosts
being gathered, both of cavalry and of infantry, and their leaders and in what
countries they wish to make the attack. 7.3 And being informed of the
setting-out of the host and by what road it is about to make the going-out
against Romania, gather all the armies and come to the edges, and send a most
experienced tourmarch or some other of the great and useful officers with
chosen horsemen to meet the enemy before they make the inroad into our
countries, so that they may both follow them and shadow them and report their
movements to the general. 8.τ Concerning the shadowing of a host and the
following of it 8.1 And their shadowing ought to be made thus. While the
general with all the people is running beside them in a fit and strong place,
and the villages through those who send them into the strongholds are being
fled into the fortresses or, if there are no fortresses, into high and most
strong and unassailable mountains, and likewise all their cattle, it is
necessary to send also the one who ought to shadow from the second hour or
even the third of the day. 8.2 And taking the prayer of the general, he is to
take up his people 8.2 and the most lightly equipped and set out toward the
road by which the enemy pass, leaving his baggage and the remounts with the
general, carrying nothing else but one day's food, and that with scarcity,
only breads and cheese or even salt-fish, and the feeds of the horses and
mules. 8.3 And camping in a village and finding the needs and the food of the
animals, let him rest there his people and the day's food of the animals,
holding watches in higher places. And that leader of the people himself, going
up onto a most high lookout, is to watch and see dusts of the enemy and
smokes, and from these to understand and estimate in what places they are
passing and are about to camp; for by experienced men the places in which it
is fitting that camps be made are also recognized. And when they perceive the
place in which the enemy have bivouacked, they immediately make it known to
the general. 8.4 And he with his own army, wearing breastplates and the
so-called surcoats over the klibania that are not white, each holding in his
hands the weapon he handles, let him make the march. And when the sun has
declined and the enemy going out from the host for plunder are turning back to
their tents and the covering forces that guard them, then let him also enter
with them and draw near the enemy's host. And let him march with aim and exact
attention and secretly, and let him hide himself so as not to be seen by the
enemy. And when night is already coming on, let him come near the camp. 8.5
And if there is a


high hill, on which the enemy have bivouacked, from one side or even from two
near their camp, they will keep the shadowing in that place without toil; for
dismounting from the horses and going up the hill on foot, men two in number
or even three and drawing near the camp, from those high and most strong
hills, which cannot give an easy ascent to the enemy, they are to see the host
and hear the voices of the people and the neighings of the horses and mules.
And they are to send also another four squads of four to stand mounted
themselves on this side and on that of the host, separated from one another,
so that they too may hear the neighings of the horses and mules and the tumult
of the people. 8.6 And if in the place of the camp there is no such most
strong hill, likewise four squads of four are to be sent to stand quietly on
this side and on that of the host, and to give one another signals signifying
withdrawal, if there should be need, either by whistling or by speech and a
call. And they are to be relieved by others, either a second time in the night
or even once, so that both they and their horses may rest. And a tourmarch or
some other head is to go to their relief, and to look where and how they
stand, and, setting those in their place well again, to turn back to where he
stood before. 8.7 And when a disturbance is heard signifying a movement of the
raid, let the four squads of four draw back a little, yet let them not become
far off, and let them report to the tourmarch the setting-out of the enemy.
And let him report to the general by what road they made the onset and in what
hour, so that the general, learning the time and the hour of their
setting-out, may be able to estimate in what villages they will arrive by
morning. 8.8 And those sent by the tourmarch to report to the general, in the
place where the general was bivouacking, the setting-out of the raid, when
they do not find him there but the reception-men left by him, it is necessary
that, coming to that place, they call out; and the reception-men, watching
accurately, when they perceive the voice, are to join them and quickly bring
them to the general or lead them again to other reception-men, if the general
has perhaps moved again. For he must, either once or even twice in the night,
change camp for much security, so as not to be ambushed by the enemy, and he
is to have also double watches, secure, for his guard. 9.τ Concerning the
movement of the raid and the following of it 9.1 When therefore the general
learns the movement of the raid, immediately he is to send also another
officer with chosen horsemen, subordinate to the tourmarch who is following
the raid, toward him, the one sent having with him one of those sent by the
tourmarch to report the movement of the raid to the general, so as to be
guided by him to join easily. 9.2 And the general himself also, behind the
officer who is sent, let him set out with the people under him and hasten to
reach the tourmarch, so that he too behind the tourmarch may follow the raid
of the enemy; for the exact and unfailing following ought to be made from
behind the enemy according to their tracks and their march. 9.3 And the
tourmarch, when the raid of the enemy sets out, taking three pairs,
experienced and fit men, let him come to the road of the enemy; and having
appointed how the first and second and third pair ought to walk and follow the
enemy, let him turn back to his people. And let the first pair walk near the
enemy, hearing also the tumult of the people and the neighings of the horses;
and let the second, the follower of the first, march as far as they can see
the one in front of it and be seen by it, and neither come near nor draw far
so as not to be seen. 9.4 And three


squads of four he will set behind the three pairs, the first squad of four to
see the third pair, and the second squad of four likewise to see the first;
and let the third squad of four, the follower of the second, have six men, so
that two of them may carry to the tourmarch what is reported by a pair, and
the tourmarch to the general. 9.5 For if the enemy walk more quickly, the
first pair ought to report this, so that both the tourmarch and the general
may themselves also walk more quickly and not draw far from the enemy for
long; and if again the enemy walk more slowly, let both the tourmarch and the
general likewise walk more slowly, so that neither coming near they may be
recognized, nor drawing far they may neither know the management of those
following nor be known by them, which brings no ordinary harm. 9.6 And
otherwise also, when the enemy camp in order to feed the horses and the
general is informed of this, let him too camp in a fit place in order to feed
the horses, sending outer watches for his own guard. And when again the
movement of the enemy is reported to him, immediately he too will move
following, sending on this side and on that of himself thirty horsemen each,
not far but nearby, hearing the voices of his people. And behind him let him
have an officer following with horsemen, whom they name the saka. 9.7 And let
the general march at a distance and make every security so as not to be
recognized as following them from behind; but let him walk securely and
command those following near the enemy to watch accurately, lest the enemy
leave some companies in ambush for those following them and for the general
himself, which has often happened from the Tarsites. And when the tourmarch
was following them and not accurately watching the gullies in front and the
places able to hide people, they have fallen unforeseen into their ambush. For
these reasons the one who follows needs much sharpness and exactness, so that
no such thing may happen. 9.8 And before it dawns the general must, since he
has known in what places and villages the enemy are about to ride out, incline
either to their right or to their left, in which a stronger place is seen by
him; and walking more quickly and coming to be on their flank at about two
miles, before the day, as has been said, dawns, so that the enemy, seeing the
dust, may not know the presence of the general, and the general, occupying the
strong place, let him hide his own people. 9.9 And let him with a few horsemen
come nearer the enemy and, going up onto a high lookout, hasten to see the
enemy clearly. And when they set out to go forth and scatter for plunder, let
the general hold out in that place until the third or even the fourth hour of
the day, watching the array of the emir and estimating accurately the
multitude of the people. =9.10= Since those about to ride out have come
somewhat far from the array of the emir, so that it is not possible for them
again to turn back or to know the war kindled against the array of the emir,
each hastening to reach the villages and to procure as much plunder as
possible, having made ready his own arrays as he ought, let him make the
attack against the array of the emir that is short of men, and if with the
help of God he holds the victory, he will work a complete destruction upon the
enemy. =9.11= And if he will not dare to go against the array, seeing a great
and considerable force in it beyond his own, then from the flank and from far
off by a hidden and fit road let him set out with the fitting charge to reach
the scattering of the people of the enemy. And through the whole day he will
strive to run upon them, being scattered, and he will accomplish a
considerable work with the alliance of God. He ought therefore also to have a
useful officer with a few


horsemen behind, watching and observing the array of the emir and reporting to
him the things about it, as he marches. =9.12= And if the general also meets a
covering force that is for the guard of the enemy scattering for plunder,
having a multitude, let him divide his people in two and in front send those
who ought to join battle with the covering force, and when these have clashed,
immediately let the general also with his array, following near, swiftly and
boldly with a cry and a shout set out against them with a charge; and having
routed them with the help of God he will pursue and completely overcome them.
=9.13= If then with the help of God the general is prospered and treads down
the enemy who have run out for plunder, if he works a great and considerable
service, it is likely that the enemies will turn back toward their own country
with shame. And if they still plan to linger in ours, let the general take up
his own people and become distant from the enemy, in fit villages, that is,
and let him rest his people, being exhausted, for three days; only those who
ought to shadow, as we have already set out, are to be sent each day. =9.14=
And the general, knowing them setting out for the return, immediately with
much speed having sent a useful officer, let him gather the infantry army into
the difficult ground of the roads. And the general himself also let him
hasten, not to rest himself even a little either by night or by day, but with
much speed let him come in front of them, and having well prepared all his
army of infantry and cavalry let him dispose the war against them, as we have
set out in detail above. And if he does thus, by the grace of Christ and power
and the intercessions of his all-undefiled mother he will hold the battle
against the enemies. And these things thus. 10.τ Concerning the separation of
the raid and of the people following behind 10.1 And what we have seen we will
in no way shrink from setting out. The host of the enemy variously: both the
cavalry together with the infantry used to go out together from their country
and cities, and having walked together for two days, and sometimes even more,
they used to be separated from one another; and the cavalry, who wished to
overrun and plunder our countries, used to go ahead an interval of some days,
standing off from their infantry people, because they hastened to reach
unannounced the inhabitants of the villages remaining at home, and the baggage
train with the infantry and their baggage following behind, when it had passed
the difficult ground and the narrows, having observed a place fit for their
guard and security, pitching the tents in it they used to bivouac, waiting
there for the horsemen who had run out on raid, so that they might pass those
returning well through the difficult ground. 10.2 The general therefore, being
informed of their going-out, let him send the shadowings and do everything
according to the arrangement above. And if it is easy for the general to reach
those who have run out for plunder, being scattered, and to tread them down,
let him hasten to fulfill this in deed; and if through some faults and the
opposing things that happen an obstacle meets him, let him be prepared for the
war against the host, and let him observe accurately the place in which they
pitched the tents, lest ever there is a river furnishing security to them or a
gully. And he must be prepared also toward the situation of the place, and
with much haste, if possible, gather the infantry army to himself. 10.3 And if
the general is expecting their setting-out and a move into another camp, if
perhaps he learns this through deserters, it is necessary to send horsemen by
night, having as leaders experienced and considerable men, and to prepare two
ambushes on this side and on that of the road by which the passage of the
enemy will take place. And from those sent, having chosen a hundred men,


let him set them in a company, guarding villages that lie near the passage of
the enemy. And the general himself also must stand hidden in a fit place with
all his people and from a lookout watch the crossing of the enemy. 10.4 The
horsemen of the enemy therefore, who search the villages lying near them for a
collection of needs, when they enter the guarded villages and, dismounting
from the horses, search the houses of the villagers, let the hundred horsemen
standing in the company set out against them and as many as they can kill or
take alive they will immediately set out to flight, fleeing the road that
passes between the two ambushes, drawing the enemy to pursuit; and being
pursued they will prepare the enemy to fall into the ambushes. Then therefore
let the ambushes standing on either side come out and make the onset against
them violently; and they will make many of the enemy a work of the sword. And
when the general sees these things, immediately let him too with the greater
part of the people with him make the attack upon the array of the enemy with a
most violent onset, and let him prepare the remainder of his people to come at
the back of the enemy and join battle. And if no obstacle comes from our sins,
they will prevail over the enemy. 10.5 And if the enemy, fighting strongly,
are able to hold out and stand in that very place, unloading the
burden-bearing animals and making a camp, as it were, from much necessity, and
they try to array themselves opposite, they will find much difficulty in this,
but still the general must also bring the war against them in a circle and, if
it is possible also for an infantry people to reach on the same day of the war
with much speed, prepare this to come. And if this is impossible because they
happen to be far, let him appoint that the fitting of the horsemen dismount
from the horses and fight the enemy on foot with the horsemen, using bows and
slings and spears with shields. 10.6 The general must therefore also bring his
baggage and the baggage train, if it happens to be near, and camp nearby the
enemy for their dismay and despair. And if in that very place there is no
water at all, this too will cause them much faintheartedness. And if he does
not completely overcome them, still he will enslave many of these also, and
will put very many to death and make them wounded and cast down their boastful
mind, so that they may not dare without fear to make the assaults against the
Roman villages. 10.7 And if the general does not know beforehand their move
from their camp into another camp either, as I said, through deserters or
through captives, so as to send by night and prepare the ambushes in their
passage, also by day, while the enemy are marching, having prepared openly let
him dispose the war against them, if indeed he does not possess an army
altogether small and easily numbered. For the war against their baggage train
has never quickly caused opposition or harm to our army, it having few
fighting horsemen for its own guard; but as often as war has been joined
against them, the army of the Romans both enslaved and killed many of them,
and they took up many of their pack-animals and mules loaded. 10.8 And if the
infantry army also happens to be present on a day of war, the armed one, that
is, and trained for wars, and he prepares the war against them in a circle in
a general's way, he will give them over to complete destruction. 10.9 For such
things have happened in the baggage train of the enemy, but also by them in
ours, and we have both seen and read in histories and learned from the more
ancient. For these reasons therefore the war against the baggage train is
useful, as bringing no harm or opposition, but rather victory and


glory; for even if the army of the Romans does not find the winning in the war
against them, still it will undergo no harm. =10.10= And if, in the place
where they were bivouacking before, they remained in it, in no way setting out
from there because of the strength of the place, waiting for the raid, the
general will likewise bring the war against them, lest ever, while he delays
because of the gathering of his own infantry people or through some other
pretext, the raid turn back and prevent the war. =10.11= And thus the general
must go against them: to observe through the watchmen who are sent the
villages lying beside and being nearby the camp of the enemy, and there to
hide chosen horsemen in a fit place with a most experienced and brave officer,
and when they enter them searching for foods, let them set out against them.
And to furnish other horsemen into a company for help and defense of those
sent in front; and if the horsemen of the enemy wish to pursue these, let them
fall upon them, and they will rout and pursue, because the horsemen left for
the guard of the enemy's baggage train are always few. =10.12= And if the
enemy do not enter the villages, even if this happens to be impossible, let
the general appoint the watchmen sent by him of experienced men and let them
observe in what part of their camp the enemy lead the camels out to pasture.
And let him separate either a tourmarch or a useful lieutenant with brave
horsemen; and let them look for a gully, if a fit one is found in the place,
and let them be drawn aside hidden in it, until they come near, and then they
will make the attack against the camels openly. =10.13= And let those sent
divide the people into two; and let the half take up the camels being pastured
and the asses, and let the others be for their guard and defense and help. And
if a covering force happens to be standing outside the enemy's host and goes
against those who have attacked the camels, or horsemen also come out from the
host pursuing, those behind will overcome these. =10.14= And immediately the
general also, not being far but near in the ambush, let him go out against
them, and as he sees the war joined, so also let him dispose himself, either
go violently against the enemy or, those of his people who were sent being
more in control in order and not scatteredly, he will make the attack against
the camp of the enemy. And bringing all his people, both horsemen and
infantry, since he knows thoroughly the situation of the place, he will divide
the stations among all the orders of both horsemen and infantry, and let him
dispose the war against them, if it is possible, in a circle. =10.15= And if
there is a river and a gully, as we have said above, guarding the enemy
instead of a palisade, if it admits a crossing in any way whatever, from there
too he will station people. And he will prepare also to pitch their tents, in
order to show that he is about to camp there for their frightening; and thus
to make the war. And if he disposes the things of the war well and in good
order and bravely, he will be greatly prospered. =10.16= And if on the first
day he will not be able to tread them down, they standing strongly against him
and having an infantry force and being helped from the situation of the place,
it is necessary both to shadow them and to call more infantry. And in the
night he will prepare the light-armed and the slingers to strike against them,
and to light very many fires around them, and to exhort the light-armed
bravely and boldly to fight from the hand and to run risks in front, until
they enter inside the camp of the enemy, and take up horses and mules and some
goods by night, striking continually and killing the enemy. =10.17= And if
such a thing altogether happens toward one part with the help of God and is
seen and known by the other orders, they too will set out


despising death into the seizing of the spoils because of the hope of gain;
and they will prevail over these by the grace of Christ easily. And if he will
not completely tread them down through some faults perhaps and failures, still
he will both master many of these and kill them, and his people will take very
many spoils. =10.18= And while the general is thus carrying out the things of
the wars of the host, he must also send a useful and experienced officer with
forty horsemen at a distance to guard the road by which the raid of the enemy
is about to turn back. And when he sees them coming toward their host and in
front of them, as is the custom with the Tarsites, the so-called beredon among
them, coming nearby to report to the host the presence of their raid,
immediately let the officer sent to the watch report to the general. And if he
sees the beredon coming far from the array of the raid, let him send a useful
officer with chosen horsemen to attack them, if possible unexpectedly, and he
will master them easily; and if it walks near and nearby the array of the raid
in front of them, let him not send against them, but rather let him be content
with what God has helped him in the war against the host; and let him turn
back with all the people, taking hold of the fit and stronger places. =10.19=
And since the host of the enemy has for this reason passed all the difficult
ground, waiting for the presence of the raid, so that what has been seized by
the raid, whether they be captives or cattle, it may save well with them as it
passes the difficult ground, the general must strive to come in front of them
and occupy the narrows with all the people of infantry and cavalry; and he
will furnish the war against them more carefully, as has been shown above.
=10.20= And never when they are turning back let them be left without a fight;
for if he disposes the things of the war well in the difficult ground, he will
tread them down greatly. And if through not having a very great and
considerable infantry force he is unable to tread them down greatly, still at
least those whom they have taken captive, even if not all, they will recover,
and all their things, and they will both wound not a few of the enemy and make
them prisoners. 11.τ Concerning posting the infantry on this side and on that
in the difficult ground 11.1 The general must also otherwise strive to rout
the enemy, even if the people about him is small. Let him observe a fit and
most strong place, if a fortress happens to be near also, so that the
situation of the strong place admits of being held with infantry, and on this
side and on that of the road hide the infantry army in companies. And behind
the infantry let the general stand nearby with the cavalry army, very near the
infantry. And let him have also the cavalry ambush and infantry near him, and
let him stand almost joined according to the hinder part. 11.2 And let there
be sent also into the ambush by the general chosen men having hardy horses, as
far as a hundred, having a brave and most experienced leader and knowing a fit
village, in which he will hide his own people. And by night let him occupy
such a place and hide them well. 11.3 And when it dawns, going up onto a
lookout, let him see the crossing of the enemy as they walk. And seeing those
running out for plunder and searching the villages in order to gather spoils
and money and whatever else they can find, let him hold out until he sees them
dismounting from the horses and searching the horses of the villages. Then of
the horsemen with him, either seventy or sixty, the home people being more
than the enemy (for those of the general ought to be more than the enemy in
number), let him appoint these with a brave onset and with a most swift onset
of the horses to attack the opponents, and of God


with the help he will be prospered. 11.4 And if it happens that these are
pursued by the enemy, it is necessary to meet them with the forty left to him,
and either to kill the captives who have been seized or to send them in front,
to go more quickly and occupy the stronghold. And he, fighting with experience
and standing against the enemy and sometimes turning back against them,
sometimes fleeing, will provoke them to pursuit. 11.5 And if they pursue as
far as the places in which the infantry are hidden, and some of the enemy pass
these, then the infantry coming out of the ambushes will stand against the
pursuing enemy and will push them back. And the enemy who have passed the
infantry will fall into the ambush of the horsemen and will perish all
together utterly; for the infantry, being helped by the place, will not allow
the pursuers to pass through, but they will leave the pursuit and will turn
back altogether without result, having lost those of their own already seized
beforehand. 12.τ Concerning a sudden going-out of the enemy before the Roman
armies have been gathered 12.1 And if a sudden and swift going-out of the
enemy takes place, such as has often been accustomed to happen from them, the
imperial armies not yet having been gathered, but only the general, and he
himself not having been able, because of the swiftness of their going-out, to
gather the whole people of his own theme, but he has a small and easily
numbered one, and he sees the enemy hastening and pressing toward plunder, and
he knows that the country in which they make the onset has not yet been sent
into the strongholds and fled into the fortresses or into the strongholds, he
must send with much haste either the tourmarch of such a country or other
officers and come in front of the enemy and, as they are able, both send the
inhabitants of the villages and their cattle into flight and into the
strongholds. 12.2 And the general, seeing the enemy already about to attack
the villages toward the dawning of the day, following behind them as we have
said above, must on that same night show himself to the enemy, preparing as if
for war, so that from this he may be able to check their onset and keep the
country unharmed. 12.3 And if there is a river hard to cross on the road, and
the enemy are about to cross it, or a defile or difficult ground on the road,
when the greater part have crossed the river or have been received into the
defile, send against the people behind them, and let that general himself come
with chosen officers and well-mounted men and show himself to the enemy and
prepare as if for battle against them to join war, separating from himself the
remounts and those carrying horse-feeds and sending them into strongholds or a
fortress, if one happens to be near. 12.4 And if there is no river nor
difficult ground on the road, likewise he must take a little extra counsel and
set out to battle, until he is pursued by them in the night, so that the
enemy, recognizing clearly that the general is following, may be contracted
and in no way dare to ride out and make the attack against the villages. And
when this has happened he will deliver them and free them from the raid about
to take place in the villages and from captivity. 12.5 And with much exactness
and consideration let him make the showing and the putting-forth against them
through few and chosen horsemen, as we said, and immediately let them turn
back fleeing toward the general and the stronghold, and let them not charge
against the enemies beyond measure and beyond what is needed; for if a people
perhaps beyond their force is loaded upon them they will become a consumption
of a hostile hand too quickly, and especially if the night happens to be a
full moon. 12.6 And if it is without light and gloomy, they will work such a
deed unharmed and will appear to have succeeded in one of two things
altogether: either having prepared the enemy not to ride out for plunder at
all, or, if they do run out at all, until the day dawns


completely they will in no way dare to ride out, and then only some few; for
the greater part, suspecting the war from the general, will not stand off from
the array of the emir. And if the general does thus, let the inhabitants of
the villages escape into the strongholds and the fortresses, and let them be
kept unharmed. 13.τ Concerning laying an ambush against the so-called
mensuratores in the camps 13.1 And when the enemy are wasting our countries in
host-fashion and are not sending raids from far off but securing themselves,
he will be eager to wound them through another device. You must observe and
estimate from what place of the enemy there is a camp, and in what place on
the coming day he is about to camp. 13.2 And if the interval of the camp is
most long, about sixteen miles and beyond, so that the length of the road is
able to make both them and their horses exhausted, and near the place in which
they are about to halt, having observed and found a fit company-place,
choosing fighting and chosen horsemen, three hundred or even fewer than these,
you will hide them in it. And you will set also another ambush with all your
people in a fit place having also a stronghold for its security. 13.3 And if a
fortress also happens to be near, it too is for your more abundant salvation
and benefit. And if need of the infantry arises, let them too come out of the
fortress fighting with you and helping you. 13.4 And those of the people of
the enemy who run ahead to occupy the place in order to make ready their
camps, as was their custom, whom it has been the custom for Romans to call
mensuratores, and while they are busy in them, let those set beforehand by you
in the first company near the camp come out against them swiftly with a great
charge, and they will master these with the help of God. And if the enemies
pursue these as far as the place in which you have been able to set an ambush,
coming out against them with a manly and noble onset you will prevail over the
pursuers and will raise a trophy worthy of memory. 13.5 And if it happens that
forces of the enemy arrive fighting against you, having the help of the
stronghold and of the infantry, thus you will put them to shame and will
prepare them, having lost many of their own, to turn back without result. 14.τ
Concerning the withdrawal of the cavalry from the infantry when they are
marching together 14.1 You must therefore also look at this accurately, O
general, and, when the enemy are striving to escape your notice and to send
raids and to plunder our countries, keep the mind sleepless, lest ever their
device and craft escape you; for what I will say they themselves have also
worked in other cases. 14.2 For while they, both the cavalry and infantry with
the baggage, are marching together, as they were doing on the days behind and
that have passed, those wishing to ride out on raids, when the sun comes
toward the west, their baggage and all the service with this infantry army and
the fighting horsemen left for their guard, having pitched the tents, camp as
is their custom; and those made ready for a raid and wishing to overrun our
countries, so that their dust may not appear, set out toward raid about late
afternoon. 14.3 And for the one who ought to shadow the host of the enemy,
whether he is a tourmarch or another officer, coming with his people it is
necessary to draw near the place in which they bivouac. Since, when night is
coming on, they ought then according to custom to enter and draw near their
tents after the first hour or even the second of the night, and if they will
not find in the camp the horsemen of the enemy, because they have already gone
on raid, it is not possible otherwise for these officers entrusted with the
shadowing to know this accurately and to report the truth to the general,
except thus as I will say. 14.4 It is necessary therefore that those sent each
day to shadow, from those who have shadowed the days behind and that have
passed


the host and standing in the squads of four, as we have said above, two of
them, most experienced and brave of those going to the shadowing, take from
the general, so that they may be well taught by them what those also did and
saw before. And mixing the two themselves with one of the four squads of four
let the tourmarch send them, according to the type of shadowing set out
before, to draw near the host so as to hear their voices and the neighings of
the horses. The two men therefore who shadowed the host before in other camps,
when also all their people were together and camped together, will be able to
estimate from the tumult and disturbance of the people and the neighing of the
horses, and from the space in which they pitched the tents (for the space will
not be found having the third part of what they occupied in the camps behind,
when all camped together), and from this it is possible to estimate and
understand that the people of the enemy is lacking in that space and is not
with them in the camp. And how is it not possible to estimate this, when six
thousand men, more or less, and horses as far as twelve are lacking from the
camp, all of whom together those who shadowed before used to see and estimate.
14.5 Because of the doubtful, then, so that those shadowing may take a
complete grasp of the truth about this, the tourmarch sent to the shadowing
must also do this: choosing eight horsemen, men of the most experienced and
practiced in the roads to the height, to appoint them either on the right or
on the left from behind, where the tourmarch stands, to come in front of the
enemy standing off from the tents half a mile, and having come in front to
search accurately and to examine the roads. And if the horsemen of the enemy
have passed and were as far as two hundred, those sent will know their
crossing, not to mention horses as far as twelve thousand, more or less. For
the tracks of so many horses as having passed freshly those sent will know
clearly, and turning back with much speed to the tourmarch let them report
about this and by what road they made the onset. And let the tourmarch
immediately with much haste report to the general about this and by what road
they went. 14.6 The general must therefore take counsel about these things
with the generals with him and the rest of the experienced. And if he sees his
own army having force to array itself against the host of the enemy, let him
be prepared for the battle against them, if indeed his army of infantry is
also near, able to be joined with him on the same day. If therefore the space
in which the enemy bivouac has a stronghold for their security or a gully hard
to cross, one must not be fitted for the war against them; and if it is level
from every side, and especially if the infantry also are able to come down on
the day of the attack of the war, let him be prepared for this. 14.7 And if
the space has strength, as we said, and the infantry army is lacking, it is
necessary to set out with much speed toward reaching those of the enemy who
are raiding, and to send a useful officer to take hold of the tracks of the
enemy who have set out toward the running-out of the raid; and he himself with
all the people, walking on the flank, let him hasten, walking more quickly, to
come near, as he knows thoroughly the roads in which the enemy walk; and let
him walk with security. And when the dawning of the day draws near, let him
send watchmen for a spying of the enemy; and going up onto high hills let them
strive to see them. 14.8 And the general, when day has come, let him hide his
own people, so that the enemy, seeing his dust, may not rather be ambushed by
them. And being informed, either


from those following the raid or from those sent to observation and a watch,
in what place the array of the enemy is that has their leader, taking some few
with him and going up onto a high lookout let the general hasten to see the
array of the enemy with his own eyes. 14.9 And having seen this, since also
the multitude of their people has been scattered for plunder, let him estimate
with those with him the multitude of the array of the enemy and the strength
of his own people; and if his own army is more than the enemy, let him set out
against them, as we have already appointed above. And if God grants him help
and he is able to wound their array and pursue, he will accomplish a great
work and one worthy of memory. =14.10= And if there is a multitude of people
in the array of the enemies, it is necessary for the general to be eager to
come in front, in the villages in which the enemy have made the raids, having
come from behind them; and meeting these scattered and slackened by the
charge, both they and their horses, he will easily overcome them, searching
them through the whole day in the villages; and those he will set in the
account of captives and will use them as he wishes against them, and he will
free the farmers from captivity and whatever of theirs they happened to take.
=14.11= And if he also meets a covering force following for the guard of those
riding out, let him be prepared for the war against the covering force, as we
set out before, and with the help of God he will hold the battle against them.
=14.12= And if contrary meetings have met those sent on that same night to
shadow the enemy, because they have not yet occupied beforehand and come to
the places in which they were able to see the host and hear their voices, and
their raid has gone out beforehand, as we said, and, because of their taking
an exact grasp of the truth about this, from such a meeting it has happened
that the sure report of the matter sent to the general was late and lingered,
and the mandate sent to the general, if the general is informed about this
toward the ninth hour of the night and beyond and sees the narrowness of the
time, since also the raid of the enemy made the movement on the day that has
passed while the day was still prevailing and through the whole night was
about to become far off, and it is not possible to reach them scattered, he
must be made ready for the war against the host. =14.13= And if they wish to
spend two days in the place in which they bivouac, let him be prepared for the
battle against them, as we have set out above that the war against the host is
to take place. But they would not spend two days in that space, because they
are pressed to be joined more quickly with their cavalry forces that have run
out for plunder; and the general, meeting them as they march, let him set out
eagerly into the war against them, as we have also said before concerning the
host on the march separated from the raid. And if the general does everything
carefully according to the former arrangement, having the help of God and of
his holy and all-undefiled mother and Theotokos, he will accomplish a great
and considerable service. 15.τ Concerning security 15.1 You must therefore,
general, have all security and guard so as not to undergo a surprise and have
the enemy unexpectedly work the assault against you. And you will have the
guard and security, if each day those who ought to shadow the host of the
enemy, in the place where they bivouac, are sent by you; for those setting it
as a matter of haste and applying every device to meet you unexpectedly in
order to overcome you, to the harm and destruction of the people named from
Christ and the dishonor of the most mighty Romans, and to the lifting-up and
boasting of the arrogant sons of Hagar and deniers of Christ our God. For it
happens that there are seized


among them captives of ours, or also that they become deserters and from them
they learn in what place you bivouac. 15.2 So that they may not by night take
up their cavalry forces and ride out to reach you and fall upon you
unexpectedly, for these reasons those shadowing must, when they perceive the
disturbance of the people of the enemy toward movement, immediately with much
speed report that the people has been disturbed toward a movement, and again
when they set out by whatever road they make the march, report again both that
a multitude of people has set out and that they are going this road, whether
all with the infantry, or the horsemen alone. 15.3 And let the general not
trust only in the mandates coming to him from those shadowing the enemy, but
let him have also double watches, outer watches and inner watches, and at
times even triple; and by night to guard the roads and in the places in which
a crossing of enemy is suspected; and by day let the watchmen not only guard
the roads, but also go up onto most high places so as to be able easily to see
not only smokes and dusts, but also peoples of the enemy, if they are coming.
And we said that the watches are to be more, because, if ever it happens that
the first is taken by the enemy, the one after that, seeing the enemy, reports
to the general the coming of the enemies. 15.4 And twice in the night the
general must change camp, and by day likewise. And as he changes camp let him
take the watchmen with him, and let him leave in the place where he was
bivouacking six men or eight, the so-called reception-men, with also some
useful officer, so that those sent by those shadowing the enemies, bringing
mandates, coming, may be brought through them to the general. And in whatever
place the general camps again, let him send the outer watches. And let there
be four men in each post, so that while two sleep by night the two may keep
watch. 16.τ Concerning the separation of the baggage train 16.1 You must
therefore, general, also guard this, and, when you are about to draw near the
enemy, separate the baggage train from you and send it far off in a strong
place or in a fortress, setting some useful and experienced man to care for
it, and give a few horsemen of fighting men to the officer of the baggage
train, so that through them the watches may be held for the guard of the
baggage train. And when again you come to need of it, report to the officer
entrusted with its care to come to the place that was appointed for him to be
joined with you. When the baggage train is being separated from you, you must
take up provision of the people and feeds of the horses of two days or three,
carried on swift mules or also horses in the pack-saddles. 16.2 And when you
are about to follow a raid of the enemy by night, let all the fighting army be
in breastplates, each having in his hands the weapon fit for fighting. But
have also the so-called saka following behind you. And if the march of the
enemy takes place through hard-to-cross places, it is necessary that each
theme separately, or also a tagma if they are present, march; the general in
front, following the tourmarch who is following the enemy at a great distance,
then thus also the rest, whether they be themes or tagmata, each following the
other, so as also to march well by night and without cry and tumults. 16.3 And
command the tourmarch who is following the raid and secure that he have
exceeding exactness and wakefulness, lest ever the enemy, perceiving that they
are being followed by you, separate their chosen fighting men, the greater
part of the people under him, and hide them in companies and unexpectedly
attack you, since we have seen such an ambush made by the Tarsites in former
times; for when the general then was watching dusts of those riding out for
plunder, not in truth, but in the shape of a raid, few and these more cheap,
and was pressing to go against them, and the tourmarch


following the raid having been careless and not having searched the companies
well, he has fallen unforeseen into the ambush. And so that no such thing may
happen, you must, O general, make much security and search the hollow and
hidden places through horsemen having swift horses and an exact knowledge of
the places. 16.4 And about the dawning divide the army in two, if it is in a
multitude and is numbered into three thousands, and send the remounts and
those carrying the feeds of the horses far off in a strong place, and the
arrays one must prepare thus because of the unexpected ambushes of the enemy.
And let the first have a leader of the more considerable generals and the
third part of the people under you, and let it march behind the tourmarch, and
you with the great array follow the first, having also the saka behind you
following with a few horsemen, so that, if it happens that the enemy ambush
you and set the greater part of their fighting men in companies and go against
the tourmarch following them, the first array walking in front of you may
receive him, helping; then, war having clashed with it and the enemy being
scattered in the battle and loosing the order, finding them scattered you will
prevail over them. 16.5 And if the enemy have not been prepared for such an
ambush, but are pressing to run out and plunder the countries, one must before
it dawns hide the orders of the army under you in fit places, so as not to be
detected by the enemy, and hold out in such ambushes until the third hour or
even the fourth of the day, until the enemy ride out for plunder; and when
these become far from the array of the emir, it being left with few men and
not having a multitude of people, go against it. 16.6 And send three arrays in
front, equal in front, to attack war; and you with another three or even four
arrays following the first, follow near; and when the first arrays begin the
fight from the hands, as you see your own fighting, so also give help. 16.7
And first send the arrays following you on this side and on that to be joined
with the enemy and from the edges, if possible, and from the flank to attack
and fight from the hand; then you yourself also, if there is need, join, if
indeed the people under you has not become more in control. But when the array
of the emir is left with a small people, as we said, it will not be able to
hold out against the army numbered under you around three thousands. 16.8 But
these things, as experience teaches, we set out; and you must be disposed
toward the pressing need and circumstance of the time. For it is not the
tradition that gives, but as the help of God strengthens, the outcome of the
war follows. And if you are with only your own theme, O general, and the army
under you is small, likewise you must make the following of the raid of the
enemy far off with security, so as not to be known by them, and make the
attack against only those riding out into the villages and being scattered, as
has been shown above. 17.τ Concerning enemies who overrun our lands with a
great force and the preparation with ambushes 17.1 And if the whole gathering
of the enemy, both of cavalry and of infantry, having gone out with a great
and heavy force, overruns and plunders our countries and plans to go deep and
search them, and if the Roman army happens at the same time to be gathered,
and the enemy hear of their presence, they make much security of their own
people, not allowing any to be scattered in the villages without a guard, and
they rather devise to work ambushes against our people, and to reach


them unexpectedly they hasten both to pursue and to overcome, you yourself
also ought to show all security so as not to be found unexpectedly by them,
and rather to counter-devise so as to rout them and work something noble and
considerable. And when they are wounded by you, they will in no way linger
plundering our countries. 17.2 It is needful therefore for you to observe fit
villages in which those of the enemy who run out enter for the sake of
searching foods and spoils, and to set in them a cavalry company that is about
to attack them. And let the horsemen sent into the company be beyond two
hundred, having a brave leader and most experienced altogether in such
ambushes. And the leader of the whole army, having six or five thousand
fighting horsemen and the help of God, will not come to need of more. 17.3
Dividing these therefore in two, let him set the two thousand in front in a
fit company, in which there is also a high lookout having a view, so as to see
from far off those of his people being pursued and the pursuers. And behind
the two thousand set the three thousand and an infantry army with them in a
hidden place into an ambush, the place having strength, that is. 17.4 And if a
fortress happens to be near, he will have that too for help; and let the
general not look, nor have this in mind, if multitudes of enemy attack him and
a violent war takes place, to come inside the fortress in order to save his
own people; for this brings not only dishonor and contempt, but also harm and
destruction of the country and utter ruin. But outside in the strongholds near
the fortress let him fight strongly against them with the infantry army,
having fallen into necessity, inasmuch as he has much help from the place and
the infantry people of the fortress for his alliance. 17.5 The leader
therefore of the three hundred sent for the guard of the villages, standing on
a lookout and seeing the enemy entering the villages, when they dismount from
the horses and search the houses of the villagers, having separated a hundred
and a little more from the people of the three hundred let him send them,
having appointed them to attack the enemy swiftly. And let those sent be more
than the enemy entering the villages. And having attacked them thus, by the
grace of Christ let them both kill many and take captives. 17.6 And the
remaining of our people, as many of the enemy as are able to mount their own
horses and use flight, will pursue these carefully, until they are pursued by
the covering forces of the enemy standing for the guard of their companions.
And the officer of the three hundred, seeing the pursuers not being in a
multitude, but pursuing without order and without control, let him hold out in
the ambush, until those of his people being pursued arrive and pass through.
Then thus let him too come out violently from the ambush against the enemy
with those with him and set out, and he will be prospered by the power and
grace of Christ; for he will take prisoners, and he will both kill and wound
many. 17.7 When therefore the enemy are still adding and increasing into a
multitude and pursuing violently, let him send the captives who have been
seized, both their horses and weapons, in front to arrive in the place where
the general stands. And let him flee in good order with those with him,
provoking the enemy to pursuit and drawing them with experience. And let no
one of his people know in what places the ambushes of the general stand,
except only their officer. 17.8 And when the enemy are attacking him with a
multitude of people and pursuing without control, let those who are brave and
most noble and have strong horses make turnings and strike the pursuers, so
that they may both save the wounded of their own and prepare those whose
horses are failing or also being struck to mount


others; then thus giving the reins of the horses with a charge let them draw a
little far from the pursuers, so that they may be lightened a little from the
weight of the enemy lying upon them and those, driving the horses more quickly
toward pursuit, may make them failing and faint. And when those of our people
being pursued draw near the ambush, let them pass through either on the right
or on the left, so that when the ambush comes out they may not meet them in
the same place and harm one another; and after these things, those being
pursued turning back with those who are in the ambush, and their onset against
the enemy taking place from two sides, this too will be for the benefit of our
army. 17.9 And let the place of the ambush be fit so as to hide them well; and
let it in no way have a defile or difficult ground at the going-out, but let
it be level and roomy, hindering nothing their onset against the enemy and
swift attack, and especially when they are around two thousand; for upon such
a number the going-out of such a space must be broad and straight. And let the
lookout also, on which the leader of the people in the ambush stands watching,
be fit so as to see from a great interval the pursuing enemy and to estimate
and understand the number of their people and how they make the pursuit.
=17.10= And when the pursuers have drawn near the ambush, having called upon
the help of God, let them set out against them with a swift and most violent
and brave onset with a cry and a shout. And let those being pursued also turn
back, and, their onset against the enemy having taken place from two sides,
this too will be for the benefit of our army. And if all things are done
correspondingly according to the present appointment, having before all also
the help of God taking hold with them, what a overthrow and destruction of the
enemy will then take place, those who have experience of such things know. And
how then, the horses having failed by the long and intense pursuit and the men
having given out by the much toil of the battle, will not the last of evils
happen to these? =17.11= And if, which is altogether unhoped, the enemy are
able to stand, all their force having come in such a war, and a stubborn
battle takes place, they fighting with the whole army in order to recover
their defeat, and this happens according to a second account, the leader of
the army must from some signal and appointment and a recalling trumpet
separate his own from the fight from the hands, they giving their backs, yet
not with a charge and disorderly flight, but in good order, and provoke the
enemy to pursuit. And when the arrays of the enemy are lying upon them and
pursuing, they will fall into the greater ambush. And those being pursued,
passing through in one part of the ambush, as we said, when those who are in
the great ambush set out against them, let those being pursued also turn back
immediately, and they will be found fighting the enemy from two sides; and
this will cause much benefit to the army. =17.12= If then the pursuers arrive
as far as the ambush and fall into it unforeseen and with a daring and brave
onset they will charge against them from the hiding, by the grace of Christ
they will defeat these by force and will procure great glory for themselves.
And if through some faults they are not able completely to prevail over the
arrays of the enemies, as having a heavy force and fighting strongly with
their whole army, having also the infantry army fighting with you, you rather
will prevail over them. And if they are not defeated by force, still you will
make many of them a work of the sword, and more than these you may seize in
the account of captives, and they will be cast under great fear and dismay, so
as no longer to dare without fear to overrun and plunder our countries; rather
they will hasten to reach their own. =17.13= And these things, as those before
us have handed down to us and the from the


long time experience, we have written. And it belongs to you to come to be
toward the circumstance that is likely coming; for not as the counsels of men,
but as the providence of God from above will weigh the things in each case and
the end of the war follows. 18.τ Concerning when the general ought to make a
skirmish from two sides of the enemy 18.1 And if the enemy still remain, which
is unhoped after having been thus wounded, going about our countries in
host-fashion, both burning and destroying, in no way daring to send their
people on raid far off, the general must send a considerable force to the
other side of the enemy, in which he himself is not running beside; and to
attack and wound those who run out from three or four miles of the host on
this side and on that, so as to prevent them from taking up foods, so that
from this, having scarcity of foods, they may be forced to turn back. 18.2 And
if you see, O general, the enemy still guarding themselves and not allowing
those sent for a collection of needs to ride out far, this ambush too must be
devised for their harm; to observe the covering forces of the enemy, which go
out for their guard and stand far from the host, and whichever of them turns
back more slowly to their tents, perhaps holding out until their companions
searching the villages near them turn back to their tents, hasten to ambush
it. 18.3 And through experienced watchmen, if possible, estimate the number of
the enemy standing in the covering force, and choosing fighting men of the
horsemen more than these you will prepare a company to come to be for the
enemies, setting over them a brave and most experienced leader. And appoint
him that some few of the people under him put on tunics of farmers; and mix
with them in truth also some of the country people and farming people. And let
all be unarmed and the heads uncovered, and some also unshod, but all mounted,
holding very short wooden javelins, so as to deceive the enemy and produce in
them the opinion that they are not of the army, but some farmers, the
so-called house-guards. And let these not be more than twenty. And bring them
into villages, six each or even more, near one another, having some cattle in
the same villages, whether they be pack-animals and oxen, or horses and mules.
18.4 And about the eleventh hour of the day appoint them to show themselves to
the enemy, entering from village to another village at a run and being
distracted, so that they may take up their cattle and save them toward the
strongholds. These then, when the enemy standing in the covering force see
them, some dragging their cattle, others driving them, and they suppose these
to be country people and farmers, they will pursue them without control and
without guard. And those being pursued who have put on the shape of
house-guards and farmers will be eager to arrive where the ambush stands, and
the enemy pursuing these will fall into the ambush unguardedly. And when they
draw near those in ambush, let such men charge against them with an orderly
mind and onset and clash; and it is clear that the enemy will not stand even
for a little, but giving their backs many of these will both be killed and
become prisoners. 18.5 And if the space in which such things are accomplished
should be nearby the army of the enemies, and a multitude of the people of the
enemy comes out for the defense of those being pursued, the general must also
behind such an ambush set another people, a place into a fit one, into an
ambush, distant from the former ambush two miles. And if, as we said, a people
of the enemy pursues those in the first ambush, those coming out of the second
hiding against them will both save their own and will wound and put to death
the enemy. 18.6 One must therefore work such a device, O general, about the
settings of the sun;


so that, if multitudes of the enemy attack those in the ambush, night coming
on and dissolving the war, you may keep the people under you unharmed. 19.τ
Concerning the condition and arming and training of an army 19.1 And if they
dare to separate a raid, you must handle these skillfully, sending someone of
those under you adorned with bravery and experience with an armed people
sufficient to overrun those of the enemy who are scattered and to kill them
and make them captives. And let your array never be dissolved, so that, if it
happens that you meet covering forces of enemy having a multitude of people,
following for the guard, that is, of the enemy riding out, having your own
array standing you may join war with the covering forces in a general's way
and with experience, as we have set out, overcome these and give them over to
complete destruction. 19.2 And these things we have recalled to be kept for
your security, O general, and of the people, if the army under you is small
and easily numbered and much less than that of the enemy. And if the fighting
force under you is numbered around six or five thousands, rather hasten to
array yourself against the enemy face to face. And thus through mechanical
devices of generalship and practices, if there is need also of unexpected
attacks against them, and if you devise the war against them well and with
experience, having the unconquerable help of Christ our God fighting with you,
you will work great trophies of victory against the enemies. 19.3 And it is
not possible otherwise for you to be prepared for war in a general's way and
with experience, unless first you train and educate the army under you,
accustoming it and exercising it both in the handling of the weapons, and to
endure the sharpness and toil of labors and distresses; and not to commit
their whole to slackness and laziness and drunkenness and luxuries and the
other prodigalities. 19.4 It belongs indeed that they receive their pays and
grain-allowances without shortfall, and other gifts and benefactions more than
the customary and appointed, so that, having what is not lacking, from these
they may be able also to acquire very fine horses and their other panoplies,
and with a rejoicing soul and eager and exulting heart they may choose to run
risks in front for our holy emperors and for the whole Christian fullness.
19.5 And the better of all and more necessary, which wakes their eagerness and
increases the bravery and provokes them to dare the undared, is to have
complete freedom for their own houses and for the soldiers serving them and
for all those about them. For this from the beginning and from of old was both
kept toward them and guarded; and you will find this legislated by the ancient
holy and blessed emperors and written in their tactical books. 19.6 And
besides the freedom they must also enjoy the due honor and not be despised and
dishonored; for I am ashamed to say that such men are even struck, who do not
prefer their own life above the service of the holy emperors and the freedom
and defense of the Christians; and these things by little tax-men, and such as
cause no benefit to the commonwealth, but only squeeze and wear out the poor
and from injustice and many bloods of poor gather to themselves many talents
of gold. 19.7 And they must not be dishonored even by the judges of the themes
and be dragged as captives and whipped, and bonds and collars (O the
misfortune!) be put upon the defenders and, after God, saviors of Christians
and those who die each day, so to speak, for the holy emperors, the law itself
commanding that each officer have authority over his own people and judge
these. 19.8 And of whom else, then, is the household people of the theme, if
not of the general alone, whom the holy emperor appoints in it? For this
reason also from the most ancient Romans and from the law the of his own


theme the general possessed the authority, judging those who bring charges
upon military matters and administering the things arising in the theme,
having the judge as a helper and being helped by him, and helping also the
protonotary and the others entrusted with the services of the public. And the
tourmarch also had from the law and the imperial command the judgments of his
own tourma upon authority according to the types that have prevailed and their
privileges. 19.9 If then the army of the holy emperors takes the ancient
condition, and the things that grieve them and lead them into poverty become
far from them, they will take great eagerness and joy and exultation, and they
will be more noble and more daring, and they will appear unconquerable and
unfightable to the enemy; and if these things happen thus, our holy emperors
will not only defend their own countries, but will also subdue very many
others of the enemy. 20.τ Concerning enemies who linger against our land, so
that our army may go against their land 20.1 When the enemy are going about
our countries with a great and heavy force, both wearing them out and
destroying them and planning to go deep into sieges of fortresses, and
guarding themselves so as not to be ambushed by the Roman armies, but rather
devising to ambush them, if there is not a considerable Roman force for an
opposing array against these, able to overcome them, this you must do, O
general, which has also happened in former times, as the strategic book
composed by the celebrated and most wise emperor Leo sets out; and those who
have met it know clearly the things about what will be said. 20.2 For at that
time the whole people of the Cilicians, having gone out with a heavy force
against the theme of the Anatolikoi and besieging the fortress of Mistheia
violently, the emperor then commanding ordered the leader of the army with the
themes and tagmata to campaign against the country of the Cilicians, and two
of the generals, of the Anatolikon and of the Opsikion, to be left for the
defense, as they were able, both of the fortress and of the rest of the
country, to stand against the enemy. 20.3 The leader of the armies (and it was
then Nikephoros, whose surname is Phokas), having made the attack by the road
called of Maurianos against the country of Adana, drove as much plunder as
possible. The people of Adana came out to a meeting with him and, having
arrayed themselves about two miles from their city, at the same time as they
fell upon one another the men of Ishmael, turning their backs, set out in
disorderly flight and scatteredly straight toward the city. And the leader of
the Roman armies, as many of the Ishmaelites as he found not able to come
inside the gates, some he cut down with the sword, and some he led into
slavery; and camping near the town, having cut down all the fruit-bearing of
vineyards and trees, he dimmed the brilliance and good order around the city.
20.4 And on the next day his people, having run as far as the sea, taking up
as much captivity as possible and very many cattle and having marched through
the whole day, camped at the river Cydnus, which is also called Hierax
locally, outside the bridge on which the road leads toward Adana. And on the
coming day, setting out from there, he was going to reach his own. 20.5 The
enemy sitting around Mistheia therefore, having heard of the attack of the
Romans against their own country, having loosed the siege turned back with
much speed toward the defense of their own country; but having accomplished
nothing they missed both; for the leader of the Roman armies with many spoils
and plunder and captives reached the haunts of the Romans by the road called
of Karydion. 20.6 In addition also those who generaled the Anatolikoi and
Cappadocia in former times, when the Tarsites were making the going-out
against the Roman country, by another road


entering into their countries, sometimes those themselves, and sometimes

the one entrusted with the leadership of Lykandos and of the frontier themes

And you yourself therefore, O general, when you are at a loss to wound the

guard themselves, and again you do not have a considerable force so as to

infantry force against the country of the enemy and linger in them and burn
and destroy and besiege fortresses; so that the enemy, hearing this, even if
their leader does not wish, will force him to turn back toward the defense of
his own country. 20.9 And whenever you have well sent our countries into the
strongholds and fled both the people dwelling in them and their flocks into
high and hard-to-cross mountains, and the enemy wish to search and examine
such mountains and from there to enslave these, and they wish to draw near
such places with the whole army and to pitch the tents there, you yourself
also ought to come near in those parts for the defense of your own. =20.10=
And if you see the space in which the enemy bivouac having a mountain or a
high place fit for attacking them by night, you will study this, and, having
observed well the situation of the place, and having help from there, you will
prepare the war against them by night with an infantry force together with
horsemen, as has been said below concerning night fighting, and you will wound
them greatly and frighten them and prepare them to withdraw from there.

=20.11= guarding, and wishing to search the mountains in which the refuges of
the villagers happen to be, the entries must be guarded and occupied

guarded in the places in which the refuge of the farmers with their families
and their children has taken place, if they wish to enter through those narrow
and hard-to-cross roads, they will be defeated, and especially if an infantry
force attacks them. And if they themselves wish to go with infantry and search
the mountains, hasten to bring the inhabitants of the villages into more
inward and stronger and unassailable places, and you with all the people
occupying the roads that lead in against them, that is, and guarding them
securely. 21.τ Concerning the siege of a fortress 21.1 And perceiving the
enemy being made ready for a siege of a fortress, you too, general, must, as
many as can be besieged (for there are also many fortresses not fearing a
siege), in such ones before the going-out of the enemies prepare the people
fleeing into them to lay up foods of four months for each one, if possible
even of more, according as you foresee the siege will be, and to care for the
waters in the cisterns and for the

providence of God. 21.3 And if because of the difficulty and hardness of the

collection of the needs. And you, disposing with experience and in a general's
way, will be able to wound these with ambushes and tread them down; and from
this, being forced by the lack of foods, they will loose the siege. 21.4 And
if you see them still holding out and remaining upon the siege and those
inside being straitened, it is necessary also to go toward public night
fights, having gathered an infantry army together with horsemen. Having
arrayed this fittingly according to the situation of the place, you will
prepare the war in the night to take place, as I will show going on concerning
night fighting, and in this manner you will overcome them, and you will force
them to leave the siege of the fortress. 21.5 And if some cause prevents the
war of the night and those being besieged inside are lacking a people that
helps, but they do not have scarcity of foods, you will be able also to bring
an addition of other men, as also of food, if they are lacking, in such a
manner. Having gathered all the army under you and having divided them in two,

which they ride, giving them also other lightly equipped horsemen carrying
nothing except their weapons and being for their guard only. And you with the
rest of the fighting army on another side, according to the hour in which
those carrying the grain are about to come near the fortress, about midnight
appear suddenly with a cry and trumpets, as already about to war them. And
while they are preparing for your war and making the onset toward you, those
carrying the grain, finding leave, will both bring it into the fortress and
return unharmed. And through such a device you may grant them both an addition
of men and of food, if they are in need of both, and let those being besieged
obtain salvation. 22.τ Concerning a separation of the people of half the enemy
or even of the third part 22.1 And if the enemy are not being made ready for a
siege of fortresses, but with a great and violent force they go about our
countries, the one generaling the Roman forces must carefully both command and
prepare the farmers, that is the


citizens of the villages, and all their things to be saved in fortresses and
strong places, and those of the enemy going out for plunder, as being
intelligent and sleepless, continually wound, so that they may be fearing to
ride out far from their army and to bring foods; and from there they will be
held by a straitening of foods. 22.2 Yet, as we said, having a multitude of
people, they will perhaps practice another method. Separating at times the
half of their people or even the third part, they will send them far an
interval of a day's road or even beyond, standing off from their host in some
villages, in which they hope to find more expenses. And necessity [is] that
these spend two days or even three outside their host. Having learned this
then and understood it, O general, it is necessary to ambush them with
calculated consideration and to come near them by night and, finding a fit
place, hide your own army. 22.3 Since therefore they are scattered not into
two and three villages, but also into more, searching foods, and it is likely
also that the so-called covering force stands for their guard, you must divide
the army under you in two, making security that you not be detected or
recognized by the enemy searching the companies, so that you may not rather be
ambushed by them; and hold out in the hiding until the eleventh hour of the
day, the sun already being toward setting. And if the covering force has
turned back, as is the custom, into the villages in which their companions
bivouac, you will send into the villages far off those whom you separated in
front, following behind them and accompanying them from nearby. And as the
situation of the place gives, walking secretly and without cry swiftly hide
yourself, until you come very near the villages. 22.4 And when it is not
possible for the people under you to be hidden by some gully, but there is
necessity to come into the open, then with a youthful riding let the onset
against the enemy who are in the villages take place from those sent in front.
And having attacked these unexpectedly they will both kill many, and some they
may also seize alive in the account of captives. And if the covering force for
their guard still stands outside the villages, let those sent in front make
the onset against it first, about the settings of the sun, as I said. And you
accompanying behind after those leading you, arrayed to join war you yourself
also will set out against them and will overcome these by the grace of God.
22.5 And if the leaders of the enemy, who are there, having a great force are
able to stand and strive to recover the defeat, which is impossible, night
already taking hold, nothing contrary will happen to you. If you do thus then,
both will the enemy wonder and be frightened of you and they will not dare
without fear to go out of their host; and from there necessity [is] that
these, lacking foods, return toward their own country. 23.τ Concerning the
return of the enemy and the occupying of the pass 23.1 And when the enemy are
turning back and are pressed to reach their own, it is necessary to send the
infantry forces ahead toward the passes and to occupy the roads by which they
are about to pass. For by whatever road they wish to pass, both from the
passes in Seleukeia and of the theme of the Anatolikoi, where the Tauric
mountains bound both Cilicia and Cappadocia and Lykandos, in addition also
those lying beside Germanikeia and Adata and Kaisoun and that of Daouthas, and
Melitene and the Kaloudia, and those beyond the river Euphrates bounding both
the country called Chanzit and the enemy country as far as Romanopolis, in all
such themes, by whatever road turning back they wish to pass toward their own,
without hesitation with the help of God hasten to dispose the war against them
and you will find the winning by


his grace. 23.2 And when four stations, that is camps, before, you know the
road by which the enemy have set out, it belongs to you, the leader of the
whole people, with much speed to come in front of them and to reach the
infantry forces sent in the pass, and to leave the better of the rest of the
generals to run beside them because of the raids behind and their
runnings-out; and to have with him also the rest of the cavalry army obeying
his word, and to appoint him that, when the enemy draw near the pass about two
camps away, he too with all the army is to pass through and arrive in the
place where the war against them is being fitted. 23.3 And when you arrive in
the pass and are joined with the infantry orders, it is necessary to be eager
also to gather another infantry people especially, as much as you can, and to
exhort them with freedom, anointing them toward bravery and daring, and to
stir their eagerness, as a best general, with honey-dropping exhortation:
"Men," saying, "Romans, let us stand unbending and firmly; let us stand
manfully and nobly; let us show arm and strength to enemies; let us show that
they have the onset toward stronger men, that they array themselves toward
those about to strike rather than be struck; for they do not have a nature of
stone or bronze, so as not to receive wounds; for the body is not of iron, so
as not to be slackened by the toil and grow numb." In addition to these
showing also the difficulty of the place in which they are about to be
prepared for battle and how much help it grants them. 23.4 And after having
sharpened them well and woken them and made them more eager and more daring,
you will set them in the places in which they ought to fight the enemies. And
first it is necessary to occupy the high parts of the mountains and to hold
and secure all the roads. And in the places where horsemen also have room to
fight with the infantry, you will set them too in fit places. And in all your
arrays you must set useful leaders and keep and do all the other things, as
many as at the beginning of the account we set out concerning how one must
fight the enemy in the difficult ground. 23.5 And when the enemy arrive near
and perceive the security made by you on the road, and that it is impossible
for them to pass, if they dare to do this more rashly, the counsel will not
stand for them fortunately, but being wasted and choked by your people they
will give their backs even unwilling, hastening by another road to reach their
own. And when these thus turn back ignobly and without order, your people
seeing these, with how much joy and gladness they will be filled, it is
impossible to set this beside in an account. 23.6 When therefore the enemy, as
has been said, are turning back, then pursue behind them, sending in front
horsemen with swift light-armed; and you yourself also walking more quickly
hasten to reach them. And the enemies, because they are fleeing, to pass the
difficult ground with speed and to escape the war in them and to reach their
own, since they will come near the pass, suspecting this so that the infantry
orders may not again come in front of them and occupy their road, if they try
to make the march in the night, you may reach these swiftly, their horses
having failed from the long march and the men having given out you may meet,
as they march by night; and you have found, general, what is always sought by
you. 23.7 And when you reach these by night, it is necessary immediately to
join war upon the so-called saka with infantry following with them and
horsemen. And send others of the light-armed with horsemen on this side and on
that of the road to come in front of the enemy, and command them also from the
flank to attack war. And if you do thus they will not be able to stand, but
they will set out to flight. And if they do this by night, pursue them and you
will give them over to complete destruction. 24.τ Concerning night fighting
24.1 And if they come to another counsel,


according to what seems profitable to them, and, when they perceive that you
have reached them, they will also camp by night, having pitched the tents,
then you will work a night fight against them. 24.2 Yet thus you must dispose
the war against them. You from behind with an infantry array will make the
attack. And divide the rest of the infantry orders into six parts, and set
three on the right of the enemy from their flank, and three on the left, if
the situation of the place forces them to set their camp into a length,
distant from one another about a bowshot, or even a little less, leaving open
and unguarded only the road that saves the enemy toward their own, so that, a
most violent war having been set upon them and they having found the road
open, by the deceit of being saved and escaping the war and reaching their
own, they may mount the horses and set out to flight through it, each
procuring only his own salvation. 24.3 And if they have not made their camp
long, but the situation of the place has worked it circular, you must,
standing the infantry arrays around, command these to be prepared for war, and
only, as we said, leave free and open the road that leads toward their own.
24.4 And after having arranged the infantry in this manner, you will command
them to camp beside the enemy and to light fires into a multitude, many. And
in each one array of the infantry you will also set an officer of the useful
and brave; and with them horsemen in each one array, standing from behind, if
the space also gives, having as leaders considerable men; and you will command
both them and all the people of the infantry orders to obey. 24.5 And after
having been prepared thus, separate of the light-armed men brave and swift in
the feet, and send them in front to come down in silence and come very near
the enemy, and appoint them that first those standing in the middle will make
the attack, then those in front. And if from both sides the situation of the
place brings the infantry orders more highly against the enemy, the enemy
being struck with stones from the hands and slings and with bows from above
from either side will easily be dissolved. And if the situation of the place
is not thus, but from one side the high lies above, likewise from there more
easily let them send the casts of the stones and bows against them. Yet from
the level one must take hold of the battle more carefully. And if the enemy
wish to mount horses and set out against them, they will work nothing terrible
for the light-armed, the place helping them, but they will rather cause great
harm to themselves. 24.6 And let all the infantry orders come down together
from both sides and let them all trumpet with a cry and a shout. Then let the
general also from behind kindle the war strongly. And if the enemy still hold
out and will not set out to flight, let the swift light-armed and those sent
in front be urged by their officers to go inside into the tents of the enemy;
for this will be easy for them from the hardness of the place. And when they
begin to take up horses or mules and goods of the enemy and to enslave men,
and such a beginning takes place, all will set out into such a seizing and
will go inside the tents unsparingly, cutting them down with the sword. 24.7
And then all will set out to flight, as many as are able to mount horses, and
others also on foot, so as to be able to be hidden in mountains and ravines
and obtain salvation. And if such is the end of the war with the help of God,
through the intercessions of his all-undefiled mother and Theotokos, the holy
emperors will take glory, and all the Roman army power, the enemy no longer
being able to stand against their face. 24.8 And if they happen upon a level
space as the day is appearing, and into


it they will halt, having pitched the tents, and it is not easy in such a
place for the war in the night to be fitting to take place, the general must
with all the infantry and cavalry forces come in front again and occupy the
higher parts of the mountains and likewise secure the crossing of the road.
And since all, as we said, the roads that lead into the enemy country, through
all the themes which I numbered and I have seen with my own eyes, happen to be
hard to cross in the mountains that bound both countries, hasten to occupy
their crossing beforehand and dispose the war against their face without
hesitation, and by the grace of Christ our God you will prevail over them and,
having cast them down, you will give them over to complete destruction. 25.τ
Concerning another occupying of a road that makes a difficult descent 25.1 And
if the road by which they turn back is found bringing the enemy from the upper
parts and is level, not having difficult ground for standing against their
face, but when it inclines toward the downward, then it is narrow and hard to
cross, confusing their arrays and forcing few to pass and preparing the others
likewise to follow and pass, in those narrows it is necessary to set infantry
orders on this side and on that of the enemy, two on the right, and two on the
left, distinguished from one another. But also from behind set into a company
an infantry force of people with horsemen and a brave and most experienced
officer, if they have left their so-called saka to stand above and to guard
the road, but not nearby, so as not to be recognized. 25.2 And if those
standing in the company above are eager to join war, when our arrays standing
below make the attack against the enemy passing in front, let those standing
in the company, both infantry and horsemen, also come out and join war after
them against the enemy. And if those standing in the company will not dare to
go against it from below, a saka standing in a level place, they will hold out
in the company, and, since it is pressed to pass through and to reach its own
(for it is not of the things admitted to stand there for long), when they take
hold of the road leading into descent, let those in the company also come out,
and having occupied the road and having come from above let them set out
against them. 25.3 And they will not be able to stand and defend themselves,
being struck by them, the place being contrary to them; but they will hasten
to reach their own, being warred in the defiles by our arrays standing below,
in hope to pass through and reach their own. But they may in no way succeed in
this, if indeed the war against them is prepared as we have set out, but
rather they will be defeated by force by the grace of our true God; to whom
the glory and the power with Son and the Holy Spirit now and always and unto
the ages of the ages. Amen.
