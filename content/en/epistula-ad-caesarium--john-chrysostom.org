Of John Chrysostom, from the letter to Caesarius after his second exile
1.1 And you would surely say that you have come from error to the
better, and that you give thanks to those who brought that marvelous
book, which your most excellent letters call the best, as clearly
declaring that an essential concurrence and a divine mixing of divinity
and of flesh has come to be, and that from this one nature has been
completed. This, admirable one, is the absurdity of the insane
Apollinaris; this is the most impious heresy of those who introduce a
blending and a coalescence. 1.2 From the same letter: But when you say
Christ, you have joined both. Whence the same one would be called both
passible and impassible: passible in flesh, impassible in divinity. And
these things are predicated also of Son and of Jesus and of Lord; for
these names are common and indicative of the two essences. 1.3 From the
same letter: Thus here too, the divine nature being seated in the body,
the two together completed one Son, one person, recognized however by an
unconfused and undivided account, not in one nature only, but in two
perfect ones. For in the case of one, how would the unconfused, how the
undivided, how the union ever be spoken of? For it is impossible for the
one to be united to itself, or to be confused, or to be divided. What
Hades then belched forth the saying of one nature in Christ? 1.4 And
toward the end of the same letter: Turning aside from the empty talk of
these, beloved, let us return to the matter at hand. It is pious, and
exceedingly pious, to confess Christ who was clothed with death as
perfect in divinity and perfect in humanity, one only-begotten Son, not
divided into a duality of sons, yet bearing in himself the unconfused
properties of the two inseparable natures, not one and another, God
forbid, but one and the same Lord Jesus Christ, God the Word, clothed in
flesh, and that not without soul and without mind, as the impious
Apollinaris said. Let us then hold to these things. Let us flee those
who divide; for even if the nature is twofold, still the union is
undivided and untearable, confessed in one person of sonship and one
hypostasis. Let us flee those who invent marvels of one nature after the
union; for by the notion of the one they are eager to attach passion to
the impassible God, denying the economy.
2.t Of Chrysostom, from the letter to Caesarius the monk 2.1 What Hades
belched forth the saying of one nature in Christ after the union? For
either, holding the divine, they deny the human, I mean our salvation;
or, holding the human, they make denial of the divine nature. Let them
choose which lost its own. For if the union is still sound, then the
properties of the union must altogether be preserved. Otherwise this is
not union, but confusion and disappearance of the natures. Christ then
is not in one nature only, but in two perfect ones. For in the case of
one, where is the unconfused? Where the undivided? Where the union? For
it is impossible for a thing to be united to itself or to be divided.
2.2 Of the same: And the saints, interpreting both the division and the
difference, say: division is that which does not cut the subjects
through and through, that is entirely, and set them as existing
privately and in parts by themselves and separated from one another; but
difference is an account according to which the subjects differ from one
another, that is, that the flesh is by nature and by essence what it is,
and again that God the Word is by nature and by essence what he is. 3.t
Of Chrysostom, to Caesarius 3.1 And it is pious to confess Christ who
was clothed with death as perfect in divinity, one Son

only-begotten, not divided into a duality of sons, yet bearing in
himself the unconfused properties of the two inseparable natures, not
one and another, God forbid, but one and the same Lord Jesus, God the
Word, clothed in flesh, and that not without soul and without mind, as
the impious Apollinaris said. But if to some it seems impossible that
two things be in one, and at once be held and at once be crucified, and
that one of these undergo the outrage, I will try to show this by a
human example. The imperial purple was wool; the blood of the shellfish
mixed with this gave it a purple color. When then it was spun with the
fingers and twisted, having become warp, clearly the wool and not the
dye underwent the twisting; likewise, likening the man to the wool, God
the Word to the purple color, who was united in the passion and the
cross. But he did not fall under the passion at all. Again, let there be
a tree, having in itself the ray of the sun; when then this is being
cut, we see that the striking iron is first carried from above against
the ray in it. And the ray first, before the tree is struck, appears to
receive the blow. As then the brightness, though it is there, is not
cut, nor is it chopped through, so also the divinity could neither be
separated nor be cut and suffer, but the flesh fell under the passion,
which could also be cut and suffer, as there the tree.
