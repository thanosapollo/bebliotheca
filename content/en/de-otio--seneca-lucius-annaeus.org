** Book VIII,
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:
To Serenus:
On Leisure

** Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h1
:END:

[omitted] they commend vices to us with great unanimity. Even if we
attempt nothing else that is salutary, it will still be profitable
merely to withdraw; we shall be better when we are alone. What of
this, that it is permitted to withdraw to the best men and to choose
some example by which we may steer our life? And that is not done
except in leisure. Then what has once pleased can be held fast, when
no one comes between who, with the people as his helper, twists a
judgment still weak; then life can go forward on an even course and
a single tenor, which we split by setting before ourselves the most
diverse aims.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h2
:END:

For among other evils this is the worst, that we even change the
vices themselves. Thus we do not even have the fortune of remaining
in an evil already familiar. One thing after another pleases us, and
this too torments us, that our judgments are not only crooked but
also fickle. We toss, and we seize one thing after another; we
abandon what we have sought, we seek again what we have abandoned;
there is an interchange between our desire and our repentance;

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h3
:END:

for we hang wholly upon the judgments of others, and that seems best
to us which has many seekers and praisers, not that which ought to
be praised and sought; nor do we estimate the good road and the bad
in themselves, but by the crowd of footprints, among which there are
none of those returning.

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h4
:END:

You will say to me: "What are you doing, Seneca? Are you deserting
your side? Surely your Stoics say: 'We shall be in action to the
last bound of life; we shall not cease to give our effort to the
common good, to help individuals, to bring aid even to enemies with
an aged hand. We are the ones who grant exemption to no years, and,
as that most eloquent man says,
/
We press gray hair beneath the helmet.
/
We are the ones among whom nothing before death is at leisure, so
that, if the case allows, death itself is not at leisure.' Why do
you speak the precepts of Epicurus in the very first principles of
Zeno? Why, if the party disgusts you, do you not desert in good
earnest rather than betray it?"

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2-h5
:END:

For the present I will answer you this: "Do you want anything more
than that I show myself like my leaders? What then? I shall go not
where they have sent me, but where they have led me."

** Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h1
:END:

Now I will prove to you that I am not falling away from the precepts
of the Stoics; for they themselves have not fallen away from their
own; and yet I would be most excusable even if I followed not their
precepts but their examples. What I say I will divide into two
parts: first, that someone may, even from earliest age, give himself
wholly to the contemplation of truth, seek a rule of living, and
practice it in secret;

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3-h2
:END:

next, that someone may, with his campaigns already served, in an age
that is spent, do this with the best right, and turn his mind to
other acts, after the manner of the Vestal virgins, who, with their
years divided among duties, learn to perform the rites and, when
they have learned, teach.

** Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h1
:END:

I will show that this is pleasing to the Stoics as well, not because
I have laid down a law for myself to commit nothing against the word
of Zeno or Chrysippus, but because the matter itself allows me to go
into their opinion; since if anyone always follows one man, he is
not in a senate but in a faction. Would that everything were already
held fast, and that truth were confessed in the open, and that we
changed nothing of our decrees! As it is, we seek the truth together
with those very men who teach.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h2
:END:

Two schools especially disagree also in this matter, the Epicureans
and the Stoics, but each sends us to leisure by a different road.
Epicurus says: "The wise man will not approach public affairs,
unless something intervenes"; Zeno says: "He will approach public
affairs, unless something hinders."

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h3
:END:

The one seeks leisure from a set purpose, the other from a cause;
and that cause stretches wide. If the commonwealth is too corrupt to
be helped, if it is occupied by evils, the wise man will not strain
himself to no purpose, nor will he spend himself when he will profit
nothing. If he will have too little authority or strength, and the
commonwealth will not admit him, if health will hinder him,

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h4
:END:

just as he would not launch a shattered ship upon the sea, just as a
cripple would not give his name for military service, so he will not
enter upon a journey which he knows is unfit. Therefore even he for
whom all things are still intact, before he tries any storms, may
remain in safety and at once commend himself to the good arts and
spend an unimpaired leisure, a cultivator of the virtues, which can
be practiced even in the quietest circumstances.

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4-h5
:END:

This, surely, is required of a man, that he be of use to men: if it
can be done, to many; if not, to a few; if not, to those nearest; if
not, to himself. For when he makes himself useful to the rest, he is
doing the common business. Just as he who makes himself worse does
not harm himself only, but also all those whom, had he been made
better, he could have helped, so whoever deserves well of himself
benefits others by this very thing, that he prepares what will be of
use to them.

** Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h1
:END:

Let us embrace two commonwealths in the mind, one great and truly
public, in which gods and men are contained, in which we do not look
to this corner or to that, but measure the bounds of our city with
the sun; the other, to which the condition of birth has enrolled us.
This will be that of the Athenians or of the Carthaginians, or of
some other city, which does not belong to all men but to certain
ones. Some at the same time give their effort to both commonwealths,
the greater and the lesser; some only to the lesser; some only to
the greater.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5-h2
:END:

This greater commonwealth we can serve even in leisure; indeed I do
not know whether better in leisure, that we may inquire what virtue
is, whether it is one or several; whether nature or art makes good
men; whether this is one thing which embraces seas and lands and
what is set in sea and land, or whether God has scattered many
bodies of that kind; whether all matter from which all things are
born is continuous and full, or divided, with void mixed among
solids; what God is; whether he sits idle and watches his work or
handles it; whether he is poured around it from without or set
within the whole; whether the world is immortal or is to be counted
among things that fall and are born for a time. He who contemplates
these things, what does he furnish to God? That so great a work of
his may not be without a witness.

** Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h1
:END:

We are accustomed to say that the highest good is to live according
to nature. Nature has begotten us for both, both for the
contemplation of things and for action.

Now let us prove what we said first. What then? Will this not be
proved if each one has consulted himself, how great a desire he has
of knowing the unknown, how he is stirred at every tale?

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h2
:END:

Some sail and endure the toils of a very long journey for one wage,
that of learning something hidden and remote. This thing draws
peoples together to spectacles; this forces us to pry into what is
shut, to search out what is more secret, to unroll antiquities, to
hear the customs of barbarian nations.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h3
:END:

Nature has given us a curious mind, and, conscious of her own art
and beauty, has begotten us as spectators for so great a show of
things, about to lose the fruit of herself if she displayed things
so great, so bright, so finely drawn, so shining and beautiful not
in one kind only, to solitude.

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h4
:END:

That you may know she wished to be gazed at, not merely glanced at,
see what place she has given us. She has set us in the middle of
herself and given us a view around of all things; and she has not
only set man upright, but, about to make him fit for contemplation,
so that he might follow the stars gliding from rising to setting and
turn his face about with the whole, she has made his head high and
set it on a bending neck; then, leading six signs through the day
and six through the night, she has unfolded every part of herself,
so that through these things which she had offered to his eyes she
might make a desire even of the rest.

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h5
:END:

For we see neither all things nor things as great as they are, but
our sight opens for itself a path of investigation and lays true
foundations, so that inquiry may pass from what is open into what is
dark and find something older than the world itself: from where
those stars came forth; what the state of the universe was before
the several things departed into parts; what reason, when they were
sunk and confused, drew them apart; who assigned places to things,
whether by their own nature the heavy descended and the light flew
up, or whether besides the striving and the weight of bodies some
higher force laid down a law for each; whether that is true by which
it is most proved that men are of a divine spirit, that a part and,
as it were, certain sparks of the stars leaped down to earth and
stuck in a place not their own.

*** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h6
:END:

Our thought breaks through the ramparts of heaven and is not content
to know what is shown. "That," it says, "I search out, which lies
beyond the world, whether it is a deep vastness or whether this
itself also is shut by its own bounds; what the condition is of
those shut out, whether they are formless and confused, occupying as
much space in every direction, or whether they too are arranged into
some order; whether they cohere with this world, or have withdrawn
far from it and roll here in the void; whether they are indivisible
things through which all that is born and will be is built, or
whether their matter is continuous and changeable through the whole;
whether the elements are contrary among themselves, or do not fight
but breathe together through different things."

*** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h7
:END:

Born for these inquiries, consider how little time he has received,
even if he claims it all for himself. Though he suffer nothing to be
snatched by ease, nothing to fall away by negligence, though he keep
his hours most greedily and go on to the last bound of human age,
and though fortune shake nothing from him of what nature has
established, still man is too mortal for the knowledge of things
immortal.

*** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6-h8
:END:

Therefore I live according to nature if I have given myself wholly
to her, if I am her admirer and cultivator. But nature wished me to
do both, both to act and to have leisure for contemplation. I do
both, since not even contemplation is without action.

** Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7-h1
:END:

"But it matters," you say, "whether you have approached it for the
sake of pleasure, seeking from it nothing else than constant
contemplation that never issues forth; for it is sweet and has its
own allurements." Against this I answer you: it equally matters with
what mind you live the civic life, whether you are always restless
and never take any time for yourself in which you may look from
human things to divine.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7-h2
:END:

Just as it is by no means to be approved to pursue affairs without
any love of the virtues and without the cultivation of the mind and
to put forth bare labors (for those things ought to be mixed and
sown together), so it is an imperfect and feeble good when virtue is
cast into leisure without action, never showing what it has learned.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7-h3
:END:

Who denies that it ought to try its advances in work, and not only
think what ought to be done, but also at times exercise the hand and
bring what has been meditated through to the real? But if the delay
is not through the wise man himself, if the actor is not lacking but
the things to be acted are lacking, will you not permit him to be
with himself?

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7-h4
:END:

With what mind does the wise man withdraw to leisure? That he may
know that then too he will do those things through which he may
benefit posterity. We certainly are the ones who say that both Zeno
and Chrysippus did greater things than if they had led armies, held
offices, carried laws. Laws which they carried not for one city, but
for the whole human race. Why then is such leisure not fitting for a
good man, through which he orders future ages and does not harangue
among a few, but among all men of all nations, both those who are
and those who will be?

*** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7-h5
:END:

In short I ask whether Cleanthes and Chrysippus and Zeno lived
according to their own precepts. You will without doubt answer that
they lived as they had said one ought to live. And yet none of them
administered the commonwealth. "They did not have," you say, "either
the fortune or the rank which is accustomed to be admitted to the
handling of public affairs." But all the same they did not lead a
sluggish life; they found how their own quiet might profit men more
than the running about and sweat of others. Therefore they seemed no
less to have done much, although they did nothing in public.

** Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8-h1
:END:

Besides, there are three kinds of life, among which it is usual to
inquire which is the best. One is free for pleasure, another for
contemplation, the third for action. First, laying down contention
and laying down the hatred which we have declared implacable against
those who follow different things, let us see that all these come to
the same under one title and another. Neither he who approves
pleasure is without contemplation, nor he who serves contemplation
is without pleasure, nor he whose life is appointed for actions is
without contemplation.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8-h2
:END:

"There is a very great difference," you say, "whether a thing is the
purpose or an addition to another purpose." Let the difference be
great indeed; still the one is not without the other. Neither does
that man contemplate without action, nor does this one act without
contemplation, nor does that third, of whom we have agreed to think
ill, approve an idle pleasure, but that which he makes firm for
himself by reason;

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8-h3
:END:

so even this very pleasure-school is in action. Why should it not be
in action? since Epicurus himself says that he will at times
withdraw from pleasure, that he will even seek pain, if either
repentance will threaten pleasure or a lesser pain will be taken on
in place of a heavier one? What is the point of saying these things?

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8-h4
:END:

That it may appear that contemplation is pleasing to all; others
seek it; for us this is a station, not a harbor.

** Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9
:END:

*** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9-h1
:END:

Add now that it is permitted to live at leisure by the law of
Chrysippus; I do not say that he suffer leisure, but that he choose
it. Our people deny that the wise man will approach any and every
commonwealth; but what does it matter how the wise man comes to
leisure, whether because a commonwealth is lacking to him, or
because he is lacking to the commonwealth, if the commonwealth is
not going to exist everywhere? And it will always be lacking to
those who seek fastidiously. I ask, to what commonwealth is the wise
man to approach? To that of the Athenians, in which Socrates is
condemned, Aristotle, lest he be condemned, flees? in which envy
crushes the virtues? You will deny to me that the wise man will
approach this commonwealth.

*** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9-h2
:END:

Will the wise man then approach the commonwealth of the
Carthaginians, in which there is continual sedition and a liberty
hostile to every best man, the utmost cheapness of the just and the
good, inhuman cruelty against enemies, even hostile against their
own? This too he will flee.

*** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9-h3
:END:

If I shall wish to go through them one by one, I shall find none
which the wise man can endure, or which can endure the wise man. But
if that commonwealth which we imagine for ourselves is not found,
leisure begins to be necessary for all, because the one thing that
could be preferred to leisure is nowhere.

*** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9-h4
:END:

If someone says that it is best to sail, and then denies that one
should sail on that sea in which shipwrecks are accustomed to happen
and sudden storms are frequent which snatch the helmsman into the
contrary, I think this man forbids me to loose the ship, although he
praises navigation.
