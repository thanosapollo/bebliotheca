* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

It is a rare and difficult thing that those whom another's victory has made tyrants should be well committed to writing, and therefore scarcely all things concerning them are fully held in monuments and annals.

For first, those things which are great for their honor are distorted by writers; then other things are suppressed; lastly no great diligence is sought in their family and life, since it is enough to set forth their audacity and the war in which they were conquered, and their punishment.

Pescennius Niger, then, is said to have been of modest parents, as some relate, of noble, as others, his father Annius Fuscus, his mother Lampridia, his grandfather a curator of Aquinum, from which the family drew its origin; which indeed is even now held doubtful. He was moderately trained in letters, fierce in manners, immoderate in riches, sparing in his way of life, of unbridled lust toward every kind of desire. He long commanded the ranks, and by many commands he reached the point of ruling the Syrian armies by order of Commodus, chiefly by the suffrage of the athlete who strangled Commodus, as all things then were done.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

When he learned that Commodus had been killed, that Julianus had been named emperor and the same killed by order of Severus and the senate, and that Albinus also in Gaul had taken the name and right of emperor, he was named emperor by the Syrian armies which he ruled, as some say, more from hatred of Julianus than in rivalry of Severus. Because of detestation of Julianus, in the first days of that man's empire he was so favored at Rome, by the senators at least, who also hated Severus, that amid the stonings and curses of all, success was wished for him, and the people shouted
/May the gods keep him as prince and him as Augustus/.
But the commons hated Julianus, because the soldiers had killed Pertinax and had named that man emperor against the will of the people. Finally there were huge seditions on this account. But Julianus had sent a primipilar to kill Niger, foolishly, against one who had armies and could protect himself; as if any sort of emperor could be killed by a primipilar. With the same madness Julianus had also sent a successor to Severus, already prince. Finally he had also sent the centurion Aquilius, known for the slaughter of generals, as if so great an emperor could be killed by a centurion. Equal, lastly, was the insanity, that he is said to have dealt with Severus by an interdict concerning the empire, that he might seem to have forestalled the principate by law.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And the people's judgment of Pescennius Niger appeared from this, that when Julianus was giving circus games at Rome, and the benches of the Circus Maximus had been filled without distinction, and he had been struck with huge injury from the people, Pescennius Niger was sought by all with one consent for the protection of the city, from hatred, as we have said, of Julianus and from love of the slain Pertinax; when indeed Julianus is said to have said that neither to himself nor to Pescennius was a long empire owed, but to Severus, who was more to be held in hatred by senators, soldiers, provincials, and the commons. Which the event of the matter proved.

And Pescennius indeed was most friendly to Severus at the time when he was governing the Lugdunensian province; for he himself had been sent to seize deserters, who then in countless number were harassing the Gauls, in which office, because he conducted himself honorably, he was most pleasing to Severus, so that Septimius reported of him to Commodus, asserting that he was a man necessary to the republic, and in truth he was vehement in military matters. Never under him did a soldier wring from a provincial wood, oil, or labor. He himself received nothing from a soldier; when he was serving as tribune, he suffered nothing to be received. For even as emperor already he ordered two tribunes, who were shown to have taken stellaturae, to be buried with stones by the auxiliaries.

There is extant a letter of Severus, in which he writes to Ragonius Celsus governing the Gauls:
/It is a wretched thing that we cannot imitate the military discipline of him whom we have conquered in war. Your soldiers wander, the tribunes bathe at midday, they have cookshops for dining rooms, hired lodgings for bedchambers; they dance, they drink, they sing, and they call this the measures of banquets when they have drunk without measure. Would these things be done if any vein of ancestral discipline were living? Reform therefore first the tribunes, then the soldier, whom, as long as he shall fear, so long you will hold. But know this also from Niger, that the soldier cannot fear unless the tribunes and the leaders of the soldiers have been sound./

These things Severus Augustus wrote of Pescennius. Of this man while still a soldier Marcus Antoninus to Cornelius Balbus:
/You praise Pescennius to me, I recognize him; for your predecessor also said that he was strenuous of hand, grave in life, and even then more than a soldier. Therefore I have sent letters to be read at the standards, by which I have ordered him to command three hundred Armenians and one hundred Sarmatians and a thousand of our own. It is yours to show that the man has come not by ambition, which does not suit our manners, but by virtue to that place which my grandfather Hadrian, which Trajan my great-grandfather, used to give only to the most thoroughly tried./

Of this same man Commodus:
/I know Pescennius as a brave man and I have already given him two tribunates; I will soon give him a command, when through old age Aelius Corduenus has refused the republic./
Such were the judgments of all concerning him, but Severus himself also often said that he would pardon Pescennius, unless he persisted.

Finally Pescennius, declared consul by Commodus, was set above Severus, and indeed to Severus angry, because Niger was earning the consulship by the recommendations of primipilars. In his own life Severus says that, before his sons were of that age that they could rule, when he was sick he had this in mind, that if anything by chance had happened to him, Niger Pescennius and likewise Clodius Albinus should succeed, who both stood forth as Severus' gravest enemies. Whence it appears what even Severus' judgment of Pescennius

was. If we believe Severus, Niger was greedy of glory, feigned in his way of life, base in manners, of advanced age when he invaded the empire, from which he accuses his desires, as if Severus had come to the empire younger, who contracts his years, when he ruled eighteen years and died in his eighty-ninth.

Indeed Severus sent Heraclitus to hold Bithynia, but Fulvius to seize Niger's adult sons. Yet Severus said nothing to the senate concerning Niger, when he had already heard of his empire, and he himself was setting out to settle the state of the East. This only he did as he set out, that he sent legions to Africa, lest Pescennius occupy it and press the Roman people with famine. And he seemed able to do this through Libya and Egypt neighboring to Africa, though the journey and the sailing were difficult. And Pescennius indeed, as Severus was coming to the East, held Greece, the Thraces, Macedonia, many illustrious men having been killed, calling Severus to a sharing of the empire, by whom, because of those whom he had killed, he was named an enemy together with Aemilianus, then fighting through Aemilianus he was conquered by Severus' generals. And when that man promised him a safe exile if he withdrew from arms, persisting he fought again and was conquered, and at Cyzicus about a marsh, fleeing, he was wounded, and so brought to Severus and at once

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

dead. His head was carried about on a pike and sent to Rome, his sons killed, his wife slain, his patrimony confiscated, his whole household extinguished. But all these things were done after the rebellion of Albinus was known; for before that he had sent both Niger's sons and their mother into exile. But he blazed up in the second civil war, nay already the third, and was made harder; then when Severus killed countless senators and received from some the name of the Punic Sulla, from others of Marius.

He was of tall stature, handsome of form, with his hair turned back to the crown for grace, of a sonorous voice, so that speaking on the field he was heard through a thousand paces, unless the wind were against him, of a modest face and always ruddy, with a neck so black that, as many say, he received from it the name of Niger, in the rest of his body white and rather fat, eager for wine, sparing of food, of the matter of Venus wholly ignorant except for the begetting of children. Finally he even undertook certain rites in Gaul, which they always by public consent decree to be celebrated by the most chaste.

We see this man pictured in mosaic in the gardens of Commodus in the curved portico among Commodus' dearest friends, bearing the rites of Isis; to which Commodus was so given that he both shaved his head and carried Anubis and fulfilled all the pauses.

He was therefore an excellent soldier, a singular tribune, a distinguished commander, a most severe legate, a notable consul, a man conspicuous at home and abroad, an unlucky emperor; lastly he might have been of use to the republic under Severus, a grim man,

if he had been willing to be with him. But he was deceived by the sinister counsels of Aurelianus, who, betrothing his daughters to that man's sons, made him persist in the empire.

He was of such authority that he wrote first to Marcus then to Commodus, when he saw the provinces overthrown by the ready change of administrations, first that no governor or legate or proconsul of a province should be succeeded to before five years, because they laid down power before they knew how to administer. Then, that new men should not come to governing the republic except for military administrations, he intimated that assessors should administer in those provinces in which they had sat as assessors. Which afterward Severus and thereafter many held, as the prefectures of Paulus and Ulpian prove, who were in Papinian's council and afterward, when one had served for the records, the other for petitions, were at once made prefects. This also was his, that no one should sit as assessor in his own province, that no one should administer unless a Roman at Rome, that is born from the city. He added besides salaries for counsellors, lest they burden those to whom they sat, saying that a judge ought neither to give nor to receive.

He was of such censure toward the soldiers that when in Egypt the frontier troops asked wine of him, he answered
/You have the Nile and you seek wine?/
; since indeed so great is the sweetness of that river that the dwellers do not seek wines. The same, when those who had been conquered by the Saracens were in tumult and saying,
/We have not received wine, we cannot fight,/
/Blush,/
he said,
/those who conquer you drink water./
The same, when the Palestinians asked that their assessment be lightened because it had been made heavy, answered:
/You wish your lands to be lightened of assessment; I indeed would wish even to assess your air./

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

Finally the prophet of Delphic Apollo, in the greatest motion of the republic, when it was announced that there were three emperors, Severus Septimius, Pescennius Niger, Clodius Albinus, being consulted whom it would profit the republic to rule, is said to have poured forth a Greek verse of this kind:
/Best is the Dark One, good the African, worst the White./

From which it was understood that the Dark One was named Niger by the prophecy, Severus the African, but the White Albinus.

Nor was another curiosity wanting, by which it was asked who would obtain the republic, to which he answered another verse of this sort:
/The blood of the White and of the Black living one shall be poured out; one come from the Punic city shall rule the empire of the world./

Likewise, when it was asked who would succeed that man, he is said to have answered likewise in a Greek verse:
/He to whom the gods above have given to have the name of Pius./

Which was not understood at all until Bassianus received the name of Antoninus, which was the true mark of Pius.

Likewise when it was asked how long he would be emperor, he is said to have answered in Greek:
/With twice ten ships he mounts the Italian sea, if nevertheless one bark shall leap across the deep./
From which it was understood that Severus would fill out twenty years.

These are the things, Diocletian greatest of Augusti, which we have learned of Pescennius from many books. For it is not easy, as we said at the beginning of the book, for anyone to commit to books the lives of those who either were not princes in the republic or were not named emperors by the senate, or, being killed too quickly, could not come to fame.

Hence it is that Vindex is hidden, that Piso is unknown, that all those who were either only adopted or were named emperors by the soldiers, as Antonius under Domitian, or being swiftly destroyed laid down their life together with the usurpation of empire. It follows now that I speak of Clodius Albinus, who is held as if an ally of this man, because they likewise rebelled against Severus and were conquered and killed by the same. Of that man himself things standing forth are not clear enough, because his fortune was the same as Pescennius', even if his life was sufficiently unlike.

And lest we seem to have passed over anything of those things which pertain to Pescennius, though they can be known from other books, the soothsayers said of this man to Septimius Severus that neither living nor dead would he come into Severus' power, but that he must perish beside the waters, which some say Severus himself said from astrology, in which he was skilled. Nor was truth wanting to the answers, since that man was found half alive beside a marsh.

* Caput 10

:END:

He was of such severity that, when he had seen certain soldiers drink from a silver cup in the time of an expedition, he ordered all silver to be removed from expeditionary use, adding that they should use wooden vessels. Which indeed stirred military hatred against him. For he said that it could happen that the soldiers' packs should come into the power of the enemy, and that barbarian nations should not make themselves more glorious by our silver, since other things less fit seemed to belong to a foeman's glory. The same ordered that no one should drink wine on an expedition, but that all should be content with vinegar. The same forbade bakers to follow an expedition, ordering the soldiers and all to be content with biscuit. The same, for the snatching of one cock, ordered ten comrades of the maniple, who had eaten what one had seized, to be struck with the axe; and he would have done it, had he not been asked by the whole army almost even to the fear of sedition. And when he had spared them, he ordered that the ten who had lived together on the theft should restore to the provincial the prices of ten cocks each, adding that in the whole expedition no one in the company should make a hearth, lest they ever take food newly cooked, but should feed on bread and cold water, spies being set who should see to this. The same ordered that soldiers about to go to war should not carry gold or silver coins in their belt, but should deposit them publicly, being about to receive after battles what they had given, adding that it was certainly to be restored to their children and wives as heirs, to whom it had come, lest any plunder come to the enemy, if by chance fortune had done something adverse. But all these things, as the looseness of Commodus' times had been, were adverse to him. Finally even if there was no one who in his own times seemed a sterner commander, such examples availed for his ruin more while he lived than when he was dead, when both envy and hatred had been laid down.

* Caput 11

The same on every expedition took the soldiers' food before all, before the tent, nor did he ever seek for himself the help of a roof against sun or against rains, if the soldier did not have it. Lastly in time of war, the reason having been shown to the soldiers, he thought for himself and his slaves or messmates as much as was borne by the soldiers, loading his slaves with rations, lest those walk unburdened and the soldiers laden, and this be seen by the army with a sigh. The same swore in assembly that, as long as he had been on expeditions and was still to be, he had not acted otherwise, and would not act, than as a soldier, having Marius before his eyes and such commanders, nor did he ever have other tales except of Hannibal and the rest of such men. Finally when, after he had been made emperor, a certain man wished to recite a panegyric, he said to him:
/Write praises of Marius or of Hannibal or of any best commander who has finished his life, and say what that man did, that we may imitate him. For to praise the living is mockery, most of all emperors, from whom hope is had, who are feared, who can advance publicly, who can kill, who can proscribe./
But he wished to please while living, and when dead to be praised also.

* Caput 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

He loved among the princes Augustus, Vespasian, Titus, Trajan, Pius, Marcus, calling the rest men of straw or venomous; most of all, however, in histories he loved Marius and Camillus and Quinctius and Marcius Coriolanus. But being asked what he thought of the Scipios, he is said to have said that those men had been fortunate more than brave; and that their domestic life and youth proved it, which in both had been less fair at home. It is agreed among all that, if he had gained power over affairs, he would have corrected all things which Severus either could not emend or would not, and indeed without cruelty, nay even with mildness, but a military mildness, not slack and foolish and ridiculous.

His house is visited at Rome today in the Field of Jupiter, which is called Pescenniana. In it his image stands in a three-chambered room, set in Theban marble, which he had received, made in the likeness of himself, from the people of Thebes. There is also extant a Greek epigram, which in Latin has this sense:

/Huge Niger stands, the terror of the Egyptian soldier, ally of the Thebaid, willing golden ages. Him kings, him nations love, him golden Rome, he is dear to the Antonines and to the empire. He has the name Niger, we ourselves have formed him black, that the form might agree, O metal, with you./

Which verses indeed Severus would not have erased, when both the prefects and the masters of offices suggested this to him, adding:
/If he was such, let all know what sort of man we conquered; if he was not such, let all think that we conquered such a man; nay let it stand so, because he was such./
