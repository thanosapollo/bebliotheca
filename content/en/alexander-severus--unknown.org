When Varius Heliogabalus had been killed (for so we prefer to say rather than
Antoninus, because that plague showed nothing of the Antonines and this name
was erased from the annals by authority of the senate), Aurelius Alexander,
born in the city of Arca, son of Varius, grandson of Varia and cousin of
Heliogabalus himself, received the empire as a remedy for the human race,
having already been named Caesar by the senate, namely after Macrinus died. He
received the name of Augustus as well, with this added, that on one day, at
the senate's offering, he assumed both the name of Father of the Fatherland
and the proconsular right and tribunician power and the right of the fifth
relation.

And lest this heaping of honors seem hasty, I will set out the reasons why the
senate was forced to do it and he to endure it. For it did not suit the
dignity of the senate to confer all at once, nor a good prince to rush to
seize so many dignities at once. The soldiers had already grown used to making
emperors for themselves by a tumultuous judgment and likewise changing them
easily, sometimes bringing in defense that they had done it because they did
not know the senate had named a prince. For they had made emperors of
Pescennius Niger and Clodius Albinus and Avidius Cassius and before that
Lucius Vindex and L. Antonius, and Severus himself, when the senate had
already named Julianus prince, and that matter had sown civil wars, in which
it was necessary that the soldier, made ready against the enemy, perish in a
parricida-liter

way. For this reason, then, it was hurried that Alexander should receive all
at once as if already a veteran emperor.

To this was added the excessive leaning of both senate and people after that
disaster which not only stained the name of the Antonines but also dishonored
the Roman empire. At last all things were decreed in rivalry, both kinds of
names and of powers. He was, in short, the first of all to receive at once
every insignia and every kind of honor, aided by the name of Caesar, which he
had earned some years before, and aided still more by his life and character,
since great favor had been won for him because Heliogabalus tried to kill him
and could not, the soldiers resisting and the senate opposing.

And these things are small, except that he showed himself worthy of the
senate's saving him, of the soldiers' wishing him safe, and of every good
man's judgment naming him prince.

Alexander, then, whose mother was Mamaea (for so he is also called by most),
was from earliest boyhood trained in good arts, civil as well as military, and
did not let even one day pass of his own will in which he did not exercise
himself both in letters and in arms. For in earliest boyhood he had as tutors
Valerius Cordus and Titus Veturius and Aurelius Philippus, a freedman of his
father, who later put his life into writing; in his native city a Greek
grammarian, Nehon, a rhetorician, Serapion, a philosopher, Stilion; at Rome
the grammarians Scaurinus, son of Scaurinus, a most famous teacher, and the
rhetoricians Julius Frontinus and Baebius Macrianus and Julius Granianus,
whose declaimed pieces are still in circulation. But in Latin he made little
progress, as appears from the speeches he delivered in the senate, or from the
addresses he made to the soldiers or to the people. Nor did he greatly love
Latin eloquence, but he loved men of letters intensely, even fearing them,
lest they write something harsh about him. In short, those he saw as fit for
this he wished to learn, with himself as teacher, each thing he did in public
and in private, if they had not been present, and he asked them that, if the
things were true, they put them into writing.

He forbade anyone to call him Lord. He ordered letters to be written to him as
to a private man, only the name of emperor being kept. He took the gems from
the shoes and clothes which Heliogabalus had used. He used a white garment, as
he is also painted, and not a gilded one, and common cloaks and togas. He
lived with his friends so familiarly that he often sat with them in common,
went to their banquets, and had some as daily guests even uninvited. He was
greeted like one of the senators, with the curtain open and the ushers
removed, or only those who had been servants at the doors, whereas before it
had not been allowed to greet the prince, because one could not see them.

And he was of such a body that besides the grace and manly beauty which we
still see in painting and in statues, there was in him the strength of a
soldier's stature, the health of a soldier who knew the force of his own body
and always tended it. He was besides beloved by all men and was called Pius by
some, and by all at least holy and useful to the state. This lot came out for
him in the temple of the Praenestine goddess when Heliogabalus was laying
snares for him: If you break any harsh fates, you will be Marcellus.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:

He received the name Alexander because he was born in a temple dedicated to
Alexander the Great at the city of Arca, when by chance his father had gone
there with his wife on Alexander's feast day to fulfill the rite. Proof of
this is that this Alexander, Mamaea's son, has his birthday on the same day on
which the Great one departed from life. He refused the name Antoninus offered
him by the senate, though he was joined to Caracallus by kinship more than
that counterfeit was; if indeed, as Marius Maximus said in the Life of
Severus, Severus, while still a private man and of no great enough station,
married a noble woman of the East, whose nativity he had learned was that she
should be an emperor's wife, and from that kinship came this Alexander, to
whom Varius Heliogabalus was truly a cousin through his mother. He also
refused the name Great offered him as if to an Alexander by the senate's
judgment.

After this there was a cry: /Aurelius Alexander Augustus, may the gods keep
you/ and the rest according to custom.

When the senate had been dismissed, though many other things had been done
that day, he went home as if in triumph. He seemed much more illustrious for
not taking others' names than if he had taken them, and from that he gained a
name for constancy and full gravity, if indeed the whole senate could not
persuade one man, or rather a youth.

But although, with the senate asking, he could not be persuaded to take either
the name of Antoninus or of the Great, yet because of the huge vigor of his
spirit and his wonderful and singular constancy against the insolence of the
soldiers the name of Severus was given him by the soldiers. This won him huge
reverence at the time and great glory among later men, since it was added that
he received the name from the virtue of his mind, if indeed he was found the
only one who dismissed tumultuous legions, as will be shown in its place, and
punished soldiers most severely who happened to run into something that seemed
unjust, as we will also declare in its own places.

He had these omens of empire: first, that he was born on the day on which
Alexander the Great is said to have died; next, that his mother brought him
forth in that man's temple; third, that he received that man's name; then
besides, that an old woman offered his mother a dove-egg of purple color, laid
on the same day he was born; from which the soothsayers said he would indeed
be emperor, but not for long, and would come quickly to the empire. Then
besides, that a tablet of the emperor Trajan, which hung over his father's
marriage-bed, fell onto the bed while he was being brought forth in the
temple. To these was added that a nurse Olympias was given him, by which name
Alexander's mother was called. A foster-father Philip appeared by chance, one
of the country people, which was the name of Alexander the Great's father. It
is said that on the first day of his birth a star of the first magnitude was
seen all day at Arca Caesarea, and the sun around his father's house was
crowned with a shining circuit. When the soothsayers commended his birthday,
they said he would hold the height of affairs, because the victims had been
brought from a farm that belonged to the emperor Severus, and which the
tenants had prepared in that man's honor. A laurel born in the house beside a
peach tree outgrew the peach tree within one year, whence the interpreters
also said that the Persians were to be

conquered by him. His mother, the day before she gave birth, dreamed that she

was carried to the sky on the wings of Roman Victory, the one in the senate.
When he himself consulted a seer about the future, he is said to have received

gods as well; "Empire awaits you, which holds empire." From this it was
understood that he would be prince of the Roman empire, for where is the
empire that holds empire if not among the Romans? And these things indeed are
handed down from Greek verses. He himself, however, when at his parent's
urging he was turning his mind from philosophy and music to other arts, was
illumined by Virgilian lots of this kind: Others will hammer out breathing
bronze more softly, I believe, and will draw living faces from marble; they
will plead causes better and mark the courses of the sky with the rod and tell
the rising stars; you, Roman, remember to rule peoples with empire; these will
be your arts, to impose the custom of peace, to spare the subjected and war
down the proud.

There were many other signs by which it was clear he was prince of the human
race. An excessive brightness of the eyes, heavy for those who looked too
long; a most frequent divination of mind; a singular memory of things, which
Acholius said was aided by a mnemonic. And when as a boy he had come to the
empire, he did all things with his mother, so that she too seemed to rule
equally, a holy woman but greedy and hungry for gold and silver.

When therefore he began to act as Augustus, he first removed from the state
and from offices and duties all the judges whom that impure man had raised
from the filthiest kind of men; then he purged the senate and the equestrian
order; then he purged the tribes themselves and those who rest on military
prerogatives, and his Palace and the whole court, casting out from palace
service all the obscene and infamous; nor did he allow anyone in the Palatine
household except a necessary man. He then bound himself by oath not to have
anyone supernumerary, that is vacantivum, lest he burden the state with
rations, saying that an emperor is a public evil who feeds from the vitals of
the provincials men neither necessary nor useful to the state. He ordered that
thieving judges never be seen in any cities, and if they were seen, that they
be deported by the governors of the provinces. He inspected the soldiers'
rations carefully. Tribunes who had taken anything from the soldiers through
illegal stoppages he punished with death. He directed that business and cases
be first treated and arranged by the chiefs of the bureaus and the most
learned men skilled in law and faithful to him, of whom Ulpian was then first,
and so referred to him.

He enacted moderate and countless laws on the right of the people and of the
fisc, and he consecrated no constitution without twenty men skilled in law and
the most learned and wise men, and the same most eloquent, no fewer than
fifty, so that there might be no fewer opinions in the council than would make
a senate decree. And this indeed in such a way that they went through the
opinions of each man and wrote what each had said, yet with time given to
inquire and think before they spoke, lest they be forced to speak unthought on
huge matters. He had besides this custom, that if he treated of law or of
business he admitted only the learned and eloquent; but if of military
affairs, old soldiers and aged men of good desert and skilled in places and
wars and camps, and all men of letters and most of all those who knew history,
asking what the old emperors, either Roman or of foreign nations, had done in
such cases as were under debate.

Encolpius, whom he used as a most familiar friend, related that if he ever saw
a thieving judge he had a finger ready to gouge out the man's eye; so great a
hatred held him of those about whom it had been proved before him that they
had been thieves. Septimius, who treated his life not moderately, adds that
Alexander had such a bile against those judges who suffered from a rumor of
thefts, even if they had not been condemned, that if he saw them by some
chance, in a stirring of mind he would vomit bile of the stomach, his whole
face in-ardescente, so that he could say nothing. For when a certain Septimius
Arabianus, infamous on a charge of thefts and already freed under
Heliogabalus, had come among the senators to greet the prince, he cried: /O
Marna, O Jupiter, O immortal gods, Arabianus not only lives, but even comes
into the senate, perhaps he even hopes something of me; does he judge me so
foolish, so stupid?/ He was greeted, however, by name, that is /Hail,
Alexander./

If anyone bent his head or said something too bland, as a flatterer, he was
either thrown out, if the quality of his place allowed, or laughed at with a
huge guffaw, if his dignity could not be subjected to a heavier injury. When
greeted he offered a seat to all senators, and indeed he admitted to the

He also forbade himself to be adored, when Heliogabalus had already begun to
be adored in the manner of the kings of the Persians. He had besides this
opinion, that only thieves complain of poverty, while they wish to cover the
crimes of their life. He also added a known saying about thieves, and in Greek

He appointed a prefect of the guard for himself by authority of the senate. He
received a prefect of the city from the senate. He made a second prefect of
the guard, a man who had even fled lest he be made so, saying that the
unwilling, not those who canvassed, should be placed in the state. He never
made a senator without the counsel of all the senators who were present, so
that he was created through the opinions of all; the highest men gave
testimony, and if the witnesses or those who gave opinions had deceived, they
were afterward thrown back to the last place of citizens, with a condemnation
applied, as men proved guilty of falsehood, without any offer of indulgence.
He also made senators only on the vote of the highest men in the Palace,
saying that he who made a senator ought to be a great man. He never raised
freedmen to equestrian rank, asserting that the equestrian rank is the nursery
of senators.

He was of such moderation that no one was ever moved from his side, that he
showed himself bland and affable to all, that he visited friends who were ill,
not only of the first or second place but even the lower, that he wished all
to say freely to him what they felt, and when it was said he heard it, and
when he had heard he amended and corrected as the matter demanded; if
something had been done less well, he would even himself convict it, and that
without arrogance and without bitterness of heart. He always offered a seat to
all except those whom a denser rumor of thefts had grazed. He always asked
after the absent. In short, when both his mother Mamaea and his wife Memmia,
daughter of Sulpicius a man of consular rank, granddaughter of Catulus, often
cast his too great civility at him and said, /You have made your power softer
and the empire more contemptible,/ he answered, /But securi-orem and more
lasting./ In short no day passed when he did not do something gentle, civil,
pious, yet so as not to overthrow the treasury.

He ordered condemnations to be very rare 4, but those that had been made he
did not remit. He assigned the taxes of cities to their own buildings. He
worked a public interest of one third, so that to most of the poor he gave
money without interest for buying fields, to be repaid from the fruits.

He added senatorial rank to his prefects of the guard, so that they both were
and were called Most Distinguished Men. This before had been rare or had not
been at all for a long time, to the point that if any of the emperors wished
to give a successor to a prefect of the guard, he would send him the broad
stripe through a freedman, as Marius Maximus said in the life of many. But
Alexander wished the prefects of the guard to be senators for this reason,
that no one not a senator should judge a Roman senator.

He knew his soldiers so everywhere that he had in his bedroom briefs
containing both the number and the times of those serving, and always, when he
was alone, he reviewed their accounts and number and ranks and pay, so that he
might be most instructed for all things. In short, when something was done
among the military, he would speak many men's names; he also noted for himself
those to be promoted and read through all the slips, and so he did, with the
days likewise noted and who had been advanced on whose introduction.

He so aided the grain-supply of the Roman people that, when Heliogabalus had
ruined the grain, he bought it with his own

skilled in law; Aelius Gordianus, parent of the emperor Gordian, a
distinguished man; Julius Paulus, most skilled in law; Claudius Venacus, a
most ample orator 3; Catilius Severus, his kinsman, the most learned man of
all; Aelius Serenianus, the holiest man of all; Quintilius Marcellus, than
whom not even the histories contain a better. With these so many and other
such men, what evil could be thought or done, when they consented to the good?
And these indeed the cohort of bad men had driven off, which had circumvented
Alexander in the first days, but by the prudence of the youth, those men being
killed and driven off, that holy friendship also grew strong. These are they
who made a good prince of a Syrian, and likewise bad friends, who have handed
down even to later men the worst Romans, laboring in their own vices.
