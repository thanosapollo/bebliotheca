* On the Nature of Man
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:
A summary discourse of Nemesius bishop of Emesa on the nature of man 1 That
man, constructed in the best way from an intelligent soul and a body, and so
well that it was not possible for him to have been constituted otherwise, has
seemed good to many excellent men. And since the soul is called intelligent,
this having an ambiguity whether intellect coming to the soul as one thing to
another made it intelligent, or whether the soul possesses the intelligent
from itself and by nature and this is its fairest part as an eye in a body,
some, among whom is also Plotinus, having dogmatized that the soul is one
thing and the intellect another, want man to be composed of three, body and
soul and intellect; and Apollinarius who became bishop of Laodicea followed
them, for having laid this as the foundation of his own opinion he also built
the rest on according to his proper dogma. But some have not distinguished the
intellect from the soul, but hold that the intelligent is the ruling part of
its substance. And Aristotle holds that the potential intellect was
constructed together with man, but that the intellect in actuality enters us
from without, contributing not to the being and existence of man but to
progress in the knowledge and contemplation of natural things. He affirms at
any rate that altogether few of men, and only those who have philosophized,
have the intellect in actuality. And Plato does not seem to say that man is
the compound, soul and body, but a soul using a body of such a kind, imagining
the things concerning man more worthily and turning us at once to the divinity
and care of the soul alone, in order that believing ourselves to be the soul
we may pursue only the goods of the soul, the virtues and piety, and may not
love the desires of the body, as not being of a man insofar as he is man, but
of an animal primarily, and of a man secondarily, since man is also an animal.
And otherwise it is confessed by all men that the soul is more sovereign than
the body, for from it the body is moved as an instrument. And death makes this
clear: for when the soul has been separated the body remains wholly unmoved
and inactive, as when a craftsman has been separated the instruments remain
unmoved. And it is well known that he also has community with the inanimate
and shares the life of the irrational animals and has partaken of the
intellection of the rational. For he has community with the inanimate
according to the body and the blend from the four elements; with plants
according to these and also the nutritive and seminal power; with the
irrational also in these, and besides according to the movement by impulse and
according to appetite and spiritedness and the sensitive and respiratory
power, for all these are common to men and to the irrational, even if not all
to all. And through the rational he is joined to the incorporeal and
intelligent natures, reasoning and thinking and judging each thing and
pursuing the virtues and embracing piety, the summit of the virtues. Therefore
he is as it were on the borders of intelligible and sensible substance, joined
according to the body and the bodily powers to the other animals and to the
inanimate, and according to the rational to the incorporeal substances, as was
said before. For the Creator seems to join the different natures to one
another little by little, so that the whole creation is one and akin. From
which it is especially shown that there is one Creator of all the things that
are; for he not only unified the existence of the particular individuals, but
also fitted each to the others fittingly. For just as in each of the animals
he unified the insensible with the sensitive, bone and fat and hairs and



the other insensible things with the sensitive nerves, and made the animal
composed of insensible and sensitive, and not only composed but also showed it
to be one; so also he has done in the rest of creation according to kind,
joining them to one another by the gradual kinship and variation of nature, so
that the wholly inanimate may not stand far apart from the plants that have
the nutritive power, nor these in turn from the irrational and sensitive
animals, nor indeed the irrational be wholly alienated from the rational and
incompatible and without some connatural and physical bond. For even stone
differs from stone by some power, but the magnet-stone seems to have gone out
beyond the nature and power of the other stones in openly drawing to itself
and holding the iron, as if wishing to make it food for itself, and not only
doing this upon one iron but also holding another through another by imparting
its own power to all that are held; at any rate the iron also holds iron when
it is held by the magnet. Then again next, passing from plants to animals, he
did not rush all at once upon the locomotive and sensitive nature, but little
by little advanced to this in a well-tuned way; for he constructed pinnae and
sea-nettles as it were sensitive trees (for he rooted them in the sea after
the manner of plants and put shells around them like wood and set them as
plants, but he gave them the tactile sense, the sense common to all animals),
so as to have community with plants according to being rooted and standing,
and with animals according to touch. Aristotle at any rate recorded that the
sponge, although grown onto the rocks, both contracts and defends itself when
it perceives something approaching; therefore the ancient wise men are
accustomed to call all such things zoophytes. And again he joined to the
pinnae and such things the generation of animals that are locomotive but not
able to go far, moving just there from the spot; such are most of the
shell-skinned and the so-called earth-guts. Then thus adding in part to some
more senses, to others locomotion to the greatest extent, he arrived at the
more perfect of the irrational animals; and I mean by more perfect those that
have all the senses and are able to go far. And again passing from the
irrational to the rational animal, man, he did not construct even this all at
once but first put into the other animals also certain natural understandings
and devices and cunning for preservation, so that they appear near to the
rational, and thus he brought forth the truly rational animal, man. In the
same way also concerning voice you will find if you inquire that from the
simple and uniform utterance of horses and oxen it was led on in part to the
varied and diverse voice of ravens and mimicking birds until it ended in the
articulate and perfect voice of man; and again he attached articulate speech
to thought and reasoning, making it a messenger of the movements according to
intellect. And thus he fitted all things to all musically and bound them and
gathered into one both the intelligible and the visible through the middle
generation of men. Therefore also Moses, setting out the creation, well said
that man had been generated last, not only because, all things having come to
be for him, it was consequent that first the things for his use be prepared
and thus he who would use them be brought forth, but because, intelligible
substance having come to be and again visible, there needed to be also some
bond of both, in order that the all might be one and sympathetic with itself
and not alien from itself. The animal man therefore became that which binds
both natures. Such then, to speak briefly, are the things of the wisdom of the
Creator. Man then having been stationed on the borders of the irrational and
rational nature, if he inclines toward the body and loves the things of the
body more, he embraces the life of the irrational and will be numbered with
them and will be called earthy according to Paul and will hear: you are earth
and to earth you shall go, and: he was compared to the senseless cattle and
was made like them; but if toward the



rational he proceeds, having despised all bodily pleasures, he pursues the
divine and most God-beloved life and that which is primarily as of a man, and
he will be such as the heavenly according to what was said: as is the earthy
such also are the earthy, and as is the heavenly such also are the heavenly.
And the chief point of the rational nature is to flee and turn away from
evils, and to pursue and choose goods. And of goods some are according to
community of soul and body, these also having their reference to the soul, as
of a soul using a body in addition, as the virtues; others are of the soul
itself by itself alone not needing a body, as piety and the contemplation of
beings. Those then who choose to live the life of a man as a man and not as an
animal only pursue the virtues and piety. And what the things of the virtues
are and what those of piety will be distinguished in what follows when we give
the account concerning soul and body; for while it is not yet known what in
substance our soul is, it is not consequent to treat of its activity. But the
Hebrews say that man was generated from the beginning neither admittedly
mortal nor immortal but on the borders of each nature, in order that if he
should follow the bodily passions he might fall into them and into the bodily
changes, but if he should prefer the goods of the soul he might be deemed
worthy of immortality. For if God had made him mortal from the beginning, he
would not have condemned him to death when he sinned, for no one condemns the
mortal to mortality; but if again immortal, he would not have constructed him
in need of food (for none of the immortals needs bodily food) nor would he so
easily have changed his mind and immediately made mortal him who had become
immortal, for he does not appear to have done this in the case of the angels
who sinned, but they remained immortal according to their nature from the
beginning, awaiting another penalty of the things they had sinned but not
death. Better then either to understand the matter at hand in this way, or
that he was constructed mortal, but able by being perfected from progress to
become immortal, that is potentially immortal. And since it was not
advantageous for him to know his own nature before perfection, he forbade him
to taste of the tree of knowledge. For there were, or rather there still are
even now, very great powers in plants; but then as at the beginning of the
world-making, being unmixed, they had the strongest activity. There was then
also a tasting of a certain fruit that produced knowledge of one's own nature;
but God did not wish him to know his own nature before perfection, lest
knowing himself to be in need of many things he should care for the bodily
need, leaving the providence of the soul, and for this cause he prevented him
from partaking of the fruit of knowledge. But having disobeyed and known
himself he fell from perfection, and became of the bodily need; at any rate he
immediately sought covering. For Moses says that he knew that he is naked. But
previously he had made him in ecstasy and in ignorance of himself. Having
fallen then from perfection he likewise fell also from immortality, which he
will later receive again by the grace of him who made him. And after the fall
the enjoyment of meats was also conceded. For previously he commanded him to
be content with the things from the earth only (for these were in paradise);
but when perfection had been despaired of, by condescension the things of
enjoyment were thereafter conceded. And since man is of body, and every body
has been composed of the four elements, he must necessarily fall into these
affections which the elements also do, cutting and change and flux, which
belong only to the body; change according to quality, and flux according to
emptying, for the animal is always emptied both through the evident pores and
through the unevident, concerning which we shall speak later. It is necessary
then either that equals be brought in against what is emptied or that the
animal be dissolved by lack of what comes in. And since what is emptied is of
dry and wet and of breath, the animal must need both dry and wet food and
breath. And our food and drink is through the



elements from which we also were composed. For each thing is nourished by what
is proper and like, and is healed by the contrary. And of the elements some we
take both immediately, and some also through certain intermediates, as water
sometimes by itself and sometimes also through the medium of wine and oil and
all the so-called wet fruits (for wine is nothing other than water qualitied
by a vine). And likewise we also partake of fire, sometimes being warmed by it
immediately and sometimes through the intermediates of what we eat or drink
(for in all things a greater or lesser portion of fire has been sown in); in
the same way also of air, immediately by breathing and having it poured around
us and drawing it in eating and drinking, and through the intermediates of all
the other things we take; but earth we take immediately in no way, but through
certain intermediates (for earth becomes grain, and we eat grain); for larks
and doves often and partridges feed on earth; but man through the
intermediates of seeds and tree-fruits and fleshes. And since not only for
seemliness but also for the fineness of sense according to touch, in which man
most of all surpasses all the animals, he did not put around us either a thick
skin as on oxen and the other thick-skinned, or large and dense hairs as on
goats and sheep and hares, or scales as on snakes and fishes, or shells as on
tortoises and oysters, or soft shells as on crayfish, or wings as on birds, we
necessarily needed clothing, which fills up in us what nature granted to the
others. For these reasons then we need food and clothing; and dwelling both
for these reasons for which we needed clothing and food, and not least also
for escapes from wild beasts; and because of the bad blends of the qualities
and the loosening of the continuity of the body we have come to be in need of
physicians and healing; and since the change comes about according to quality,
it is necessary through the opposite quality to bring the condition of the
body to the commensurate. For it is not, as some think, the task set before
physicians to cool down the heated body, but to shift it to the well-blended.
For if they should cool it down, the condition is turned about into the
contrary disease. Need then for man is of food and drink, because of the
emptyings and dissipations; of clothing, because he has from nature no strong
covering; of a house, both because of the bad blends of the surrounding air
and because of the wild beasts; and of healing, because of the changes of the
qualities and the sense given into the body. For if sense were not present to
us we would neither feel pain nor, not feeling pain, would we need healing,
and we would have been destroyed in ignorance of the evil, not curing the
affection. And because of the arts and the sciences and the needs from these
we need one another. And because of needing one another, many coming together
to the same place share with one another according to the needs of life in
transactions, which gathering and dwelling together they named a city, in
order that



from nearby and not from far we may reap the benefits from one another. For by
nature man has come to be a gregarious and political animal. For no one is
self-sufficient to himself for all things. It is clear then that cities were
constituted because of transactions and lessons. And of two of these
privileges man obtained exceptional ones. For he alone obtains forgiveness
when he repents, and the body of this one alone, being mortal, is made
immortal, and of the bodily, because of the soul, and of the psychic, because
of the body. For man alone of the rational had as exceptional to be deemed
worthy of forgiveness in repenting. For neither daemons nor angels, when they
repent, are deemed worthy of forgiveness. And in this especially God is shown
and said to be both just and merciful. For to the angels, who have no
compulsion dragging them around into sin, but are free by nature from bodily
passions and needs and pleasures, reasonably no forgiveness is given when they
repent; but man is not only rational, but also an animal. And the needs of the
animal and the passions often drag around the reasoning. When then having come
to himself he flees these and proceeds to the virtues, he obtains just mercy,
forgiveness. And just as the capacity to laugh is proper to his substance,
since it belongs also to this one alone and to every one and always; so among
the things according to grace it is proper to man beside all the rational
creation to strip off through repentance the charges of the things previously
sinned. For this has been granted to man alone and to every one and always
during the time of the life in the world; but after death no longer. And thus
some also want the angels no longer after the fall to obtain the forgiveness
from repentance; for their death is the fall; but before the fall, according
to the likeness of the life of men, they too were deemed worthy of
forgiveness; which not having done, they have thereafter the fitting penalty
of the punishment unforgiven and everlasting. It is clear then from these
things that those who do not accept repentance circumscribe the exceptional
gift and that which is proper to man. And it is proper to him and exceptional
also that of the other animals his body alone rises after death and proceeds
to immortality; and he obtains this because of the immortality of the soul, as
he obtained that because of the weakness and many-passions of the body. And
proper to him also are the lessons of the arts and sciences, and the
activities according to these arts. Therefore they also define man as a
rational animal, mortal, receptive of intellect and science; animal because
man too is an ensouled sensitive substance. For this is the definition of an
animal. And rational, in order that he may be separated from the irrational;
and mortal, in order that he may be separated from the immortal rational. And
receptive of intellect and science, because the arts and sciences come to us
through learning, having a receptive power both of intellect and of the arts,
but acquiring the activity from the lessons. And they say that this was added
to the definition later. For the definition is sound even without this; but
since some introduce nymphs and certain other kinds of daemons, long-lived but
not immortal, in order that they might distinguish man also from these they
added the receptive of intellect and science. For none of those learns but by
nature knows what it knows. And it is a doctrine of the Hebrews that this
whole has come to be because of man; immediately because of him such as beasts
of burden and oxen for farming, and grass because of these. For of the things
that have come to be some have come to be for themselves, and some for others.
For themselves all the rational; for others both the irrational and the
inanimate. And if these have come to be for others, let us consider for what
kind they have come to be. Is it then for angels? But no one of sound mind
would say that they have come to be for angels. For the things that have come
to be for others have come to be for the constitution and continuance and ease
of those. For either for the succession of the kind or for food or covering or
healing or passing of time and rest. But an angel needs none of these; for he
neither has succession of kind nor is he in need of bodily food nor of
covering nor of the others. And if not an angel, it is clear that neither any
other nature that has gone up above the angel; for by how much it is superior,
by so much more also the being without need belongs to it. One must then seek
a nature that is rational but in need of the things said before. What other
such then would appear if we leave man aside? Therefore it is gathered that
the irrational and the inanimate have come to be because of him. Since then
they have come to be because of him as was shown, for this reason he was also
set as ruler of them. And the work of a ruler is to use those ruled according
to the measure of need and not to outrage wantonly toward luxury nor to deal
burdensomely and heavily with those ruled. Those then sin who do not use the
irrational well, for they do not do the work of a ruler nor of a just man
according to what is written: a just man pities the souls of his cattle. But
perhaps someone will say that nothing is for another, but each has come to be
for itself. Therefore first separating the ensouled from the inanimate let us
see if the inanimate can have come to be for themselves. For if these are for
themselves, how or from where will the animals be fed? For we see nature
supplying to the animals foods from the earth of fruits and plants except the
fewest, the flesh-eaters;



and even these flesh-eaters themselves make food of the animals that graze the
earth, as wolves and lions lambs and goats and swine and deer, and eagles
partridges and wood-pigeons and hares and the like, which graze the fruits of
the earth. For even the nature of fishes, being one that eats one another,
does not extend through all eating flesh, but ended in those that graze
seaweeds and certain other things growing in the water. For if all the kinds
of fishes were flesh-eating, and there were nothing fleeing the feeding on
flesh, it would not have sufficed even for a little, but would have been
destroyed, some by one another, and some through lack of food. And in order
that this may not happen, some of the fishes were constructed abstaining from
fleshes, grazing the, as one might say, marine pasture, in order that from
these the others also may be preserved. For the seaweeds become food of these,
and those of the others, and again these of the others, so that because of the
food of the last, uninterruptedly supplied from the earthy according to the
sea, the subsistence of the others also is preserved. The argument then has
shown that the generation of plants has come to be not for itself, but for
food and constitution of men and of the other animals. And if these are
because of man and the animals, it is clear that also the causes of their
growth and generation have come to be because of these. Therefore the motions
of stars and heaven and seasons and rains and all such things have come to be
because of these, in order that, the foods being supplied continuously as in a
circle, the nature also of those that bring forth the fruits may remain
without lack; so that these are found to be because of the fruits, and the
fruits because of the animals and man. It remains to examine further whether
the nature of the irrational also has come to be for itself or because of man.
But perhaps it is absurd to say that things without a share of thought and
living according to natural impulse alone and bent down toward the earth
below, and showing slavery through their form, have been brought forth for
themselves. And since there are many things that can be said to this, and they
almost need a writing of their own because of the multitude, the present
subject not admitting the length of the arguments, it is fitting to arrive at
what is brief but timely. If then we should also mirror the things of those
outside in man as in an image, we would be making the demonstrations from the
very substance of the things sought. We see then in the soul according to us
the irrationality and its parts, I mean appetite and spiritedness, given for
service to the rational, and the one ruling and the others ruled, and the one
commanding and the others being commanded and serving the needs which reason
may propose, when man preserves what is according to nature. And if the
rational in us rules the irrational in us, how is it not likely that it also
master the irrational outside and that they have been given for its needs? For
the irrational has been ordered by nature to be served to the rational, as was
shown in the things according to us. And this is shown also by the
construction of the many animals, having come to be suitable for service to
man, oxen and all the beasts of burden for farming and carrying loads, and
most of the winged and of those in water and of those on land for enjoyment,
and the mimicking for delight and ease. And if not all serve such needs, but
some also harm man; one must know that primarily of those that came to be for
service all the other possible things also have been constructed in order that
nothing of the things that can come to be may be lacking to creation. Nor have
even these wholly escaped the benefit of men, but reason also reaps the
harmful for proper help. For it uses them for healing of the harm from those
very ones and for the cure of the other illnesses. Such are certain of the
so-called theriac preparations which reason devised in order that it may
master even these through themselves, and as from enemies that have been
mastered may be helped, and man has countless counteracting powers of these
given by the Creator able to check and defend and set right their plots. For
different ones fit different needs; and in common all


are by nature fitted to contribute to the healing of man, even those not
useful for the other needs. And let these things be said as toward the present
condition of the life according to us; since of old none of the other animals
dared to harm man; but all things were slaves to him and subjected and
obedient so long as he mastered his own passions and the irrationality in him;
but not mastering his own passions but being mastered by them, he was
reasonably mastered also by the wild beasts from outside. For with the sin the
harm from these also entered in. And that this is true is clear from those who
have pursued the best life. For these were seen incontestably stronger than
the plot of the wild beasts, as Daniel of lions, and Paul of the bite of the
viper. Who then would worthily wonder at the nobility of this animal, which
binds in itself the mortal to the immortal and joins the rational to the
irrational, which bears in its own nature the image of the whole creation, for
which reasons it has also been called a little world, which has been deemed
worthy of so great providence from God, for whom are all things both the
present and the future, for whom also God became man, which ends in
incorruption and flees the mortal; he reigns over heavens, having come to be
according to the image and likeness of God, he lives with Christ, he is a
child of God, he sits before every rule and authority? And who would be able
to tell out the advantages of this animal? He crosses seas, he walks in heaven
by contemplation, he understands the motion and intervals and measures of
stars, he reaps earth and sea, he despises wild beasts and sea-monsters, he
accomplishes every science and art and method, he converses through letters
with those he wishes beyond the borders, in no way hindered by the body, he
prophesies the things to come. He rules all, he masters all, he enjoys all, he
converses with angels and God, he commands creation, he orders daemons, he
searches out the nature of the things that are, he inquires into God, he
becomes house and temple of God; and all these he buys with the virtues and
piety. But lest we should seem to some to write inartistically an encomium of
man and not only to set out the nature as we proposed, let us somewhere here
stop the account, even if most of all in speaking the advantages of the nature
we are narrating the nature itself. Knowing then of how great nobility we have
partaken and that we are a heavenly plant, let us not shame the nature nor be
shown unworthy of such gifts nor deprive ourselves of so great authority and
glory and blessedness, betraying for a little time and a brief pleasure the
enjoyment of all the eternal things; but through good works and abstinence
from the base and the right aim with which the Divine is especially accustomed
to cooperate and through prayers let us preserve the nobility. Concerning
these things then even these are enough. And since the common account suggests
that man is from soul and body, come, having divided, let us first treat of
soul, leaving aside the things too subtle and hard and hard for the many to
understand of the things sought. 2 On the soul The account concerning the soul
is almost in disagreement among all the ancients. For Democritus and Epicurus
and the whole system of the Stoic philosophers declare the soul to be a body.
And even these themselves, who declare the soul to be a body, differ
concerning its substance. For the Stoics say it is a warm and fiery breath,
Critias blood, Hippon the philosopher water, Democritus fire. For the
spherical shapes of the atoms being combined, fire and air, complete a soul.
And Heraclitus says that the soul of the all is an exhalation from the wets,
and that the one in animals is by nature of the same kind from both the
exhalation from outside and that in them. And again even of those who say the
soul is incorporeal the disagreement has become boundless, some saying it is a
substance and immortal, others incorporeal but not a substance nor immortal.
For Thales first said the soul is ever-moving and self-moving; and Pythagoras
a number moving itself.


And Plato an intelligible substance movable from itself according to a
harmonic number; and Aristotle a first entelechy of a natural body, organic,
having life potentially. And Dinarchus a harmony of the four elements, instead
of the blend and concord of the elements. For he wants to speak not of that
which is constituted from sounds, but of the harmonic blend and concord in the
body of hots and colds and wets and dries. And it is clear that of these the
others say the soul is a substance; but Aristotle and Dinarchus substanceless.
And still besides these some have thought there is one and the same soul of
all, being chopped into the particulars, and again coming together into
itself, as the Manichaeans and certain others; and some many and different
according to kind; and some both one and many. There is every necessity then
to lengthen the account, contradicting so many opinions. In common then
against all who say the soul is a body the things said by Ammonius the teacher
of Plotinus and by Numenius the Pythagorean will suffice. And they are these.
Bodies, being by their proper nature changeable and scatterable and throughout
cuttable to infinity, nothing unchangeable being left in them, need that which
composes and gathers and as it were binds together and holds them, which we
call soul. If then the soul is a body of whatever kind even if most
fine-parted, what again is that which holds that together? For it was shown
that every body needs that which holds it together, and thus to infinity,
until we arrive at the incorporeal. And if they should say, as the Stoics,
that there is a certain tonic motion about the bodies moving at once inward
and outward, and that the one outward is productive of magnitudes and
qualities, and the one inward of unity and substance, one must ask them, since
every motion is from some power, what this power is and in what it is
substantiated. If then the power also is some matter, we shall use the same
arguments again; but if it is not matter but enmattered (and the enmattered is
other than the matter, for that which partakes of matter is called enmattered)
what then is that which partakes of the matter, whether it too is matter or
immaterial? If then matter, how enmattered and not matter? But if not matter,
then immaterial; and if immaterial, not a body, for every body is enmattered.
And if they should say that bodies are three-dimensional, and the soul going
through the whole of the body is three-dimensional and therefore wholly also a
body, we shall say that every body is three-dimensional, but not everything
three-dimensional is a body; for even place and the qualitative, being
incorporeal in themselves, are quantified in bulk accidentally. Thus then also
to the soul in itself belongs the without-dimension, but accidentally, with
the three-dimensional in which it is, it too is contemplated as
three-dimensional. Further every body is moved either from outside or from
inside; but if from outside, it will be soulless; if from inside, ensouled.
And if the soul is a body, if it should be moved from outside, it is soulless;
if from inside, ensouled; but it is absurd both to call the soul soulless and
ensouled; therefore the soul is not a body. Further the soul, if it is
nourished, is nourished by the incorporeal, for lessons nourish it; but no
body is nourished by the incorporeal; therefore the soul is not a body.
(Xenocrates inferred thus; but if it is not nourished, and every body of an
animal is nourished, the soul is not a body.) And these things in common
against all who say the soul is a body. And particularly against those who
hold the soul to be blood or breath, since when the breath or the blood is
separated the animal is made dead, one must not say that which some of those
who think there is something have written, saying: therefore when a part of
the blood flows away a part of the soul has flowed away; for such a thing is
light-talk; for in the case of the homoeomerous even the remaining part is the
same as the whole; water at any rate, both the much and the little, is the
same, and likewise silver and gold and all things whose parts do not differ
from one another according to substance; thus then also the remaining blood,
however much it may be, is soul, if indeed the blood is soul. That then rather
must be said, that if that is soul upon whose removal the animal dies, then
wholly also phlegm is soul


and both the biles. For whichever of these may fail, it brings an end to the
life. And further also the liver and the brain and the heart and the stomach
and the kidneys and the intestines and many other things. For of which of
these, having been removed, will the animal not have died? And otherwise there
are many things bloodless but ensouled, as the cartilaginous fishes and
molluscs, such as cuttlefish and squids and smyli and all the shell-skinned
and soft-shelled, as crayfish and crabs and lobsters. If then there are things
ensouled but bloodless, it is clear that the soul is not blood. And against
those who say the soul is water, because water appears to generate life in all
things, and without water it is impossible to live, many things contradict.
For it is not possible to live without food either; therefore according to
them one must call all the foods also soul, each one. Then many of the animals
do not drink, as has been recorded of certain eagles. And a partridge also can
live without drinking. And why rather is water soul, and not air? For of water
it is possible to abstain even for long; but of breathing the air not even the
least; but neither is air soul. For there are many things living but not
breathing the air, as all the insects, such as bees and wasps and ants, and
the bloodless, and most of those of the sea, and all that do not have a lung.
For nothing that does not have a lung breathes the air; and it converts,
nothing that does not breathe the air has a lung. And since also of Cleanthes
the Stoic and of Chrysippus certain arguments not to be despised are current,
one must set out the solutions of these also as those from Plato solved them.
Cleanthes weaves a syllogism of such a kind. Not only, he says, do we become
like the parents according to the body, but also according to the soul, in the
passions, the characters, the dispositions; but the like and the unlike belong
to a body and not to an incorporeal; therefore the soul is a body. But first
the universals are not constructed together from the particulars. And besides
this also the and not of an incorporeal is false. For we say numbers are like
whose sides are proportional, as six and twenty-four. For the sides of six are
two and three; of twenty-four four and six. And two has analogy to four, and
three to six. For it is contemplated in a double ratio. For four are double of
two and six of three; but numbers are incorporeal. And figures also are like
figures as many as have the angles equal and the sides about the equal angles
proportional; and they themselves confess the figure to be incorporeal.
Further as of quantity the equal and unequal is proper, so also of quality the
like and the unlike; but quality is incorporeal. There is then also an
incorporeal like an incorporeal. Further he says: nothing incorporeal suffers
with a body, nor a body with an incorporeal; but the soul suffers with the
body when it is sick and cut and the body with the soul; at any rate when it
is ashamed it becomes red and when it is afraid pale; therefore the soul is a
body. But the other of the premises is false, lying according to additional
assumption, the one saying nothing incorporeal suffers with a body. For what
if this belongs to the soul alone? As if someone should say nothing animal
moves the upper jaw; but the crocodile moves the upper jaw; therefore the
crocodile is not an animal. For the proposition is false here too according to
additional assumption, the one saying nothing animal moves the upper jaw. For
look, the crocodile is both an animal and moves the upper jaw. And in the same
way also when he says that nothing incorporeal suffers with a body. For he
assumes in addition what is sought by the denial. And if someone should
suppose it true that nothing incorporeal suffers with a body; still what is
brought on is not agreed, that the soul suffers with the body when it is sick
and cut. For it is disputed whether the body alone is that which feels pain,
having received sense from the soul, while it itself remains unaffected, or
whether it also feels pain with the body; and among the more reputable the
former rather prevails; and one must not infer syllogisms from the disputed
but from the agreed. And from surplus it is shown also that certain
incorporeals suffer with the bodies; the qualities at any rate, being
incorporeal, when the bodies suffer, themselves also suffer with them, both in
the


corruption and in the generation, being altered together with the body. And
Chrysippus says: death is a separation of soul from body; but nothing
incorporeal is separated from a body; for neither does an incorporeal touch a
body; but the soul both touches and is separated from the body; therefore the
soul is not incorporeal. Of these, that death is a separation of soul from
body is true; but that an incorporeal does not touch a body is false when said
universally, but true in the case of the soul; false because even the line,
being incorporeal, touches a body and is separated, and likewise also
whiteness, but true in the case of the soul. For the soul does not touch the
body; for if it touches, clearly it lies beside it; and if this, it does not
lie beside the whole; for it is impossible for a body to lie beside a body,
whole beside whole; and thus the animal will not be wholly ensouled. So that
if it touches, the soul will be a body, but the animal will not be wholly
ensouled; but if it is wholly ensouled, the soul neither touches nor is a
body; and the animal is wholly ensouled; therefore it neither touches nor is a
body, and it is separated being incorporeal. That then the soul is not a body
is clear from what has been said; and that it is not substanceless must be
said next. And since Dinarchus also defined the soul as a harmony and Simmias
contradicting Socrates said the soul is a harmony, saying that the soul is
like a harmony and the body a lyre, one must set out the solutions of this
that lie in the Phaedo of Plato. One then is that from the things previously
demonstrated by him. For it has been previously demonstrated that learnings
are recollections. Taking this then as agreed he constructs the argument thus.
If learnings are recollections our soul was before it came to be in the human
form; but if it is a harmony it was not previously but came to be later when
the body was attuned. For every composition is not otherwise than as those
things from which it is composed are. For the composition, being a harmony, is
a certain community of the things composed; and nothing prevents it from not
preceding those from which it was composed, but following them; that the soul
is a harmony then fights with learnings being recollections; but that of the
recollections is true; therefore it is false that the soul is a harmony.
Further the soul both opposes the body and holds the ruling account, inasmuch
as it rules it; but a harmony neither leads nor opposes; therefore the soul is
not a harmony. Further a harmony is more and less a harmony than a harmony by
being slackened and tightened (not by the ratio of the harmony; for it is
impossible for a ratio to have the more and less; but in the fitting; for if a
high sound and a low having been blended are then let go, they preserve the
same ratio in the magnitudes of the sounds, but the harmony takes a variation
according to the fitting being more and less tightened); but soul is not more
and less than soul; therefore the soul is not a harmony. Further the soul
admits virtue and vice; but harmony does not admit harmony and disharmony;
therefore the soul is not a harmony. Further the soul, by admitting the
contraries in turn, is a substance and a subject; but harmony is a quality and
in a subject; and substance is other than quality; and the soul therefore is
other than a harmony. Yet it is nothing absurd for the soul to partake of
harmony, but it is not on this account a harmony; for neither because it
partakes of virtue is the soul virtue. And Galen declares nothing but even
testifies in the demonstrative accounts that he has declared nothing
concerning soul; but he seems from what he says to approve rather that the
soul is a blend; for to this the difference of characters follows;
constructing the argument from the things of Hippocrates. And if this, it is
clear that he also thinks it to be mortal; not all, but only the irrational
soul of man. And concerning the rational he is in doubt, speaking thus; but
that the blend of the body cannot be soul is clear from here. Every body both
ensouled and soulless has been blended from the four elements. For the blend
of these works the bodies. If then the soul is the blend of the body, nothing
will be soulless. And the argument is inferred thus: if the blend


of the body is soul, and every body has a blend, every body has a soul; and if
every body has a soul, no body is soulless; therefore neither stone nor wood
nor iron nor any other thing will be soulless. And if he should not say
universally that every blend of a body is soul but such and such a one, one
must ask what kind of blend it is that makes an animal and holds the account
of soul; for whatever kind of blend he should say, we shall find such also in
the soulless. For there being nine blends as he himself showed in On Blends,
eight of the badly blended and one of the well-blended, he says man has been
blended according to the well-blended, not all but the one of the middle
blend, and according to the other bad blends the other animals according to
kind with the slackening and tightening toward the more and less; and the nine
blends are found also in the soulless with the more and less, as he himself
again demonstrated in the simple things. Further if the soul is a blend, and
blends change according to both the ages and the seasons and the diets, the
soul changes; and if it changes, we do not have the same soul, but according
to the blend sometimes of a lion sometimes of a sheep, sometimes of some
other, which is absurd. Further the blend does not oppose the desires of the
body, but even cooperates; for it itself is that which moves; but the soul
opposes; therefore the blend is not soul. Further if the blend is the soul,
and the blend is a quality, and a quality both comes to be and passes away
without the corruption of the subject, the soul also then will be separated
without the corruption of the subject; but this is not true; therefore the
soul is not a blend, nor a quality. For they will not surely say that the
other of the opposites belongs naturally to the animal as the hot to fire. For
this is unchangeable. But the blend appears being changed, or rather they
themselves are those who through the medical art change the blends. Further
the qualities of every body are sensible; but the soul is not sensible but
intelligible; therefore the soul is not a quality of a body. Further the
well-blend of body and breath with juxtaposition of fleshes and nerves and the
others is strength; and the well-blend of hots and colds and dries and wets is
health; and the symmetry of the limbs with the good complexion makes the
beauty of the body; if then the harmony of health and of strength and of
beauty is soul, it was necessary that the living man neither be sick nor weak
nor shapeless; but it happens often that not one only but together these three
well-blends are lost and the man lives; for it happens that the same one
together is both shapeless and weak and sick; therefore the well-blend of the
body is not the soul. How then do certain natural vices and virtues follow
men? This comes to be truly from the blend of the body. For as they are
naturally healthy and sickly from the blend; so some being naturally
bitter-biled are irascible, others cowardly, others given to appetite. But
some master and prevail; and it is clear that they master the blend; and that
which masters is other and that which is mastered other; therefore blend is
other and soul other. For the body being an instrument of the soul, if it is
constructed suitably, cooperates with the soul and itself has suitably; but if
unsuitably, it hinders, and then there is need for the soul of dealings
fighting against the unsuitability of the instrument; and if it does not very
much come to itself, it is also distorted together with it just as a musician
sins together with the distortion of the lyre if he does not first set it
well. Therefore also there is need for the soul of the care of the body in
order that it may fit it as a suitable instrument for itself. And this it does
through both reason and the characters, as in a harmony slackening some things
and tightening others, in order that it may construct it harmonious for itself
and use it as a suitable instrument, unless it itself also is distorted
together with it; for this also happens. And Aristotle saying the soul is an
entelechy no less agrees with those who say it is a quality. But let us first
make clear what he calls entelechy. He says substance in three ways, the one
as matter a subject, which in itself is nothing, but has a power toward
generation; another form and kind, according to which


the matter is given form; and third the compound, that which has come to be
from the matter and the form, which is thereafter ensouled. The matter then is
power, and the form entelechy. And this too in two ways: the one as science,
the other as contemplating according to science, that is the one as
disposition and the other as activity. As science, because in the soul's
existing there is both sleep and waking; and analogously waking is to
contemplating, and sleep to having and not being active. And science is prior
to the activity. Therefore he calls the form first entelechy, and the activity
second. As an eye is from a subject and a form; and the subject in it is that
which has received the sight, matter of an eye. And this too is called
homonymously an eye; and the form and first entelechy of an eye is the sight
itself, which provides to it the being able to see; and second entelechy of
the eye is the activity according to which it sees. As then the puppy just
born has neither entelechy, but a power of receiving the entelechy, so one
must take also that of the soul; for as there the sight having been generated
perfects the eye, so here the soul having been generated in the body perfects
the animal, so that the soul is never without a body nor is a body. For it is
not a body, but is of a body, and therefore it exists in a body and in a body
of such a kind; but in itself it does not exist. But first he calls the
passive part of the soul soul, separating from it the rational. But he ought
to have taken the whole soul of man together, and not from a part and that the
weakest to declare concerning the all. Then he says the body has living
potentially even before the soul comes to be; for he says the body has life
potentially in itself. But the body that has life potentially must first be a
body in actuality; but it cannot be a body in actuality before receiving the
form, for it is qualityless matter and not a body; it is impossible then that
what is not in actuality have a power toward something coming to be from it.
And if it is even a body potentially, how can the potential body have life
potentially in itself? And otherwise, in the case of the others it is possible
having something not to use it; as having sight not to use it; but in the case
of the soul it is impossible. For not even the sleeper is without psychic
activity. For he is also nourished and grows and imagines and breathes, which
is especially a token of life. From these then it is clear that living
potentially cannot belong to anything, but wholly in actuality. For primarily
that which gives form to the soul is nothing other than life. For to the soul
life is connatural; but to the body according to participation. He then who
says that health is analogous to life does not speak of the life of the soul
but of that of the body, and thus he sophizes. For the bodily substance is
receptive of the contraries in turn; but that according to the form in no way.
For if the difference according to the form is changed, the animal also is
changed. So that it is not the substance according to the form that is
receptive of the contraries, but that according to the subject, that is, the
bodily. The soul then cannot in any way be an entelechy of the body; but a
self-complete substance, incorporeal. For it admits the contraries in turn,
vice and virtue, which the form was not able to admit. Then he says that the
soul being an entelechy is unmoved in itself, but is moved accidentally. And
it is nothing unlikely that being unmoved it moves us. For even beauty, being
unmoved, moves us. But even if this, being unmoved, moves; still that which
has a nature to be moved moves, and not the unmoved. If then the body also had
motion in itself, it was nothing absurd for it to be moved by an unmoved; but
as it is it is impossible for the unmoved to be moved by an unmoved; from
where then to the body the being moved, if not from the soul? For the body is
not self-moved. Wishing then to show a first generation of motion, he showed
not a first but a second. For if not being moved it moved, it made a first
motion; but if that which is moved from itself also otherwise moves, he
explains a generation of a second motion. From where then a first generation
of


motion to the body? For to say that the elements are moved from themselves,
some being light by nature, and some heavy, is false. For if lightness and
heaviness is motion, the light and heavy will never stand; but they do stand,
having occupied their proper place; therefore heaviness and lightness are not
causes of first motion, but qualities of the elements. And if this also is
granted, how can reasoning and opining and judging be works of lightness and
heaviness? And if not of these, neither of the elements; and if not of the
elements, neither of the bodies. Further if the soul is moved accidentally,
and the body from itself, even if the soul is not, the body will be moved from
itself; and if this, it will also be an animal without the soul; but these
things are absurd; therefore also that from the beginning is absurd. But
neither is it true to say that everything moved by nature is also moved by
force, and everything moved by force is also moved by nature. For the cosmos,
being moved by nature, is not moved by force; nor indeed if something is moved
according to nature does this also rest according to nature. For even the
cosmos and the sun and the moon, being moved according to nature, cannot rest
according to nature, in the same way also the soul, being moved according to
nature, cannot rest according to nature. For rest is corruption of the soul
and of every ever-moving thing. Besides these also the difficulty from the
beginning remains unsolved, from where the body is held together, being by
nature such as to be scattered. Even these things from many suffice to show
that the soul is neither an entelechy nor unmoved nor generated in a body. And
Pythagoras, being always accustomed to liken both God and all things to
numbers symbolically, defined the soul also as a number moving itself, whom
Xenocrates also followed, not that the soul is a number but that it is among
the numerable and among the multiplied, and that it is the soul that
distinguishes the things by imposing forms and types on each. For this is what
separates the forms from the forms and declares them different both by the
otherness of the forms and by the multitude of the number, and therefore makes
the things numerable. Whence it is not wholly released from the community
according to numbers. And the self-moved even he himself testified to it. That
it is not a number is clear from here. Number is a quantity; but the soul is
not a quantity but a substance; therefore the soul is not a number, even if
most of all they want the number also among the intelligibles to be a
substance, as we shall say in what follows. Further the soul is continuous;
but number is not continuous; therefore the soul is not a number. Further
number is either even or odd; but the soul is neither even nor odd; therefore
the soul is not a number. Further number increases according to addition; but
the soul does not increase according to addition. Further the soul is
self-moved; but the defined number is unmoved. Further the number remaining
one and the same in nature cannot change any quality that comes to be in
numbers; but a soul remaining one and the same according to substance changes
the qualities, falling from ignorance into science and from vice into virtue;
therefore the soul is not a number. Such then are the doctrines of the
ancients concerning soul. And Eunomius defined the soul as an incorporeal
substance created in a body, having gathered together all things at once; for
the incorporeal substance he took from the truth, and the created in a body
from the teaching of Aristotle, not having thought, though being sharp, that
he tries to gather into the same the incompatible. For everything that has a
bodily generation and at the same time a temporal one is corruptible and
mortal. And the things of Moses also agree with these; for outlining the
generation of the sensibles, he did not say expressly that in it the nature of
the intelligibles also was given subsistence; but some guessing hold this,
with whom not all agree. And if someone from the soul having been put in after
the fashioning of the body should think it has come to be after the body, he
misses the truth. For neither does Moses say it was created then when it was
put into the body; nor does it hold thus according to reason. Either then let
him say it mortal as Aristotle, having said it is generated in a body, and as
the Stoics; or saying a substance


incorporeal let him decline to say it is created in a body, lest he present to
us a notion of a mortal soul and wholly irrational. And otherwise according to
him the cosmos is not yet full, but still even now half-finished and always
needing addition. At any rate fifty thousand at least each day intelligible
substances are added to it, and the most difficult, when it has been completed
then it will be dissolved according to him, the last men toward the
resurrection filling up the psychic number; than which what would be more
irrational, to say that the cosmos is destroyed then when it is filled, for
this is altogether the manner of infant children, who in sand of play
immediately after filling confuse the things that have come to be. For to say
that now the souls come to be by an account of providence and not of creation,
for a new substance is not introduced nor another beside that which is, but
that which is is multiplied according to providence, belongs to those who do
not know the difference of creation and providence. For the work of providence
is to preserve from generation from one another the substance of the
corruptible animals (and I mean of those not coming to be according to
putrefaction, since another putrefaction again guards the succession of
these); and the strongest work of creation is to make from things that are
not. If then the souls come to be from generation from one another, they come
to be by an account of providence and are corruptible, just as the other
things that come to be from succession of kind; but if they are brought forth
from things that are not, what comes to be is creation and the thing of Moses
is not true, God rested from all his works. And both are absurd. Therefore the
souls do not now come to be. For my Father works he thinks also has been said
not upon creating but upon providing. And it seems to Apollinarius that the
souls are born from the souls, as the bodies from the bodies. For the soul
proceeds according to succession of the first man into all those from him,
just as the bodily succession. For souls are neither stored up nor now
created. For those who say these things make God a co-worker with the
adulterers; for even from these children are born; and it is also false that
God rested from all his works which he began to make, if even now he still
crafts souls. But if all things generated according to succession from
generation from one another have been shown mortal; for it generates and is
generated for this, in order that the kind of the corruptible may remain; this
man also must either say the soul is mortal coming to be from generation from
one another, or that the souls are not generated from one another according to
succession. For that concerning those generated from adultery we leave to the
account of providence, which is unknown among us. And if one must also aim at
providence, it wholly knows that what is being born will be useful either to
the life or to itself and for this reason it concedes the ensouling to come to
be. And we take as a sufficient token of this Solomon generated from Uriah's
wife and from David. Next let us also examine the opinion of the Manichaeans
which they have concerning the soul. For they say it is immortal and
incorporeal, but that there is one only soul of all, being chopped and cut
into the particular bodies both soulless and ensouled; and some partake of it
more, and some less; more the ensouled, and less the soulless, and much more
the heavenly; so that the souls according to each are parts of the universal
soul. And if they said this is divided undividedly, as the voice in those who
hear, the evil would be moderate; but as it is they say the very substance of
the soul is divided; and the most difficult of all, that they want it to be
properly in the elements, and to be divided with these in the generation of
the bodies, and again to come together to the same when the bodies are
dissolved, as water divided and again gathered and mixed, and the pure souls
go to the light, being light, and those defiled by matter go to the elements,
and again from the elements to the plants and the animals; and thus cutting
its substance and declaring it bodily and subjecting it to passions, they say
it is immortal. And they also fall into the contraries saying


that the defiled souls run back upon the elements, and are mixed with one
another, they say again they are punished in the transmigrations, according to
the magnitude of the sins uniting them and again separating them according to
hypostasis; for even the shadows when there is light are separated, and when
cloudiness has come to be are united; which it is impossible to come to be
upon an intelligible nature. For even the shadows are of the sensibles, even
if someone should grant that they are separated and again united. And Plato
declares the souls to be both one and many; for the soul of the all is one,
and there are also others of the particulars, so that the all has been
ensouled privately by the soul of the all, and again the particulars privately
by the proper soul of each. And he says the soul of the all has been stretched
from the center of the earth to the limits of heaven, saying it has been
stretched not locally but intelligibly; and this soul is that which leads
around the all spherically and holds together and binds the body-like of the
cosmos; for that bodies need that which holds them together was shown in what
went before; and that the form-giving soul does this; for each of the things
that are lives its proper life and is corrupted its proper corruption; for so
long as the body is held together and bound, it is said to be; but being
dissolved, to be corrupted; and all things live, but not all are animals. For
they distinguish plants from the soulless by growing and being nourished, that
is by the nutritive and vegetative power; and the irrational animals from the
plants by sense; and the rational from the irrational by the rational; and
thus saying all things live they distinguish the nature of each. They say then
that even the wholly soulless live a hectic life according as they are held
together by the soul of the all for being only and not being dissolved. And
this is the soul that steers the all and that sends upon them the particular
souls that were previously generated by the Creator, the Creator himself of
course having also given it laws according to which it ought to carry through
this all, which he also calls fate, and supplying a power sufficient to manage
us; and these things have been said also in the things concerning fate. In
common then all the Greeks who have declared the soul immortal dogmatize
transmigration; but they differ concerning the kinds of souls. For some say
one kind, the rational, and that this also passes into plants and into the
bodies of the irrational, some according to certain stated periods of times,
and some as it happens. And others not one kind of souls but two, rational and
irrational. And some many, as many as the kinds of animals. And especially
those from Plato differed concerning this dogma. For Plato having said that
the spirited and irascible and rapacious souls put on bodies of wolves and
lions, and those occupied about incontinence take up bodies of asses and such;
some heard the wolves and the lions and the asses properly; and others
discerned that he had spoken tropically, indicating the characters through the
animals. For Cronius in On Paligenesis (for thus he calls transmigration)
wants all to be rational, and likewise also Theodorus the Platonist in That
the soul is all the kinds, and Porphyry likewise; but Iamblichus having run
the contrary to these says there is a kind of soul according to kind of
animals, or rather different kinds. At any rate a single book has been written
by him entitled that transmigrations do not come to be from men into
irrational animals nor from irrational animals into men but from animals into
animals and from men into men. And he seems to me rather on this account to
have aimed well not only at the mind of Plato but also at the truth itself, as
it is possible to show from many other things also, but especially from these.
For no one of the rational motions appears in the irrational animals. For
neither arts nor lessons nor counsels nor virtues nor any other of the
dianoetic things is in them; from which it is clear that they have no share of
a rational soul. For it is also absurd to call the irrational rational. For
even if to


infants being quite young the irrational motion alone belongs, still we say
they have a rational soul, since growing they show forth the rational
activity; but the irrational, showing the rational at no age, would
superfluously have a rational soul, the rational power being about to be
wholly unused. For it has also been agreed by all in concert that nothing
superfluous has come to be from God; and if this, the rational soul would
superfluously have been put into the cattle and the wild beasts, never being
able to show its own work; and there would have been a charge against the
giver of an unfitting soul to the body; for it is neither the work of a
craftsman nor of one who knows order or harmony; and if someone should say
that the animals are moved according to disposition rationally, but that their
fashioning is unreceptive of artistic action, confirming the argument from
men; for when only the fingers of the hands have been taken away, most of the
arts are lost; he does not solve what is sought; for the same absurdity still
remains, that God fitted to the body a soul not suitable, but superfluous and
unprofitable and inactive, being hindered through all their age into their
proper activities, besides that they also construct the argument from things
unevident and not agreed. For from where that the animals are moved according
to disposition rationally? Better then to think that a suitable soul has been
fitted to each body, and that the animals have nothing more according to
disposition than the natural simplicity that appears in their works; for each
kind of the irrational is moved according to a proper impulse, toward the need
and activity for which it came to be from the beginning; and toward these it
also had the fashioning suitable. Yet the Creator has not left them wholly
unaided, but he put into each a natural, not rational, understanding; and into
some he also put cunning, as an image of art and a rational shadow, for these
two reasons, in order both to turn aside the plots that are present and to
guard against those to come, and in order to join the whole creation to
itself, as has already been said. And that they do not do these things
rationally is clear from each animal according to kind doing the same things
likewise and their activities not differing in the multitude, except according
to the more and less, but the whole kind being moved according to one impulse.
For every hare crafts likewise and every wolf is cunning likewise and every
ape mimics likewise, which is not so upon man. For the ways of human actions
are countless. For the rational is something free and self-determined; whence
there is not one and the same work for all men, as for each kind of the
irrational animals. For these are moved by nature alone; and the things by
nature are likewise among all. But the rational actions are different among
different ones and not from necessity the same among all. And if they should
say that the soul is sent down into such bodies for punishment of the things
previously sinned in the human life, they make the demonstration from the
later. For why were rational souls put into the first generated bodies of the
animals? For not surely as having sinned in human bodies before even coming to
be into a human body. And the admirable physician Galen also seems to set
himself with this opinion and to hold that according to each kind of animal
there is also a different kind of soul. For he says immediately at the
beginning of the first book of the treatise On the Use of Parts thus: and if
this, many of the animals will be parts, some greater, some lesser, and some
wholly uncuttable into another kind; and there is need of all for the soul;
for the body is an instrument of this and therefore the parts of the animals
have differed much from one another because the souls also have. And again
progressing in the same book he adds upon the ape also this: and indeed, O
wisest accuser, nature would say to you, to a ridiculous animal it was
necessary that a ridiculous construction of body be given to the soul. Thus he
knows that different souls are in the bodies different according to kind. And
these things concerning these. And if we have shown the soul to be neither a
body nor a harmony nor a blend nor any other quality, it is clear from these
that the soul is some incorporeal substance. For that it is has been agreed by
all; if


neither a body nor an accident it is clear that it is an incorporeal substance
and nothing of those that have their being in another. For these both come to
be and pass away without the corruption of the subject; but when the soul is
separated, the body is wholly corrupted. And using the same things one can
show the soul to be immortal. For if it is neither a body, which was shown by
nature dissoluble and corruptible, nor a quality nor a quantity nor any other
of the things that are corrupted, it is clear that it is immortal. There are
then many demonstrations of its immortality both in Plato and in the others;
but those are hard and hard to understand and barely known to those reared in
those sciences; but for us the teaching of the divine oracles suffices toward
demonstration of its immortality, having the trustworthy from itself because
it is God-breathed; and toward those who do not accept the writings of the
Christians it suffices to show that the soul is nothing of the things that are
corrupted. For if it is nothing of the things that are corrupted, and it is
incorruptible, it is also immortal so that this being sufficient must be left
aside. 3 On the union of soul and body And one must seek how a union of soul
and of a soulless body comes to be; for the matter is difficult. And if man
has been composed not only from these, but also from intellect, as some have
said, still more difficult. For all things that come together into the
hypostasis of one substance are wholly united; and all things that are united
are altered and do not remain what they were before, as will be shown upon the
elements; for having been united they have become something else. How then
either does the body united to the soul still remain a body or again the soul
being incorporeal and substantial in itself, how is it united to a body and
becomes a part of the animal, preserving its proper substance unconfused and
uncorrupted? For it is necessary either that the soul and the body have been
united and both altered together and corrupted together, as the elements; or
that they have not been united because of the aforesaid absurdities, but lie
beside as dancers in a chorus or pebble beside pebble, or have been blended,
as wine and water. But that the soul cannot lie beside the body has been shown
in On the Soul. For only that part of the body neighboring the soul would have
been ensouled, and that not joined soulless; besides not being able to say
that the things according to juxtaposition are one, as woods for example or
iron or something such. And the blend of wine and water also destroys both
together, for the mixture is neither pure water nor wine, although such a
blend comes to be according to a juxtaposition that escapes sense because of
the fine-partedness of the things blended; and it is clear from being able
again to be separated from one another; a sponge at any rate having been oiled
draws up the water pure, and papyrus; but to separate perceptibly the things
accurately united is wholly impossible. And if it has neither been united nor
lies beside nor has been blended, what is the account of the animal being said
to be one? Plato then even because of this difficulty does not want the animal
to be from soul and body; but a soul using a body, and as it were having put
on the body. But this account also has something difficult. For how can the
soul with the garment be one? For the tunic with the one who has put it on is
not one. And Ammonius the teacher of Plotinus solved what is sought in this
way. He said the intelligibles have such a nature as both to be united to
those able to receive them just as things corrupted together, and being united
to remain unconfused and uncorrupted, as things lying beside. For upon bodies
the union wholly works an alteration of the things coming together, since they
are changed into other bodies, as the elements into the compounds, and the
foods into blood, and the blood into flesh and the remaining parts of the
body; but upon the intelligibles a union comes to be, but alteration does not
follow. For an intelligible is not by nature altered according to substance,
but either it stands out or it is corrupted into what is not, and it does not
admit change; but neither is it corrupted into what is not, for they would not
have been immortal; and the soul being life if in the blend it were changed
would have been altered and would no longer have been life. And what did it
contribute to the body if it did not provide it with life? Therefore


the soul is not altered in the union. This then having been shown, that the
intelligibles are unalterable according to substance, it necessarily follows
that even being united they are not corrupted together with those to which
they have been united. And so the soul both has been united and has been
united unconfusedly to the body. For that it has been united the sympathy
shows; for the animal as being one sympathizes wholly with itself; and that it
also remains unconfused is clear from the soul being in a way separated from
the body in sleep and as it were leaving it lying dead, only evaporating it
with life, lest it perish wholly, and being active in itself in dreams,
prophesying the future and approaching the intelligibles. And the same happens
also when by itself it examines some of the things that are. For then too as
far as possible it separates itself from the body and comes to be by itself,
in order that thus it may impose itself upon the things that are. For being
incorporeal it has gone through the whole as things corrupted together,
remaining uncorrupted and unconfused and preserving the one according to
itself and, in whatever things it may come to be, turning those according to
its own life and not being turned by those. For as the sun by its presence
changes the air into light making it light-like and the light is united to the
air unconfusedly poured together with it, in the same way also the soul being
united to the body remains wholly unconfused, differing in this only that the
sun being a body and being circumscribed by place is not everywhere where its
light also is (as neither is fire; for it too remains in the woods or bound in
a wick as in a place); but the soul being incorporeal and not being
circumscribed by place goes through the whole both of its light and of the
body, and there is no part being illuminated by it in which it is not present
whole. For it is not mastered by the body, but it itself masters the body, nor
is it in the body, as in a vessel or a wineskin, but rather the body is in it.
For the intelligibles not being hindered by bodies but going through every
body and passing through and going out, are not such as to be held by a bodily
place. For being intelligible they are also in intelligible places, for either
in themselves or in the overlying intelligibles, as the soul is sometimes in
itself, when it reasons, and sometimes in the intellect, when it thinks. When
then it is said to be in a body, it is said to be in the body not as in a
place, but as in a relation, and by being present, as God is said to be in us.
For even by the relation and the inclination toward something and the
disposition we say the soul has been bound by the body, as we say the lover
has been bound by the beloved, not bodily nor locally but according to
relation. For being without magnitude and without bulk and without part, it is
better than the local circumscription according to parts. For that which has
no part by what kind of place can it be circumscribed? For place is given
subsistence together with bulk. For place is a limit of that which contains
according as it contains that which is contained. And if someone should say:
therefore my soul is both in Alexandria and in Rome and everywhere? he escapes
himself saying place again. For even the in Alexandria and wholly the in this
is a place. And it is not wholly in a place but in a relation. For it has been
shown that it cannot be comprehended by place. When then an intelligible comes
to be in a relation of some place or of a thing that is in a place, we say
more abusively that it is there, because of its activity there, taking the
place instead of the relation and the activity. For one ought to say it is
active there, we say it is there. And this account would fit more purely and
especially to the union of the Word of God toward man, according to which
having been united he remained unconfused and uncomprehended, not according to
the manner of the soul. For that being of the multiplied seems also somehow to
suffer with the body through kinship and sometimes to master and to be
mastered; but God the Word, himself being in no way altered from the community
concerning the body and the soul nor partaking of their weakness but imparting
to them of his own divinity, becomes with them one, remaining one what he was
also before the union; this manner of blend or union is newer; and he is
blended and remains wholly unmixed and unconfused and uncorrupted and
unchanged, not suffering with


but only cooperating, nor being corrupted together and altered together but
increasing those together, himself not being lessened by them, besides
remaining unchanged and unconfused, since he is also purely without share of
all alteration. A witness of these things is Porphyry who moved his own tongue
against Christ. And the testimonies of the enemies on our behalf are strong
and admit no contradiction. This Porphyry then in the second account of the
Mixed Questions writes according to the wording thus: one must not then
despair that it is possible for some substance to be taken along into the
completion of another substance and to be a part of a substance remaining
according to its own nature after completing another substance, both having
become one with another and preserving the one according to itself, and what
is greater itself not being turned, but turning those in which it may come to
be into its own activity by its presence. And he says these things concerning
the union of the soul and the body. And if upon the soul the account is true
because of the incorporeal, much more upon God the Word, who is incomparably
more and according to truth incorporeal. Which straightway stops the mouths of
those who attempt to accuse the union of God toward man. For this most of the
Greeks make a laugh, saying it is impossible and unpersuasive and unfitting
for the divine to come together with a mortal nature according to blend and
union; but we using their reputable as witnesses strip off the charge. And it
is said by some, and especially by the Eunomians, that God the Word has been
united to the body not according to substance but according to the powers of
each. For it is not the substances that have been united or blended, but the
powers of the body have been blended with the divine powers; and they say the
powers of the body are wholly of course of the organic according to Aristotle
the senses; with these then the divine powers being blended, they worked the
union according to them. But no one I think would concede to them declaring
the senses bodily powers. For it has been distinguished in what went before
clearly what are proper of body, what of soul, and what of the compound; and
among the proper of the compound we set the senses through organs; and the
organs themselves we say are of the body. Better then as was said before
according to the proper nature of the incorporeals that the union of the
substances come to be unconfusedly, the more divine being in no way harmed
from the lesser; but this only being helped from the more divine; since the
purely incorporeal nature goes unhindered through all, but nothing through it;
so that by going through all it has been united; and by nothing through it it
remains unmixed and unconfused. The manner of the union then is not by good
pleasure, as it seems to some of the reputable men, but nature is the cause.
For that he took up a body one might reasonably say has come to be according
to good pleasure; but that what is united is not confused according to the
proper nature of God does not come to be according to good pleasure. For the
degrees of souls and the ascents and descents which Origen introduces,
belonging in no way to the divine scriptures, nor agreeing with the dogmas of
the Christians, must be left aside. 4 On body Every body is a compound of the
four elements and has come to be from these; and immediately the bodies of the
blooded animals from the four humors, blood, phlegm, and yellow bile and
black; and those of the bloodless from the other humors and from that which in
them is analogous to blood. And immediately is said when something comes to be
from those very things without a medium. So that of the four elements the
compound is the four humors; and of the humors the homoeomerous, which are
parts of the body. And they liken black bile to earth; and phlegm to water;
and blood to air; and yellow bile to fire. And every compound of elements is
either solid or wet or breath. And Aristotle wants the bodies of animals to
come to be immediately from blood alone. For from this also all the parts of
the animal are immediately nourished and grow; and the seed also has its
generation from blood. But since from one and the


same it did not seem to hold well that both the hardest bones and the softest
flesh and fat come to be, it pleased Hippocrates first that the bodies of
animals are compacted immediately from the four humors; in order that from the
more earthy and thicker-parted the more solid may come to be, and from the
softer the soft. And often the four humors are found in the blood, as it is
possible to see in bloodlettings, sometimes the whey-like phlegm overflowing
in it, sometimes the black or yellow bile; whence the men seem somehow to
agree with one another. And of the parts in animals some are homoeomerous, and
some anhomoeomerous; as homoeomerous brain, meninges, nerve, marrow, bone,
tooth, cartilage, glands, ligament, membranes, fibres, hairs, nails, fleshes,
veins, arteries, pores, fat, skin, and the as-it-were immediate elements of
these, blood as blood pure, phlegm, black bile and yellow. For the tendon is
composite from both ligament and nerve-like fibres; and anhomoeomerous head,
thorax, hands, feet, and the other limbs of man. For the head is not divided
into a head, as the nerve into nerve and the vein into vein and the flesh into
flesh; and every anhomoeomerous has been composed from homoeomerous, as the
head from nerves and fleshes and bones and such; and they also call these
organic. And the definition of the homoeomerous is this: those whose parts are
like both to the whole and to one another; and the like now one must take
instead of the same. And not every animal has acquired all the parts of the
body; but some are truncated. For some are footless, as fishes and snakes; and
some headless, as crabs and crayfish and some of the swimming; for they have
the sense-organs in the chest because of not having a head; and some have been
deprived of a lung, as many as do not breathe the air; and some do not have a
bladder, as birds and all that do not urinate; and the shell-skinned are
without share of most of the limbs; for in few things being animals belongs to
them; and some though having seem not to have; as deer seem not to have the
yellow bile, since they have it scattered in the intestines and unevident; but
man has both all and complete and as it could not otherwise hold well, all.
And thus also according to the placing of the parts there is much difference
for the animals. For some have the breasts in the chest; some in the belly;
some under the thighs; and some two, some four, some more. For nature has
almost measured the multitude of the breasts to the number of those generated.
And if someone should wish to select these accurately, let him read
Aristotle's History of Animals. For it is not of the present writing to make
these accurate, but to set out as it were certain outlines and types. Let us
then pass over to the account of the elements, sought from consequence. 5 On
elements The cosmic element is a least part of the compound of the bodies. And
there are four elements, earth, water, air, fire, arranged according to the
said order from the things below to the things above, themselves also bodies
first and simple as toward the other bodies. For every element is of the same
kind as those of which it is an element. For the beginning is not of the same
kind as those from it; but the element is wholly of the same kind. But that
earth and water and air and fire are the elements is clear. For even in these
the qualities appear extreme in power and in actuality; yet none of these
sensible elements is unmixed and unmingled with another element; for all these
have been somehow gently adulterated and have partaken of one another more and
less; but even in the mixture their nature is evident. And each of the
elements according to a pairing has two qualities that give it form. For earth
is dry and cold; and water cold and wet; and air wet and hot according to its
own nature; and fire hot and dry. Yet the qualities by themselves cannot be
elements. For it is not possible for bodies to be constituted from incorporeal
qualities, nor can the other bodies that do not have the quality extreme and
according to actuality be elements. For the


elements would have been infinite, all the things that are having acquired the
quality according to the more and less and not being able to be distinguished
of which which are elements. It is necessary then that the element be both a
body and a simple body and have according to actuality the extreme qualities,
I mean heat coldness wetness dryness. For these alone of the qualities change
the whole substance through and through; and none of the other qualities does
this. For neither does the white approaching a body whiten it throughout, as
the hot heats and the cold cools, nor any of the others. And the elements
contrary to one another are those contrary according to the two qualities, as
water to fire, being cold and wet, to that which is hot and dry; and earth to
air, being cold and dry, to the hot and wet. And since the contraries were not
able to be joined to one another without some middle bond arranged that binds
them the Creator arranged in the middle of earth and air, being contraries,
water, giving it two qualities, coldness and wetness, according to which being
joined to the extremes it was able to bind them. For by the cold it is made
akin to earth; and by the wet it is joined to air. Again in the middle of
water and fire, themselves also being contraries, he arranged air, by the wet
quality being made akin to water, and by the hot to fire; and thus he joined
the contraries to one another through certain middles that bind both
themselves and the things bound. For such a bond is best. Each of them then
according to the one quality he joined to that before it, and according to the
other to that after it; as water is cold and wet; but according to the cold it
is joined to earth, which is before it according to the ascent; and according
to the wet to air, which is after it. Likewise also air according to the wet
is joined to water, which is before it; and according to the hot to fire which
is after it. And fire also according to the heat is joined to air which is
before it; and according to the dryness to earth according to a bending and
turning toward the extreme; and thus also earth according to the cold to
water; and according to the dry to fire according to a bending. For in order
that the elements may have not only the relation of the descent and ascent
toward the above and the below but also that according to a circle he somehow
bent and turned the extremes toward one another; and I mean fire and earth;
for even fire having cast off the heat alone becomes earth; and this is clear
from the thunderbolts; for fire being carried down and being cooled from the
over-roasting is turned to stone. Therefore every thunderbolt is carried with
stone and sulphur; and sulphur is as it were fire that has been cooled, no
longer hot according to actuality but potentially, but the dry also in
actuality. And the elements alone have the qualities according to actuality;
and all the others potentially if they do not approach an element. And in
order that neither the elements nor the compounds from them may ever fail the
Creator wisely devised so that the elements are both changed into one another
and into the compounds, and again the compounds are dissolved into the
elements. And thus being constituted from a sufficient generation from one
another they are preserved through all. For earth having been made mud becomes
water; and water having been thickened and compressed becomes earth; and
having been heated and evaporated becomes air; and air having been gathered
and thickened becomes water; and having been dried changes into fire; and thus
also fire having been quenched and having cast off the dryness is made air.
For air is a quenching of fire and a vapor of water having been heated. From
both then it is clear that its generation is from heat; for even water having
been heated and fire having been quenched become air. It is then according to
its proper nature hot; but it is cooled by the neighboring toward water and
earth; so that its lower parts toward the earth are cold; and the upper and
toward the fire hot. And this happens because of the softness and
easy-affectedness of the air; for it quickly stands out of its proper nature
and is changed. And Aristotle says there are two kinds of air; the one
vapor-like, that from the exhalation of water; and the one smoke-like, that
from the quenching of fire; and the smoke-like is hot; and the vapor-like
where it is born hot also itself,


but going forward in part to be cooled, and progressing to be made water. And
in order that he may also escape certain other things that seem to be absurd
and besides these that the higher and standing far from the earth appear
colder, he supposed its nature double. And all bodies have their generation
from the coming together of these four elements, both those of plants and
those of animals; nature drawing the purest of the elements into the
generation of these bodies. And Aristotle calls these bodies natural; not
being composed according to a heap but being blended whole through whole into
a union and making the body one something and other besides themselves; for
thus they have been united so that it is not possible to distinguish them; nor
to behold earth privately, and privately water and air and fire, by one
something and other besides these having come to be from the coming together
of the four, as upon the fourfold-drug. For even there the fourfold-drug is
other besides the things from which it is composed, yet not likewise. For the
elements do not make the bodies according to juxtaposition of the most
fine-parted, as in the fourfold-drug; but according to change and unity. And
again the bodies being corrupted are dissolved into the elements, and in this
way all things remain continuously and suffice toward the generation of the
things that are, neither overflowing at any time nor being lessened; whence
they also say that the generation of one is the corruption of another and the
corruption of one the generation of another, not only according to the soul as
was said before but also according to the body. And it seems to Plato that the
three elements change into one another. But earth remains unchangeable. For
likening to each element the solids of the rectilinear figures (to earth the
cubic figure since it is most unmoved of the rest, and to water of the rest
the most hard-to-move the icosahedron, and the most easy-to-move the
pyramid-like to fire, and the octahedron to air, which is more easy-to-move
than water but more hard-to-move than fire), from these figures he makes the
demonstration that the three change into one another, but earth does not
suffer this. For the three, both the pyramid and the octahedron and the
icosahedron, are constituted from scalene triangles, and the cube from
equilateral triangles. As many then as were constituted from the scalene
triangles, these being dissolved and again coming together can be changed into
one another; and neither a cube having been dissolved can change into any of
the other three figures (for it has been constituted from equilateral
triangles from which none of the other three can be constituted), nor again
any of the three figures into a cube. It is necessary then that the bodies
given form according to these forms also hold thus toward one another as those
held. Yet earth did not remain unaffected but is divided by the more
fine-parted bodies, being re-elemented but not also changed into those that
divide it. For again coming together into itself it is restored, which appears
in water. For if having thrown a little earth into water you stir it the earth
is dissolved into water; but if you stop stirring, the water having taken a
stand, it settles; in the same way one must think also upon all earth; and
this is not a change but a distinguishing of the mixed. And Plato says earth
is also loosed by the sharpness of fire; and being loosed it is carried in it
or in a bulk of air, clearly when air dissolves it, or in water when it is
loosed by water. And he says dividing in another way that each of the elements
has three qualities; fire sharpness rarity motion, and the other extreme of
the elements that is earth the qualities contrary to these bluntness density
rest, so that according to these qualities earth and fire are contraries
(which was not according to the other qualities in pairing) and qualities have
been taken from each of the extremes and thus the middle elements have come to
be; for two qualities of fire are taken, rarity and motion, and one of earth,
rarity, and air is constituted having form-giving qualities bluntness rarity
motion. Again two qualities of earth are taken, bluntness and density; and of
fire one only


motion; and water comes to be being given form itself also by bluntness
density motion; as then sharpness is toward bluntness so fire toward air; and
as rarity toward density, so air toward water; and as motion toward rest, so
water toward earth. As then fire toward air, so air toward water; and as air
toward water, so water toward earth. For planes are by nature held together by
one middle, that is analogy; and solids by two middles. And in another way
they say the elements have qualities; for earth and water have heaviness
according to which they are carried below by nature, and air and fire
lightness according to which they are carried above by nature. And the Stoics
say of the elements some are active and some passive; active air and fire;
passive earth and water. And Aristotle also introduces a fifth body the
ethereal and circular-carrying, not wanting heaven to have come to be from the
four elements (and he says the fifth body circular-carrying because it is
carried in a circle about itself), Plato saying expressly that it has been
composed from fire and earth. And he says thus: and the generated must be
body-like and visible and tangible, and without fire nothing would ever become
visible, nor tangible without some solid; and solid not without earth; whence
beginning to constitute the body of the all from fire and earth God was
making. And two only cannot be well constituted without a third, for a bond in
the middle must become a gatherer of both; and of bonds the fairest is that
which would most of all make both itself and the things bound one; and this
analogy is by nature to complete most fairly. Saying bond the two middle
elements taken from the analogy said before. And those who champion the things
of the Hebrews differ concerning heaven and earth. For the others almost all
say heaven and earth have come to be from no previously underlying matter,
since Moses they say in the beginning God made heaven and earth; but
Apollinarius wants God to have made heaven and earth from the abyss; for Moses
did not mention the abyss in the generation of the cosmos as having come to
be; but in Job it has been said: who made the abyss; from this then as from
matter he wants all the other things to have come to be; yet not ungenerated
but generated, having been laid down before all the bodily by the Creator
toward the subsistence of the others; and the name of the abyss also makes
clear the infinite of the matter. But how this may hold makes no difference.
For even thus God is shown Creator of all and having made all things from
things that are not. And against those who say there is one element only,
either fire or air or water, the things said by Hippocrates will suffice; if
man were one he would never have felt pain; for there would not have been that
by which he would feel pain being one; and if he also felt pain that which
heals would have been one. For that which is about to feel pain must come to
be in a change with sense; and if the element were one only there was not that
into which it would be changed; and not being turned but remaining upon itself
it would not have felt pain, even if it were sensible; and that which suffers
must also suffer from something; and if the element were only one there would
not have been another quality beside that of the one element from which the
animal would have suffered; and if it was able neither to be changed nor to
suffer, how would it have felt pain? Having shown this impossible by
concession he brings on: and if he also felt pain that which heals would have
been one; but now that which heals is not one but many. Therefore man was not
one. But also from the things from which they themselves each try to construct
their proper dogma, from these especially it is shown that the elements are
four. For even Thales saying water only is an element tries to show the other
three coming to be by this. For its sediment becomes earth; and the more
fine-parted air; and of air the more fine-parted fire. And Anaximenes saying
air only himself likewise tries to show the other elements completed from air;
and Heraclitus and Hipparchus of Metapontum saying fire have used these same
demonstrations. From which then these also say fire is


generative of the other elements and of the others one water, and one air,
from these it is shown that all the elements are changed into one another; and
all being turned into one another, it is necessary that all be elements. For
whichever of the four you take this too will be found coming to be from
another. And the body being an instrument of the soul is co-divided with the
psychic powers. For it has been constructed suitable and fitting to these

account going forward will show. For the soul holds the rank of craftsman, and
the body of instrument; and matter is the things about which the action is,
and the completion is the action itself. As for example the woman underlies as
matter, for about this the action is; and the action is adultery or
fornication or marriage. And the psychic powers are divided into the
imaginative and the dianoetic and the mnemonic. 6 On the imaginative The
imaginative then is a power of the irrational soul being active through the
sense-organs; and the imagined is that which falls under the imagination, as
the sensible to sense; and imagination is an affection of the irrational soul
coming to be from some imagined; and a phantasm is an empty affection in the
irrationals of the soul coming to be from no imagined. And the Stoics say
these four, imagination, imagined, imaginative, phantasm; saying imagination
the affection of the soul that shows in itself also that which has made it
imagined. For when we see white some affection comes to be in the soul from
the taking of it. For as an affection comes to be in the sense-organs when it
senses; so also in the soul when it thinks. For it receives in itself an image
of the intelligible. And the imagined is the sensible that has made the
imagination, as the white and everything that can move the soul; and the
imaginative the empty drawing without an imagined; and a phantasm that which
we draw according to the imaginative empty drawing, as upon the mad and the
melancholic. And the disagreement for these has come to be about the exchange
of the names only. And its organs are the front ventricles of the brain and
the psychic breath in them and the nerves from them soaked with the psychic
breath and the construction of the sense-organs. And the sense-organs are
five; but sense is one, the psychic, which recognizes through the sense-organs
the affections coming to be in them; and by the most earthy and most bodily of
the sense-organs, which is touch, it senses the earthy nature; and by the most
ray-like, which is sight, the ray-like; as also by the air-like the affections
of air; for air is the substance of voice or a blow of air; and by the
sponge-like and water-like, that according to taste, it takes hold of the
humors. For each of the sensibles is by nature recognized through the proper.
It was necessary then according to this account, since there are four
elements, that the senses also be four; but since vapor and the kind of smells
is by nature between air and water; for it is thicker-parted than air, and
more fine-parted than water; and such becomes clear through the affection of a
cold in the head; for those with a cold draw the air through breathing; but
drawing this they do not take hold of the vapors; for because of the blockage
the thicker-parted does not arrive at the sense; for this sake a fifth
sense-organ, smell, has been found by nature, in order that nothing of the
things able to come into knowledge may escape the sense. And sense is not an
alteration, but a diagnosis of an alteration, for the sense-organs are
altered, and sense distinguishes the alteration; and often the sense-organs
also are called sense. And sense is a taking-hold of the sensibles. And this
definition seems not to be of the sense itself but of its works. Therefore
they also define sense thus: an intelligent breath stretched from the ruling

sensibles, and a sense-organ an organ of

homonymously; for both the sense-organ and the sensitive power. And Hipparchus
says rays being stretched from the eyes, attaching at their limits as with
touches of hands to the bodies outside, give up the taking-hold of them toward
the seeing. And the geometers describe certain cones coming to be from the
falling-together of the rays sent out through the eyes. For they send rays,
the right eye upon the left, and the left upon the right; and from their
falling-together a cone is completed, whence the sight takes in many visibles
together, but sees accurately those where the rays may fall together. Thus at
any rate often looking into the ground we do not see the coin lying in it,
gazing for a long time, until the meetings of the rays come to be in that part
where the coin lies, and then we fall upon the sight of it, as then first
beginning to attend. And the Epicureans say images of the appearing things
fall upon the eyes. And Aristotle not a bodily image but a quality through
alteration of the surrounding air arrives from the visibles as far as the
sight. And Plato according to a co-shining of the light from the eyes flowing
off to a quantity into the air of the same kind, and of that being carried
back from the bodies, and of that about the air between, being easily poured
and easily turned, being co-extended with the fire-like of the sight. And
Galen in agreement with Plato concerning sight in the seventh of the Concord
says, writing somehow thus scatteredly; for if some portion or power or image
or quality of the seen bodies arrived into the eye, we would not have known
the magnitude of what is seen, as of a mountain if it should happen a
greatest. For so great an image to fall into our eyes is wholly irrational;
but indeed also the optic breath being secreted cannot take so great a
strength as to be poured around all that is seen; it remains then that the
surrounding air becomes such an organ for us during the time in which we see
as in the body the optic nerve is. For the air surrounding us seems to suffer
something such; for both the ray of the sun, touching the upper limit of the
air, gives the power through into the whole; and the ray being carried through
the optic nerves has a breathy substance, and falling upon that which
surrounds and working the alteration by the first approach, gives it through
as far as most continuous with itself, until it falls upon a resistant body.
For the air becomes an organ for the eye toward the diagnosis of the seen such
as the nerve is for the brain; so that the account which the brain has toward
the nerve, such the eye has toward the air ensouled by the solar ray. And that
the air is by nature made like together with the neighboring bodies is clear
from even a reddish or cyan or even a bright silver being carried through
while there is light the air being altered by that which was carried through.

a cause of seeing but the soul itself encountering the visibles recognizes
itself being the visibles by the soul holding together all the things that are
and all things being a soul holding together different bodies. For wanting one

a first account the colors, and it co-diagnoses with them also the colored
body and its magnitude and the figure and the place where it is and the

and uneven and the sharp and the blunt and the constitution whether it is
watery or earthy, as wet or dry. Its proper sensible then is color; for
through sight alone we take hold of the colors; and immediately with the color
also the colored body and the place in which what is seen happens to be, and
the interval between the seeing and the seen. For as many of the senses as
also have the body co-appearing, by these immediately also the place is
co-diagnosed, as by touch and by taste; but these then only when they approach
the body co-sense, except those that will be distinguished next; but sight
also from far. And since from far it takes hold of its proper sensibles it has
necessarily followed that it alone also sees the interval, and the magnitude
then only it diagnoses when the appearing can take it in through one approach.
And in those in which the seen is greater than to be taken in according to one
approach, in these sight needs also memory and thought. For beholding it in
part and not whole under one, it necessarily passes from one to another, and
that which falls in always according to the passing is sensible; and the
things seen before memory guards. And thought gathers both, both the sensible
and the memorable. Of magnitude then it takes hold in two ways, sometimes
alone, and sometimes with memory and thought; and of the number of the seen
above three or four, of that not seen according to one approach, and of the
motions and of the polygonal figures, never alone, but always with memory and
thought. For it cannot gather the five and six and seven and more without
memory. Thus neither the hexagonal and octagonal and polygonal figures. And
motion coming to be according to a passing has the one earlier and the other
later. And where there is earlier and later and third, this memory alone
guards; and the above and below and the uneven and even, and likewise also the
rough and smooth and the sharp and blunt are common of touch and of sight,
since they alone also diagnose the place. And they also need thought. For only
that, that which falls upon the sense according to one approach, is a work of
sense alone; and those according to more not of sense alone, but also of
memory with thought, as was shown before. And of the transparent sight is by
nature to go even as far as the depth, of air first and most; for it goes
through the whole; and second of water being still and pure; the fishes at any
rate we see swimming; and less through glass and the others of such a kind,
and it is clear, when they have been illuminated; and this too is proper to
it. And let it not deceive some that the taking-hold of the hots also is of
sight, since seeing fire we know immediately that it is also hot; for if you
lead the account up to the first sight you will find that then when first
sight beheld the fire, it had the taking-hold of the color and figure only;
and touch also having approached we recognized that it is also hot, which
memory guarded having received from touch; now then, when we behold fire, we
see nothing else than the figure and the color of the fire; and thought
co-takes with the seen through memory also the heat. The same account also
upon the apple. For if the apple is not given form by the color and figure
only, but also by the smell and the quality according to taste, it is not also
taking hold of these that sight knows that it is an apple; but the soul
preserves the memory from both smelling and tasting and at once with the
beholding it co-thinks these also with the figure and the color. When then we
think a waxen apple to be a true apple

proper sensibles was not tripped; for it saw together both the color and the
figure. The three then of the senses, both sight and hearing and smell, take
hold of the things from outside and not approaching through the medium of air;
but taste does not otherwise sense if it does not converse with the sensible;
and touch goes both ways; for both approaching the bodies and sometimes
through a staff alone. Sometimes then sight needs the co-witness of the other
senses, when the appearing has been crafted toward deceit, as upon painting;
for it is a work of painting to deceive the sight with projections that are
not and hollows, if the thing has such a nature; where there is need toward
diagnosis especially of touch, and sometimes also of taste or smell, as upon
the waxen apple; and sometimes by itself it presents the appearing things
clearly when it does not see from far; the tower at any rate, the square one,
it sees round from far; and it is also tripped when we look through mist or
smoke or some of the similar things that cloud the sight. Likewise also when
through water being moved; for even in the sea it sees the oar as broken;
likewise also when it looks through some transparent matter, as upon mirrors
and glass and the others of such a kind; or when what is seen is moved
sharply; for the swift motion confuses the sight, so as to see as round the
not round and as standing the moving; or when thought is occupied about other
things, as upon one who proposed to meet a friend and having met passed him
by, by his thought being about others. And this is not even a miss of the
sight, but of the intellect. For it saw and reported; but the intellect did
not attend to the things reported. And sight needs especially four things
toward a clear diagnosis, an unharmed sense-organ, commensurate motion and
interval, air pure and bright. 8 On touch Each of the other sense-organs the
Creator constructed double and circumscribed by some place and part of the

and he also grew two tongues in all the animals; but in some divided as in
snakes, and in some joined and united as in men; for this reason he also made
the two front ventricles only of the brain, in order that the sensitive nerves
being sent down from each ventricle may make the sense-organs double. And he
made them double because of exceeding care; in order that if the one has
suffered the remaining may preserve the sense. But when most of the
sense-organs are destroyed nothing toward the life itself is harmed for the
animal; but when touch is lost the animal also is lost together. For touch
alone of the senses is common of all the animals. For every animal has touch;
and the other senses not all all, but some some; and the more perfect have
all. Since then in this the animal was in danger of being an animal or not
being, the Creator did not allot one part only to touch; but almost the whole
body of the animal; for except bones and nails and horns, ligaments and hairs,
and certain other such, every part of the body takes hold of touch. It has
happened then that each of the sense-organs has double senses, the one of the
proper sensibles, and the one of touch, as sight; for it both distinguishes
the colors and takes hold of the hots and colds; but of these as a body, and
of the colors as sight. And likewise also taste and smell and hearing. How
then is touch of the whole body, if we say the senses are from the front
ventricles of the brain? Or it is clear that nerves being sent down from the
brain and being scattered into every part of the body the sense of touch is
added. And since often having been struck in the foot by a thorn we
immediately bristle the hairs of the head some thought the affection or the
sense of the affection is sent up into the brain and

being cut that felt pain but the brain. Better then to say that the nerve is
brain. For it is also some portion of brain, having through all itself the
psychic breath, as iron that has been fired has the fire; for this reason
wherever a sensitive nerve is implanted that part partakes of sense from it
and becomes sensitive. And perhaps it is nothing absurd to say that not the
affection is sent up to the beginning of the nerves the brain but some
co-sense and report of the affection. And the proper sensible of touch is the
hot and cold, and the soft and hard, and sticky and brittle, and heavy and
light; for through touch alone these are recognized. And common of touch and
sight are the sharp and blunt and the rough and smooth, and the dry and wet,
thick and thin, above and below; for even the place, and the magnitude when it

And likewise it also senses the motion of the body approaching it with memory
and thought. And likewise also of number; but as far as two or three and these
small and easily taken in; and rather than touch sight takes hold of these, as
also of the even and of the uneven. For it is of the kind of the smooth and
rough, for unevenness mixed with hardness makes roughness, and evenness with
density smoothness. It is clear then from what has been said that these senses
have much community toward one another. For even the misses of the one the
other makes clear. Upon painting at any rate sight sees certain projections of
a nose and of the others; but touch having approached convicted its miss. And
as sight always and all things sees through the medium of air, so also touch
through the middle of the staff senses the hards and softs and wets, but
syllogistically and with thought; for man has this sense most accurate. By
this at any rate and by taste he has more than the other animals, although
being inferior in the other three. For another animal according to another of
the three senses surpasses man; and the dog in the three together; for it both
hears and sees and smells more sharply, as is clear from the tracking dogs.
And the whole body is a sense-organ of touch, as has also been said before;
and especially the insides of the hands, and of these still more the tips of
the fingers; for these we have as it were accurate gnomons of touch. For the
Creator constructed the hands not only as a taking-hold organ but also as a
tactile. For this reason they also have the skin finer and muscle has been
spread under them whole from inside and they are without share of hairs, in
order that they may rather take hold of the tangibles. And of their not
growing hairs the muscle spread under has become a cause. And of the hands the
harder are stronger toward taking-hold; and the softer more accurate toward
touch; as also of the nerves the hard are more suitable toward motion, and the
soft toward sense. For these themselves are organs of touch; for through these
the sense of touch comes to be. 9 On taste We said in what went before that
sight sees according to straight lines; but smell and hearing not according to
a straight only but from everywhere. And touch and taste recognize neither
according to straight lines nor from everywhere; but then only when they
approach the proper sensibles themselves, except those already distinguished.

cartilaginous kind, for cartilage is suitable toward noises and sounds. And
man alone and the ape do not move the ears, of all the other animals that have
ears moving them. 11 On smelling And smelling comes to be through the
nostrils, and is completed into the limits of the front ventricles of the

easily. For it has also been said before that each of the sense-organs takes

difference is both fragrance and foulness, and the middle of these, which is
neither fragrant nor foul. And fragrance comes to be of the wets in the bodies
having been accurately concocted; and the middle condition moderately; and
foulness more deficiently or not at all. 12 On the dianoetic Of the

prophesies to us the future, which alone the Pythagoreans say is true
divination, having followed the Hebrews. And an organ of this also is the
middle ventricle of the brain and the psychic breath in it. 13 On the mnemonic
And the mnemonic is both cause and treasury of memory and recollection; and
memory is as Origen says an imagination left behind from some sense appearing
according to actuality; and as Plato, a salvation of both sense and thought.
For the soul takes hold of the sensibles through the sense-organs and opinion
comes to be; and of the intelligibles through the intellect, and thought comes

the thought properly but the dianoia. For the sensibles are remembered
according to themselves and the intelligibles accidentally, since even the
memory of the dianoetic comes about from a preceding imagination; and the

also when the foot has suffered, we are hindered into walking. For the foot
