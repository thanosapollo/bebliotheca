* On the Nativity of John the Baptist
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of our father among the saints John, archbishop of Constantinople, the
Chrysostom, on the birthday of Saint John the Forerunner and Baptist.
Today the demand of what is owed has come; the day is here that
pursues us for the debt; the appointed sharpness of the loans is upon
us. For we have truly borrowed both the present season and what remains
for the discourses. But why do I speak of what remains, when even as it
is repaid the debt increases, and as it is fulfilled it works for us
another beginning of obligation? For such are the discourses made upon
Saint John the Baptist: they strive to repay the debt, yet they always
fall under the obligation. For what is there to say concerning the
greatness of the righteous man's deeds, or concerning so great a
splendor of his achievements? Perhaps it is right to use only that
voice: "Amen I say to you, among those born of women there has not
arisen one greater than John the Baptist."
For this remembrance is worthy of the encomia; this is the truth of
the honor that is owed. For it is the Master who both speaks without
falsehood and praises worthily. "There has not arisen," he says, "among
those born of women one greater than John the Baptist." For among those
born of the righteous none has arisen thus. Jeremiah the prophet was
sanctified from the womb, but John the Baptist has been honored even
more than he. Nay, even while he is being carried he is filled with the
Holy Spirit itself: "For he shall be filled," it says, "with the Holy
Spirit from his mother's womb." Therefore even within the belly he
leaps and evangelizes, and when he is born he at once works the healing
upon his father. Indeed all who heard, it says, were saying: "What then
will this child be?" For they are distressed as they behold the Baptist
working wonders before the time, and they marvel at the infant's
dignity beyond his power. They see the child superior both to the years
of life and to its manners. Learning the things before the birth they
are struck with awe; seeing the things after the birth they are
astonished. And being at a loss they have cried out: "What then will
this child be?" Do you wish to learn the child's progress? Do you wish
to know the dignity that comes upon him? Hear the angel crying out
concerning him: "He shall be," he says, "great before the Lord."
See, again the greater thing is allotted to the righteous man, the
beloved of the desert. He had not yet learned the weaving of affairs,
and anticipating he keeps the simplicity of the soul. For he does not
endure to traffic in human wealth; he does not love glory that is
dissolved in this life; he does not give the belly the unsated taste;
he does not know the variety of foods; he does not clothe himself with
the adornment of apparel. "For his garment," it says, "was of camel's
hair, and a leather belt about his loins." For the proper habit of the
desert's ascesis was a fitting garment of his courage. For he wore the
hairs of a burden-bearing beast, signifying that he himself also
endures lightly to bear the weight laid upon him according to the
desert; for truly heavy was the life toward the desert. And he had also
a leather belt, through which he both showed the mortified desire of
the belly and at the same time manifested the courage of the soul. "And
his food," it says, "was locusts and wild honey." And this was sweeter
in the desert than the most luxurious fare. And since the Master and
Maker has declared that among those born of women none greater than
John the Baptist has arisen, and since the angel has spoken first and
said that he would be great before the Lord, nothing upon the earth,
nothing in heaven has been left out of the Baptist's honor. For truly
it was needful that the Baptist be magnified both with God and with us;
for one may see the paradoxical birth from the barren woman, and the
wonders of his growth, and the achievements of his way of life. "For he
grew," it says, "and was strengthened in spirit." These are the marks
of age and of grace; for together with the youth's body the virtue of
the soul also grew. And what is more wonderful is that "he was," it
says, "in the deserts until the day of his manifestation to Israel." He
was in the deserts. And what is greater than this achievement? A youth
being trained in the desert: a child not yet taking the experience of
life seizes the desert's way of life; he does not yet know the world's
disturbance and he embraces stillness.
But why do we still contend to linger in these words, when already the
people also constrain us, running toward him into the desert? For the
man's bright way of life was drawing each one to go to him; for the
virtuous life is always noised abroad and draws every virtue-loving
soul toward itself. But the crowds were flowing together toward John
still more by the strange wonder of the preaching; for after he was
manifested, as was said before, "he came," it says, "preaching in the
wilderness and saying: Repent, for the kingdom of heaven has drawn
near"; and again: "Prepare the way of the Lord, make his paths
straight." And these sayings were strange to those of that time and
paradoxical; for he was a forerunner, making known the approaching
kingdom, making ready for the Master who was coming to sojourn,
preparing the king's journey, rousing all to meet him, expounding the
wonders that were to come, procuring the heavenly wealth. For he was
introducing repentance, the healing of souls, and he was also giving a
baptism drawing near to grace. Whence "there went out to him," it says,
"all Jerusalem and all Judea and all the region around the Jordan, and
they were baptized by him." He showed the preaching of his way of life
to be trustworthy; or rather, the way of life received in addition the
gift of the preaching. And he who was formerly most solitary toward the
desert was now even making the desert itself into a city. Why do I say
a city? He made it wholly heaven; for hear henceforth the heavenly
things from this place. "Then," it says, "Jesus comes from Galilee to
the Jordan to John to be baptized by him." For the Master of the
heavens came to the desert-dweller; he who takes away the sin of the
world came to him who was preaching repentance; the true light to the
shining lamp; "for this one," it says, "was the lamp that shines." The
fountain of life came to a baptizing drop; the river of God to a very
small stream; the sea of salvation to a passing channel. For this was
Christ coming to John. Whence also the Baptist was rightly hindering
him, saying: "I have need to be baptized by you, and do you come to
me?" "I," he says, "have need of your light, and you come to me? I was
formed by you, and you come to be baptized by me? I came as a servant
preaching concerning you, the Master, not as one who ought to baptize
my Master. Permit my head rather to bow beneath your hand, for I know
to fear the authority of my Master. Do not make my right hand bold,
already numb toward the work of baptism." What then does the Master say
to him? "Suffer it now, for I have not come now to lay bare my own
dignity, nor to show the purity of the nature, but to fulfill the
economy that is expedient for you." "Then," it says, "he suffers him."
O how much vigor the word signifies. The servant suffers the Master,
the subject the king; for the voice was not able to prevail over the
Word, nor the lamp to withstand the rays of the sun, but drawing in his
own light he yielded to the conquering ray of righteousness.
Thus the Savior gives grace to baptism; thus the Lord's pure and
sinless body washed away the stain that remained in the waters, so that
henceforth the nature of the waters has not only the cleansing from
nature, but also the illumining from grace. Whence those who are
baptized henceforth are baptized in spirit and in truth, and this John
the Baptist himself has clearly cried out, saying: "I baptize you with
water; but in your midst stands one whom you do not know; he will
baptize you in spirit and in truth." You see the Baptist, after so
great honor and dignity, still diminishing his own work and marveling
at the Savior's gift. For he always yielded what was fitting to Christ;
therefore he himself also receives the testimony that came upon him
from the heavens, being worthy of the heavenly words and deeds. "For he
saw," it says, "the heaven opened and the Spirit of God descending as a
dove and coming toward him, and a voice bearing witness: This is my
beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." The words are equal to the
deeds; for John both sees and hears as he baptizes. This man made the
desert into heaven; this man showed the inhabited world to be God's
paradise, watered by the grace of baptism. Truly the law and the
prophets are until John; from then the kingdom of heaven is
evangelized. For from him the prophets' preachings stood still, when he
pointed out him whom they had foretold. The weight of the law stood
still, for he preached repentance announcing God's long-suffering. The
many baptisms of the Jews stood still, for through him one baptism was
shown, which he himself began to give unto repentance, but which Christ
granted unto remission of sins and eternal life, to whom belongs the
glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
