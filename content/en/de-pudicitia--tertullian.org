* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Modesty, the flower of morals, the honor of bodies, the grace of the sexes,
the integrity of blood, the pledge of the race, the foundation of holiness,
the first judgment of every good mind, although rare and not easily perfected
and scarcely perpetual, will nevertheless remain in the world to some extent,
if nature has laid it down beforehand, if discipline has persuaded, if censure
has constrained, since every good of the soul is either born or taught or
compelled. But as evils more and more prevail, which is the account of the
last times, goods now cannot even be born, so corrupted are the seeds, nor
taught, so deserted are the studies, nor compelled, so disarmed are the laws.
In short, that from which we begin has now so far grown obsolete that not the
swearing off of lusts but their moderation is believed to be modesty, and he
is held chaste enough who has not been too chaste. But let the world's modesty
see to itself with the world itself, with its own nature if it was being born,
with its own study if it was being taught, with its own bondage if it was
being compelled; except that it would be even more unhappy if it had stood as
unfruitful, not having acted before God. I would rather have no good than a
vain one. What does it profit to be what it does not profit to be? The state
of our goods is already sinking; the reason of Christian modesty is being
shaken, which draws all things from heaven, both nature through the washing of
regeneration, and discipline through the instrument of preaching, and censure
through the judgments from either testament, and, being compelled, more
constantly from fear and desire of eternal fire and of the kingdom.

Could I not have dissembled against this? I hear even that an edict has been
posted, and indeed a peremptory one. The Pontifex Maximus, that is, the bishop
of bishops, edicts: I remit the sins of adultery and of fornication to those
who have done penance. O edict to which GOOD DEED cannot be inscribed! And
where will this liberality be posted? In the same place, I suppose, on the
very doors of lusts, under the very titles of lusts. There a repentance of
this kind is to be promulgated where the delinquency itself will be practiced.
There pardon is to be read, that with hope of it one will enter. But this is
read in the church, and is pronounced in the church, and she is a virgin. Far,
far from the bride of Christ be such a proclamation! She who is true, who is
modest, who is holy, will be without even a stain of the ears. She has none to
whom she can promise this, and if she has had them, she does not promise it,
since even the earthly temple of God could sooner be called by the Lord a den
of robbers than of adulterers and fornicators.

There will therefore be here too a title against the psychics, against the
former fellowship of my own opinion also, which was with them, who may more
throw this at me as a mark of levity. Never is a divorce of fellowship a
prejudgment of a crime. As if it were not easier to err with the many, when
truth is loved with the few. But indeed a useful levity will not disgrace me
more than a harmful one would have adorned me. I am not ashamed of an error I
have left, because I delight to have left it, because I recognize myself
better and more modest. No one blushes at making progress. Knowledge too has
its ages in Christ, through which even the apostle passed. When I was a child,
he says, I spoke as a child, I understood as a child; but when I became a man,
I put away the things that had been of the child. So he turned from former
opinions, and did not therefore sin because he became a zealot not of paternal
traditions but of Christian ones, wishing even that those might be cut off who
were urging that circumcision be kept. And would that these too, who maim the
mere and true integrity of the flesh, cutting off not the outermost surface
but the inmost image of modesty itself, when they promise pardon to adulterers
and fornicators against the principal discipline of the Christian name, which
even the world itself so far testifies that, if ever, it strives to punish it
in our women by defilements of the flesh rather than by torments, wishing to
snatch away what they prefer to life.

But now this glory is being extinguished, and indeed by those who ought so
much more constantly to subscribe no pardon to stains of this kind, inasmuch
as, whenever they will, they marry, so as not to be forced to fall into
adultery and fornication, since it is better to marry than to burn. To be
sure, for the sake of continence incontinence is necessary; a fire will be put
out with fires. Why then do they afterward also indulge crimes under the name
of repentance, whose remedies they provide beforehand by the right of many
marriages? For both the remedies will be idle when the crimes are indulged,
and the crimes will remain if the remedies are idle. So on both sides they
play with anxiety and with neglect, most vainly taking precautions against
those they spare and most foolishly sparing those they take precautions
against, since either there should be no precaution where there is sparing, or
there should be no sparing where there is precaution. For they take
precautions as if they did not want something to be admitted, but they indulge
as if they wanted it admitted; whereas if they do not want it admitted they
ought not to indulge, if they want to indulge they ought not to take
precautions. For adultery and fornication will not be classed among both
slight and greatest sins, so that both may fit, both the anxiety that takes
precautions and the security that indulges. But since they are those which
hold the peak of crimes, it does not follow that they be both indulged as
slight and guarded against as greatest. For us, however, the greatest or
highest are guarded against in this way too, while not even second marriages
after faith are permitted to be known, different, if it so happens, by nuptial
and dowry tablets from the work of adultery and fornication, and therefore,
most harshly infaming us, we set the twice-married outside by the Paraclete's
enormity of discipline. We fix the same limit of the threshold for adulterers
also and fornicators, who will pour out tears of peace that go hungry, and
will take back from the church nothing more than the publication of their
disgrace.

For the rest, they say, God is good and most good, and merciful and pitiful
and of much mercy, which he prefers to every sacrifice, not counting the death
of a sinner of so much worth as his repentance, the savior of all men and
especially of the faithful. Therefore the sons of God also will have to be
merciful and peacemakers, forgiving one another as Christ also has forgiven
us, not judging, lest we be judged. For to his own Lord a man stands or falls:
who are you, that you judge another's servant? Forgive, and it will be
forgiven you. Such and so great are their empty things, by which they both
flatter God and pander to themselves, more unmanning discipline than
strengthening it. With how many contrary things too can we strike back, which
both try God's severity and provoke our constancy? Because although God is
good by nature, yet he is also just. For from a cause, as he knows how to
heal, so also to strike, making peace, but also creating evils, preferring
repentance, but also charging Jeremiah not to pray for a sinful people.
Because if they have fasted, he says, I will not hear their entreaty. And
again: And do you not worship for the people, and do not ask for them in
prayer and supplication, because I will not hear in the time when they have
called on me, in the time of their affliction. And still above, the same
preferrer of mercy to sacrifice: And do you not worship for this people, and
do not ask that they obtain mercy, and do not approach me for them, because I
will not hear those who are asking for mercy, of course weeping from
repentance and fasting, and offering their affliction to God. For God is
jealous, and is not mocked with the nose, namely of those who flatter his
goodness, and who, though patient, yet through Isaiah threatens an end of
patience: I have been silent; shall I always be silent and hold out? I have
been quiet like a woman in labor; I will rise, and I will make dry. For fire
will go forth before his face, and will burn up his enemies, killing not only
the body but also souls in gehenna.

For the rest, how the Lord threatens those who judge, he himself shows. For
with what judgment you have judged, it will be judged concerning you. So he
did not forbid judging, but taught it. Whence the apostle also judges, and
indeed in a case of fornication, that a man of this kind is to be handed over
to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, rebuking even that brothers were
not being judged among the saints. For adding he says, What is it to me to
judge those who are outside? But you forgive, that it may be forgiven you by
God. Sins are cleansed which one has committed against a brother, not against
God. Finally we profess in prayer that we will forgive debtors. But it is not
fitting any further to stretch a contentious rope of this kind from the
authority of the scriptures by alternate pulling into opposite directions, so
that these seem to tighten the reins of discipline, those to loosen them, as
if of an uncertain thing, and those to lay down the aid of repentance through
mildness, these to deny it through austerity. Further, the authority of
scripture will stand in its own bounds without mutual opposition. And the aid
of repentance is determined by its own conditions without a passive
concession, and first the very causes of it are distinguished without a
confused proposition. We assign sins as the causes of repentance. These we
divide into two issues. Some will be remissible, others irremissible.
According to which it is doubtful to no one that some deserve chastisement,
others damnation. Every sin is either discharged by pardon or by punishment,
pardon from chastisement, punishment from damnation. Concerning this
difference we have already also sent ahead certain altercations of the
scriptures, retaining sins on this side, dismissing them on that. But John
also will teach: If anyone knows that his brother is sinning a sin not unto
death, he will ask, and life will be given him; because he does not sin unto
death, this will be remissible. There is a sin unto death; I do not say that
anyone should ask for that, this will be irremissible. So where there is force
of asking, there also of remission; where not even of asking, there equally
not of remission. According to this difference of sins the condition of
repentance also is discriminated. One will be that which can obtain pardon,
namely in a remissible sin, another which can by no means obtain it, namely in
an irremissible sin. And it remains specially to examine concerning the status
of adultery and fornication, into which part of sins they ought to be reduced.

But first I will decide an intervening answer from the other side to that kind
of repentance which we are just now defining as lacking pardon. For if, they
say, some repentance lacks pardon, then it is not even to be done by you at
all. For nothing is to be done in vain. Further, repentance will be done in
vain if it lacks pardon. But every repentance is to be done. Therefore let
every repentance obtain pardon, lest it be done in vain, because it will not
be to be done if it is done in vain. Further, it is done in vain if it will
lack pardon. They oppose us with reason, then, since they have usurped into
their own power the fruit of this repentance too, that is, pardon, so far as
concerns them, from whom human peace is obtained. But so far as concerns us,
who remember that the Lord alone grants sins, and mortal ones at that, it will
not be done in vain. For, sent back to the Lord and thereafter prostrate
before him, it will work pardon all the more by this very fact, that it
entreats it from God alone, that it does not believe human peace enough for
its sin, that it prefers to blush before the church rather than to
communicate. For it stands at her doors, and by the example of its own brand
warns the rest, and calls the tears of the brothers to itself as well, and
returns having transacted more up to that point, namely compassion rather than
communication. And if it does not reap peace here, it sows it with the Lord.
It does not lose the fruit, but prepares it. It will not be idle of profit if
it has not been idle of duty. So neither is a repentance of this kind vain,
nor is a discipline of this kind hard. Both honor God. That, by flattering
nothing to itself, will more easily obtain; this, by assuming nothing to
itself, will more fully help.

We can therefore, the distinction of repentance having been charged, go back
now to the census of the sins themselves, whether they are such as can obtain
pardon from men. First, that we name adultery and fornication, usage demands.
Faith too has a familiarity with certain names. So in every little work we
keep usage. For the rest, if I say adultery and if I say stuprum, it will be
one charge of contaminated flesh. For it does not matter whether one attacks
another's married woman or a widow, so long as it is not his own woman; just
as it does not matter by places whether modesty is slaughtered in chambers or
in towers. Every homicide even outside a wood is robbery. So also wherever or
in whatsoever way he adulterates and debauches himself who uses himself
otherwise than by marriage. Therefore among us even hidden unions, that is,
not first professed before the church, run the risk of being judged next to
adultery and fornication. Nor let those joined from that point elude the crime
under the pretext of matrimony. But the remaining impious furies of lusts,
both against bodies and against sexes beyond the laws of nature, we remove not
only from the threshold but from every roof of the church, because they are
not sins, but monsters.

Therefore adultery, which is also the matter of fornication, of how great a
crime it is to be valued according to the work of the crime, the first law of
God is at hand. Since after the superstition of other gods was forbidden and
the fabrication of the idols themselves, after the veneration of the sabbath
was commended, after a religion toward parents second from God was commanded,
(??)o other precept did he lay under for confirming and warning in such titles
than, You shall not commit adultery. For after spiritual chastity and
holiness, bodily integrity followed. And this too he fortified, at once
forbidding its enemy, adultery. Understand already what sort of sin it is,
whose restraint he ordered after idolatry. Nothing second is far from the
first. Nothing is so next to the first as the second. What is done from the
first is in a way another first. Therefore adultery is akin to idolatry. For
idolatry too, often thrown in the people's teeth under the name of adultery
and of fornication, will also be joined to it by lot, as also by series; it
will also cohere with it by damnation, as also by arrangement. Still more,
putting first, You shall not commit adultery, he adds, You shall not kill. He
has honored adultery, at any rate, which he sets before homicide, on the first
front of the most holy law, in the first titles of the heavenly edict, marked
of course by the proscription of the principal sins. From the place you may
know the measure, from the order the status, from the neighborhood the desert
of each. There is even a dignity of evil, which is placed at the top or in the
middle of the worst. I see a certain pomp and platform of adultery, on this
side the leadership of preceding idolatry, on that the company of following
homicide. Between two most eminent peaks of crimes it has without doubt sat
worthy, and through the middle of them, as if a vacant place, it has filled it
with equal authority of crime. Who will tear it, shut in by such flanks,
propped round by such ribs, from the body of those that cohere, from the knot
of neighboring crimes, from the embrace of kindred wickednesses, so as to set
it alone apart for the fruit of repentance? Will not idolatry on this side,
homicide on that, hold it, and if there is any voice, will they not cry back,
This wedge is ours, this is our joint? We are measured from idolatry; we are
joined by that which distinguishes; we are united to that which flashes out
from the middle; divine scripture has embodied us, the letters themselves are
our glue, now not even it can be without us.

I indeed, idolatry, most often supply the occasion of adultery. My groves
know, and my mountains, and living waters, and the very temples in the cities,
how much we procure for the overthrow of modesty. I too, homicide, sometimes
work out adultery. To omit tragedies, poisoners know today, magicians know,
how many paramours I avenge, how many rivalries I defend, how many keepers,
how many informers, how many accomplices I take away. Midwives also know how
many adulterous conceptions are slaughtered. Even among Christians there is no
adultery without us. There idolatries are, where there is the matter of
unclean spirits; there homicide also is, where a man, when he is defiled, is
killed. Therefore either the aids of repentance will not fit them, or they
will fit us also. Either we hold it, or we follow. These things the very facts
speak. If the facts fail in voice, the idolater stands by, the homicide stands
by, in the midst of them the adulterer also stands by. Equally they sit in the
duty of repentance, they bristle in sackcloth and ashes, they groan with the
same weeping, they canvass with the same prayers, they entreat on the same
knees, they call on the same mother. What are you doing, most soft and most
humane discipline? Either you will have to be this to all of them (for blessed
are the peacemakers), or if not to all, you will have to be ours. Do you
indeed damn the idolater and the homicide once for all, but take the adulterer
out from the middle, the successor of the idolater, the predecessor of the
homicide, the colleague of both? It is respect of persons: you have left the
more pitiable repentances.

Plainly, if you show from what patronages of heavenly examples and precepts
you throw open the door of repentance to adultery alone and in it to
fornication also, our contest will now fight to this line. Yet I must
prescribe you a form, that you not put out a hand to old things, that you not
look back behind. For old things have passed away according to Isaiah, and the
renewal has already been renewed according to Jeremiah, and forgetting the
things behind we stretch to the things before according to the apostle, and
the law and the prophets until John according to the Lord. For even if we have
just now begun from the law in demonstrating adultery, we do so with reason
from that status of the law which Christ did not dissolve but fulfilled. For
the burdens of the law were until John, not the remedies. The yokes of works
have been thrown off, not those of disciplines. Liberty in Christ has not done
injury to innocence. The whole law remains of piety, of holiness, of humanity,
of truth, of chastity, of justice, of mercy, of benevolence, of modesty. In
which law Blessed is the man who will meditate day and night. Of which the
same David again: The law of the Lord is blameless, converting turning souls;
the rights of the Lord are straight, delighting hearts; the precept of the
Lord shining far(??), enlightening the eyes. So also the apostle: Therefore
the law indeed is holy, and the precept holy and most good; namely: You shall
not commit adultery. But also above: Do we then make void the law through
faith? Far from it, but we establish the law, namely in those things which
even now in the new testament are forbidden by a still more piled precept.
For, You shall not commit adultery, He who has looked to lust has already
committed adultery in his heart, and for, You shall not kill, He who has said
to his brother Racha will be guilty of gehenna. Ask whether the law of not
committing adultery is safe, to which has been added not even lusting.

For the rest, if any examples applaud in your bosom, they will not be set
against this discipline which we defend. For in vain has the law been built
over, condemning the origins of sins also, that is, lusts and wills, no less
than deeds, if today pardon will be granted to adultery because it was also
granted sometime. To what profit is a fuller discipline constrained today,
unless that it may perhaps be indulged by a greater pandering of yours? You
will therefore give pardon also to the idolater and to every apostate, because
we find the people itself, so often guilty of those things, so often restored
of old. You will communicate even with the homicide, because Ahab also by
entreaty wiped out the blood of Naboth, and David by confession purged the
slaughter of Uriah together with its cause, adultery. You will already also
forgive incests because of Lot, and fornications with incest because of Judah,
and foul marriages from prostitution because of Hosea, and not only frequent
ones but even several at once, because of our fathers. For it is of course
worthy that grace too should now be equalized around all things formerly
indulged, if from some ancient example the pardon of adultery is claimed. We
too have examples of the same antiquity for our opinion, not only of
fornication not indulged but even of judgment presented at once. And it is
enough that so great a number, twenty-four thousand of a people fornicating
with the daughters of Midian, fell by one plague.

But I prefer to draw discipline from Christ to the glory of Christ. Let former
times have had, if the psychics will, even a power of every immodesty. Let the
flesh have played before Christ, or rather have perished before it was sought
by its Lord: it was not yet worthy of the gift of salvation, not yet fit for
the duty of holiness. It was still being counted in Adam with its vice, easily
lusting after what it had seen as fair, and looking down to lower things, and
keeping the itch from fig leaves. The poison of lust was clinging everywhere
(this is what filth from milk has), a thing which not even the waters
themselves had yet washed. But when the Word of God descended into flesh, not
even opened by marriage, and the Word was made flesh, not even to be opened by
marriage, which would go to a wood not of incontinence but of endurance, which
would taste from it not something sweet but something bitter, which would
belong not to the lower regions but to heaven, which would be girt not with
the fronds of wantonness but with the flowers of sanctity, which would hand
its cleansings to the waters, from then on whatever flesh in Christ has loosed
the remaining old filths is already another thing, a new thing emerges, no
longer from the slime of seed, not from the dung of lust, but from pure water
and a clean spirit. Why then do you excuse it from the old? It was not being
called the body of Christ, not the members of Christ, not the temple of God,
when it was obtaining pardon of adultery. Therefore if from the time when it
changed its status and, dipped into Christ, put on Christ and was redeemed at
a great price, namely the blood of the Lord and Lamb, you hold any example or
precept or form or sentence of fornication and adultery indulged or to be
indulged, you have also from us a definition of the time from which the age of
the question is to be counted.

You may begin from the parables, where there is the lost sheep sought by the
Lord and brought back on his shoulders. Let the very pictures of your cups
come forward, if even in them the interpretation of that beast will shine
through, whether it aligns with a Christian or a pagan sinner concerning
restoration. For we prescribe from the discipline of nature, from the law of
ear and tongue, from the soundness of mind, that those things always be
answered which are provoked, that is, to those things which provoke. What was
provoking, I suppose, was that the Pharisees were muttering in indignation
that the Lord was admitting publicans and pagan sinners and sharing food with
them. To this, when the Lord had figured the restoration of the lost beast, to
whom else is it to be believed that he configured it than to the lost pagan,
of whom the matter was, not of the Christian, of whom as yet there was none?
Or what sort of thing is it, that the Lord, as if a quibbler of an answer,
omitting the present case which he ought to have struck back, should labor
over a future one? But a sheep is properly a Christian, and the Lord's flock
the people of the church, and the good shepherd Christ, and therefore the
Christian is to be understood in the sheep, who has wandered from the flock of
the church. You wish then that the Lord answered nothing to the muttering of
the Pharisees, but to your presumption. And yet you will have to claim it so
as to deny that what you think fits a Christian fits a pagan. Tell me, is not
the whole race of men one flock of God? Is not the same God both Lord and
shepherd of all nations? Who perishes more from God than the pagan, so long as
he wanders? Who is more sought by God than the pagan, when he is called back
by Christ? Finally this order goes first in pagans; since Christians are not
otherwise made from pagans unless they have first been lost and sought by God
and brought back by Christ. So this order too ought to be kept, that we
interpret such a thing of those in whom it is first. But you, I suppose, would
want this, that he make the sheep lost not from the flock but from a chest or
a cupboard. Thus even if he says the remaining number of pagans is just, he
does not therefore show them to be Christians, dealing with Jews and at that
very moment beating them because they were indignant at the hope of the
pagans: but that he might express against the envy of the Pharisees his grace
and goodwill even toward one pagan, he set the salvation of one sinner from
repentance before theirs from justice. Or were the Jews not just, and those
for whom there would be no need of repentance, having the helms of discipline
and the instruments of fear, the law and the prophets? He therefore placed
them in the parable, even if not such as they were, but such as they ought to
have been, that they might the more be put to shame, hearing repentance
necessary for others and not for themselves.

In the same way we interpret the parable of the drachma, as provoked from the
same matter, equally of the pagan, even if lost in a house, as if in the
church, even if found at the light of a lamp, as if at the word of God. But
this whole world is one house of all, in which the grace of God shines more on
the pagan, who is found in darkness, than on the Christian, who is already in
the light of God. Finally one error is ascribed both to the sheep and to the
drachma. For if they were already being fashioned of a Christian sinner, after
faith lost both a repeated loss of them and a restoration would be noted.

I will now step down a little from this grade, that I may the more commend it
even by stepping down, when I have in this way too blocked the presumption of
the other side. I grant that a Christian already a sinner is portrayed in
either parable, yet not therefore is he to be affirmed who can be restored by
repentance from the crime of adultery and fornication. For although he is said
to have perished, there will also be a retracting of the kind of perdition,
because both the sheep perished not by dying but by wandering, and the drachma
not by being destroyed but by lying hid. So that which is safe may be said to
have perished. Therefore a faithful man also perishes who has slipped into a
spectacle of chariot-rage and of gladiatorial gore and of scenic foulness and
of athletic vanity, or if in games, in banquets of a secular solemnity, in an
office, in a ministry of another's idolatry he has applied some arts of
curiosity, if he has struck against a word of two-sided denial or of
blasphemy. For such a thing he has been put outside the flock, or he himself
perhaps by anger, swelling, rivalry, or finally, as often happens, by disdain
of chastisement has broken away. He ought to be sought and called back. What
can be recovered does not perish unless it has persisted outside. You will
interpret the parable well if you still call back a living sinner. But who has
not pronounced an adulterer and a fornicator dead at once upon the deed? With
what mouth will you restore a dead man into the flock from the authority of
that parable which does not call back a dead beast?

Finally if you remember the prophets, when shepherds are rebuked, I think it
is the voice of Ezekiel: Shepherds, behold you devour milk and clothe
yourselves with wool; what is strong you have killed, what is weak you have
not healed, what is broken you have not bound, what is driven out you have not
turned, what has perished you have not sought. Does he also reproach
concerning a dead one, that they have not taken care to restore that too into
the flock? Plainly he heaps it on, that they have made the sheep perish and be
eaten by the beasts of the field. Nor can they either perish into death or be
eaten, if they are left. Not that those lost into death and eaten may be taken
up again, according to the example of the drachma also, even within the house
of God, the church, may there be some sins moderate by the measure and weight
of the drachma itself, which, lurking in the same place, soon found in the
same place, may at once in the same place be transacted with the joy of
amendment? But of adultery and fornication it is not a drachma but a talent,
for seeking which there is need not of the point-light of a lamp but of the
spear of the whole sun. As soon as it has appeared, the man is at once
expelled from the church, nor does he remain there, nor does he confer joy on
the finding church, but mourning, nor does he call the congratulation of
neighbors, but the grief of nearby brotherhoods. Therefore this interpretation
of ours too having been joined with theirs, the arguments of both the sheep
and the drachma will look the more to the pagan, inasmuch as they cannot fit
even a Christian of that sin on account of which they are forced by the other
side upon a Christian.

But indeed the same issue deceives most interpreters of parables which very
often happens in inspecting purple garments. When you have thought you have

namely of baptism, namely of the Holy Spirit and thereafter of eternal hope,
having wandered far from the father, has squandered it living as a pagan, if,

who does not know the Lord? Who is safe and sound, except he who knows the
