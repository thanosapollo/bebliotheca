OF GREGORY, BISHOP OF NYSSA, ON "BUT HE WHO COMMITS FORNICATION SINS
AGAINST HIS OWN BODY"
Fearful is the trumpet of the apostolic command, charging the army of
piety with many other things as well, but above all driving it from the
pits of shamefulness, and adding even a soldier's order toward the end.
For "Flee fornication," he says. "Every sin that a man may do is outside
the body." For the soldiers of perceptible wars now use a face-to-face
clash, now flight, and so they outwit the battle-lines. There is also a
war for souls, directed by resistance and by flight. And Paul, knowing
this, as one who leads the army of piety's tactics by either skill, now
commands persistence in the fight: "Stand, having girded your loins with
truth"; now he advises outwitting what makes war by flight: "Flee
fornication." If a war of unbelief attacks, resistance is useful against
it; if a battle-line of deceits threatens, a close engagement against
those antagonists is good; if the bow of slander is brought to bear, a
face-to-face clash against the lie is profitable. =9.212= But when a
fornicating form shoots its arrows, it is good to give one's back and
flee the face-to-face encounter; for fornication hits the mark at the
eyes, and one must remember the general commanding:
Flee fornication. For it has something more to be fled than the other
wicked deeds. For the other kinds of sin seem to spare the flesh of those
who do them, and the deed stops at the one who receives the action. For
example, in robberies the loss is of those robbed alone; in the force of
the envious the passion bursts out upon those envied; in slanders, when
they happen to be believed, the danger is again of those slandered alone;
likewise among those who dare murder, the misfortune is of the one who
received the slaughter. If one goes through every deed of unjust works,
one will find the portion of the wrongdoers gaining, and that of the
wronged suffering loss. But fornication does not know such a division,
nor does it separate the act of the one who suffers from that of the one
who does it, but it overthrows both together into a common dishonor, and
it joins them with a bond of defilement. For the greedy it is possible,
having harmed =9.213= another, to have suffered no harm themselves; for
fornicators it is not possible, having shamed a body, not to be shamed
together with the one shamed; for murderers it is possible, having worked
the murder, not to have been slaughtered together with those slain; for
fornicators it is not possible, having defiled flesh, to be clean of the
defilement. And consider with me Paul's precision on this: "Flee
fornication," he says. Why? Because "Every sin that a man may do is
outside the body" (that is, not injuring the nature of the body, not
contracted together with the outrage of the members, not completed by the
defilement of the flesh, but worked outside the harm of the doer's body).
"But he who commits fornication sins against his own body": not as the
murderer does against another's, keeping his own body unwounded; not as
the greedy man does against another, guarding against harm to his own
flesh; but the fornicator is himself the corrupter of himself, himself
pierced by himself with the dart of dishonor. The thief dares the offense
in order to feed the body; the fornicator, however, attends to the
robbery of his own flesh. A profit-loving calculation stirs the greedy
man to plunder; but fornication is the loss of the body's dignity. For
the envious man, another being glorified prepares the passion; but the
fornicator is the self-worker of his own ill-fame. For what is more
dishonorable than a baggage-bearer of fornication? For every slavery of
sin is without honor (for it dishonors the nobility of the soul), but the
fornicator is a more dishonorable slave of sin: appointed by her to draw
up mire, =9.214= he gathers a heap of defilement and performs an unclean
service. Or is it not terrible to wallow in mud, to be rubbed with
shamefulness, to have a body no different from a rag? For what difference
is there between a rag and a fornicator? He has been torn from the body
of piety; he is corrupted by daily rotting; he is thrown away on the
roads of sin as a useless rag; he lies as a trampling-place for all
demons; upon him the devil wipes off his own putrefaction.
Nor is the evident and clear misfortune of the fornicator less than his
intelligible one. For he is to be fled by households, to be cursed in
encounters, an outrage to those who draw near, a reproach to those who
are hostile, a shame to kinsmen, accursed to those who dwell with him, a
grief to parents, a spectacle to household servants, a laughing tale to
neighbors; eager to marry, he is rejected; after marriage, a suspected
bridegroom; a father hated by children; a counselor easy to despise;
unpleasant when granting favors, more unpleasant when asking; painful
when seen from afar; more wretched when ill; more dishonored when dead.
Paul, seeing fornication as the mother of so great a multitude of evils,
was just now crying out to us, commanding the victorious flight: "Flee
fornication." This voice now reminded me of a temperate young man who won
the prize against Egyptian fornication by flight. And yet many things
were preparing persuasion for the young man: an age that loves pleasure,
the yoke of slavery, a mistress's erotic flattery, continual conversation
of wantonness, a secret summons to intercourse. For there came such a
day, and Joseph went into the house =9.215= to do his work, and there was
no one of those in the house within, and the lady caught hold of his
garments, saying, "Lie with me." Great is the dignity of temperance: it
made the mistress of the slave a slave; for he was being entreated, and
she was entreating: "Lie with me, lie with me." The dart of fornication
was set on fire, but it did not find matter of soul that would burn; it
had its dissolution in the garment, which the shamelessness of
fornication, clasping it, cried: "Lie with me." She bellows the hunger of
fornicating desire, but she rather closed the ears of the temperate man.
For she said, "Lie with me," but temperance proclaimed in reply to the
young man: "Watch with me," and by his deeds he showed the wakefulness;
for his tension did not nod at the flatteries, his judgment did not sleep
at the spells, the sobriety of temperance did not lie down, he was not
slackened by the holds that had seized him, he was not hooked by the
adornments of form, he was not broken by the blandishments of erotic
words, but the voice of the mistress flattering him was more bitter to
him than abuse: "Lie with me." The devil stood ready as bridesman of
adultery and was tightening the garment with the fornicating woman and
sharing in the holds from her side, but he did not know that he was
wrestling with a skilled athlete of temperance, who was well stripping
off her holds. For "leaving his garments in her hands," it says, "he fled
and went out." O nakedness holier than clothing! What then of the frenzy
of Egyptian incontinence? She puts her own evils upon Joseph =9.216= and,
running to her husband, says: "You brought in to us a Hebrew servant to
mock us; for he said to me, your wife who until now has kept your bed
temperately, 'I will lie with you.' And when I raised my voice and cried
out, he left his garments with me and fled and went out." Again Joseph is
slandered through a garment; his brothers earlier, taking his tunic,
slandered him through it as eaten by a wild beast, wickedly; now she,
taking the tunic, accuses him as a fornicator. Fitting for Joseph is the
Master's voice: "They divided my garments among themselves and upon my
clothing they cast a lie." Sweet to the army of the temperate is the
rhetoric of temperance, but toilsome to the weakness of the flesh. O the
just protection of God over Joseph! He did not honor Joseph before the
trials, but he showed through dreams what was to come, teaching that from
far off he was preparing glory for the righteous, and he allowed the
trials to test the young man, shutting out the voices of those who love
to blame; for if Joseph had not given a proof, the fault-lovers would
have said that the things done were of blind chance: Joseph rules
Egyptians, a young boy is master of barbarians; having shown what virtue?
Having obtained this for what virtue? So that these things might not be
said about the righteous man, God, anticipating, =9.217= allows the
trials against him, so that from them testimony might come to the
righteous man and sew shut the mouths of those who love to blame.
Let us therefore turn away the shots that come from a fornicating form;
let us shut the gaze against wanton acts; let the disorders of pleasures
be laughed at among us; let temperance garrison the flesh; let purity lie
in the members; let us be exercised in thoughts that love the beautiful;
let us shine with the rays of works; let us gleam round about with a life
that has been scoured clean; let us keep the body a pure temple of the
Spirit's dwelling; let us inscribe it with an inscription that cries the
command fearful to the unchaste: "If anyone destroys the temple of God,
God will destroy him." I would have wished not to be separated from you
even for a little; for what is sweeter to a father than the company of
beloved sons? But since the Word calls to the contests of piety, I must
run to the wrestling-grounds of the church, having taken prayers as
fellow-workers. But this I ask of your love: keep the good order of the
church, and if some throw in disturbances, conquer the disturbances by
long-suffering; for the correction of the tumults will not be long in
coming. Do not be disturbed by rumors, do not be moved by idle talk, but
send prayers with us as fellow-travelers, so that, strengthened by your
prayers, we may say at every time, helped by the divine power: "I can do
all things in Christ who strengthens me, to whom be the glory unto the
ages of ages, amen."
