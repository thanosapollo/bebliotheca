OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA, A FUNERAL ORATION FOR THE EMPRESS FLACILLA
The faithful and prudent steward (for I take my opening from what has been
read from the divine Gospel), whom the Lord set over this household to give in
due season the measure of grain to those under his stewardship, having well in
the time before this condemned the word to speechlessness, having well
perceived the greatness of the calamity and honored the mourning with silence,
I do not know how in the present assembly he again brings the word back to the
church, himself undoing his own vote against the word. And though I greatly
admired the teacher in many things for his understanding, in this especially I
admired him still more, as having well silenced the word in the calamity. For
silence seems to me a fitting and suitable medicine for those who mourn,
digesting by quiet, with time and dejection, the swelling of the soul. For if
anyone, while the passion is still heating the soul, should stir up the word,
=9.476= the wound of grief will become harder to heal, being scraped by the
memory of the painful things as by certain thorns.

If it is not too bold for me also to correct something of the teacher's,
perhaps it was well that silence should prevail over us until now, lest the
word, dragged down toward the passion, should pain the hearing. For the time
in between is not yet so great as to accustom the mind to the evil. Still new
in the soul is the passion (and perhaps the pain will be forever new to life),
still our heart is troubled and, like some sea swelling with the hurricane of
the calamity, is turned up from the depths, still the reasonings swell,
boiling up at the memory of the evils. When then the soul is unstable in such
a surge, how is it possible to lead the word forward in a straight course,
storm-tossed as it is by the passion of grief as by some squall? But since it
is necessary to obey him who commands, I do not know how I shall use the word;
for I cannot find how to aim at the mind of the teacher. Or perhaps he wishes
to grant something even to the passion, and by the more passionate of words to
stir up the tear in the church? And if this is what he intends, he does this
also rightly, at least in my judgment. For it is altogether necessary, just as
we are eager for the enjoyment of good things, so also to be fittingly
disposed toward the grievous among the things that befall us. For this
Ecclesiastes also advises: A time, he says, to laugh and a time to weep. For
we learn through these things that the soul too must be disposed
correspondingly to what lies before it. Do affairs run with the stream?
Gladness is in season. Has the bright been changed into dejection? It is
fitting to change =9.477= good cheer also into a tear. For just as laughter
becomes a sign of the brightness within, so also the pain in the heart is
interpreted by laments, and the tear becomes as it were the blood of the
wounds of the soul. This also the Proverb of Solomon says, that When the heart
is glad the face blooms, but when the soul is in griefs it looks sullen.
Therefore there is every necessity that the word put on the same gloom with
the disposition of the heart. And would that it were possible to find some
such words as the great Jeremiah once lamented over the calamity of the
Israelites. For the present things are more worthy of those than anything of
the ancient that is remembered among the gloomy. Hard are the narratives of
Job; but what need is there to set against so great an evil the easily
numbered sufferings of one house? And even if you go through the great and
more common of evils, earthquakes and wars and floods and chasms, these too
are small if they should be judged against the present. Why? Because the
calamity of war does not touch the whole inhabited world at once, but some
part of it is at war, and some is at peace. Again some part a thunderbolt has
burned, or the water has flooded, or it has been swallowed by the chasm. But
the present evil is a blow upon the whole inhabited world at once. It is not
one nation or one city that one must bewail, but it is fitting perhaps to
utter the voice of Nebuchadnezzar, which he made toward those under his hand:
To you I speak, peoples, tribes, tongues. Or rather grant me to add something
to the Assyrian proclamation and more loudly =9.478= to cry the calamity, and
to say, as one might cry out upon a stage: O cities and peoples and nations
and the whole earth and as much of the sea as is navigable and as much as is
inhabited, O as much of all the inhabited world among us as is steered by the
scepter of the empire, O all men from every side, groan together at the
passion, strike up together the symphony of the lament, bewail together the
loss of all. Or do you wish, as far as I am able, that I should recount the
loss to you as well? Human nature in our generation brought forth, having gone
beyond her own bounds and overcome the accustomed measures; nature brought
forth, or rather the Lord of nature, a human soul in a woman's body beyond
almost all the former patterns of virtue, in whom every virtue of body and
every virtue of soul, running together, showed to human life an unbelievable
wonder, how great a confluence of goods one soul contained in one body. And
that the good fortune of the generation might become most manifest to all, she
is raised to the high throne of the empire, so that, like the sun, from the
high dignity she might shine upon the whole inhabited world with the rays of
the virtues. And having been joined to him who by divine vote was set over the
whole inhabited world, in partnership of life and of empire, she made the
subject people blessed through herself, truly, as the Scripture says, becoming
a help =9.479= to him toward every good. If it was a time of humanity, she
either ran with him toward this good or even ran before him. The yoke was
equal from either side in the scale of humanity. And there bear witness to the
word both the former things that surpass number and the proclamations of the
present, which we have now heard from the herald of the truth.

If you seek piety, the course toward piety was common to both; if providence,
if justice, if any other of the things pursued toward the better, all was in
rivalry, they striving to outdo one another in good deeds, and there was no
one defeated. There was a kind of equal grace of both upon one another: she
had as the prize of virtue him who was set over the inhabited world, and he
counted the authority of earth and sea a small thing in comparison with the
good fortune in her. They gave equal joys to one another, seeing one another
and being seen by one another. He being such as he is (for what beauty could
one show beyond what appears? and may what is seen last even unto the life of
descendants), but her, of what sort she was, it is not possible to exhibit by
speech. For no likeness of her has been left behind with accuracy by art, but
even if something has been made in paintings or in molded figures, all is more
deficient than the truth.

Such are the narratives even up to this point. But those that follow upon
these, of what sort: again I am compelled to cry out, and pardon me =9.480= as
I cry the passion beyond measure. O Thrace, the name to be fled, O unfortunate
nation known from calamities, O you who formerly were laid waste by hostile
fire in the inroads of the barbarians, but now have received in yourself the
sum of the common calamity. From there the good is snatched away, there Envy
revelled against the empire, there the shipwreck of the inhabited world took
place, there, as in a surge, having struck against the mole, we sank into the
depth of grief. O that wicked sojourn abroad, which did not give back the
return, O bitter waters, whose springs she longed for, as she ought not, O
place in which the passion occurred, named for the dark of the moon because of
the passion. (For I hear that according to their ancestral tongue the place is
named Scotoume.) There the lamp was darkened, there the light was quenched,
there the rays of the virtues were dimmed.

Gone is the ornament of the empire, the rudder of justice, the image of
humanity, or rather the archetype itself. The pattern of wifely love has been
taken away, the pure dedicated offering of chastity, the approachable dignity,
the gentleness not to be despised, the lofty humility, the outspoken modesty,
the blended harmony of the goods. Gone is the zeal of the faith, the pillar of
the church, the adornment of the altars, the wealth of the poor, the
all-sufficient right hand, the common harbor of those worn down. Let virginity
mourn, let widowhood lament, let orphanhood wail, let them know what they had
now that they do not have it. Or rather, what need is there for me to divide
the lament by parts and ranks? Let the whole generation groan, sending up from
the midst of the heart the groan from the deep =9.481=. Let the priesthood
itself also mourn with us, Envy having stripped away the common adornment. Is
it too bold to say the word of the prophet? Why have you rejected us, O God,
unto the end, and why has your wrath been kindled against the sheep of your
pasture? Of what sins are we paying the penalties? For what are we being
scourged with successive calamities? Or perhaps because the impiety of the
various heresies has abounded, this vote has prevailed against us? For you see
with what evils we have met in a short time. Not yet having drawn breath after
the former blow, not yet having wiped the tear from the eyes, we have again
come to be in so great a calamity: then we lamented the newly blooming flower,
now the shoot itself from which the flower sprang; then the hoped-for season,
now that which had come to its prime; then the good that was expected, now
that which had come into experience.

Will you pardon me, brothers, if through the passion I should talk some
nonsense? Perhaps, as the apostle says, even the creation itself groaned
together with our evil. I will remind you of the things that happened, and I
think that the many will agree with what is said. When the empress was being
borne to the city, covered with gold and purple (and it was a bier that was
bearing her), and every rank and every age, poured forth from the city,
cramped the whole open country from the multitude, all on foot, even those
outstanding in ranks, escorting in procession =9.482= the passion (you
remember altogether how the sun covered its own rays with the clouds, that it
might not perhaps see with pure light the empress driving into the city in
such a form, not upon some chariot or gold-bound carriage according to the
imperial array, glorying in the bodyguards, but covered in a coffin, her form
hidden by that gloomy covering, a sight both dreadful and pitiable, set forth
as an occasion of tears for those who met her, and how the whole people of
those gathered, both the stranger and the native, received her as she entered
not with acclamations but with laments), then the air also grew sullen in
mourning, wrapping the gloom about itself as some mourning garment, and the
clouds too, as far as was possible for them, wept upon it, sending forth upon
the passion soft drops instead of tears. Or are these things truly nonsense
and not even worthy to be said? For if something of this kind also happened in
the creation, such as to mark the calamity, it did not happen from the
creation at all, but from the Master of the creation, honoring through what he
did the death of the holy woman. For precious, he says, in the sight of the
Lord is the death of his holy ones. And I saw then another sight more
paradoxical than the things said: I saw a double rain, one from the air and
one from the tears, flowing down upon the earth, and the shower from the eyes
was not more deficient than that from the clouds. For among so many myriads of
those present there was no eye that was not wetting the earth with the drops
of tears.

But perhaps we have not aimed well at the mind of the teacher, having gone
deeper into the gloomy things than was needed. =9.483= For perhaps he wishes
to heal the hearing rather than to pain it, and we have now done the contrary,
as if some physician, taking a wounded man, should not only neglect the
treatment, but should further wear down the one in pain with certain corrosive
drugs. Therefore the oily word must be poured upon the swelling wound; for the
evangelical healing too knows how to mix the oil with the astringency of the
wine. Let us then incline toward you, taking from Scripture the flask of oil,
as far as it is possible, turning the gloom of what has been said back into
consolation. But let no one disbelieve me in what is said, even if it be
paradoxical. There is, brothers, the good that we seek; it is, and it has not
perished. Or rather I have said something smaller than the truth. For not only
is the good, but it is also in higher things than before. Do you seek the
empress? She has her dwelling in the palaces. But do you long to know this
with the eye? It is not permitted you to be curious about the sight of an
empress. Fearful around her is the guard of the spearmen, not these spearmen,
I say, whose weapon is iron, but those armed with the flaming sword, whose
form the sight of men does not receive. Her dwelling is in the secret places
of the kingdom. Then you will see, when you too peer forth =9.484= from the
body; for it is not otherwise possible to come within the inner shrines of the
kingdom, unless one has parted the veil of the flesh. Or do you think it
better to share in life through the flesh? Then let the divine apostle school
you, he who has shared in the unspeakable mysteries of paradise.

What does he say concerning this life here, speaking perhaps from the common
lot of men? Wretched man that I am: who will deliver me from this body of
death? Why does he say this? Because To depart and to be with Christ is far
better, he says. And what of the great David, who plumes himself with so great
power, who has everything toward pleasure and, according to his authority, for
enjoyment? Is he not cramped by this life? Does he not name this life here a
prison? Does he not cry to the Lord, Bring my soul out of prison? Does he not
chafe at the prolongation of life, saying, Woe is me, that my sojourning has
been lengthened? Or did the saints not know how to distinguish the good from
the worse, and for this reason think the departure from the body more
honorable for the soul? But you, tell me, what good do you see in this life?
Consider in what things life is beheld. I do not say to you the voice of the
prophet, that All flesh is grass; for that one rather dignifies by the example
the misery of our nature. For perhaps it were better to be grass rather than
what it is. Why? Because grass has no unpleasantness from nature, but our
flesh is a workshop of smell, making useless everything taken into corruption.
=9.485= And to be subject the whole time to the service of the belly, of what
punishment is it not more grievous? For you see this perpetual tax-collector,
I mean the belly, how great a necessity of exaction it brings upon us each
day, to whom even if we ever pay in advance more than the appointed amount, we
have prepaid nothing of the next debt. Do we not, after the likeness of the
beasts that toil in the mill-house, go round the mill of life with covered
eyes, always circling through the same things and turning back upon the same?
Shall I tell you this circular period? Appetite, satiety, sleep, waking,
emptying, filling. Always from those come these, and from these those, and
again these, and never, going round in a circle, do we cease, until we come to
be outside the mill-house. Well does Solomon name this life here a jar bored
through and a house belonging to another. For truly it is a house belonging to
another and not ours, because it is not in our power either when we wish or
for as long as we long to be in it. But we are also brought in as we do not
know, and we are evicted when we do not know.

And the riddle of the jar you will understand if you look to the unfillable
nature of the desires. Do you see how men pour upon themselves honors, powers,
glories, and all such things? But what is thrown in flows away underneath and
does not remain with the one who has it. For the zeal concerning glory and
power and honor is always at work, but the jar of desire remains unfilled. And
what of the love of money? =9.486= Is it not truly a jar bored through,
flowing out through the whole bottom, which, even if you pour the whole sea
upon it, cannot by nature be filled? What then is grievous if the blessed
woman has been separated from the evils of life, and, having cast off the
filth of the body as if some rheum, migrates with a pure soul toward the
undefiled life, in which deceit does not hold citizenship, slander is not
believed, flattery has no place, falsehood is not mixed in, and pleasure and
grief and fear and rashness and poverty and wealth and slavery and lordship
and all such unevenness of life have been banished as far as possible from
that life? Pain and grief and sighing have fled from there, as the prophet
says. And in place of these, what? Impassibility, blessedness, alienation from
every evil, converse of angels, vision of the invisible, participation of God,
gladness that has no end. Is it then fitting to grieve concerning the empress,
having learned what things she has exchanged for what? She left an earthly
kingdom, but she has taken hold of the heavenly. She laid aside the crown of
stones, but she has put on =9.487= the crown of glory. She stripped off the
purple, but she has put on Christ. This is the truly imperial and precious
garment.

The purple here I hear is dyed with the blood of a certain sea-shell, but the
blood of Christ makes the purple above to shine. You have seen how great the
difference in the garment. Do you wish to be persuaded that she is among
those? Read the Gospel. Come, you blessed of my Father (the Judge says these
things to those on the right), inherit the kingdom prepared for you. Prepared
by whom? Which you prepared for yourselves, he says, through the works. How? I
hungered, I thirsted, I was a stranger, naked, sick, in prison. Inasmuch as
you did it to one of these least, you did it to me. If then the zeal
concerning these things becomes the procurer of a kingdom, number, if indeed
it is possible to number them out, how many were covered by the garments from
her, how many were fed by that great right hand, how many of the imprisoned
were deemed worthy not of visitation only but of complete release. And if to
visit the imprisoned procures a kingdom, to free from punishment is clearly
worthy of greater honor, if indeed there is anything higher than a kingdom.
But her praise does not stop at these things. For she even goes beyond the
things commanded in her achievements. How many through her came to know in
themselves the grace of the resurrection, who having died by the laws and
received the sentence unto death were again through her called back to life.
The testimony of what has been said is before the eyes. =9.488= You saw beside
the altar a youth who had despaired of salvation, you saw a poor woman
lamenting over the condemnation of a brother, you heard him who proclaims the
good things to the church, how by the memory of the empress the gloomy
sentence of death is dissolved into life. Are these things alone? And the
humility, where shall we set it, which the Scripture makes more honorable than
every achievement according to virtue? She who, jointly holding the reins of
so great a rule with the great emperor, while every power bowed, so many
nations paid tribute, and earth and sea each brought gifts from their own, did
not give pride a passage against herself, always looking toward herself, not
toward the things outside herself. For this reason she becomes heir of the
beatitude, having purchased the true height through the temporary humility.
Shall I say something also as a proof of her wifely love? When the bodily
union was being dissolved, the precious among the goods belonging to them had
of necessity to come to a division. How then did she make the distribution?
There being three children (for these are the chief of the goods), she left
the males to the father, so that they might be the supports of the empire
=9.489=, and of her own portion she has made only the daughter. You see how
both considerate and affectionate, having yielded the greater share in the
precious things to her husband.

But having added this which most of all must be said by us, I will bring the
word to a close. Hatred of the idols is common to all who share in the faith,
but it was peculiar to her to abhor the Arian unbelief in the same way as
idolatry. For those who think the divine to be in the creation she thought to
be no less impious than those who make idols of the materials, judging this
well and piously. For he who worships the creature, even if he does this in
the name of Christ, is an idolater, putting the name Christ upon the idol. For
this reason, having learned that there is no recent god, she worshipped one
Godhead, that which is glorified in Father and Son and Holy Spirit. By this
faith she was grown, by this she came to her prime, to this she committed her
spirit, by this she was brought to the bosom of Abraham the father of the
faith, beside the fountain of paradise (whose drop does not come upon the
unbelieving), under the shadow of the tree of life planted by the outgoings of
the =9.490= waters, of which may we also be deemed worthy in Christ Jesus our
Lord, to whom be the glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
