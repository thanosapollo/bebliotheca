* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Having already written several books, not in a historian's style nor in an
orator's, but in a pedestrian address, we have come to that series of times in
which, through the years when Gallienus and Valerian held the state, thirty
tyrants arose, Valerian being occupied with the great necessities of the
Persian war, when not only men but even women held Gallienus in contempt, as
will be proved in their proper

places. But since there was such obscurity of those men who flocked to empire
from diverse parts of the world that not much about them can either be said by
the learned or sought out, then by all

historians who have written in Greek and Latin some are so passed over that
even their

names are not often mentioned, lastly since various things have been handed
down by most writers about them, I have gathered them into one little book,
and indeed a short one, especially since it is agreed that most things about
them have already been said in the life of Valerian or of Gallienus and need
not, however, be repeated.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

** Cyriades
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

This man, fleeing his father Cyriades, being rich and noble, since by his
luxury and ruined habits he weighed on the holy old man, after snatching a
great part of gold, and also an infinite

weight of silver, made for the Persians, and from there joined and allied to
King Sapor, after he had been the encourager of bringing war on the Romans,
first drew Odomastes, then Sapor, onto Roman soil; Antioch also

being taken, and Caesarea, he earned the Caesarean name, and from there was
called Augustus, and while he shook the whole East with terror of his strength
or of his daring, but after he had slain his father (which other historians
deny was done), he himself was killed by a plot of his own men, when Valerian
was already coming to the Persian

war. Nor has anything more of history been committed concerning this man which
seems worthy of record, whom a famous desertion and

parricide and a harsh tyranny and

the highest luxury have given to letters.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

** Postumus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h1
:END:

This man was most brave in war, most constant in peace, grave in his whole
life, so much so that Gallienus entrusted his own son Saloninus, placed in
Gaul, to him, as a guardian of his life and habits and as an instructor of
imperial

acts. But, as most assert (which does not fit his character), he later broke
faith

and, Saloninus being slain, took the empire. But as more have more truly
related, since the Gauls hated Gallienus most fiercely, and could not bear
that a boy should rule among them, they named emperor the man who was
governing the entrusted empire, and, soldiers being sent,

they killed the youth; when he was killed, Postumus, received with thanks by
the whole army and by all the Gauls, showed himself such for seven years

that he restored the Gallic provinces, while Gallienus was free for luxury and
cookshops and was growing old in love of a barbarian woman.

War was nevertheless waged by Gallienus against this man then,

when Gallienus was wounded by an arrow, if indeed there was too great a love
toward Postumus in the mind of all the Gallic

peoples, because, all the Germanic nations being driven off, he had recalled
the Roman empire to its former

security. But while he was conducting himself most gravely,

by that custom by which the Gauls are always greedy of revolution, he was
slain with Lollianus acting.

If anyone indeed seeks Postumus's merit, he will understand Valerian's
judgment of him from this letter which that man sent to the Gauls:

/We have made Postumus duke of the trans-Rhenane frontier and governor of
Gaul, a man most worthy of the severity of the Gauls, in whose presence no
soldier in camp, no laws in the forum, no lawsuits on the tribunals, no
dignity in the senate-house, should perish, who keeps for each man what is his
own and proper, a man at whom I marvel before the rest, and who deserves the
place of a prince by right, of whom I hope that you will thank me, but if the
opinion I have of him has deceived me, know that nowhere among the nations is
a man found who can be thoroughly approved. To his son, Postumus by name, I
have given the tribunate of the Vocontii, a youth who will show himself worthy
of his father's habits./

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

** Postumus the Younger
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

Of this man there is almost nothing that can be said, except that, named
Caesar by his father and then Augustus in his honor, he is said to have been
slain with his father, when Lollianus, substituted in Postumus's place, had
taken the empire offered him by the

Gauls. He was, however (which alone is worthy of record), so eloquent in
declamations that his controversies are said to have been inserted into
Quintilian, a reading of even one chapter of whom, at the first front, at once
shows him the sharpest declaimer of the Roman race.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

** Lollianus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1
:END:

By this man's rebellion in Gaul Postumus, the bravest of all men, was slain,
when, Gaul already tottering,

he had formed the empire, through Gallienus's luxury, into the old Roman

state. This man too was indeed most brave, but by the look of rebellion he
held less authority among the Gauls from his own

strength. He was slain, however, by Victorinus, son of Vitruvia or Victoria,
who afterward was called Mother of the Camp and was touched with the name of
Augusta, when she herself, fleeing by herself the mass of so great a weight,
had first conferred the empires on Marius, then on Tetricus and his

son. And Lollianus indeed profited the state not a little, for most of the
cities of Gaul, and also some camps which

Postumus had built for seven years on barbarian soil, and which after Postumus
was killed had been both plundered and burned in a sudden inrush of the
Germans, he

restored to their old state. Then he was killed by his own soldiers, because
he was too much in labor.

So, while Gallienus was losing the state, in Gaul first Postumus, then
Lollianus, Victorinus next, last Tetricus (for of Marius we say nothing) stood
forth as champions

of the Roman name. All of whom I believe were given by heaven, lest, when that
plague of unheard-of luxury was hampered by evils, the Germans should be given
the chance of possessing Roman

soil, who if they had then escaped in that way in which the Goths and Persians
did, with the nations agreeing on Roman soil, this venerable empire of the
Roman

name would have been finished. Lollianus's life, however, is obscure in many
things, as is Postumus's own, but in private; for they lived famous by virtue,
not by the weight of nobility.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

** Victorinus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

When Postumus the elder saw himself attacked by many forces of Gallienus and
that help was needed not only of soldiers but also of another prince, he
called Victorinus, a man of military industry, into a share of the empire and
with him fought against Gallienus.

And when, German auxiliaries being brought in in great numbers, they had drawn
out the wars for a long time, they were beaten. Then, Lollianus also being
slain, Victorinus alone remained in the empire, who himself also, because he
gave his attention to corrupting the marriages of soldiers and of military
men, was struck at Agrippina by a certain actuarius, whose wife he had
debauched, a faction being formed, Victorinus the son having been named Caesar
by his mother Vitruvia or Victoria, who was called Mother of the Camp, and the
boy himself was at once slain, when his father had been killed at Agrippina.

Of this man, because he was most brave and, apart from lust,

an excellent emperor, many things have been said by many, but we think it
enough to set down a part of a certain book of Julius Atherianus,

in which he speaks of Victorinus thus:
/To Victorinus, who ruled the Gallic provinces after Julius Postumus, I think
no one is to be preferred, not Trajan in virtue, not Antoninus in clemency,
not Nerva in gravity, not Vespasian in governing the treasury, not Pertinax or
Severus in the censorship of his whole life and in military severity, but lust
and the desire of womanish pleasure so destroyed all these things that no one
dares to send his virtues into letters, whom it is agreed deserved, in the
judgment of all, to be punished./
Therefore, since writers have had that judgment of Victorinus, I seem to
myself to have said enough of his character.

** Victorinus the Younger
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1

Of this man nothing more has been put into letters than that he was Victoria's
grandson, Victorinus's son, and was named Caesar by his father or by his
grandmother in the same hour in which Victorinus was slain, and at once was
killed by the soldiers

in anger. There exist, lastly, tombs around Agrippina, low, stamped on a short
marble, on which a title

is inscribed:
/Here the two Victorini, tyrants, are laid./

** Marius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h1
:END:

Victorinus, Lollianus, and Postumus being slain, Marius, from a smith, as it
is said, a worker in iron, ruled only three days. What more should be sought
of this man I do not know, except that the shortest empire made him more
notable. For as that consul who held a suffect consulship for six midday hours
was sprinkled by Marcus Tullius with such a joke:
/We have had a consul so severe and so censorial that in his magistracy no one
lunched, no one dined, no one slept,/
so of this man also it may seem able to be said, who on one day was made
emperor, on another was seen to rule, on the third was slain.

And he was indeed a strenuous man and raised through military grades all the
way to empire, whom most called Mamurius,

some Veturius, as being a worker in iron.

But of this man too much, of whom it is enough to have added this, that no
one's hands were stronger either for striking or for pushing,

since he seemed to have sinews in his

fingers, not veins. For he is said even to have pushed back incoming wagons
with his greeting-finger, and to have so dashed the bravest men with one
finger that they hurt as if struck by a blow of wood or of blunter iron. He
crushed many

things by the striking together of two fingers. He was killed by a certain
soldier who, when he had once been in his smithy, was despised by him, whether
when he was a leader

or when he had taken the empire. The slayer is said to have added the words:
/This is the sword which you yourself made./

This man's first address is said to have been of this kind:
/I know, fellow-soldiers, that my former craft can be thrown at me, of which
you are all witnesses, but let each say what he wishes. Would that I may
always work iron, not perish by wine, not by flowers, not by little women, not
by cookshops, as Gallienus does, unworthy of his father and of the nobility of
his line. Let the iron-working craft be thrown at me, so long as foreign
nations too recognize by their disasters that I have handled iron. I will

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

** Ingenuus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

In the consulship of Tuscus and Bassus, when Gallienus was free for wine and
cookshops and had given himself to pimps, mimes, and whores and was losing the
goods of nature by a continuance of luxury, Ingenuus, who then ruled the
Pannonias, was named emperor by the Moesian legions, the rest of the Pannonias
being willing, nor did it seem that the soldiers had better consulted the
state in anything than that, the Sarmatians pressing, an emperor was created

who could by his virtue heal exhausted affairs. The cause, however, of his
then snatching the empire was, lest he be suspected by the emperors, because
he was most brave and necessary to the state and, which vehemently moves those
who rule, most acceptable to the soldiers. But Gallienus, as he was worthless
and ruined, so also, when necessity had compelled, was swift, brave, violent,
cruel. Lastly he beat Ingenuus in a battle held, and, him being killed, raged
most harshly against all the Moesians,

soldiers as well as citizens. Nor did he leave anyone free of his cruelty, so
harsh and truculent that he left most cities empty of the

male sex. Ingenuus is indeed said likewise, the city being taken, to have
plunged himself into water

and so to have ended his life, lest he come into the power of a cruel tyrant.

There exists indeed a letter of Gallienus, which he wrote to Celer Verianus,
by which the excess of his cruelty is shown. Which I have inserted for this
reason, that all may understand that a luxurious man is most cruel, if
necessity demand:

/Gallienus to Verianus. You will not satisfy me if you only kill the armed,
whom chance also could have slain in wars. Every male sex is to be destroyed.
If old men and those not yet of age could be killed without our blame, whoever
has wished ill is to be killed, whoever has spoken ill against me, against
Valerian's son, against the father and brother of so many princes, is to be
killed. Ingenuus has been made emperor. Tear, kill, cut. Understand my mind,
rage with my mind, I who have written these things with my own hand./

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:

** Regalianus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h1
:END:

It was of public fate that in Gallienus's time whoever could leaped forth to
empire. Regalianus, lastly,

holding a command in Illyricum, was made emperor, the Moesians being authors
of the empire, who had been beaten before with Ingenuus, against whose parents
Gallienus

had raged heavily. This man nevertheless did many brave things against the
Sarmatians, but, the Roxolani being authors and the soldiers agreeing and the
provincials fearing lest Gallienus do heavier things again, he was slain.

It may perhaps seem wonderful if it is declared what the origin of his empire
was. For by a deadly joke

he earned kingdoms.

For when certain soldiers were dining with him, there arose a tribune's deputy
who said:
/From where do we believe Regalianus's name is said?/
another at once,
/We believe from a kingdom./
Then a scholar who was present began as if to decline grammatically
and to say,
/Rex, regis, regi, Regalianus./
The soldiers, as that kind of men is prone to what they are thinking,
/Therefore can he be a king?/
likewise another,
/Therefore can he rule us?/
likewise another,
/A god has set on you the name of a king./
Why more? These things being said, when he had gone forth in the morning on
another day, he was hailed emperor by the headquarters. So what to others
either daring or judgment conferred, to this man a joking cleverness
conferred.

He was, which cannot be denied, a man always approved in military

affairs

and already before suspected by Gallienus, because he seemed worthy of empire,
of the Dacian nation, a kinsman, as it is said, of Decebalus

himself. There exists a letter of the deified Claudius, then a private man, in
which he thanks Regalianus, duke of Illyricum, for Illyricum restored, when
all things were perishing by Gallienus's sluggishness. Which I, finding it
among authentic papers, thought should be inserted; for it was public.
/Claudius to Regalianus much greeting. Happy the state which has deserved to
have such a man as you in the camps of war, happy Gallienus, even if no one
reports to him the true things either of the good or of the bad. Bonitus and
Celsus, attendants of our prince, have brought to me of what sort you were in
fighting at Scupi, how many battles you finished in one day and with what
speed. You were worthy of a triumph, if the old times still stood. But why
more? Mindful of a certain man, I would have you conquer more cautiously. I
would have you send me Sarmatian bows and two cloaks, but with clasps, since I
myself have sent of ours./

By this letter is shown what Claudius felt of Regalianus, whose most grave
judgment in his own times it is not doubtful existed.

Nor indeed was this man promoted by Gallienus but by his father Valerian, as
also Claudius and Macrianus and

Ingenuus and Postumus and Aureolus, who were all slain in empire, though they
deserved empire.

This, however, was wonderful in the prince Valerian, that all, whoever he made
dukes, afterward by the soldiers' testimony came to empire, so that it appears
the old emperor in choosing dukes of the state was such as Roman happiness, if
it could have been continued by fate

under a good prince, required. And would that either those who had snatched
empires could have reigned, or that his son had not been longer in the empire,
so that our state would have held itself as it pleased in its own condition.
But Fortune thought she should indulge herself too much, who both took away
good princes with Valerian and reserved Gallienus for the state longer than
was fitting.

** Aureolus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h1
:END:

This man also, ruling the Illyrian armies, in contempt of Gallienus, as all at
that time, forced

by the soldiers, took the empire, and when Macrianus with his son Macrianus
was coming against Gallienus with very many, he captured his army, and some,
corrupted, he bound to his own

faith. And when he had been made from this a strong

emperor, and when Gallienus had tried in vain to storm the brave man,

he made peace with him,

about to fight against Postumus. Most of which things have both been said and
are to be said.

This same Aureolus, Gallienus already being slain, Claudius killed in a battle
held at that bridge which is now named Aureolus's Bridge, and there granted
him, as a tyrant, a rather low tomb. There exists even now a Greek epigram in
this form: The victor after many battles of a tyrant, now happy Claudius,
follows Aureolus with the gift of tombs, with a mortal gift, and by right a
survivor, whom he would have wished to live, if the love of the excellent
soldiery had allowed, which by right denied life to all the unworthy and more
to Aureolus. He nevertheless is clement, who, saving the last things of the
body, dedicates both Aureolus's bridge and his mound.

These verses, translated by a certain grammarian, I have so set that I might
keep faith, not that they could not have been better translated, but that
historical fidelity might be kept, which I have thought should be guarded
before the rest, I who care nothing for what belongs to eloquence. For I have
proposed to bring you the matter, not the words, especially with so great a
plenty of things as in the lives of thirty tyrants at once.

** Macrianus the Younger
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h1
:END:

Many things of this man have been tasted beforehand in his father's empire,
who would never have been made emperor unless it had seemed that his father's
prudence was trusted. Of this man indeed many wonderful things are said which
belong to the fortitude of a youthful age, but what avails fate, or how much
does one man's fortitude avail in wars? For this violent man, with a most
prudent father, by whose merit he had begun to rule, was beaten by Domitianus
and stripped of thirty thousand soldiers (I said above), noble by his mother,
by his father only brave and ready for war and coming from the lowest
soldiering to the highest command in sublime splendor.

** Quietus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14-h1
:END:

This man, as we have said, was Macrianus's son. With his father and brother,
by Ballista's judgment, he was made emperor. But when Odaenathus, who already
long held the East, learned that Macrianus, Quietus's father, with his brother
Macrianus had been beaten by Aureolus, and that the soldiers had passed into
his power, as if he were vindicating Gallienus's party, he slew the youth
together with Ballista, lately prefect.

This youth also was most worthy of the Roman empire, so that he truly seemed
Macrianus's son, also Macrianus's brother, which two, in afflicted affairs,
were able to govern the state.

It does not seem to me that I should pass by saying of the family of the
Macriani, which even today flourishes, that which they have always had as
special. The men have always had Alexander the Great, the Macedonian, carved
on rings and on silver, the women also on hair-nets and bracelets and on rings
and on every kind of ornament, so far that tunics and borders and matrons'
cloaks in his family even today exist which show Alexander's likeness in
varying threads. We saw lately Cornelius Macer, a man of the same family, when
he was giving a dinner in the Temple of Hercules, offer to the pontiff an
electrum bowl which had Alexander's face in the middle and contained the whole
history around it in short and tiny figures, which indeed he ordered to be
carried around to all who were most eager of that great man. Which I have set
down for this reason, because they are said to be helped in every act of
theirs who carry Alexander expressed either in gold or in silver.

** Odaenathus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15-h1
:END:

Unless Odaenathus, prince of the Palmyrenes, Valerian being captured, the
strengths of the Roman state being exhausted, had taken the empire, affairs in
the East would have been lost.

Wherefore, having first taken the royal name, with his wife Zenobia and his
elder son, whose name was Herodes, the younger Herennianus and Timolaus,
having gathered an army, he set out against the Persians. Nisibis first and
most of the East, with all Mesopotamia, he received into his power, then
forced the king himself, beaten, to flee. Last he pursued Sapor and his
children all the way to Ctesiphon, the concubines being captured, great booty
also being captured, and turned to the East, hoping that he could crush
Macrianus, who had begun to rule against Gallienus, but that man having
already set out against Aureolus and against Gallienus. Him being slain, he
killed his son Quietus, Ballista, as most assert, usurping the kingdom, lest
he himself also could be killed. The state of the East therefore being
composed in great part, he was slain by his cousin Maeonius, who himself also
had taken the empire, with his son Herodes, who himself also after the return
from Persia was named emperor with his father. I believe the god was angry at
the state, who, Valerian being slain, did not wish to keep Odaenathus. That
man plainly, with his wife Zenobia, would have restored not only the East,
which he had already reformed to its former state, but also all parts of the
whole world altogether, a man sharp in wars and, as most writers speak, always
famous for a memorable hunt, who from first age spent the sweat of a man's
duty in taking lions and leopards, bears and the other forest animals, and who
always lived in woods and mountains, bearing heat, rains, and all the evils
which the pleasures of hunting contain in themselves.

Hardened by these, he bore sun and dust in the Persian wars, his wife also
being accustomed no otherwise, who by the opinion of many is held to have been
stronger than her husband, a woman the noblest of all eastern women and, as
Cornelius Capitolinus asserts, the most beautiful.

/Valerian to Ragonius Clarus, prefect of Illyricum and of the Gallic
provinces. If there is in you any good fruit, which I know there is, parent
Clarus, you follow Ballista's arrangements. Form the state by these. Do you
see that he does not burden the provincials, that he keeps horses there where
there are fodders, that he assigns the soldiers' grain-supplies there where
there are grains, that he does not force a provincial, not a landholder, to
give grain there where he does not have it, a horse there where he cannot feed
it? Nor is there any better provision than that those things which are born be
spent in their own places, lest they burden the state either by vehicles or by
costs. Galatia abounds in grains, Thrace is stuffed, Illyricum is full; let
the infantry be placed there, although in
Presumably Daphne near Antioch.
Otherwise unknown and probably, like the letter, fictitious.
Thrace even cavalry can winter without harm to the provincials, for much hay
is gathered from the fields. Then wine, bacon, then the other species are to

It is 1 familiar to the people of the Egyptians that as if raging and mad they

** Zenobia
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h30-h1
:END:

All shame is now consumed, if indeed, the state being exhausted, it has come
to this, that while Gallienus acted most worthlessly, even women ruled
excellently. And indeed a foreign woman, Zenobia by name, of whom many things
have already been said, who boasted herself of the line of the Cleopatras and
the Ptolemies, after her husband Odaenathus, with the imperial cloak poured
over her shoulders, adorned in the dress of Dido, a diadem also being
received, in the name of her sons Herennianus and Timolaus ruled longer than
the female sex suffered, if indeed while Gallienus was still ruling the state
the proud woman obtained a royal office, and, Claudius being occupied with the
Gothic wars, at last scarcely, beaten and led in triumph by Aurelian, she
yielded into Roman rights.

There exists a letter of Aurelian which bears testimony to the captive woman.
For when he was blamed by some because, a most brave man, he had triumphed
over a woman as if over some general, letters being sent to the senate and the
Roman people he defended himself by this attestation:
/I hear, conscript fathers,
enim P, def. by Tidner; etiayn Peter;
peregrina
enim, Petschenig, Hohl.
Didonis Salm.; doniis P.
that it is thrown at me that I have not filled a manly office by triumphing
over Zenobia. Those who blame me would praise me enough, if they knew what
that woman is, how prudent in counsels, how constant in arrangements, how
grave toward the soldiers, how generous when necessity demands, how stern when
severity asks. I can say that it is hers that Odaenathus beat the Persians
and, Sapor being put to flight, came all the way to Ctesiphon. I can assert
that the woman was of such fear among the eastern and Egyptian peoples that
the Arabs, the Saracens, the Armenians did not stir themselves. Nor would I
have kept life for her, unless I had known that she had profited the Roman
state much, when she kept the empire of the East for herself or for her
children. Let those therefore have the poisons of their own tongues to whom
nothing is pleasing. For if it is not seemly to have beaten and triumphed over
a woman, what do they say of Gallienus, in whose contempt she ruled the empire
well? What of the deified Claudius, a holy and venerable general, who is said
to have suffered her to rule because he himself was occupied with Gothic
expeditions? And that advisedly and prudently, that she keeping the eastern
bounds of the empire he himself might more securely carry out what he had
begun./
This speech shows what judgment Aurelian had of Zenobia. Whose chastity is
said to have been such that she did not even know her own husband except for
testing conceptions. For when she had lain with him once, waiting for her
monthlies she held herself, if she were pregnant; if not, she again gave the
power for seeking children. She lived with royal pomp. She was adored more in
the Persian manner. She banqueted in the manner of the Persian kings. In the
manner of Roman emperors she went forth to assemblies helmeted, with a purple
border, gems hanging along the last fringe, bound also in the middle with a
cockle as if with a woman's brooch, her arm often bare. She was of a somewhat
swarthy face, of a dark color, with eyes black and living beyond measure, of a
divine spirit, of incredible charm. Such whiteness in her teeth that most
thought she had pearls, not teeth. Her voice was clear and manly. Severity,
where necessity demanded, of tyrants; clemency of good princes, where piety
required. Generous prudently, a keeper of treasures beyond a woman's measure,
she used a wagon, rarely a carriage, a horse more often. She is said, however,
often to have walked three or four miles with the infantry. She hunted with
the eagerness of Spaniards. She often drank with the generals, though at other
times she was sober; she drank also with the Persians and Armenians, that she
might beat them. She used golden gemmed vessels for banquets, already used
Cleopatran ones. In service she had eunuchs of a graver age, girls too rare.
She had ordered her sons to speak Latin, so that they spoke Greek either with
difficulty or rarely. She herself was not everywhere skilled in Latin speech,
but so that she spoke with shame held in; she spoke Egyptian also to a perfect
measure, so skilled in Alexandrian and eastern history that she is said to
have epitomized it; Latin, however, she had read in Greek.

When Aurelian had captured her and, her being brought into his sight, had so
addressed her,
/What is it, Zenobia? Have you dared to insult Roman emperors?/
she is said to have said:
/I know that you are an emperor, who conquer. Gallienus and Aureolus and the
other princes I did not think so. Victoria, believing her like me, wished to
come into a share of the kingdom, if the chance of the places allowed./

She was led therefore through a triumph in such appearance that nothing more
pompous seemed to the Roman people. First already she was adorned with huge
gems, so that she labored under the weight of the ornaments. For the most
brave woman is said very often to have resisted, saying that she could not
bear the weights of the gems. Her feet besides were bound with gold, her hands
also with golden chains, nor was a golden bond wanting to her neck, which a
Persian jester carried before. Life was granted her by Aurelian, and she is
said to have lived with her children already in the manner of a Roman matron,
a possession being given her at Tibur, which even today is called Zenobia, not
far from Hadrian's palace and from that place whose name is Conchae.

A man plainly military and of ancient dignity in the senate-house, twice

After all honors, nevertheless, when as an old man he was living on his own
farm and limping on one foot from a wound which he had received in the Persian
war in Valerian's times, he was made emperor and by a joke of jesters was
called Claudius.

And when he conducted himself most gravely and could not be borne by the
soldiers because of censorial discipline, he was slain by those same men by
whom he had been made. His tomb exists around Bononia, on which in large
letters all his honors are cut; on the last verse, however, is added 2:
/Happy in all things, most unhappy emperor./
His family exists, crowded with the name of the Censorini, part of which from
hatred of Roman affairs sought the Thracian lands, part Bithynia. There exists
also a most beautiful house, joined to the Flavian Gentes, which are held once
to have been of the prince Titus.

You have the full number of thirty tyrants, which you pleaded, indeed with the
ill-willing but with a good mind. Give now the little book to whom you will,
written not so much eloquently as faithfully. Nor do I seem to myself to have
promised eloquence, but the matter, I who do not write these little books
which I have published of the life of the princes, but dictate, and dictate
with that haste which, if I myself have promised anything or you have asked,
you so press that I have not the faculty of breathing.
