* Homilies on Genesis

23 The question is what sort of shape one must understand the ark to have. I
think, he says, that it is pyramidal, beginning from a length of 300 cubits
and a width of 50, and being drawn together over 30 cubits of height, so that
the peak comes to be a cubit in length and in width. Let its lower parts,
which have been named the ground stories, be of 2 decks, and the parts above
the two decks, further upward, of 3 decks. This Symmachus also set out clearly
when he said: "You shall make it according to the two-storied and
three-storied parts." And let compartments be understood as marked off on each
deck, so that animals of unlike kinds may be separated, which he calls nests,
and Symmachus cells. Let the timbers be square: first, so that much time may
not be spent in splitting them; then, which is also nearer the truth, so that
being made of whole timbers it may prove 24 more secure. For it was going to
draw near to mountains and to undergo no small storm, against which it would
not have held if it had not been built of whole timbers. The nests both
separated the animals and served as a binding for the construction. And so
that the water might have no way in, let it be smeared round with asphalt both
outside and inside. It is also well that the length is greater than the width;
for thus it was easy to move; for such are the hulls when the proportion is
kept in the shape. At the same time, against so great a water it proved
secure, able neither to be overturned by winds or waves because of the shape,
nor to lean or list; the ark stood in the water in the manner of a house that
has been founded. And no one could devise a more secure shape against so great
a rush and force. If someone should say that it was made square below, this
too is not unreasonable; since it came to be not primarily for sailing, but
for keeping safe; and besides it would have sunk quickly if it had not been
square. And there has been handed down, not unpersuasively, both the need of
the two ground decks, set apart from those above, and of the three named
separately. For since he kept all the animals in the ark for a year, there was
every need that provision be made both of the 25 fitting food for all,
sufficient for a year, and of a place for the excreted dung able not to
trouble the animals and especially the human beings. They say, then, that the
lower story, toward the bottom of the two-storied part, was assigned to dung;
and the one above this to the gathering of the animals' food, <so that the
flesh-eaters might have a supply of flesh, and the other animals besides those
brought in for preservation might be brought into the ark for the sake of
food; and those not of that kind might each have a supply of the fitting food.
These things, then, toward the account of the ground two-storied parts.> The
three-storied part after these was assigned to the animals according to the
nests that have been named. And it is likely that to the race of serpents and
of wild beasts the space of the lowest of the three-storied part was allotted,
and to the cattle and the rest of the tamer or gentler, in order and reason,
the upper parts. And man ought, as by nature ruler of the irrational
creatures, <upon which he himself has been set>, so also to be in the highest
space, about the third 26 story, <having the rest of the animals under his
hand>. And it is plausible that the side door, which he mentioned, was made
about the first story from below, so that the animals, having entered from
there, might go to the appointed nests; for if it had been from above, the
animals would both have gone up to the ark with much difficulty and have come
down to their own places. The security of this door being shut had need of a
more divine power, since no human being from outside was any longer able to
asphalt it so that water might not slip in. Therefore "God shut" behind Noah,
and thus "the flood came to be." And after the flood we observed 27 that Noah
no longer opened a door, as in the things above, but a "window," so that he
might send out the raven. And someone would have taken the observation as
forced, if the other editions did not also contain, second, a window opened by
Noah, and first said that God had commanded Noah to make a door or an opening
from the sides of the ark; for Theodotion and Symmachus made "door," and
Aquila "opening." And it has been written concerning the foods that God both
commanded them to be brought in and Noah did bring them in. The passage of the
animals' dung has been well passed over in silence, both Noah being able to
consider what follows concerning these things, and we to conjecture what has
been left out. Some raised the difficulty whether so great an ark can hold
even the smallest fraction of all the animals upon the earth. And especially
Apelles, the disciple of Marcion, who became father of another heresy besides
that one, wishing 28 to set aside the writings of Moses as not holy, having
raised this difficulty adds this: the tale, then, is false; the Scripture,
then, is not from God; not understanding of what sort the three hundred cubits
were, and of what sort the fifty, and how great the thirty, that the Scripture
named at the construction of the ark. And we learned from one of those held in
honor among the Hebrews that the 300 cubits were named as what is called among
the geometers the power of the square on three hundred, so that the cubits of
the length in the lower plane are 9 myriads; and so also of the width two
thousand five hundred, and of the height nine hundred. For it would be
altogether most absurd that the one "educated in all the wisdom of the
Egyptians," who are especially most geometric, and brought up in a king's
house, should not have seen that the 300 of the length and 50 of the width and
30 of the height, understood more commonly, would perhaps not even have held
some 4 elephants and their food for a year, <God having 29 commanded "two by
two" of the unclean animals to enter into the ark>. And if one must, toward
understanding more clearly the: "gathering it together you shall make the ark
and you shall finish it to a cubit above," set out the as it were
architectural account concerning the ark, come, as it has occurred to us and
as we have learned, let us set it out. Think for me that at the very bottom
the length of the ark is of the three hundred cubits spoken of according to
the so-called power, and fifty in width, then that it is drawn together cubit
by cubit, of the length 10 cubits, and of the width one and two-thirds; so
that the thirtieth of the height becomes a cubit, ten of the length, and of
one and two-thirds of the width; then after this the gathering together is
finished into a cubit. And if to anyone the cubit, being the thirty-first of
the height, should seem forced, let him attend to the wording that says: "and
30 cubits its height; gathering it together you shall make the ark, and you
shall finish it to a cubit <above>." For I think that after the: "and 30
cubits the height" the thirty-first cubit is indicated through the: "and you
shall finish it to a cubit above." And it came to be thus so that the onset of
the waters might have the slip, and the waves might not dash as against a rock
having height from every side. And that the door should come to be from the
sides was also fittingly commanded; for if it had been made in a roof it would
have filled the ark from the rains, 30 but being set from the sides it
furnished security to those within, the ark being borne upon water, and the
window being above this.
