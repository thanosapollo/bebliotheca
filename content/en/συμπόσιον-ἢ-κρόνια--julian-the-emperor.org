* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Since the god grants us to play (for it is the Kronia), and I know nothing
laughable or pleasant, not saying ridiculous things seems worth the care, my
friend.

Then who is so dull and old-fashioned, Caesar, as even to play with studied
care? I thought play was a relaxation of the soul and a release from cares.

You are right in taking it so, but the thing does not seem to meet me in that
way. For I am by nature in no way fitted either to jest or to parody or to be
comic. But since one must obey the law of the god, do you want me, by way of
play, to go through a myth that perhaps has many things worth hearing?

You may speak, and I shall be very glad, since I myself do not despise myths
nor drive out altogether those that are sound, thinking in agreement with you
and with your friend, or rather our common friend, Plato, since he too has
treated many things seriously in myths.

Yes, by Zeus, you say these things truly.

And what is the myth, and of what kind?

Not one of the old ones, such as Aesop made, but whether you would call it a
fiction of Hermes (for having learned it from there I will tell it to you), or
whether the truth is so, or whether it is some mixture of both, the thing
itself, they say, will show.

This prologue of yours has already been worked out both in mythic and in
rhetorical fashion. But now go through the story itself, whatever it is.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

You may learn it.

Romulus, sacrificing the Kronia, was calling all the gods, and indeed the
Caesars themselves as well. Couches happened to have been prepared for the
gods above, at the very height of heaven, they say, /to Olympus, where they
say the seat of the gods is always sure./ For it is said that after Heracles,
Quirinus also went there (for by that name one must call him, obeying the
divine rumor). So for the gods the symposium had been prepared there. But
under the moon itself, on the height of the air, it had been decided that the
Caesars should dine. And they were held up both by the lightness of the bodies
they happened to be clothed in, and by the circuit of the moon. Now four
couches were laid, ready for the greatest gods. Cronus's was of gleaming
ebony, hiding in the black a great and divine radiance, so that no one was
able to look against it. And the eyes suffered the same thing toward that
ebony, through excess of brilliance, that I think they suffer toward the sun,
whenever one looks more intently at its disk. Zeus's was brighter than silver
and whiter than gold. Whether one should call this electrum or say something
else, Hermes was not very clearly able to tell me. And gold-throned on either
side sat the mother and the daughter, Hera beside Zeus, Rhea beside Cronus. As
for the beauty of the gods, he did not even go through it in his account,
saying it was greater and visible to the mind, but not easy to bring forward
to hearing and words, nor possible to receive. No one will be or appear so
loud-voiced as to tell the greatness of that beauty, how much it befits the
appearance of the gods.

For the other gods too a throne or couch had been prepared for each according
to seniority. And no one disputed, but what Homer said, rightly in my view,
having heard it from the Muses themselves, that each of the gods has a throne
on which it is altogether lawful for him to sit firmly and unmoved. For even
when they rise at the presence of the Father they in no way disturb the seats,
nor change places, nor snatch from one another, but each recognizes what is
proper to himself.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

So when all the gods were seated in a circle, Silenus, seeming to me to be in
love with Dionysus, who was beautiful and young and like his father Zeus, sat
near him, as a nurse and tutor, delighting the god who is otherwise fond of
play and of laughter and a giver of grace, and especially by jesting much and
being comic.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

When the symposium of the Caesars too had been assembled, Julius Caesar
entered first, wanting through ambition to rival Zeus himself for the
monarchy. Silenus looked at him and said, See, Zeus, that this man does not,
through love of rule, plan to take even the kingship from you. For, as you
see, he is tall and handsome. To me at least, if nothing else, he is like you
about the head. While Silenus was still playing such things and the gods were
not paying him much attention, Octavian comes in next, changing many colors
like chameleons, now pale, then becoming red, then black and gloomy and
overcast. Then he relaxed again into Aphrodite and the Graces, and he wanted
the glances of his eyes to be such as the great Helios is. For he thought no
one of those he met worthy to look him in the face. And Silenus said, Bless
me, this beast of every kind! What terrible thing will he do to us? Stop
talking nonsense, said Apollo. For I, handing him over to this Zeno here, will
at once show you gold unalloyed. But go, Zeno, he said, take care of my
nursling. And he obeyed, then chanted over him a little of the doctrines, like
those who keep repeating the charms of Zamolxis, and showed him a man of sense
and self-control.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Third there rushed in on them Tiberius, solemn in the face and grim, looking
at once temperate and warlike. But when he turned toward the seat, countless
scars appeared along his back, some cauteries and scrapings and harsh blows
and weals, and from incontinence and cruelty certain itches and lichens as if
branded in. Then Silenus, saying /You seem to me, stranger, other now than
before/, appeared to look more serious than himself. And Dionysus said to him,
Why then, little grandpa, are you being serious? And he said, This old man
startled me, the Satyr, and made me forget myself and put forward the Homeric
Muses. But he will pull your ears, he said, for he is said to have done this
even to a schoolmaster. So let him, groaning, he said, on that little island
(hinting at Capreae) scratch the wretched fisherman.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

While they were still playing at this, a wicked beast comes in. Then all the
gods turned their eyes away, and then Justice gives him to the Punishments,
and they threw him into Tartarus. So Silenus had nothing to say about him.
When Claudius came in next, Silenus begins to sing the Knights of
Aristophanes, as if flattering Claudius instead of Demos. Then looking toward
Quirinus he said, You do wrong, Quirinus, bringing your descendant to the
symposium without the freedmen Narcissus and Pallas. But go, he said, send for
them, and if you wish, for his wife Messalina too. For without those this is
the extra of the tragedy, I almost said, even lifeless. While Silenus was
speaking, Nero comes in with the lyre and the laurel. Then he looked toward
Apollo and said, This one is preparing against you. And king Apollo said, But
I will uncrown him, because he does not imitate me in all things, and even in
what he does imitate me he is not a just imitator. And when he had been
uncrowned, Cocytus immediately snatched him.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

After this many of all kinds ran together, Vindices, Galbas, Othos, Vitellii.
And Silenus said, This swarm of monarchs, gods, where have you found it? We
are being smoked out by the smoke. For these beasts spare not even the
palaces. And Zeus looking toward his brother Sarapis and pointing to Vespasian
said, Send this little miser quickly from Egypt, so that he may put out this
flame. And of his sons, tell the elder to play with Aphrodite Pandemos, and
bind the younger with a collar like the Sicilian beast.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

After these there came an old man handsome to look at. For beauty shines
sometimes even in old age. Most gentle to meet, most just in business. Silenus
was ashamed before him and fell silent. Then Hermes said, And about this one
you say nothing to us? Yes, by Zeus, he said, I do blame you for the
inequality. For to the murderous beast you allotted thrice five years, and to
this man you barely gave one to reign. Do not blame, said Zeus. For after him
I will bring in many good men. So at once Trajan came in carrying on his
shoulders the trophies, the Getic and the Parthian. Seeing him Silenus said,
wanting at once to escape notice and to be heard, It is time now for the
master Zeus to see how Ganymede will be guarded for him.

After him there comes in a man with a deep beard, pompous in other things and
indeed practicing music, looking often to heaven and meddling with the
secrets. Seeing him Silenus said, What does this sophist seem to you? Is he
looking around here for Antinous? Let someone tell him the boy is not present
here and stop him from his nonsense and babble.

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

After these a temperate man comes in, not in the things toward Aphrodite, but
in those toward the state. Seeing him Silenus said, Bless me, the pettiness.
This old man seems to me to be one of those who split the cumin in two.
When the pair of brothers came in with him, Verus and Lucius, Silenus shrank
terribly.
For he could not play or jest, especially at Verus, though he was busy with
this man's faults about his son and his wife, her because he mourned her more
than was proper, and she was not even orderly, and him because he looked on
while the empire was being ruined with him, though he had even a worthy
son-in-law, who would have superintended the common affairs better and indeed
would have cared for the boy himself better than he did for himself. Yet
though he was busy with these things he was ashamed at the greatness of his
virtue. As for the son, not even thinking him worth being mocked, he let him
go. For he too was falling to the ground, unable to stand and keep company
with the heroes.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Pertinax comes into the symposium lamenting his slaughter. Justice, pitying
him, said, But those responsible for these things will not rejoice. And you,
Pertinax, were doing wrong in sharing the plot, so far as the designs go,
which the son of Marcus was plotted against. After him Severus, a man full of
bitterness and punitive. About this one, said Silenus, I say nothing. For I
fear his excessive harshness and implacability. And when his little children
were about to enter with him, Minos blocked them from afar. Having recognized
clearly, he let the younger go, and sent the elder to pay the penalty. Here
Macrinus, a murderous fugitive. Then the little boy from Emesa was driven far
from the sacred precincts. As for the Syrian Alexander, he sat somewhere at
the last, bewailing his own misfortune. And Silenus, jesting at him, said, O
fool and great child, being so old you did not yourself rule your own affairs,
but you gave the money to your mother and did not obey how much better it was
to spend it on friends than to hoard it. But I, said Justice, will hand over
all of them who have become joint causes of these things to be punished. And
so the youth was released.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

After this Gallienus came in with his father, the one having the bonds of
captivity, the other using a softer dress and movement like women. And Silenus
said to the former, /Who is this white-crested one, who leads the army in
front?/, and to Gallienus, /Who having gold everywhere lives in luxury like a
girl./ And Zeus told these two to leave the feast there.

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

After these Claudius comes in, and looking at him all the gods both admired
him for his greatness of soul and granted the rule to his family, thinking it
just that the family of so patriotic a man should be in command for the
longest time. After these Aurelian rushed in as if running away from those who
were holding him with Minos. For many suits were being brought against him for
unjust killings, and he was fleeing the indictments, defending himself badly.
But Helios my master, helping him in other things, not least also joined in
this very thing, saying among the gods, But he has paid the penalty, or has
the oracle given to Delphi escaped notice: /If he should suffer what he did,
would there be straight justice?/

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

With this one Probus comes in, who, having restored seventy cities in not even
whole seven years and managed very many things with self-control, but having
suffered unjust things from the godless, was honored both in other ways and by
his murderers paying the penalty to him. Yet Silenus tried to jest at him,
though many were urging him to be silent. But Let me, he said, now at least
through him let those after him be brought to their senses. Do you not know,
Probus, that doctors mix bitter drugs with honey-drink and offer them? But you
were too austere and always harsh and yielding nowhere. So you have suffered
unjust things, but still likely ones. For it is not possible to rule horses or
cattle or mules, and least of all men, without granting them something of what
gratifies them, just as sometimes doctors yield a little to the sick, so that
in the greater things they may have them obedient. What is this, little
grandpa? said Dionysus. Have you appeared to us a philosopher? Did you not, he
said, child, become a philosopher through me too? Do you not know that
Socrates also, looking like me, carried off the first prizes in philosophy
among the men of his time, if you trust my brother that he does not lie? So
let us not say all laughable things, but also serious ones.

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

While they were still talking with one another, Carus together with his sons,
wanting to slip into the symposium, was driven off by Justice, and Diocletian,
bringing with him the two Maximiani and my grandfather Constantius, came
forward in order. They held one another by the hand, and they walked not
equally, but it was like a chorus around him, some as if bodyguards and
wanting to run ahead of him, and he checking them. For he thought it right to
have no advantage. And when he perceived that he himself was growing tired,
giving them all that he was carrying on his shoulders, he himself walked
unencumbered. The gods admired the men's concord and allowed them to sit
before very many. As for Maximian, who was terribly incontinent, Silenus did
not think it right to jest, but he did not admit him to the kings' mess. For
he was not only wanton in every kind of wantonness toward Aphrodite, but also
a busybody and untrustworthy and not in all things in tune with the
tetrachord. So Justice quickly drove him out. Then he went away I do not know
where on earth. For I forgot to inquire that of Hermes.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

Upon this all-harmonic tetrachord there grows a terrible and harsh and
turbulent system. As for the two, Justice did not even allow them to touch the
forecourt of the heroes' assembly, and Licinius, having come as far as the
forecourt, committing many and strange faults, Minos quickly drove out.
Constantine came inside and sat a long time, then after him the children. For
Magnentius there was no entrance, because he had done nothing sound, though
the man seemed to have done many fine things. But the gods, seeing that these
had not been done from a fine disposition in him, let him run off groaning.

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Such then being the preparation about the dinner, the gods lacked nothing, for
they have all things, but Hermes thought it good to test the heroes
themselves, and this was not against Zeus's mind. Quirinus was already asking
to transfer someone from there to himself. But Heracles said, I will not
endure it, Quirinus. For why did you not also invite my Alexander to the
dinner? So I ask you, Zeus, he said, if you have decided to bring any of these
to us, order Alexander to come. For why should we not test the men in common
and award it to the better? The son of Alcmene seemed to Zeus to speak justly.
And when he came in among the heroes neither Caesar nor anyone else stood up.
Taking an empty seat, which the son of Severus had made for himself (for that
one had been driven out for fratricide), he sat down. And Silenus, jesting at
Quirinus, said, See that these are not a match for this one Greek. By Zeus,
said Quirinus, I think many are not worse. And my descendants have so admired
him that they name and consider him alone of all foreign leaders who have
been, great. Yet they no longer think him greater than those born among
themselves, perhaps suffering something from self-love, perhaps also it being
so. We shall know at once by testing the men. Saying these things especially
Quirinus blushed, and it was clear he was anxious for his own descendants,
lest they go off taking second prize.

* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

After this Zeus asked the gods whether one must call all to the contest, or,
as happens in the gymnastic contests, the one who has beaten the man who has
won many victories, by prevailing over one, seems no less to have become
stronger than those too who never wrestled against him but were inferior to
the one beaten. And such an examination seemed to all to be very well fitted.
So Hermes proclaimed that Caesar should come forward, and Octavian after him,
and Trajan third, as the most warlike. Then when there was silence king
Cronus, looking at Zeus, said he wondered, seeing warlike emperors being
called to this contest, but no philosopher. And to me, he said, such men are
no less dear. So call Marcus in as well. And when Marcus too, having been
called, came forward, very solemn, having his eyes and face somewhat
contracted from toils, but showing an extraordinary beauty in this very thing,
in which he offered himself unadorned and unembellished. For his beard was
altogether deep and his clothes plain and temperate, and from scarcity of food
his body was most translucent and transparent, like, I think, the purest and
most unmixed light itself.

* Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

When this one too was inside the sacred precincts, Dionysus said, King Cronus
and father Zeus, is it right that anything among the gods be incomplete? And
when they said no, Let us then bring in here also some lover of enjoyment. And
Zeus said, But it is not lawful for a man who does not emulate our things to
come inside. Then let the judgment for them be at the forecourt, said
Dionysus. But if this seems so, let us call a man not unwarlike, but more
tamed to pleasure and enjoyment. So let Constantine come as far as the
forecourt. When this too had been decided, the manner in which they should
compete was put forward as a proposal. And Hermes thought each should speak in
turn about his own affairs, and the gods should cast the vote. But this did
not seem to Apollo to be well. For among the gods the test and examination is
of truth, not of persuasiveness or wheedling. Wanting to gratify both and at
the same time to extend their gathering further, Zeus said, Nothing prevents
allowing them to speak, measuring out a little water, then later questioning
and testing each one's mind. And Silenus, jesting, But see that Trajan and
Alexander, thinking it nectar, do not gulp down all the water, then deprive
the others. And Poseidon said, It was not my water the two men were lovers of,
but your drink. So it is more fitting for you to fear for your own vines than
for my springs. And Silenus, stung, was silent, and from this paid attention
to the contestants.

* Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

And Hermes proclaimed: /The contest of the fairest prizes begins, its steward,
and the time calls no longer to delay. But hearing our herald's cry, you
former kings, who enslaved many nations and in wars sharpened a hostile spear,
together with the great wise mind of counsel, come, stand to the opposing
judgment, both those who set prudence as the end of a most blessed life, and
those for whom to do many evils to opponents and good things to friends was
the mark of a fine life, and those for whom to have the sweetest enjoyment as
the limit of toils, and feasts and marriages, delightful to the eyes, and to
wear soft clothes together with gem-set bracelets about the fingertips,
appeared most blessed. And the end of victory will be Zeus's care./

* Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

While Hermes was proclaiming such things they drew lots. And somehow the lot
ran together with Caesar's love of being first. This made that man proud and
more pompous. And because of this Alexander almost needed even to flee the
judgment. But the great Heracles, encouraging him, held him. Second after him
Alexander drew the right to speak. And for those after, the lots came forward
with each man's times. So Caesar began thus: It happened to me, Zeus and gods,
to be born in so great a city after so many men, that it rules as many as no
other city ever ruled, and for the others it is desirable even to take second
place. For what city, beginning from three thousand men, in not even whole six
hundred years, came by arms to the ends of the earth? And what nations
furnished so many men good and warlike and legislative? And who honored the
gods so? Being born then in so great and such a city I surpassed not only
those of my own time but also those who ever were, by my deeds. And of my
citizens I know well that no one will contest the first prize with me. But if
this Alexander dares, which of his own deeds does he think fit to compare with
mine? Perhaps the Persian ones, as if he had not seen so many trophies raised
by me against Pompey? And yet who has been a more formidable general, Darius
or Pompey? And which had a braver army following him? The most warlike of the
nations formerly subject to Darius, Pompey had following him, counting as
Carians, and those from Europe, who often turned Asia when it brought war, and
of these themselves the bravest, Italians, Illyrians, Celts.

* Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

But since I mentioned the Celts, shall we set against Alexander's Getic deeds
the overthrow of Celtic land? He crossed the Ister once, I the Rhine a second
time. This German work is mine again. No one at all stood against him, I
fought against Ariovistus. I first of the Romans dared to set foot on the
outer sea. And perhaps this deed was not marvelous. And yet even this daring
is worth marveling at. But the greater thing of mine was disembarking from the
ship first. And I am silent about the Helvetians and the nation of the
Iberians. I have not yet mentioned any of the Gallic things, having brought
under me more than three hundred cities, and of men not fewer than two
million. And though these deeds of mine were such, that was greater and
bolder. For I had to contend even against the citizens themselves and to
master the unconquerable and invincible Romans. So whether one judges by
number of battle-lines, I drew up three times as often as those who magnify
the things about Alexander boast for him, or by number of captive cities, I
subdued the most not of Asia only but also of Europe. Alexander went through
Egypt as a spectator, I conquered it while assembling drinking-parties. And do
you want to examine the gentleness after victory in each? I even pardoned my
enemies. At any rate I suffered from them as much as concerned Justice. But
he, besides his enemies, did not even keep off from his friends.

* Section 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

Will you then still be able to dispute the first prizes with me? And will you
not at once yield along with the others, but force me to say how you treated
the Thebans bitterly, and I the Helvetians humanely? For you burned their
cities, I restored the cities burned by their own citizens. And yet it was not
the same thing to master ten thousand Greeks and to withstand a hundred and
fifty thousand coming on. Though I still have many things to say about myself
and this man, because I have no leisure I have least practiced speaking.
Therefore you must have pardon, and from what has been said, making the equal
and just examination also of what was not said, award me the first prize.

* Section 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23
:END:

When Caesar had said such things and still wanted to speak, Alexander, who
even before had barely been holding out, no longer restrained himself, but
with some disturbance and anxiety said, And I, Zeus and gods, how long shall I
endure in silence this man's boldness? For there is no end, as you see, either
of the praises of himself or of the slanders against me. He ought perhaps most
of all to have spared both. For both somehow seem similarly offensive. And
more than pulling my things to pieces, especially when he became an imitator
of them. But he has come to such shamelessness as to dare to make comedy of
the originals of his own deeds. You ought, Caesar, to have remembered those
tears which you then let fall, listening to the memoirs that have been made
about my actions.

* Section 24
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h24
:END:

But Pompey raised you after this, flattered by his own citizens, and becoming
nobody nowhere. For to triumph from Libya, no great deed, the softness of the
consuls then made most famous. And that slave war, which was not even against
men but against the worst of the household slaves, others accomplished, Crassi
and Lucii, but Pompey had the name and the inscription. Armenia and what
borders it Lucullus conquered in war, but Pompey triumphed from these. Then
the citizens flattered him and named him Great: greater than which of those
before him? For what so great was done by that man as by Marius or the two
Scipios or that Furius beside this Quirinus here, who raised this man's city
when it had almost collapsed? For these men were not inscribed on others'
deeds, as in civic buildings and expenses laid down and completed by others
another magistrate is inscribed after plastering the wall a little, so they
were inscribed on others' actions. But becoming themselves architects and
craftsmen they were thought worthy of the finest names. So it is no wonder if
you have beaten Pompey, who scratched with his finger and was in other things
a fox rather than a lion. For when fortune betrayed him, who in the time
before had stood by him, you quickly mastered him alone. And that you became
superior by no cleverness is clear. For even having come to a lack of
provisions (and this is no small fault of a general, as you know), and joining
battle, you were defeated. But if Pompey, through folly and mindlessness or
inability to rule the citizens, neither postponed the battle when he ought to
have worn the war, nor followed up the victory when winning, he was ruined
under his own faults and not under your stratagems.

The Persians everywhere well and prudently prepared gave way before our might.

* Section 25
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h25
:END:

And since it belongs to the best man and king to claim not simply doing, but
also doing the just things, I demanded justice of the Persians on behalf of
the Greeks, and I took up the Greek wars, not wishing to hurt Greece, but
cutting down those who prevented me from crossing and demanding justice of the
Persian. But you conquered the Germans and Gauls, preparing against your own
fatherland, than which what could be worse or more polluted? And since as if
pulling to pieces you mentioned the ten thousand Greeks, that you too have
come from there and that the Greeks settled most of Italy, though I know it I
still do not accept it. And of these themselves a small nation, I mean the
Aetolians who live next to us, did you not make it a great matter to have them
as friends and allies, and when they later became hostile to you for whatever
cause, did you not force them to obey you not without danger? And you who,
against the old age, as one might say, of Greece, and not even of all, but of
a small nation, when the Greek world was in its prime almost unknown to exist,
I almost said, barely sufficed: what would you have become, if you had needed
to fight against the Greeks in their prime and in concord? For even when
Pyrrhus crossed against you, you know how you cowered. And if you think
mastering the Persians a small thing and pull to pieces so great a deed, tell
me, for what cause, warring more than three hundred years, did you not master
the very small country beyond the Tigris river ruled by the Parthians? Do you
want me to tell you? The Persians' arrows kept you off. Let Antony tell you
about them, who was trained in generalship under you. But I in not even whole
ten years became master of these and of Indians too. Then do you dare to
dispute with me, who from a little boy as a general did such deeds that the
memory, though they were not worthily sung by the historians, still remains
along with life, just as of those of Callinicus, my king, whose servant and
emulator I became, competing with Achilles my ancestor, and admiring Heracles

the time, and also bearing a grudge against him for his arrogance toward the

being angry but rejoicing at it, and respecting the man more as a tutor or
father. And I register Areius as both friend and companion in life, and
altogether nothing has been erred by us against philosophy. And seeing Rome
under the civil factions often driven to the last danger, I so arranged the
things about her that she is, except for you, gods, adamantine for the rest.
For I did not, yielding to measureless desires, plan to acquire for her in
every way, but I set two boundaries, as if marked out by nature, the rivers
Ister and Euphrates. Then having subjected the nation of Scythians and
Thracians, while you measured out the time of the kingship to me, I did not
look about from one war to another, but I disposed my leisure toward lawgiving
and the correction of the disasters from war, thinking I had planned no worse
than any before me, or rather, if one must speak boldly, better than those who
ever administered such great commands. For some died in their generalships,
when it was possible at last to be at rest and not campaign, constructing wars
from wars for themselves, as busybodies construct lawsuits. And others even
when being warred against attended to luxury, not only preferring shameful
luxury to the glory after these things, but even to safety itself. So I,
thinking these things, do not claim myself for the worse portion. And whatever
seems good to you, gods, it is fitting that I of course be content with that.

After this Trajan is given leave to speak. And he, though able to speak, from
easygoingness (for he used to leave most things to Sura to write for him),
sounding rather than speaking, displayed to them the Getic and the Parthian
trophy. And he blamed old age as not having allowed him to follow up the

luxury, do you find fault with the narrowness of time? So provoked by the jest
(for he was not outside being able to speak rhetorically, but through love of
drink he was often blunter than himself), he said, And I, Zeus and gods,
taking over the empire numb as it were and dissolved both by the tyranny that
had long prevailed at home and by the insolence of the Getae, I alone dared to
take on nations beyond the Ister, and I destroyed the nation of the Getae, who
have become the most warlike of those ever, not by courage of body only, but
also by what Zamolxis honored among them persuaded them. For thinking they do
not die but change residence they do it more readily than others endure
journeys abroad. And this deed was done by me in perhaps some five years. And
that of all the emperors before me I appeared most gentle to the subjects, and
that neither this Caesar would dispute with me about these things nor anyone
else at all, is surely quite clear. And against the Parthians, before being

I went against them, hindered in nothing by age, though the laws granted me
not to campaign. These things being such, am I not just to be honored before
the others, having become gentle toward the subjects, and especially
formidable toward the enemies, and having respected philosophy, your offspring
as well? When Trajan had said such things he seemed to surpass all in

After this they allowed Constantine to speak. And he at first was confident
about the contest. But when he looked to the others' deeds, he saw his own
altogether small. For two tyrants, if one must speak the truth, he had taken
down, the one unwarlike and soft, the other wretched and weak through old age,
but both most hateful to gods and men. As for the things against the
barbarians they were laughable for him. For he had as it were paid tribute,
and he looked toward Luxury. And she stood far from the gods about the
forecourt of the Moon. So he was in love with her, and looking wholly toward
her he cared nothing about the victory. And when he too had to say something,
he said, I am better than these in this: than the Macedonian, because I fought
against Romans and the German and Scythian nations and not against the Asian
barbarians; than Caesar and Octavian, by not, as these did, being in faction
against noble and good citizens, but going against the most polluted and
wicked of the tyrants. And than Trajan I would reasonably be preferred for the
brave deeds against the tyrants, and for recovering the country this man added
I would not unreasonably be thought equal, unless indeed recovering is even
greater than acquiring. And this Marcus, being silent for himself, yields the
first prizes to all of us. And Silenus said, But are you offering us the
gardens of Adonis as your own deeds, Constantine? And what, he said, are these

Aphrodite's husband, having heaped garden-earth in little pots. And these,
having greened for a little, immediately wither away. And Constantine blushed,
recognizing outright that his own work was of that kind.

When this too seemed to have been said rightly by Marcus, Silenus at a loss

child would be so wicked. And if youth, making great turns to either side, was

forgiving of this rashness of mine.
