* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

It is, in the main, the custom of religious travelers, when some grove or some
holy place has been met with on the road, to ask a vow, to set out fruit, to
sit a little while: so for me, having entered this most holy city, although I
am in great haste, pardon must first be asked, a speech must be offered, and
haste must be checked. For neither more justly would a religious delay meet a
traveler, whether an altar wreathed with flowers, or a cave shaded with
leaves, or an oak loaded with horns, or a beech crowned with hides, or indeed
a little hill consecrated by a fence, or a trunk shaped by carving, or turf
moistened with a libation, or a stone smeared with ointment. For these things
are small, and although worshipped by the few who inquire, yet they are passed
by those who do not know.

the soldier in Plautus, who speaks thus: /one eyewitness is worth more than
ten who have heard./ Nay rather he had turned this verse to the examining of
men: one witness who has heard is worth more than ten who have seen. For the
rest, if the judgments of the eyes prevailed more than those of the mind,
wisdom would surely have to be granted to the eagle. For we men can discern
neither things set far off nor things set nearest at hand; rather we are all
in some way dim-sighted; and if you reduce it to the eyes and that earthly and
dull gaze, truly the excellent poet said most truly that a kind of mist is
poured over our eyes, and that we have strength to see only within a stone's
throw. But the eagle, when it has raised itself most high as far as the
clouds, carried on its wings through all that space where it rains and snows,
beyond which peak there is place neither for thunderbolt nor for lightning, in
the very, so to speak, soil of the ether and the ridge of the storm: when
therefore the eagle has borne itself there, it glides with a gentle nod to the
left or to the right with so great a mass of body, turning its sail-like wings
wherever it has pleased with a slight rudder of the tail; from there, looking
down on all things, it keeps up in the same place the tireless rowing of its
outstanding wings, and for a little while, hanging in lingering flight almost
in the same spot, it looks round and seeks where most of all it may rush from
above upon its prey in the manner of a thunderbolt, unforeseen from the sky,
seeing at one gaze and in the same onset flocks in the fields, wild beasts in
the mountains, and men in the cities, from where it may pierce with its beak,
from where it may hook with its talons either a careless lamb or a fearful
hare or whatever living thing for eating or for tearing chance has offered.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Hyagnis was, as we have received by report, father and master of Marsyas the
piper, in ages still rude in music the only one before others to sing songs,
not yet indeed with a soul-bending sound, nor yet in a many-formed mode, nor
yet on a many-holed pipe; for that art was still only just arising by a new
discovery. And there is nothing at all that can be perfected at the first
beginning of itself, but in nearly all things the rudiment of hope is there
before the trial of the thing. In short, therefore, before Hyagnis most people
were skilled in nothing other than what Virgil's shepherd or herdsman was: /to
spoil a wretched song on a grating straw./ And if anyone seemed to have
advanced a little more largely in the art, even for him it was still the
custom to sound with one pipe as with one trumpet. Hyagnis first parted his
hands in playing, first animated two pipes with one breath, first mixed a
musical concert with left and right holes, with a sharp tinkle and a deep
boom. Marsyas, begotten of him, when he was taking after his father in the
craft of piping, a Phrygian in the rest and a barbarian, with a beast-like
face, grim, shaggy, with unwashed beard, covered with thorns and hair, is
said, for shame, to have contended with Apollo, the foul with the fair, the
rustic with the learned, a brute with a god. The Muses, with Minerva, stood by
as judges for the sake of a pretence, namely to laugh at the barbarism of that
monster and no less to punish his stupidity. But Marsyas, which is the
greatest specimen of folly, not understanding that he was being held as a
laughingstock, before he began to inflate the pipes, first blurted out in
barbarian fashion certain ravings about himself and Apollo, praising himself
because he was both thrown back in hair and squalid in beard and shaggy on the
chest and a piper by art and poor in fortune: against Apollo, ridiculous to
say, he found fault with opposite virtues, because Apollo was both unshorn in
hair and pleasing in cheeks and smooth in body and knowing many things by art
and rich in fortune. /First of all,/ he said, /his hair, with forelocks
smoothed in front and lockets smoothed forward, comes on ahead and hangs down;
the whole body is most pleasing, the limbs bright, the tongue prophetic,
whether you prefer speech or verses, in both alike a match in eloquence. What
of this too, that his clothing is thin in weave, soft to the touch, shining
with purple? What of this too, that his lyre flashes with gold, gleams with
ivory, is varied with gems? What of this too, that he sings both most
learnedly and most pleasingly? All these,/ he said, /are blandishments in no
way honorable to virtue, but fitted to luxury:/ and against them he displayed
the quality of his own body as the greatest beauty before himself. The Muses
laughed when they heard this kind of charge, things a wise man would wish for,
thrown at Apollo, and they left that piper, beaten in the contest, like a
two-footed bear, with the hide cut away, his entrails bare and torn. So
Marsyas piped for a punishment and fell. Indeed Apollo was ashamed of so
humble a victory.

There was a certain piper, Antigenidas, a honeyed modulator of every little
voice and likewise a skilled modifier in every mode, whether you wanted the
simple Aeolian or the varied Ionian or the plaintive Lydian or the religious
Phrygian or the warlike Dorian. He, then, though he was foremost in fame in
piping, used to say that he labored at nothing so much, and was anguished in
soul and mind, as that tomb-hornists were called pipers. But he would have
borne this sharing of names with a calm mind if he had watched mimes: he would
have noticed there that some preside in almost the same purple, others are
flogged; likewise if he watched our games: for there too he would see a man
presiding, a man fighting to the death: a toga also is made ready both for a
vow and for a funeral, and likewise corpses are covered with a cloak and
philosophers are wrapped in one.

:CUSTOM_ID: h6

The Indians, a people great in inhabitants and greatest in territories, placed
far from us toward the east, near the bend of the ocean and the rising of the
sun, at the first stars, at the last lands, beyond the learned Egyptians and
the superstitious Jews and the Nabataean merchants and the Arsacids flowing in
garments and the Ituraeans poor in crops and the Arabs rich in spices: of
those Indians, then, I do not equally wonder at the heaps of ivory and the
harvests of pepper and the wares of cinnamon and the temperings of iron and
the mines of silver and the streams of gold, nor that the Ganges among them,
the greatest of all rivers

/ruler of eastern waters, runs out into a hundred rivers,
/a hundred valleys he has and a hundred mouths,
/and he is joined to the ocean's straits by a hundredfold stream,/

nor that those same Indians, placed there toward the newborn day, yet have in
body the color of night, nor that among them immense serpents contend with
huge elephants at equal risk for mutual ruin: for having caught them with a
slippery coil they bind them round, so that those unable to free their step or
at all to break the scaly fetters of the most tenacious serpents must seek
vengeance from the fall of their own mass and crush their detainers with the
whole body. There are among them also various kinds of cultivators (I would
more gladly treat of the marvels of men than of nature); there is among them a
kind who know nothing more than to herd cattle, and therefore the surname of
cowherds has been given them. There are also men clever at exchanging wares
and strenuous at meeting battles, either with arrows from afar or with swords
at close quarters. There is besides among them a preeminent kind, they are
called gymnosophists. These I admire most, because they are men skilled not in
propagating the vine, nor in grafting the tree, nor in cutting the soil: they
do not know how to till a field or to refine gold or to tame a horse or to
subdue a bull or to shear or pasture a sheep or a goat. What then is it? They
know one thing in place of all these: they cultivate wisdom, the old as
masters as much as the younger as pupils. And I praise nothing equally among
them so much as that they hate torpor of mind and idleness. Therefore when the
table is set, before the food is served, all the young men from diverse places
and duties gather to the meal; the masters ask in turn what good deed they
have done from the rising of light to that point of the day. Here one recalls
that, chosen as arbiter between two, he has restored friends out of enemies,
the quarrel healed, grace reconciled, suspicion purged; likewise another that
he has obeyed his parents in some command, and another that he has found
something by his own meditation or learned it by another's showing; the rest,
finally, recall other things. He who has nothing to bring forward why he
should dine is driven out unfed to work abroad.

To that Alexander, far the most excellent of all kings, to whom from deeds
done and increased the surname Great was given, lest a man who had obtained a
unique glory should ever be named without praise (for he alone since the
founding of the age, as far as the memory of men extends, increased in
unconquerable empire of the world, was greater than his fortune, and his most
ample successes he both challenged as strenuous and equaled as deserving and
surpassed as better, and he alone was famous without a rival, so much so that
no one dares even to hope for his virtue, or even to wish for his fortune): of
that Alexander, then, you will weary yourself in admiring many sublime and
illustrious deeds put forth, whether ventures of war or provisions at home,
all of which my Clemens, the most learned and most charming of poets, has
undertaken to illustrate in a most beautiful poem; but among the first that
famous thing of Alexander, that he did not wish his image, so that it might be
handed down more surely to posterity, to be commonly contaminated by many
artists, but he ordained to his whole world that no one should rashly copy the
king's likeness in bronze, in color, in engraving; nay, he often wrote that
Polyclitus alone should draw it in bronze, Apelles alone should outline it in
colors, Pyrgoteles alone should strike it in engraving; besides these three,
by far the noblest in their crafts, if anyone else anywhere should be found
who had laid hands on the most holy image of the king, he would punish him no
otherwise than as a temple-robber. By that fear of all, then, it was brought
about that Alexander alone was everywhere most like his images, and that in
all statues and panels and reliefs the same vigor of the keenest warrior, the
same genius of the greatest honor, the same form of green youth, the same
grace of a brow thrown back, was seen. Would that by a like example an edict
of philosophy were in force, that no one should rashly copy its image, so that
a few good artists, likewise thoroughly learned, should contemplate the study
of wisdom in every way, and that the rude, the sordid, the unskilled should
not imitate philosophers only as far as the cloak and contaminate a royal
discipline, found as much for speaking well as for living well, by speaking
ill and living in the same manner. Both of which, of course, are very easy.
For what is an easier thing than rage of tongue and cheapness of manners, the
one born from contempt of others, the other from contempt of oneself? For to
cultivate oneself cheaply is contempt of oneself; to assail others in
barbarian fashion is an insult to the hearers. Or does he not put the highest
insult upon you, who judges that you delight in the curses of every best man,
who thinks that you do not understand bad and vicious words or, if you
understand them, take them in good part? Who among rustics, porters,
tavern-keepers is so speechless that, if he should wish to receive a cloak, he
would not curse more fluently?

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

If by chance in this most beautiful gathering some malignant man from those
enviers of mine is sitting, since, as in a great city, this kind too is found,
who prefer to carp at better men rather than imitate them, and, whose likeness
they despair of, of those same they affect enmity, namely so that those who
are obscure by their own name may become known by mine: if then anyone from
those of envy has mixed himself like some stain into this most splendid
audience, I would like him for a little while to carry his eyes around this
incredible assembly and, having contemplated so great a throng as has never
before me been seen in a philosopher's audience, to reckon with his own mind
how great a danger to the keeping of reputation he runs here who has not been
accustomed to be despised, since it is hard and exceedingly difficult to
satisfy even the moderate expectation of a few, especially for me, to whom
both the reputation already won and your kind presumption about me allow me to
open nothing at all carelessly and offhand. For which of you will pardon me
one solecism? Who will grant even one syllable pronounced in barbarian
fashion? Who will permit disordered and vicious words, arising rashly as if to
men in delirium, to be blurted out? And yet these things you easily, and
indeed most deservedly, pardon in others. But my every single saying you
examine sharply, you weigh carefully, you bring to the file and a sure line,
you compare it in truth with the lathe and the buskin. So much does usefulness
have of excuse, dignity of difficulty. I recognize therefore my difficulty and
I do not beg you not to judge so. And yet let not some small and crooked
likeness hold you false in mind, since certain cloaked beggar-things, as I
have often said, walk about. The herald of the proconsul himself also mounts
the tribunal, and he himself is seen there in a toga, and indeed he stands for
a very long time or walks or for the most part shouts at the top of his voice;
but the proconsul himself speaks with a moderate voice, rarely, and sitting,
and for the most part reads from a tablet, for the herald's garrulous voice is
a service, but the proconsul's tablet is a sentence, which once read can
neither be increased by one letter nor yet diminished, but however it has been
recited, so it is entered in the instrument of the province. I myself also
suffer in my studies some likeness, as far as my capacity goes; for whatever I
have brought forth to you has at once been excerpted and read, and I am not
allowed to recall it from there, nor yet to change it, nor to correct
anything. For which reason a greater scruple of speaking must be held, and
indeed not in one kind of studies. For more of my works exist in the Camenae
than works of Hippias in crafts. What that is, if you attend with your mind, I
will discuss more diligently and more accurately.

And Hippias is of the number of the sophists, first of all in multitude of
arts, second to none in eloquence; his age was with Socrates, his country
Elis; his birth is unknown, but his glory great, his fortune moderate, yet his
talent noble, his memory excellent, his studies various, his rivals many. That
Hippias once came to Pisa for the Olympic contest, no less to be looked at in
dress than to be wondered at in workmanship. Everything he had with him he had
not bought, but had made with his own hands, both the garments with which he
was clothed, and the footwear with which he was shod, and the things he
carried by which he was conspicuous. He had for clothing on the body an inner
tunic of finest weave, of triple thread, of double purple: he himself alone
had woven it for himself at home. He had for a girdle a belt, of the kind
varied with marvelous colors in Babylonian painting: nor had anyone helped him
in this work either. He had for a wrap a white cloak, which he had thrown
around above: I find that this cloak too was the work of his own hands. Even
the coverings of his feet, sandals, he had fastened together for himself; even
a gold ring on the left hand with a most cunningly wrought seal, which he
displayed, he himself had rounded the little orb of that ring and closed the
bezel and engraved the gem. I have not yet recalled all of his. For it will
not shame me to recall what it did not shame him to display, who at the great
beginning proclaimed that he had also fashioned for himself an oil-flask,
which he was carrying, of lentil shape, with a smooth circuit, a pressed
roundness, and beside it an honorable little strigil, with the straight taper
of the little groove, the curved channel of the blade, so that it too might
linger in the hand by the handle and sweat might slip from it in a little
stream. Who then will not praise a man so knowing in numerous art, magnificent
in knowledge of every kind, a Daedalus in the skill of so many utensils? Nay,
I too praise Hippias, but I prefer to rival the fertility of his talent by a
many-formed instrument of learning rather than of furniture, and I confess
that I am less skilled in the sedentary crafts, that I buy clothing from the
weave-shop, that I purchase these sandals from the cobbler's, that I do not
even wear a ring, that I value gem and gold on a level with lead and pebbles,
that I buy a strigil and flask and the rest of the utensils of the bath at the
market. For I do not at all deny that I do not know how to use the shuttle or
the awl or the file or the lathe or tools of that kind, but in place of these
I confess that I prefer with one paper reed to refresh poems of every kind
fitted to the rod, the lyre, the comic sock, the tragic buskin, likewise
satires and riddles, likewise various histories of things, and also speeches
praised by the eloquent, and also dialogues praised by philosophers, and these
and other things of the same sort both in Greek and in Latin, with a twin vow,
equal zeal, like style. And would that I could offer them to you, most
excellent proconsul, not one by one and separately, but all together and
heaped up, and enjoy your praiseworthy testimony for all our Camena! Not, by
Hercules, from poverty of praise, which has long been kept whole and
flourishing for me through all your predecessors down to you, but because I
wish myself approved to no one more than to him whom I myself before all
deservedly approve. For so it has been arranged by nature that him whom you
praise you also love, and further him whom you love you also wish to be
praised by. And I profess myself your admirer, bound to you by no private
grace, but by all public grace. For I have obtained nothing from you, because
I have not even asked. But philosophy has taught me not only to love a
benefit, but also to refuse an injury, and more to impart by judgment than to
serve convenience, and to prefer what is expedient in common rather than what
is expedient for me. Therefore most love the fruit of your goodness, I the
zeal. And I have begun to do that while I contemplate your moderation in the
business of the provincials, by which you have brought it about that those who
have tried you ought to love you because of the benefit, those who have not
tried you because of the example. For both by benefit you have accommodated
many and by example you have profited all. For who would not love to learn
from you by what moderation it can be obtained [lost] ; that gravity of yours
is pleasant, your austerity mild, your constancy calm, and your vigor winning.
No one of the proconsuls, so far as I know, has the province of Africa more
revered, less feared: in no year but yours did shame avail more than fear for
checking faults. No one else with equal power has more often profited, more
rarely terrified; no one has brought a son more like in virtue. Therefore no
one of the proconsuls was longer at Carthage. For even at that time when you
were going around the province, Honorinus remaining with us, we felt your
absence less, although we desired you more; a father's equity in the son, an
old man's authority and prudence in the young man, consular authority in a
legate, in short he so fashions and represents all your virtues that, so help
me, the praise won would be more admirable in the young man than in you, had
you not given it such. Would that it were allowed to enjoy it forever. What
have we to do with those relays of proconsuls, what with short years and
hurrying months? O swift days of good men, o hastened courses of the best
governors. Already, Severianus, the whole province desires you. Indeed
Honorinus his own honor calls to the praetorship, and the favor of the Caesars
shapes him for the consulship, and our love holds him for the present, and the
hope of Carthage pledges him for the future, relying on one consolation of
your example, that he who is sent as legate is soon to return to us as
proconsul.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

The parrot is a bird, a bird of India; its size is a very little less than
that of doves, but the color is not of doves; for it is not that milky or
leaden color, or both, yellowish beneath or sprinkled, but the color for the
parrot is green both in the innermost little feathers and in the outermost
little palms. Except that it is distinguished by the neck alone. Indeed its
little neck is girded and crowned with a vermilion circle, like a gold torque,
with an equal circuit of brightness. The first hardness is of the beak: when,
stirred, it plunges in highest flight upon some rock, it catches itself with
the beak as with an anchor. But the same hardness of the head as of the beak.
When it is forced to rival our speech, the head is beaten with a little iron
nail, so that it may thoroughly feel the master's command; this is the rod for
the learner. And it learns at once as a chick up to two years of its age,
while the mouth is easy to be shaped, while the tongue is tender to be
quivered: but an old one, captured, is both unteachable and forgetful. Yet the
parrot is more ready for the discipline of human speech that feeds on mast and
on whose feet five little toes, as of a man, are counted. For that mark is not
for all parrots, but this is proper to all, that they have a broader tongue
than other birds; so they articulate the words of a man more easily with a
more open plectrum and palate. What it has learned, indeed, it sings or rather
speaks so similarly to us that, if you hear the voice, you would think it a
man: for if you hear a raven, that is, it croaks, it does not speak. But in
truth both the raven and the parrot pronounce nothing other than what they
have learned. If you have taught insults, it will insult, dinning curses by
day and by night: this is its song, this it thinks a chant. When it has gone
through all the curses it has learned, it repeats the same ditty again. If you
wish to be without insult, the tongue must be cut out, or it must be sent back
as soon as possible into its own woods.

Samos is a moderate island in the Icarian sea, lying opposite Miletus to the
west of it, and it is not divided from it by much sea: a second day sets a man
sailing either way in fair weather in harbor. The land is sluggish in grain,
vain to the plow, more fertile in olive-yard; it is not blamed by the
vine-dresser nor by the grower of vegetables. All farming is in the hoe and
the slip, by whose yield the island is more fruitful than grain-bearing. For
the rest it is crowded with inhabitants and frequented by guests. It has a
town, by no means in proportion to its glory, but that it was ample the
half-ruined walls indicate in many places. Indeed the shrine of Juno has been
famous from of old; that shrine, on the shore to the right, if I remember the
road correctly, is not more than twenty stades from the town. There the
treasury of the goddess is exceedingly rich: a very great account of gold and

also remedies for healing, sought for mortals at great cost from earth and sky
and sea; but the Brahmans contributed most of his philosophy, what are the

likewise Leodamas, the pupil of Creophylus, which Creophylus is remembered to
have been the host of the poet Homer and his rival in singing. Taught by so
many doctors, having drunk from so many and such many-yoked cups of
disciplines in the whole world, a man especially of huge talent and surely
more august than the capacity of a human mind, first namer and founder of
philosophy, he taught his pupils nothing sooner than to be silent, and the
first exercise with him for the future sage was to restrain the whole tongue
and to press those words, which the poets call flying, those words with the
wings pulled off, within the wall of shining teeth. In short, I say, this was
the first rudiment of wisdom, to learn to meditate, to unlearn to chatter. Not
for the whole of time, however, did they unaccustom the voice, nor did all

space of measured silence seemed enough; the more talkative, indeed, were

be silent gladly. By that moderation I seem to have obtained from all your

Before I begin, leading men of Africa, to give you thanks for the statue which
you honorably asked for me when I was present and kindly decreed when I was
absent, I first wish to allege to you the cause why I have been away from the
sight of the audience for quite many days and have betaken myself to the

healing-places for the sick: for I have so resolved to prove to you every time
of my life, to whom I have firmly dedicated myself forever: I will do nothing
so great, nothing so small, that I do not have you both aware of it and judges
of it. Why then I postponed myself of a sudden from this most splendid sight
of yours, I will recall an example of that matter a little otherwise most
like, how unforeseen dangers suddenly arise for men, concerning Philemon the
comic poet. Of his talent you who know enough, of his death learn in a few
words. Or do you wish a few things of his talent also?

This Philemon was a poet, a writer of Middle Comedy; he dictated plays onto
the stage with Menander and contended with him, perhaps unequal, certainly a
rival. For he even defeated him often, it shames me to say. Yet you may find
in him many witticisms, plots neatly turned, recognitions clearly unfolded,
characters matching the facts, maxims fitting to life, jokes not below the
comic sock, serious matter not all the way to the buskin. Rare are seductions
in him, and as it were errors, loves that are allowed. And no less also a
perjured pander and a burning lover and a cunning little slave and a mocking
mistress and a restraining wife and an indulgent mother and a rebuking uncle
and a helping comrade and a battling soldier, and also greedy parasites and
tenacious parents and bold courtesans. With these praises long noble in the
comic art, he happened to be reciting part of a play which he had lately made,
and when already in the third act, as is wont to happen in comedy, he was
moving more pleasant feelings, a rain arose of a sudden, just as it came to me

the begun hearing to be postponed: yet the rest, various people asking, he
would read through without interruption on the following day. The next day,

does not come to the appointment; some murmur at the poet's lateness, more

had entered stood a little while, struck by the miracle of so unexpected a

therefore, since the best poet had laid down the mask of life, they must go
straight from his audience to the funeral, that his bones must now be
gathered, presently his poems.

Protagoras, who was a sophist knowing far many things and among the first
finders of rhetoric most eloquent, a fellow-citizen and of like age with
Democritus the natural philosopher (from there doctrine was supplied to him),
they say that that Protagoras pledged with his own pupil Euathlus a fee too
rich on a rash condition, that he should then at last give him that silver if
he had won at the first trial of pleading before judges. Therefore Euathlus,
after he had easily learned thoroughly all those charms of judges and snares
of adversaries and artifices of speakers, being otherwise shrewd and gifted
toward cunning, content to know what he had desired, began to be unwilling for
what he had pledged, but by cunningly weaving delays to frustrate the master,
and for some little time to wish neither to plead nor to pay, until Protagoras
summoned him before judges and, having set out the condition on which he had
undertaken to teach, proposed a two-headed argument in both directions: /For
if I win,/ he said, /you will owe to pay the fee as condemned, or if you win,
you will no less owe to pay as having pledged, since you have won this first
cause before judges. So, if you win, you have fallen into the condition; if
you are beaten, into the condemnation./ What do you ask? The reasoning seemed
to the judges sharply closed and invincible. But Euathlus, as the most
finished pupil of so great a veteran of tricks, turned that two-headed
argument back. /For if it is so,/ he said, /I owe what you seek in neither
way. For either I win and I am dismissed by the judgment, or I am beaten and I
am released by the pact, from which I do not owe the fee if I have been beaten
in this first cause before judges. So a sentence frees me in every way if I
win, the condition if I am beaten by a sentence./ Do not these arguments of
sophists seem to you to cohere, turned against one another in the manner of
thorns which the wind has rolled, with equal stings on both sides, like
penetration, mutual wound? And therefore Protagoras's fee, so rough, so full
of thorns, should be left to the shrewd and the greedy: compared with which,
of course, that other fee is worth so much more, which they remember Thales to
have advised.

Thales of Miletus, easily chief among those seven men famed for wisdom, for he
was the first finder of geometry among the Greeks and the surest explorer of
the nature of things and the most skilled contemplator of the stars, found the
greatest things by small lines: the circuits of times, the blasts of winds,
the goings of stars, the sounding marvels of thunders, the oblique courses of
constellations, the yearly returns of the sun, likewise of the moon either the
increases of her being born or the losses of her growing old or the obstacles
of her failing. The same man, already in declining old age, devised a divine
account concerning the sun, which I have not only learned but have even proved
by trying, how often the sun by its own magnitude measures the circle which it
traverses. This, lately found by himself, Thales is remembered to have taught
Mandrolytus of Priene, who, delighted at great cost by the new and unexpected
knowledge, bade him choose how great a fee he wished to be paid him for so
great a lesson. /It will have been enough of a fee for me,/ said Thales the
sage, /if what you have learned from me, when you have begun to bring it forth
to anyone, you do not claim for yourself, but proclaim me rather than another
as the finder of that discovery./ A fair fee altogether and worthy of such a
man and perpetual; for even to this day and then forever that fee will be paid
to Thales by all of us who have truly come to know his heavenly studies.

This fee I pay you, Carthaginians, everywhere in the nations, for the
disciplines which I obtained among you in boyhood. For everywhere I bear
myself as an alumnus of your city, everywhere I celebrate you with praises of
every kind, I more studiously cultivate your disciplines, I more gloriously
proclaim your resources, I more religiously venerate even your gods. Now too,
therefore, I will take a most auspicious beginning before your ears from the
god Aesculapius, who with undoubted power propitiously protects the citadel of
our Carthage. A hymn of that god in Greek and Latin song I will now at once
sing to you, already dedicated by me to him. For I am not unknown to him as a
sacrificer, nor a recent worshipper, nor an ungrateful priest, and I have
already venerated him with both prose and verse eloquence, so that even now I
will sing his hymn in both tongues, to which I have similarly prefixed a
dialogue Greek and Latin, in which Sabidius Severus and Julius Perseus will
converse, men deservedly most friendly both mutually between themselves and to
you and to public utilities, equal in learning and eloquence and goodwill,
uncertain whether quieter in modesty or readier in industry or more
distinguished in honors. For whom, though there is the highest concord, yet
this is the only rivalry and in this one contest, which of them loves Carthage
more, and they both contend with the highest strength from the marrow, neither
is beaten. Their conversation I have judged both most pleasing for you to
hear, fitting for me to compose, and most religious for a dedication; at the
beginning of the book I make one of those who studied with me at Athens ask
Perseus in Greek what I discussed yesterday in the temple of Aesculapius, and
little by little I join Severus to them, to whom for the meantime I have given
the parts of the Roman tongue. For Perseus also, although he himself too can
do it excellently, will yet today atticize for you.

That Asclepiades, among the chief of physicians, if you except Hippocrates
alone, first of the rest, was also the first to find how to aid the sick with
wine, but by giving it, of course, in season: the observation of which matter
he knew well, as one who most diligently noticed the disordered or very
irregular pulses of the veins. He, then, when he happened to be betaking
himself into the city and was returning from his suburban farm, saw in the
precincts of the city a huge funeral placed, very many men standing around in
a huge multitude who had come to the rites, all most sad and most shabby in
dress. He approached nearer, either to know, by the custom of human nature,
who it was, since to one asking no one had answered, or indeed so that he
himself might catch something in him from his art. Certainly, at any rate, he
brought fate to the man lying and almost put away. Already all the limbs of
that wretched man sprinkled with spices, already his mouth itself smeared with
fragrant ointment, already him laid out, already the pyre almost prepared,
having contemplated, and having most diligently noticed certain signs, he
handled the man's body again and again and found in him a hidden life. At once
he cried out that the man was living: therefore they should throw the torches
far away, they should remove the fires far away, they should pull down the
pyre, they should bring the funeral meal back from the tomb to the table. A
murmur arose in the meantime; some said the physician should be believed, some
even mocked medicine. At last, even with the man's kinsmen unwilling, whether
because they themselves already had the inheritance, or because they still did
not have faith in him, yet with difficulty and hardly Asclepiades obtained a
brief delay for the dead man, and so, wrenched from the hands of the
corpse-bearers, he brought him home as by a return from the underworld and at
once refreshed the breath, at once by certain medicines called forth the soul
lurking in the hiding-places of the body.

A celebrated saying of a wise man over the table is: /the first/ he said,
/bowl belongs to thirst, the second to cheerfulness, the third to pleasure,

the fourth to madness./ But in truth the bowl of the Muses, by reverse turn,
the more frequent and the more unmixed, the nearer to the health of the mind.
The first bowl wakes by the schoolmaster's rudiment, the second has furnished
the grammarian's teaching, the third arms with the rhetor's eloquence. Thus
far most people drink. I have drunk other bowls also at Athens: poetry's
stirring one, geometry's clear one, music's sweet one, dialectic's rather dry
one, and then of all philosophy the inexhaustible, of course, and nectared
one. For Empedocles sings poems, Plato dialogues, Socrates hymns, Epicharmus
measures, Xenophon histories, Xenocrates satires: your Apuleius cultivates all
these and the nine Muses with equal zeal, with greater will, of course, than
capacity, and perhaps more readily to be praised for that, because in all good
things the attempt is in praise, the outcome in chance, just as on the
contrary in evil deeds even crimes planned, not yet completed, are still
punished, with a bloody mind, with a clean hand. Therefore as it suffices for
punishment to have meditated things to be punished, so also for praise it is
enough to have attempted things to be proclaimed. And what greater praise or
surer, than to speak well of Carthage, where you are a whole city most
learned, among whom boys learn every discipline, young men display it, old men
teach it? Carthage, venerable mistress of our province, Carthage, heavenly
Muse of Africa, Carthage, Camena of the toga-wearers.

Even necessary haste has at times honorable delays, often as you would prefer
a will interrupted: for even those who need a road finished at a run, so much
so that they prefer to hang on a horse rather than sit in a carriage, because
of the nuisances of packs and the weights of vehicles and the delays of wheels
and the ruts of tracks (add also balls of stones and swellings of logs and
streams of fields and slopes of hills): those, then, who wish to avoid all
these delays and choose for themselves a carrying horse of lasting strength,
of lively swiftness, that is both strong to bear and rapid to go, /who glides
over fields and hills in one pace,/ as Lucilius says, yet when they fly along
the road at speed with that horse, if in the meantime they catch sight of some
noble man from the leading men, well advised, well known, although they are in
great haste, yet for the sake of his honor they check the course, they ease
the step, they slow the horse and at once they leap down to their feet, the
switch which they carry for beating the horse they transfer that rod into the
left hand, and so with the right made ready they approach and greet, and if he
asks anything for some little time, they walk for some little time and talk,
finally they gladly spend any amount of delay in the duty.

That Crates, follower of Diogenes, who was cultivated as a household god among
the men of his age at Athens: no house was ever closed, nor was there a secret
of a father of a family so hidden that Crates did not come in there in season,
a disputer and arbiter of all quarrels and wrangles among kinsmen; because the
poets remember that Hercules once subdued those huge monsters of men and
beasts by virtue and purged the circle of the earth, similarly against anger
and envy and avarice and lust and the other monsters and shames of the human
soul this philosopher was a Hercules: he drove all those plagues from minds,
he purged households, he thoroughly tamed malice, himself also half-naked and
marked by a club, even sprung from Thebes, from where memory holds that
Hercules was: therefore, before he was fully made Crates, he was numbered
among the Theban chiefs, a chosen family, a crowded household of slaves, a
house adorned with a wide vestibule, himself well clothed, well landed.
Afterward, when he understands that no protection in household property has
been left him on which to rely and pass his age, that all things are flowing
and weak, that whatever of riches there is under the sky, all those things for
living well are nothing at all [lost] .

Just as a good ship, craftsman-made, well compacted within, elegantly painted
without, with a movable tiller, firm rigging, a tall mast, a notable masthead,
shining sails, lastly with all tackle fit for use and honorable for
contemplation, if either a helmsman does not drive that ship or a storm drives
it, how easily with those excellent instruments either the deep has swallowed
them or the rocks have shattered them! But physicians also, when they have
entered to a sick man to visit, none of them, because they see very beautiful
picture-rooms in the house and coffered ceilings smeared with gold and boys
and youths of outstanding form standing in a flock in the bedroom around the
bed, bids the sick man to be of good mind, but, when he has sat down beside,
he takes the man's hand, handles it, catches the pulse of the veins and the
moments: if he meets anything disturbed and disordered there, he reports to
him that he is held by a bad disease. That rich man is forbidden food; that
day in his own copious house he does not receive bread, while in the meantime
his whole service are cheerful and feast, nor in that matter does he effect
anything by his condition.
