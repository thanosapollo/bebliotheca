OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA, DISCOURSE ON THOSE WHO HAVE FALLEN ASLEEP

Those who make the necessary sequence of our nature in those departing from
life a misfortune, and who grieve heavily over those being transferred from
the life here toward the intellectual and bodiless, do not seem to me to have
examined what our life is, but to suffer the experience of the many, who
through some irrational habit love what is present to them as fair, of
whatever sort it may be. And yet it was fitting that the one ranked before
irrational nature by
reason and thought should have his inclination toward this alone, which by the
judgment of reason appears both fair and choiceworthy, and not to choose in
every case that which through some habit and unjudged attachment appears to
them sweet and after their heart. Therefore it seems to me to be well, having
by some device withdrawn them from the disposition toward the customary, to
transfer them as far as possible toward the better mind that also befits those
who use reason. For thus would be banished from =9.29= human life the
irrationality that is practiced by the many concerning the passions. And the
zeal of the word would become for us sequential toward the underlying subject,
if first the truly good, of what sort it is, should be examined, and then we
should contemplate the property of life in the body, and besides these if by
comparison the things stored up for us through hope should be set against the
present things. For thus the contemplation would go forward toward the aim of
the word, so that the mind of the many would be transferred from the customary
toward the fair. For since in all human beings a certain natural relation
toward the fair is implanted, and every purpose is moved toward this, putting
forward the aim of the fair of all the zeal according to life, for this reason
the lack of judgment concerning the truly fair is accustomed to work out the
many of the things that are missed, so that if the truly good were evident to
all, we would never have missed that one whose nature is goodness, nor would
we have been brought together willingly with the experience of evils, if the
affairs had not been dyed with some false imagination of the fair. Therefore
before all things let us understand this by the word, what the truly good is,
so that by the error concerning this the worse may never be pursued instead of
the better. I say then that it is necessary to set down beforehand for the
word a certain definition and character of what is sought, through which a
secure understanding of the fair would come to be for us. What then is the
character of true goodness? Not only to have the beneficial toward something,
nor according to certain seasons either =9.30= to appear beneficial or
useless, nor to be fair for one and not such for another, but that which both
in itself according to its own nature is fair and for every one and at every
time holds in the same way. This, at least according to my judgment, is the
character of the nature of the fair, both unerring and undeceitful. For what
is fair neither for all nor at all times nor in itself apart from the outward
circumstance would not be judged properly in the nature of the fair. Wherefore
many of those who attend unexaminedly to the things that are imagined that the
fair is in the elements of the world, of which one examining would find none
discovered fair both in itself and always and for every one. For with the
useful from each of these there is mixed also the activity toward the
contrary. For example water is saving for those nourished in it, but
destructive for the land animals, if it should flood; likewise also the air is
saving for those whose nature is to live in it, but for those allotted the
watery life it is found corruptive and destructive, whenever something of the
things under water comes to be in it; so also fire, becoming useful for us
toward something, is corruptive in the more cases, and the sun itself one
would find fair for those who partake neither for every one nor at all times
nor according to all things;


for there are cases in which he becomes even exceedingly harmful, both boiling
beyond the due measure and drying the underlying in lack of measure, and often
working out causes of disease, and further wearing down the disease for the
more passionate of the eyes, and through the rotting of the moist things
generating certain harmful and unpleasant living things in the corrupted
putrefaction of the moist. Therefore as has been said it is necessary to
choose as fair from all things only that which =9.31= always and for all alike
is beheld in the nature of the fair, being always such and not changing toward
the outward circumstances. For concerning the other things, as many as by a
more irrational prejudgment seem fair to human beings, I mean concerning the
body and the outward things, such as strength and beauty and splendor of
family and money and powers and celebrity and all such things, since they are
evident to all from themselves I think it necessary to be silent and not
vainly through the things agreed upon to introduce a crowd into the word. For
who does not know the short-livedness of beauty and of power, or the
easy-falling of dominion, or the insubstantiality of glory, or the vain
attachment of human beings toward money, from which through the good color and
the scarcity in certain materials the fair was supposed? And these things
having been thus articulated for us, it would be necessary to examine
concerning the present life (this I mean, the one being worked through flesh)
whether it is such as to be contemplated in the character of the fair, or even
as it holds otherwise. For what is found concerning it by the word will
altogether guide the mind of those who have examined how it is necessary to be
disposed concerning the transfer from here. The life of our body, then, is
filling and emptying, being worked from two sides, the one through eating and
drinking, the other through the drawing and putting-away of air, without which
the life according to flesh does not have nature to stand together. For then
the human being ceases from living, whenever the succession of these
contraries no longer disturbs the nature; for after this such activity stands
=9.32= altogether, nothing of the outward things either flowing in or
departing in those who have been made dead, but the body constituted from
these having been separated and dissolved toward the kindred elements.
Thereafter nature is at rest through quiet, having laid to rest with its own
element the kindred and of the same stock, the earthy with the earth and its
own with the air and the proper with the moist and the corresponding with the
hot. For the bulk constituted from the things of other kinds no longer being
woven together both violently and by compulsion, but each of the things in us
having returned according to authority toward its own hearth, nature from this
ceases holding together by force in itself the connaturality of the things of
another stock. And if someone should also take sleep and waking toward the
form of this life together with the things said, he will not make the word
outside the truth. For nature also through these is always weary, being
dragged toward the contraries, and now being loosed by sleep, again being
tensed through waking, through both of which it prepares toward being emptied
and filled. If then filling and emptying is the property of our life, it would
be well now to examine against one another the character previously spoken
concerning the judgment of the fair with the properties of life, so as to see
whether this life is the true good or even another besides this. That filling
by itself would not reasonably be judged in the nature of the fair, =9.33=
such a thing is clear to all from this, that the contrary to it also (I mean
being emptied) is supposed to be fair. For in the case of things set against
one another from the contrary it is not possible that the account of the fair
be fitted equally to the things set against, but if this should be fair
according to its own nature, the thing opposed to it will altogether be evil.
But here nature has the useful equally from each of these. Therefore neither
filling nor emptying has power to receive the definition of the fair.
Therefore filling has been shown to be something other besides the good. For
that such a thing is choiceworthy neither for every one nor at all times nor
according to every form has been agreed by all. For not only is it destructive
that satiety come to be in the harmful things,


but also to pass by lack of measure the useful in the corresponding things
often becomes a cause of dangers and of corruption. And if ever, when a
plethoric condition seeks emptying, some other plethora should load upon the
one that is, such a thing becomes a heap of evils, going forward into
incurable passions. Therefore filling is a good neither for every one nor in
every way, but the useful from it comes to be also toward something and at
some time and according to the quantity and the quality. And thus one would
find also the thing thought from the contrary (I mean that according to
emptying) both dangerous for those who undergo it, if it should pass the
useful by lack of measure, and again coming to be not without profit, if it
should coincide toward something of the things that benefit, the season and
the quantity and the quality being taken along toward the useful of the
emptying. Since then the form of this life in which =9.34= we live does not
coincide toward the character of the fair, it would be agreed through the
sequence of the things said that the transfer from such a life is a separation
from no good. For it is clear that the truly and properly and primarily good
is neither emptying nor filling, which have been shown useful even at some
time and toward something and upon certain ones, upon which the character of
the truly being good does not sit. Since then there is opposition of the truly
good toward the not truly good, and the contrariety of these two is immediate,
it would be sequential for those being separated from the not truly being fair
to believe the transfer from here to come to be toward the fair by nature,
which is good always and for every one and through all things, neither
according to seasons nor toward something nor upon certain ones nor through
something, but always holding according to the same things and in the same
way. Toward this then the human soul passes from the fleshy life, taking in
exchange some other condition of life instead of the present, which to see
through accuracy of what sort it is is impossible for those still mixed with
the flesh, but from the subtraction of the things recognized according to this
life it is possible to take up a certain conjecture through analogy. For it
will no longer be woven together with bodily thickness nor living in the
equal-rule of the opposing elements, whose battle balanced toward one another
makes both our constitution and health (for the excess =9.35= of some one of
the contraries and the deficiency becomes passion and sickness of nature), in
which nothing is either contracted being emptied or distressed being loaded,
but it also comes to be altogether outside the unpleasant things from the air
(I mean both cold and heat) and released from all the things thought according
to the contrary, and coming to be in those things where life is both free and
unstrained of all the necessary toils, not suffering badly through farming,
not undergoing the overseas labors, not being unseemly through trade <and>
huckstering, separated from building and weaving and the hardship of the
vulgar crafts. It leads a certain still and quiet life as Paul says, not
fighting with horses, not fighting at sea, not being woven together in close
combat through the infantry array, not being occupied toward the construction
of weapons, not collecting taxes, not constructing ditches and walls, but it
is a certain one without such tasks and free, neither having affairs nor
providing them, in which slavery and lordship and poverty and wealth and good
birth and low birth and private lowliness and official power and every such
unevenness has no place. For the un-needy and immaterial of that life takes
away the necessity of all these and such things, in which what holds through
the hypostasis of the soul is not a participation of some dry and moist, but
the understanding of the divine nature, and instead of the aerial breath we do
not doubt the communion to be of the =9.36= true and Holy Spirit. Whose
enjoyment does not come to be exchanged according to likeness of the life here
through possession and deprivation, being both admitted and put away, but
always being filled and never circumscribing the filling by satiety. For the
intellectual luxury is unburdensome and


unfillable, always flooding unsatedly upon the desires of those who partake.
For this reason that life is a certain blessed and undefiled one, no longer
wandering by the pleasures of the sense-organs toward the judgment of the
fair. What then is gloomy in the affair, on account of which we look sullen at
the transfer of those close to us? Unless perhaps someone thinks this
grievous, that the transfer comes to be for them toward the passionless and
undisturbed life, which neither admits pains of blows, nor fears a threat of
fire, nor the wounds through iron, nor the misfortunes from earthquakes and
shipwrecks and captivities, nor the attacks of flesh-eating beasts, nor the
stings and bites of the creeping and venomous things, in which no one is
either swollen with conceit or trodden in lowliness, or made savage by
rashness, or terrified by cowardice, or puffed around by anger, boiling with
spirit and raving, or shaken by fear, not being able to hold against the
impulse of the one who rules, in which there is no care of what sort the
characters of the kings are, what the legislations, of what manner those
appointed over the offices, of what sort the edicts =9.37=, how great the
yearly tax, nor whether much rain has come flooding the cultivated by lack of
measure, nor whether hail has made useless the hopes of the farmers, nor
whether drought prevailing dries up everything that grows. But it has also all
freedom from the remaining evils of life; for the gloom of orphanhood does not
grieve that life, the evils from widowhood have no place, and the many-formed
sicknesses of the body are idle, and the envies against those faring well and
the disdain against those faring ill and all such things have been banished
from that life, and a certain equal speech and equal law through all peaceful
freedom is fellow-citizen with the people of the souls, each having that which
he may prepare for himself from purpose. And if something worse instead of the
better should be prepared for someone from some lack of counsel, death is
without blame of such things, purpose having chosen what seemed good according
to authority. For what then do those who lament the one gone away take it
hard? And indeed if the one who stripped off together pleasure and grief with
the body were not altogether purified of every passionate disposition, that
one would more justly have lamented those still remaining, who suffer the same
as those living in a prison, whom habit toward the gloomy things and
companionship of the gloom has made to suppose the present both gentle and
without grief. And =9.38= perhaps those too look sullen at those being cast
out of the guard, by ignorance of the brightness that receives those released
from the gloom. For if they knew the spectacles in the open air, both the
ethereal beauty and the heavenly height and the beams of the lights and the
dance of the stars and <the> solar circuits and the lunar course and the
many-formed season of the earth in the sprouts and the pleasant sight of the
sea, crisping smoothly in sunlike beam through a still breeze, and of the
buildings according to the cities the
beauties both private and public, through which the splendid and costly cities
are adorned, if then those implanted in the prison knew these and such things,
they would not have wailed over those being led forward from the guard as
being separated from some good. What therefore it is likely that those outside
the prison think concerning those still confined, as suffering beforehand in a
pitiable life, this, it seems to me, those also who have come to be outside
the guard of this life, if it were at all possible for them to show through
tears the sympathy toward those suffering badly, lament and weep over those
being stretched in the pains of this life, that they do not see the
supercosmic and immaterial beauties, thrones and rulers and authorities and
lordships and angelic armies and =9.39= assemblies of the holy and the city
above and the superheavenly gathering of those enrolled. For the beauty lying
above these, which the undeceitful word declared that the pure in heart see,
is both better than every hope and higher than the conjecture from guesses.
And not this alone


would have been supposed from us to those who have transferred as worthy of
groans and grief, but that, with so many painful things lying around the life,
so great a joint-disposition concerning the wretched things has been set in
them, that they do not bear the assault of them as a certain necessary
service, but they make the zeal that these may remain into the continuous. For
the desire concerning powers and greedinesses and these enjoymental
gluttonies, and if any other such thing is pursued, for the sake of which also
weapons and wars and mutual killings and all the hardship and craft worked
willingly, is nothing other than a certain heap of misfortunes introduced into
life from purpose with both zeal and eagerness. But there is not a passion of
tears in those gone away, because neither is there any other passion, and
those released from flesh and blood, being mind and spirit, do not have nature
to be seen by those buried in the thickness of the body, nor to admonish
through themselves the human beings to stand away from the wandered judgment
concerning the things that are. Therefore let our own mind speak in their
place, and let us say, having come to be as far as possible outside the bodies
by thought and having withdrawn the soul from the attachment toward matter,
that =9.40= "O human being, everyone who partakes of the nature, Give heed to
yourself according to the commandment of Moses and know yourself accurately,
who you are, having distinguished by reasoning what you truly are, and what is
beheld about you. Do not ever, looking at the things outside you, suppose that
you behold yourself. Learn from the great Paul, who examined the nature
through accuracy, who says that the one of us is the outer human being and the
other the inner, and that when that one is being corrupted this one is being
renewed. Do not then, looking at what is being corrupted, think that you look
at yourself (for that too will sometime be free of corruption, whenever in the
regeneration the mortal and dissoluble is refashioned toward the immortal and
indissoluble, but at least now what appears of us outwardly flows and falls
apart and is corrupted). Therefore it is not necessary to look toward this,
because it is not even fitting to look toward any other of the things seen,
Paul having said thus, that We not looking at the things seen but at the
things not seen; for the things seen are temporary, but the things not seen
eternal, but having turned the contemplation upon the invisible of the things
in us to believe that we truly are that which escapes the sensory
understanding. Let us become then according to the proverbial word Knowers of
ourselves. For to know oneself becomes a purification of the faults from
ignorance. But it is not easy to behold oneself, at least for the one wanting
to see the truly himself, unless some device makes the impossible possible for
us. For what =9.41= nature did in the case of the bodily eyes, which looking
at all the other things remain unseeing of themselves, in the same manner the
soul also, searching through all the other things and being busy about and
tracking the things outside itself, is unable to see itself. Therefore what
comes to be in the case of the eyes, this let the soul also imitate. For those
too, since there is not for them from nature a power to turn back the optic
activity toward the contemplation of themselves and to behold themselves,
having seen in a mirror the form and the shape of their own circle, behold
themselves through the image. Thus it is necessary that the soul also look
toward its own image, and whatever it may see in the character to which it has
been made like, to behold this as its own. But it is fitting to change
something of the example a little, in order that the thought may be made
proper to the word; for in the case of the form in the mirror the image is
shaped toward the archetype, but in the case of the character of the soul we
have thought the reverse; for according to the divine beauty the form of the
soul is imaged. Therefore whenever the soul looks toward its own archetype,
then through accuracy it beholds itself. What then is the divine to which the
soul has been made like? Not body, not shape, not form, not magnitude, not
resistance, not weight, not place, not time, not any other such thing through
which the material creation is recognized, but all these and such things


having been subtracted, it is necessary altogether to think the remaining
something intellectual and immaterial and intangible and bodiless and without
interval. If then you grasp such to be the character of the archetype, it is
altogether sequential that the soul, having been formed according to that
form, =9.42= be recognized through the same characters, so that this too is
both immaterial and formless and intellectual and bodiless. Let us reason then
when the human nature more nearly approaches the archetypal beauty, in living
through flesh or whenever we come to be outside this. But such a thing is
clear to every one, that just as the flesh, being material, has been made akin
to this material life, so also the soul then partakes of the intellectual and
immaterial life, whenever it shakes off the matter that surrounds it. What
then in these things is worthy of misfortune? For if body were the truly good,
we ought to take it hard toward the alienation of the flesh, as falling out of
that, the kinship toward the good being altogether thrown away together with
the body. But since the good beyond every thought, according to whose image we
have come to be, is both intellectual and bodiless, it would be sequential to
be persuaded that, whenever through death we pass over toward the bodiless, we
approach that nature which has been separated from all bodily coarseness, and,
stripping off the fleshy wrapping as a certain ugly mask, we return into the
proper beauty, in which we were formed at the beginnings, having come to be
according to the image of the archetype. And such a thought would become a
subject of gladness, not of dejection, for those being spoken to, that the
human being, having fulfilled this necessary service, no longer lives in
things belonging to others, having given back to each of the elements its own
which he borrowed from them, and having returned into the hearth proper to him
and according to nature, the pure and bodiless. For the matter of the body is
truly a certain foreign and alien thing to the =9.43= bodiless nature, with
which the mind, having been woven together by necessity in this life, suffers
badly in this, living together with the life of another stock. For of the
weaving-together of the elements toward one another, as of certain humans of
other tongues and estranged in characters filling up one people from different
nations, the communion becomes both forced and discordant, each being dragged
by its own nature toward the kindred and proper. And the mind mixed into
these, being uncompounded in a simple and uniform nature, lives in foreign and
alien things, being not of the same stock with the people surrounding it from
the elements, which, having been coiled by some necessity into the many-parted
of the body, does violence to its own nature, being united to those of another
stock. And the elements being dragged naturally toward the dissolution from
one another upon the kindred and proper, the sense is necessarily pained as
the connaturality is loosed and split; and the thinking of the soul is
pained-together with the sense, being inclined from habit toward what always
grieves. Then therefore the mind ceases being distressed and being pained,
whenever it comes to be outside the battle that stands together in the weaving
of the opposing elements. For whenever either the cold is defeated, the hot
having prevailed, or conversely the heat flees the excess of the chill, and
the moist yields to the dry through the prevailing, or the solid of the dry is
dissolved in the excess of the moistness: then, the war in us having been
loosed through death, the mind keeps peace, having left =9.44= the mid-space
of the battle, I mean the body, and having come to be outside the array of the
elements toward one another, lives by itself, recovering through quiet its own
strength that had toiled in the weaving of the body." These things then and
such things the mind discusses with those living in the body, all but sending
forth a voice, that "O human beings, you neither know accurately in what
things you are, and into what you will pass over you do not yet understand.
For of what sort the present is by nature, the word has not yet been able to
discover, but it looks toward the habit of living alone, not being able to
know what the nature of the body is, what the power of the senses, what the
arrangement of the organic members, what the economy of the inward parts, what
the self-moved activity of the nerves, how of the things in


us the one is fixed into the nature of bone, and the other is made substance
into the light-formed beam of the eye, how from the same food and the same
drink the one is thinned into hairs, and the other is broadened into nails at
the tips of the fingers, or how the fire in the heart is always kindled, being
carried through the arteries upon the whole body, or how what is drunk,
whenever it comes to be in the liver, changes both the form and the quality
through a certain alteration, being made blood of itself. Of all which the
knowledge until the present is unspeakable, so that we are ignorant of the
life in which we live. And those living together with sense are altogether
unable to behold the life separated from sense. For how would someone see
through sense what is outside sense? Both lives then being similarly unknown,
this one through our looking toward the apparent alone =9.45=, and that one
through sense not reaching, what have you suffered, O human beings, clinging
to this as to a good although it is unknown, and fearing and shuddering at
that as hard and worthy of fear for no other reason than this alone, that it
is unknown of what sort it is? And yet many other things also both of those
appearing to us according to sense and unknown we have not feared. For what
the nature of the things appearing according to heaven is, or what it is that
leads around the motion of the poles from the contrary, or what it is that
props the solidity of the earth, and how the flowing nature of the waters
always comes to be from the earth and the earth is not spent, and many other
such things we have neither known nor do we judge the ignorance worthy of
fears. But even the nature itself that surpasses every mind, the divine and
blessed and incomprehensible, that it is we have believed, but in what its
being is grasped no understanding through conjecture has yet been found, and
nevertheless we love the unknown from whole heart and soul and power, that
which cannot be grasped by reasonings. Why then upon the life alone that
receives us after this life does this irrational fear stand together, we
fearing through the ignorance alone what we do not know, as comes to be in the
case of infants who are terrified toward the insubstantial suspicions? For the
one wanting to look toward the truth of the things that are first comes to be
in a thinking-around of the affair, then calculates of what sort it is
according to nature, whether something useful and approachable or something
hard and to be turned from; =9.46= but the altogether unclear and unknown, how
would someone of those having mind judge the withdrawal of habit alone to be
hard, suspecting it as an assault of some fire or beast? And yet we are
educated through life clearly not always to look toward habit, but always to
transfer the desires toward the fair. For neither is life for those being
formed in an embryo through all, but as long as they are in the inward parts,
nature makes the life in the belly sweet and corresponding for them, nor
whenever they come to be outside do they remain through all at the teat, but
as long as such a thing is fair and corresponding for the incomplete of the
age. And after this they pass over toward another sequence of life, being
persuaded by habit to remain with the breast in nothing; then after the
infantile condition other pursuits of the youths come to be and others of
those more advanced in age, toward which the human being passes through
sequence, changing together with the ages also the habit without grief. Just
as then, if there were some voice for the one being nourished in the maternal
belly, it would have been angry at being banished from the inward parts
through birth and would have cried that it suffers terrible things, being torn
from the dear way of life (which indeed it also does, casting out together
with the first breath at the birth the tear, as being angry and wailing at the
separation from the customary life), so, it seems to me, those taking it hard
toward the change of the present life suffer the experience of the embryos, in
wanting through all to live in the place of this material unpleasantness
=9.47=. For whenever the birth-pang of death midwifes the human beings toward
another life, they themselves, whenever they go forward into that


light and draw the pure breath, they know by experience how great the
difference of that life toward the now is, but those left behind in this moist
and flabby life, being embryos simply and not human beings, call wretched the
one who has gone forward first from the constraint that surrounds us as having
come to be outside some good, not knowing that for that one, according to
likeness of the infant that has been born, the eye is opened, whenever he
comes to be outside what now holds together (and it is necessary altogether to
think the eye of the soul, by which it sees through the truth of the things
that are), and the hearing sense-organ is opened, through which he hears the
unspeakable words Which it is not permitted for a human being to speak as the
apostle says, and the mouth is opened and draws the pure and immaterial
spirit, through which he is tensed toward the intelligible voice and the true
word, whenever he is mixed into the sound of those keeping festival in the
chorus of the saints; and thus he is also deemed worthy of a divine taste,
through which he knows according to the psalmody that The Lord is kind, and
through the smelling activity he apprehends the fragrance of Christ, and he
takes on in addition the touching power, the soul touching the truth and
handling the word according to the witness of John. If then these and such
things are stored up for human beings after the birth through death, what does
the mourning want, and the sullenness, and the dejection? Let the one looking
toward the nature of the affairs answer us now, whether =9.48= he thinks it
more honorable to wander by the bodily sense-organs about the judgment of the
fair, than with the naked eye of the soul to look toward the very truth of the
affairs. For here a certain necessity sits upon the soul to be a slave to an
alien judgment in the suspicion concerning the fair. For since the perfect
power of the soul is still uncontainable for the infant-being body, and the
activity of the sense-organs is born together with the infant perfect at once,
for this reason the thought is anticipated by sense in the judgment of the
fair, and what has already appeared to the sense-organs and been supposed to
be fair and been prejudged through habit the soul receives untested, having
been persuaded that that is fair which sense, having anticipated, may bear
witness to, contemplating the fair in certain colors and flavors and such
trifles. Which no longer appearing after the exit from the body, there is
every necessity that the truly good appear to the soul, toward which it has
been made akin according to nature. For neither will the sight still be baited
toward the good-colored, this eye no longer being, nor will the inclination of
purpose be toward any other of the things that sweeten the sense-organs, all
bodily sense having been quenched, and only the intellectual activity
immaterially and bodilessly touching the intelligible beauty unhindered,
nature will receive back its own good, which is neither color nor shape nor
interval nor magnitude, but which passes by every conjectural imagining. What
then will
