OF GREGORY THE THEOLOGIAN, ON HIS OWN BROTHER CAESARIUS
A FUNERAL ORATION

1.1 You think me perhaps, O friends and brothers and fathers, the sweet both
thing and name, as one about to cast lamentations and wailings upon the
departed, to receive the discourse eagerly, or as one stretching out long and
clever speeches, in which the many rejoice; 1.2 and some of you have prepared
yourselves as if to join in mourning and lamentation, so that in my suffering
you may weep your own, as many as have anything of this kind, and may school
the pain in friendly sufferings, and others as if they will feast the hearing
and be more pleased; 1.3 for we must, they think, make even the misfortune a
display, such as our things once were, when in other matters we were
sufficiently excessive even of matter, and ambitious about speeches, before we
looked up to the true and highest Word, and, having given all to God from whom
all things come, received God in place of all. 1.4 By no means, do not suppose
this of us, if you wish to suppose anything favorable. For we will neither
lament the departed more than is well, we who do not even accept such things
in others, nor will we praise beyond measure; and yet a speech is a dear and
most proper gift, if anything else is, to the man of letters, and praise to
the one who especially loved my speeches; 1.5 and not a gift only, but also a
debt, the most just of all debts; but only so far as to discharge the law
about these things, both having wept and having admired (for not even this is
outside the philosophy among us; for "The memory of the just is with praises";
and, "Over a dead man," he says, "bring down tears, and as one suffering
terrible things begin a lament," setting us equally apart from insensibility
and from excess); 1.6 after this already we will both exhibit the weakness of
human nature, and remind you of the dignity of the soul, and apply the
consolation owed to those who are in pain, and transfer the grief from the
flesh and the things of the season to the spiritual and the eternal.

2.1 The parents of Caesarius, that I may begin from here, from where it is
most becoming for us, are those whom you all know, and whose virtue, both
seeing and hearing, you both emulate and admire, and recount to those who do
not know, if indeed there are any among men, each taking some other part;
since it is not possible for the same man to take all, nor is it the work of
one tongue, even if someone is among the most laborious and ambitious; 2.2 for
whom, though many and great things belong to them for praise, if I do not seem
to anyone excessive in admiring my own, one thing is greatest of all, and is
as it were some other distinctive mark, piety; I mean these reverend and gray
ones, no less to be honored for virtue than for old age; whose bodies have
grown weary with time, but whose souls are young toward God.

3.1 The father was well grafted out of the wild olive into the cultivated
olive, and so far shared in the fatness that he was entrusted with grafting
others also and had the care of souls put into his hands, sitting high, and in
a high manner, in front of this people, a second Aaron or Moses, counted
worthy to draw near to God and to furnish the divine voice to the others who
stand far off, gentle, without anger, calm in appearance, hot in spirit, much
in what is seen, richer in what is hidden. 3.2 Why should I paint for you what
is known? For not even if we stretch out a long discourse would we say
anything so great as is worthy, and as each both knows with us and demands of
the discourse; and it is better to yield to your thoughts than by speech to
cut away the greater part of the wonder.

4.1 The mother was from above and from her ancestors consecrated to God, and
bringing down piety as a necessary inheritance not into herself only but also
into those from her, a holy lump in truth from a holy firstfruit; and she so
increased and multiplied it that already for some, for I will speak it, even
if the word is bold, even the husband's perfection was believed and said to
have become the work of no other than this woman, and (O the wonder) a greater
and more perfect piety was given as the prize of piety. 4.2 Both were lovers
of children and lovers of Christ, the most paradoxical thing, or rather lovers
of Christ more than lovers of children. For whom even of their children there
was one enjoyment, to be both known and named from Christ, and one measure of
blessedness in children, virtue and kinship toward the better. 4.3
Compassionate, sympathetic, snatching the greater part from moths and robbers
and from the world-ruler, transferring themselves from the sojourn into the
habitation, and treasuring up for the children as the greatest inheritance the
splendor from there. 4.4 Thus indeed they also reached a sleek old age, equal
in honor both in virtue and in years, and full of days, both of those that
remain likewise and of those that are loosed, each so far not having the first
things on earth as they were hindered by one another from the first place; and
they filled the measure of every happiness, except this last, as one might
suppose, whether one must call it a trial or a dispensation. 4.5 And this is,
as my word has it, that having sent forward the more unstable of the children
because of age, they may thus already end life in safety, and be translated
household and all toward the things above.

5.1 And I have gone through these things, not wishing to praise them, nor
being ignorant that one would scarcely reach what is worthy, even if he set
their praise as a whole subject of a discourse; but that I might show the
virtue owed to Caesarius from parents, and that you may neither wonder nor
disbelieve, if having obtained such begetters he presented himself worthy of
such praises, but the contrary, if he had looked toward others, neglecting the
examples of his own and of those near. 5.2 The first things then were such as
it was fitting they should be for those truly well born and about to live
well. And that I may cut short the things in the middle, beauty and stature
and the grace of the man upon all things and as it were a harmony in tones,
because it is not ours to admire such things, even if to others they do not
appear small, I will go on to the things next of the discourse, and which it
is not easy to pass by even for one who wishes.

6.1 Having then been reared and educated under such characters, and having
been sufficiently trained in the studies here, in which that man by speed and
greatness of nature was beyond the many more than one could even say (O how
shall I pass by the memory of these things without tears, and may the
suffering not prove me unphilosophical against the promise?), 6.2 but when a
time of going abroad seemed good, and then first we were split from one
another, I remaining in the schools in Palestine, which were then in bloom,
according to a love of rhetoric, and he, having reached the city of Alexander,
which both then and now is and is held to be a workshop of every education.
6.3 What shall I say first, or what greatest, of his fair things? And what, if
I pass it by, shall I not injure the discourse with the greatest loss? Who was
more faithful to teachers than he? Who more beloved to companions? Who more
fled the companionship and converse of the worthless? Who attached himself
more to that of the best, both to others and to the most reputable and most
notable of those from the fatherland? knowing that not even this contributes a
small thing toward virtue or vice, associations. 6.4 From which, who was more
honored by rulers than he? Who to the whole city, although through its size
all are hidden, either more known for temperance, or more conspicuous for
understanding?

7.1 What kind of education did he not go through? Or rather, what kind, as
not even another the only one? And whom did he allow to come near him, even a
little, not only of those of his own time and of the same age, but even of the
elders and those older in the studies, having trained all as one, and each
instead of all, having conquered by love of labor those winged in nature, and
by sharpness of mind those noble in exercise, or rather having surpassed the
swift in speed, and the laborious in zeal, and those dexterous in both by
both? 7.2 Of geometry at least and astronomy, and of the education dangerous
to others, having chosen out so much as was useful, and this was, from the
harmony and order of the heavenly things to admire the Craftsman, he fled so
much of it as was harmful, not giving the things that are and the things that
come to be to the course of the stars, as those who raise up against the
Creator the creation that is fellow-slave with themselves, but referring to
God, as is reasonable, both all other things and the motion of these. 7.3 And
of numbers and calculations and of the wonderful medicine, so much as
philosophizes about natures and temperaments and the beginnings of diseases,
so as, the roots being taken away, to cut off together also the sprouts, who
is so unlearned or contentious as to give him second things, and not to be
content if he should be numbered immediately after him, carrying the privilege
among the second? 7.4 And these things are not a word without witness, but
both the eastern lot together and the western, and so much as he later went
through, are notable pillars of his education.

8.1 And when, having gathered every virtue and learning, as a great merchant
ship gathers merchandise of every kind, into his own single soul, he was
setting out toward his own city, that he might also give to others a share of
the fair cargo of his own education, here too some wondrous thing also came
about; and there is nothing like (for this, when remembered, gladdens me most
of all, and would make you more pleased) telling it in brief. 8.2 The mother
was praying a certain maternal and child-loving prayer, that as she had sent
both forth, so she might also see them returned with one another. For we
seemed a kind of pair, and if not to others, yet to a mother at least, worthy
of prayer and of seeing when beheld with one another, the pair now badly
dissolved by envy; 8.3 and God having so moved, who hears a just prayer and
honors a parent's affection toward well-disposed children, from no device or
signal, the one from Alexandria, the other from Greece, at the same time into
the same city, the one from land, the other from sea, we put in. 8.4 And the
city was Byzantium, the city that now sits in front of Europe; in which
Caesarius, not much time having gone by, carried off such glory that public
honors and a marriage among the reputable and a share in the senate were set
before him, and an embassy was sent to the emperor, the great one, by a common
decree, that the first city be both adorned and honored with the first of men
of letters, if he cared at all that it be truly first and worthy of the name,
8.5 and that this be added to all the stories about it, that it was beautified
with Caesarius both as physician and as inhabitant, although with the rest of
its splendor it was thriving with many and great men, both in philosophy and
in the rest of education. 8.6 But this is enough. Then at any rate what
happened seemed to the others some irrational and causeless chance, such as
the automatic brings many of in our affairs; but to those who love God it was
exceedingly clear that what had come about was nothing other than a work of
God-loving parents, gathering the children from land and sea into one
fulfillment of prayer.

9.1 Come, let us not pass by this either of the fair things of Caesarius,
which to others perhaps is small and not even worthy of memory, but to me both
then and now seemed greatest, if indeed brotherly love is among the
praiseworthy things, and I will not cease setting it among the first, as often
as I recount his things. 9.2 The city was holding him with the honors I spoke
of, and said it would not let him go even if anything should happen; but I,
pulling the other way, prevailed, I who was in all things great and

honored with Caesarius, both to fulfill the prayer for the parents, and the
debt for the fatherland, and the longing for myself, 9.3 having taken him as
companion of the road and fellow traveler, and having been preferred not only
to cities and peoples, nor to honors and revenues, which were many and from
many places, some flowing together to him, and some hoped for, but even almost
to the emperor himself, and to the commands from there. 9.4 From here I
resolved to philosophize, and to be refashioned toward the life above, having
shaken off all ambition as some heavy master and a hard sickness; or rather
the longing was older, and the life later. 9.5 And him, having dedicated the
first things of his education to his own fatherland and having been admired
worthily of the labors, after this a desire of glory and of standing in front
of the city, as at least he was persuading me, gives himself to the palaces,
doing things not altogether dear to us and according to our mind (for I will
also make a defense to you, that to have been ranked last with God
both seems and is higher than to carry the first things with the king below),
yet not worthy of blame. 9.6 For to philosophize, by how much it is greatest,
by so much it is also hardest, and the attempt is not of many, nor of others
than those called forth by the divine greatness of mind, which gives a hand to
those who have chosen well; 9.7 and it is no small thing if someone, having
set in front the second life, should claim a share of nobility, and should
have more account of God and of his own salvation than of the splendor below;
and should put the latter forward as a stage, or some mask of the many and
seasonal things, playing the drama of this world, but himself should live to
God, with the image which he knows he has received from him and owes to the
one who has given it; which indeed we also know Caesarius to have thought.

10.1 For he is ranked in the first rank among physicians, not even having
needed much labor, but having exhibited only the education, or rather some
short prologue as it were of the education, and being numbered at once among
the friends of the emperor, he reaps the greatest honors. 10.2 And he sets
forth the humanity of the art without pay to those in office, knowing that
nothing so much as virtue and being known for the fairest things leads
forward; and of those to whom he was second in rank, these he far surpassed in
glory, being beloved to all through temperance, and for this trusted with the
precious things and needing nothing of Hippocrates the oath-giver, so that
even the simplicity of Crates was nothing when viewed against his; 10.3 and
more reverend to all than according to the rank, always being counted worthy
of great present things, and being judged worthy of greater hoped-for things
both by the emperors themselves and by as many as have the first things after
them. 10.4 And the greatest thing, that neither by glory nor by the luxury in
the midst was the nobility of the soul corrupted; but though many and great
things belonged to him, first toward dignity was both to be and to be named a
Christian, and all things together were some child's play and nonsense to him
when judged against this one thing; 10.5 for the other things are as on a
stage and played for others, the stage being most quickly both set up and
taken down, and perhaps being destroyed more easily than being put together,
as one may see from the many changes of life, and from the good season that
falls both up and down; and the only proper good, and remaining securely, is
piety.

11.1 These things Caesarius philosophized even in the cloak; with these
thoughts he both lived and departed, making known to God and presenting,
greater than the piety appearing toward the common, that according to the
hidden man. 11.2 And if I must, having passed by all things, the protection of
those who had suffered misfortune from the family, the contempt of pride, the
equal honor toward friends, the freedom of speech toward rulers, the contests
and words on behalf of truth, which many times he set up many and toward many,
not only logically, but also exceedingly piously and fervently, say one thing
instead of all of his, the most notable; 11.3 the

ill-named emperor was raging against us, and having first gone mad against
himself, from the denial toward Christ he was already unbearable also to the
others, not even enrolling himself into impiety with equal greatness of soul
as the remaining Christ-fighters, but stealing the persecution in a fiction of
equity, and according to the crooked serpent, which occupied his soul, by
devices of every kind dragging down the wretched into his own pit. 11.4 And
his first artifice and sophism was, that those suffering as Christians should
be punished as evildoers, so that we might not even obtain the honor upon the
contests; for the noble man begrudged even this to Christians; and the second,
that the name of persuasion should be attached to what was happening, not of
tyranny, so that the thing of shame might be greater than the danger for those
going of their own will toward impiety; 11.5 and drawing some by money, some
by ranks, some by promises, some by honors of every kind, which he did not
even bring forward in a kingly way, but even exceedingly in a slavish way, in
the sight of all, and all by the witchcraft of words and by the example
according to himself, after many he tries also Caesarius. Alas for the madness
and the folly, if he hoped to plunder both one who was Caesarius and my
brother and of these parents.

12.1 But, that I may linger a little more with the discourse, I will take my
fill of the story, as those present of the spectacle, that noble man was going
in, having fenced himself with the sign of Christ, and putting forward the
great Word as his own, toward the one much in arms and great in cleverness of
words; 12.2 and having been dismayed at nothing toward the sight nor having
cast down anything of his mind by flattery, he was an athlete ready both in
word and in deed to contend toward the one powerful in both. 12.3 Such then
was the stadium, and so great the combatant of piety; and as president of the
games, from here Christ, arming the athlete with his own sufferings, and from
there a terrible tyrant, fawning with familiarity of words and frightening
with the bulk of authority; 12.4 and a theatre from both sides, both of those
still remaining with piety and of those snatched away by him, watching which
way the things concerning them would incline, having more agony who would win
than those about whom the theatre was.

13.1 Did you not fear for Caesarius, lest he suffer something unworthy of the
eagerness? "But take courage"; for the victory is with Christ, who has
overcome the world. The things one by one of what was then said or set forth
to recount now I at least, know well, would have valued above all; for the
discourse also has certain logical turns and clevernesses, to me at least not
unpleasant toward memory; but it would be altogether outside the season and
the discourse. 13.2 And when, having loosed all his weavings in the words, and
having thrust aside every attempt, both unseen and seen, as some child's play,
with a great and bright voice he proclaimed that he both was and remains a
Christian, not even thus is he altogether sent away; 13.3 for a terrible
desire held the emperor to be with the education of Caesarius and to be
beautified by it; when also he uttered this famous thing in the hearings of
all: "O fortunate father, O unfortunate children"; since he counted us also
worthy to honor with a share of the dishonor, whose education at Athens he
knew and the piety. 13.4 And having been reserved for a second entrance, when
justice had well armed that man against the Persians, he comes back to us a
blessed fugitive and a bloodless trophy-bearer and more conspicuous in the
dishonor than in the splendor.

14.1 This victory I judge far higher and more honorable than that great hand
of his and the high purple and the costly diadem; with this story I am more
lifted up than if he had portioned out to him the whole kingdom. 14.2 From the
wicked times then he withdraws, and this according to our legislation, which,
a season having come, commands to run risks on behalf of the truth, and not to
betray piety by cowardice, but so long as it is possible, not to call forth
the dangers

commanding, whether from fear for our souls, or from sparing those who bring
on the danger. 14.3 And when the gloom was loosed, and exile judged well, and
the polished sword cast down the impious one, and the affairs returned to
Christians, what need is there to say with what glory and honor, or of what
kind and how many testimonies, and as giving a favor rather than receiving it,
he is taken up again into the palaces, and the second honor succeeds the
former? 14.4 And time passed emperors by, but for Caesarius the thing of good
repute was indissoluble and of the first places with them, and a contest for
emperors who should more make Caesarius his own, and of whom he should more be
named friend and acquaintance. Such were the things of piety for Caesarius and
from piety. Let both young men and men hear, and through the same virtue let
them press toward the same appearance (for of good labors the fruit is of good
fame), as many as take even this through zeal and as a part of happiness.

15.1 But of what kind also is this of the wonders about him, having the
greatest proof both of the piety of the parents and of his? He was spending
time in the land of the Bithynians, holding an office not among the least from
the emperor. And it was, to keep the moneys for the emperor and to have the
care of the treasures; for from here the emperor preludes the greater offices
for him. 15.2 And of the earthquake that happened lately in Nicaea, which is
said to have become the hardest of those ever remembered, having overtaken
almost all and having made them vanish together with the beauty of the city,
he alone of the notable, or altogether with few, is preserved from the
danger, and an unbelievable salvation, having been sheltered by the collapse
itself, and carrying small signs of the danger, so much as to take the fear as
a pedagogue of the greater salvation, and to become wholly of the portion
above, having transferred the service from the things that are moved, and
having exchanged the palaces for himself. 15.3 This then he both was
intending, and was praying together with himself in zeal, as writing to me he
was persuading, I having seized the season for admonition, which not even at
other times I ceased doing, being jealous of that great nature being turned in
the worse things, and the philosophic soul thus being rolled in the public
things, and as it were a sun being covered over with a cloud. 15.4 But of the
earthquake he became stronger, of the disease no longer; for he was a man; and
the one thing was his own, the other common toward the others; and the one of
piety, the other of nature. And the consolation anticipated the suffering, so
that, having been shaken by the death, we might boast in the unexpectedness of
the then salvation. 15.5 And now the great Caesarius has been saved back to
us, honored dust, a praised corpse, being sent on with hymns from hymns, being
escorted by the platforms of martyrs, being honored by the holy hands of
parents, a mother by bright clothing bringing piety in exchange for the
suffering, with tears defeated by philosophy, with psalmodies putting the
lamentations to sleep, and of the newly created soul, which the Spirit through
water reshaped, reaping the prizes worthy.

16.1 This is the burial-gift to you, Caesarius, from me; these are the
firstfruits of my speeches, which, having often blamed them as being hidden,
you were about to strip upon yourself. This is the adornment from me, and to
you, I know well, dearest of every adornment; 16.2 not flowing and soft
threads of silkworms, in which not even while alive you rejoiced according to
the many, being adorned with virtue alone, nor weavings of transparent linen,
nor pourings of costly myrrh, which you were sending away to the women's
quarters even before, and of which one day looses the fragrance, nor any other
of the small things and things honorable to the small, all of which this
bitter stone would have hidden today together with the fair body. 16.3 Let
there perish for me also

Hellenic contests and myths, through which unfortunate youths were honored,
setting forth the prizes of small contests of small things; and as many things
as through drink-offerings and firstfruits, or crowns and newly plucked
flowers, men consecrate those who have departed, serving an ancestral law
rather and an unreason of suffering than reason. 16.4 And my gift is a
discourse, which perhaps even the time to come will take up, always being
moved, and not allowing the one who has withdrawn from here to depart
altogether, but always guarding the one honored both in hearings and in souls,
and setting forth an image of the longed-for more vivid than tablets.

17.1 The things from us then are such. And if they are small and less than the
worth, even what is according to power is dear to God. And some things we have
given back, and some we will give, bringing the honors and memories through
the year, we at least who are left remaining in life. 17.2 And may you walk
the heavens for us, O divine and holy head, and in the bosom of Abraham,
whichever these are, may you rest, and may you look upon a dance of angels,
and glories and splendors of blessed men; 17.3 or rather may you dance with
them and rejoice with them, laughing at all the things here from on high, both
the so-called riches and the thrown-down ranks, and the lying honors, and the
wandering through the senses, and the going-around of this life, and the
confusion and ignorance as in a night-battle, standing beside the great King,
and being filled with the light from there; 17.4 of which having received a
small outflow from here, so much as to imagine in mirrors and enigmas, may we
thereafter meet the very spring of the fair, with a pure mind looking upon the
pure truth, and may we find this as a wage of the love of labor here about the
fair, the more perfect participation and contemplation of the fair there;
which indeed books and theologian souls declare as the end of our mystic
initiation.

18.1 What then remains? To bring the healing from the word to those in pain.
And great is [for those who mourn] the drug from those who share the pain; and
those who have the equal of the suffering are more toward consolation for
those who suffer. Most of all then our discourse is toward such, on whose
behalf I would be ashamed if they should not, as of every other of the fair
things, so also of endurance carry the first things. 18.2 For even if they are
lovers of children more than all, yet they are also more than all philosophers
and lovers of Christ, and having themselves practiced the passage from here
from more, and having taught those from them, or rather having set the whole
life as a practice of loosing. 18.3 And if the suffering still darkens the
reasonings, and, as some rheum having come under the eye, does not allow one
to see clearly what is needed, come, receive consolation, you the elders from
the young, and you the fathers from the child, and from the one who ought to
be admonished by those of such age, you who have admonished many, and have
gathered the experience by the long time. And wonder at nothing, if a young
man admonishes old men; even this is yours, if I have anything better than
gray hair to see together. 18.4 How much time yet shall we live, O honored
gray hairs and those drawing near to God? How much shall we still suffer here?
Not even the whole life of men is long, as to compare with the divine and
endless nature, much less the remainder of life, and the loosing, as we might
say, of the human breath, and the last things of the seasonal life. How much
has Caesarius outstripped us? How much yet shall we mourn the departed? Are we
not pressing toward the same abode? 18.5 Shall we not presently put on the
same stone? Shall we not after a little be the same dust? Shall we not gain so
much in these small days, as more evils, some having seen, some having
suffered, and some perhaps even having done, to minister to the law of nature
the common and unshaken contribution, and to depart after some, and to depart
before others, and to weep some, and to be lamented by others, and from others
to receive back the contribution of tears which we first brought in for
others?

19.1 Such is our life, brothers, of those living seasonal things; such is the
plaything upon earth; not being, to come to be, and having come to be, to be
loosed. We are a dream not standing, some phantom not held, a flight of a bird
passing by, a ship upon

the sea having no track, dust, vapor, morning dew, a flower growing in season
and being loosed in season. 19.2 "Man, as grass are his days, as a flower of
the field, so he will bloom"; well did the divine David philosophize about our
weakness; and in those words again: "Announce to me the fewness of my days";
and he defines the human days as the measure of palm-breadths. And what would
you say to Jeremiah, who even blames the mother of the birth, being in pain,
and these things upon others' faults? 19.3 "I have seen all things," says the
Ecclesiast, "I have gone through in thought all human things, wealth, luxury,
power, the unstable glory, the wisdom that flees more than it is held, luxury
again, wisdom again, being cycled upon the same things many times, pleasures
of the belly, gardens, a multitude of household slaves, a multitude of
possessions, cupbearers and cupbearing women, singing men and singing women,
arms, spear-bearers, nations falling down, tributes being collected, the brow
of kingship, as many things as are excessive of life, as many as of the
necessary, in which I went beyond all the kings before, and what upon all
these? 19.4 All is vanity of vanities, all things vanity and a choice of
spirit," whether then some unreasoning impulse of soul, and a distraction of
man, this having been condemned, perhaps from the ancient fall; "the rest, the
end of the word," he says, "hear the whole, fear God." Here he stands from the
perplexity; and this alone is the gain for you of the life here, to be led
through the disturbance of the things seen and being shaken upon the things
that stand and are not moved.

20.1 Let us not then mourn Caesarius, knowing of what evils he has been
released, but ourselves, to what things we have been left, and of what kind we
will treasure up, if we do not, having genuinely attached ourselves to God and
having run past the things that run past, press toward the life above, being
still upon earth having left the earth, and having genuinely followed the
spirit that bears toward the things above. 20.2 These things are both painful
to the small-souled, and light to those manly in mind. And let us consider
thus. Caesarius will not rule; but neither will he be ruled toward others. He
will not frighten some; but neither will he fear a heavy master, often the one
not even worthy to be ruled. 20.3 He will not gather wealth; but neither will
he look under at envy, or have his soul damaged gathering badly, and seeking
always to take on so much as he has acquired. For such is the disease of being
rich, not having a limit of needing more, but always making the drink still a
drug of thirst. 20.4 He will not display speeches; but by speeches at least he
will be admired. He will not philosophize the things of Hippocrates and Galen,
and of those set against them; but neither will he suffer badly with diseases,
reaping private griefs upon others' misfortunes. He will not demonstrate the
things of Euclid and Ptolemy and Hero; but neither will he be pained at the
uneducated puffing greater things. 20.5 He will not beautify himself with the
things of Plato and Aristotle and Pyrrho and certain Democrituses and
Heraclituses and Anaxagorases, Cleantheses and Epicuruses, and I do not know
which of those from the solemn Stoa and Academy; but neither will he care how
he may loose their persuasivenesses. 20.6 What need have I to remember the
others? But these indeed, the things honorable to all and much sought: he will
not have a wife standing beside him, nor children; but neither will he lament
these or be lamented by these, or having left them to others or having been
left as a reminder of misfortune. 20.7 He will not inherit moneys; but he will
be inherited by those of whom it is most useful and whom he himself wished, so
that he may migrate from here rich, carrying all things with himself. O the
ambition! O the new consolation! O the greatness of soul of those laying it
on! 20.8 A proclamation has been heard worthy of every hearing, and a mother's
suffering is emptied through a fair and holy promise, to give all things to
the child, his wealth as a burial-gift on his behalf, and that nothing be left
behind for those who expected it.

21.1 Are these things not yet enough toward consolation? I will bring forward
the greater drug. I am persuaded by words of wise men, that every soul both
fair and God-loving, whenever, loosed from the body bound together with it, it
is released from here, immediately becoming in perception and contemplation of
the fair that awaits it, inasmuch as that which darkens has been cleaned away,
or put aside, or I do not know what one must even say, 21.2 it is pleased with
some wondrous pleasure and rejoices and goes propitious toward its own master,
having fled the life here as some hard prison, and having shaken off the
surrounding fetters by which the wing of the mind was dragged down, and as it
were already by imagination it reaps the blessedness laid up; 21.3 and a
little later, having received back also the kindred little flesh, with which
it philosophized together the things from there, from the earth that both gave
it and was entrusted with it, in the manner which God knows who both bound
these things together and loosed them, it is joint-heir with this of the glory
from there; 21.4 and as it shared in the wretched things of it through the
growing-together, so also it gives a share of its own delights, having
consumed the whole into itself, and having become with this one both spirit
and mind and god, the mortal and flowing having been swallowed by the life.
21.5 Hear at least of what kind of things is philosophized about a joining of
bones and sinews by the divine Ezekiel, how many after him by the divine Paul
about an earthly tent and a house not made with hands, the one about to be
loosed, and the other laid up in the heavens; and saying that the going-abroad
from the body is a being-at-home toward the Lord, and lamenting the life with
this as a going-abroad, and for this longing and hastening the loosing. 21.6
Why am I small-souled about the hopes? Why do I become seasonal? I await the
voice of the archangel, the last trumpet, the reshaping of heaven, the
remaking of earth, the freedom of the elements, the renewal of the whole
world. 21.7 Then I will see Caesarius himself, no longer going abroad, no
longer being carried, no longer being mourned, no longer being pitied, bright,
glorious, high, such as you appeared to me even according to a dream many
times, O dearest of brothers to me and most brother-loving, whether of the
wishing shaping this, or of the truth.

22.1 But now, having let go the lamentations, I will look toward myself, lest
I escape notice carrying something worthy of lamentations, and I will look
around at my own things. Sons of men (for the discourse turns toward you), how
long are you heavy-hearted and thick in mind? Why do you love vanity and seek
a lie, taking the life here as some great thing and these few days as many,
and turning away from this separation, the welcome and sweet, as if it were
indeed some heavy and dreadful thing? 22.2 Shall we not know ourselves? Shall
we not throw away the things that appear? Shall we not look toward the things
thought? Shall we not, if there is even need to be grieved, be distressed at
the contrary, at the sojourn being lengthened, according to the divine David,
calling the things here tents of darkness and a place of ill-treatment and
mire of a depth and a shadow of death, because we delay in the tombs which we
carry about, because as men we die the death of sin, having become gods? 22.3
This fear I fear, with this both by night and by day I am together, and the
glory from there and the courts of justice there do not allow me to breathe;
of which the one I long for, even to being able to say: "My soul fails toward
your salvation," and the others I shudder at and turn away from. 22.4 And that
I have not feared, lest this body of mine, having flowed apart and been
corrupted, should go away altogether, but lest the glorious formation of God,
for it is glorious when it succeeds, as it is without honor when it sins, in
which is word, law, hope, should be condemned to the same dishonor as the
unreasoning, and should be nothing more after the separation; as would that it
were at least for the wicked and those worthy of the fire from there.

23.1 Would that I might put to death the members upon the earth; would that I
might spend all things on the spirit, having traveled the narrow way and
passable to few, not the broad and easy;

since the things after this at least are bright and great, and a hope greater
than according to the worth. 23.2 "What is man, that you remember him?" What
is this new mystery about me? I am small and great, lowly and high, mortal and
immortal, earthly and heavenly; those things with the world below, these with
God; those things with the flesh, these with the spirit. 23.3 I must be buried
together with Christ, rise together with Christ, be joint-heir with Christ,
become a son of God, God himself. You see to what point going forward the
discourse has led us up. I almost even confess thanks to the suffering, by
which I philosophized such things, and through which I became more a lover of
the uprising from here. 23.4 This the great mystery wishes for us; this the
God who became man for us and became poor, that he might raise the flesh and
save back the image and reshape the man, that we all might become one in
Christ, who has become all things in all of us perfectly as many as he himself
is, that we may no longer be male and female, barbarian, Scythian, slave,
free, the marks of the flesh, 23.5 but may bear only in ourselves the divine
character, from whom and into whom we have come to be, having been so formed
from him and stamped that we are known even from this alone.

24.1 And may we be at least what we hope, according to the great philanthropy
of God the great-giver, who asking small things grants great things both now
and into the time after to those who genuinely love him; bearing all things,
enduring all things through the love toward him and the hope, giving thanks
upon all things, both right and left alike, I mean both sweet and grievous,
since even these the word often knows as weapons of salvation, committing to
him our souls, those of the ones who have already ended, as on a common road
of the more ready; 24.2 which indeed we ourselves also having done, let us end
the discourse here, but you also the tears, already hastening upon the tomb,
your own, which as a gift from you Caesarius has, both grievous and abiding,
prepared for parents and old age according to season, but given to a child and
youth against what is likely, and not unlikely for the one who orders our
things. 24.3 O master of all and maker and especially of this formation, O God
of your men and father and pilot, O lord of life and death, O steward and
benefactor of our souls, O you who make all things and refashion them by the
craftsman Word according to season and as you yourself know in the depth of
your wisdom and administration, now may you receive Caesarius as a firstfruit
of our going-abroad; 24.4 and if the last first, we yield to your words, by
which the whole is borne; and may you receive us also later in a well-set
season, having ordered us in the flesh for as long as may be useful; and may
you receive us at least having been prepared through your fear, and not being
troubled, nor withdrawing in the last day, and being torn from the things from
here by force, which is the suffering of world-loving and flesh-loving souls,
but eagerly toward the life from there, both long-aged and blessed, that in
Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the glory unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
