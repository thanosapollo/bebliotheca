* To Theodore After His Fall II
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of the same to Theodore the monk, considering leaving the monastery and
marrying and engaging in business.

1 If it were possible for tears and groans to be shown through letters, I
would have filled the letter with these also and sent it to you; but I weep
not because you take thought for ancestral affairs, but because you have
erased yourself from the roll of the brethren, because you have trampled the
covenants toward Christ. These things I shudder at, at these I am in pain, for
this I fear and tremble, and not unjustly. For no one would ever indict a
private man for desertion of military service, but he who has once become a
soldier, if he is caught having deserted, the danger is concerning the last
things. It is not a terrible thing, O friend Theodore, for a wrestler to fall,
but to remain in the fall; nor is it hard for one fighting to be wounded, but
after the blow, having despaired, to neglect the wound. No merchant, having
once fallen into shipwreck and lost the freight, has stood off from sailing,
but again he crosses the sea and the waves and the long deeps and recovers the
former wealth. Often we see athletes also after many falls becoming crowned;
and already a soldier too, having fled, has at last been shown a champion, and
has prevailed over the enemies. And many also of those who denied Christ
through the necessity of tortures fought the defeat again and went away having
bound on the crown of martyrdom. But if each of these had despaired from the
former blow, he would not have enjoyed the second goods. Do not then, O
friend, because the enemy has shaken you a little from the station, already
also thrust yourself upon the pit, but stand nobly and return quickly from
where you went out, and do not think this blow for a little while to be a
reproach. For neither, having seen a soldier returning with a wound from war,
would you have reproached him; for reproach is to throw away the arms and to
be outside the enemies, but as long as one stands fighting, even if he is
struck, even if he withdraws for a short time, no one is so unfeeling, nor
inexperienced toward war, as ever to accuse. For it belongs to those who do
not fight not to be wounded, but those who go against the enemies with much
spirit it is possible both to be struck and to fall down, as has also happened
in your case now. For suddenly, having attempted to kill the serpent, you were
bitten. But take courage, you have need of a little sobriety and not even a
trace of that wound will be left, or rather by the grace of God you will crush
even the head itself of the wicked one; and let it not trouble you that you
were hindered quickly and at the very beginning; for he saw, he saw keenly,
the wicked one, the virtue of your soul, and he guessed from many things that
a noble enemy would grow for him; for this one who immediately used against
him so great and so great an eagerness, if he should remain, he hoped would
easily throw him in wrestling. For this he watched, he hastened, he rose up
much against you, or rather against his own head if you are willing to stand
nobly. For who did not marvel at your keen and sincere and boiling change
toward the good things? For luxury of foods was disregarded, costly clothing
was despised, all pride was trampled, all the zeal concerning the outside
wisdom was suddenly transferred upon the divine oracles; whole days were spent
upon readings, and whole nights upon prayers; it was not worthy to remember
paternal rank, nor to take wealth into mind; but to grasp the knees and to run
to the feet of brethren, this you knew as higher than all nobility. These
things oppressed the wicked one, these things raised him to a more vehement
fight; but he did not give a vital blow. For if after a long time, after
continual fastings and the sleeping on the ground and the rest of the ascesis
he threw you down, it was not even then to despair, except that one would have
said the


damage had become great, the defeat having come after many toils and sweats
and victories; but since at the same time as you stripped against him he
tripped you, he accomplished so much, as he worked you † more earnest toward
the fight against him. For just now as you were sailing out, not returning
from the trade, nor carrying the full freight, the terrible pirate attacked;
and just as if someone, having attempted to kill a noble lion, when he has
only scraped the hide, has harmed that one nothing, but has raised him against
himself and has made him more secure and hard to catch for the rest; so also
the common enemy of all, having attempted to plant a deep blow, missed this,
but prepared you rather to watch and to be sober for the rest.

2 Human nature is slippery and keen to be cheated, and keen to recover from
the deceit, and just as it falls quickly, so also it rises more quickly. For
even that blessed man, I mean David the chosen king and prophet, when he had
accomplished many things, did not escape being a man, but he once desired a
strange woman, and he did not stop as far as this, but he worked adultery
through the desire, and he worked murder also through the adultery, but not,
since he had received two so great blows, did he already attempt to give
himself a third also, but at once he ran off to the physician, and he was
applying the drugs. What are these? Fasting, tears, lamentations, continual
prayers, the announcing the sin often; and thus through these he made him
gracious and returned into the former dignity, so that after adultery and
murder the memory of the father was able somehow to overshadow the idolatry of
the son. For the son, the name of this one was Solomon, was caught through the
same snare as also the father, and granting favors to women he stood off from
the paternal God. See how great an evil it is not to master pleasure, but to
overturn the ruling principle of nature, and being a man to be a slave of
women. This same Solomon then, being righteous, to be deprived of all the
kingdom, but because of the father's good repute God left him to have the
sixth part of the rule. If then there was zeal in you concerning the outside
discourses, and then you grew slack of law-courts and the bema and the crowns
there and the boldness of speech, reminding you I would have exhorted you to
return into the toils for those things; but since we run for the things in
heaven and we have no account of the things upon earth, I remind you of
another tribunal and a fearful and dreadful bema: "For we must all be made
manifest before the bema of Christ." And a judge sits then, the one now set
aside by you. What then shall we say at that time? And what shall we
defend, if we continue setting him aside? What shall we say, tell me? Shall we
plead cares of affairs as pretext? But he himself having anticipated said:
"For what will it profit a man, if he gain the whole world, but suffer loss
of his soul." But that we have been deceived by others? But neither did it
help Adam toward defense to put forward the woman and say: "The woman whom you
gave me, she herself deceived me," just as neither the serpent the woman.
Fearful, O
Theodore, is that tribunal, not needing accusers, not waiting for witnesses;
"For all things are naked and laid open" to the one judging, and one must
submit accounts not of deeds only but also of thoughts; "For he is a discerner
of the thoughts and intents of the heart," that judge. But perhaps you will
say weakness of nature and not being able to bear the yoke? And what defense
is this, not to have been strong to bear the kindly yoke, not to have been
able to carry the light burden? Is the rest from toils a heavy and burdensome
thing? And how does Christ call us upon this? Saying: "Come to me all who
labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." And again: "Take the
yoke upon you, for my yoke is kindly and my burden is light." For what is
lighter, tell me, than to have been released from cares and affairs and fears


and toils, and to stand outside the waves of life and to live in a calm
harbor?

3 Tell me then: what of the things in the world seems to you to be blessed and
to be emulated? You will say at all events rule and wealth and to be well
reputed among men. And what is more wretched than these, when it is compared
toward the freedom of the Christians? For the ruler is subject to the wrath of
peoples and to irrational impulses of a multitude, and to fear of greater
rulers, and to the cares on behalf of those ruled, and the ruler of yesterday
is a private man today; for the present life has differed in nothing from a
stage, but just as there this one fills the rank of a king, and that one of a
ruler, and another of a soldier, but when the night has come on neither is the
king king, nor the ruler ruler, nor the soldier soldier, so also in that day,
not from the mask, but from the works each will receive the worthy recompense.
But is glory honorable, that which falls "as flower of grass"? but wealth
which those who possess are called wretched; "For woe, he says, to the rich."
And again: "Woe to those who trust upon their power and boast upon the
multitude of their wealth." The Christian never becomes a private man from a
ruler, nor poor from a rich man, nor without glory from a glorious one, but he
remains rich when he is poor, and is raised when he is eager to humble
himself; and of the rule which he rules, not over men, but he has authority of
the principalities and of the world-ruler of the darkness, no one can depose
him. "Marriage is good"; I also agree. "For marriage, he says, is honorable
and the bed undefiled, but fornicators and adulterers God will judge." But for
you it is no longer possible to keep the just things of marriage. For for one
joined to a heavenly bridegroom to leave this one, and to join himself to a
woman, the thing is adultery, even if you call it marriage ten thousand times;
or rather even so much more terrible than adultery, as God is better than men.
Let no one deceive you saying: God did not forbid to marry. I know this also;
he did not forbid to marry, but he forbade to commit adultery; which you do,
if you are willing, which may it not be, ever to keep company with marriage.
And why do you marvel, if marriage is judged as adultery, when God is set
aside? Hear no small thing. Murder brought righteousness and loving-kindness
condemned more than murder; because the one was according to the mind, and the
other happened with God forbidding. Therefore to Phinees it was reckoned for
righteousness that he pierced through the woman committing fornication
together with the one fornicating; but as for Saul, Samuel the holy one of
God, mourning and weeping and entreating whole nights, was not able to deliver
from the condemnation which God brought out against him, since the king of the
aliens, whom he ought to have killed, he saved contrary to the mind of God. If
then loving-kindness
condemned more than murder because God was disobeyed, why do you marvel if
marriage condemns more than adultery, because Christ was set aside? For as I
said at the beginning: if you were a private man, no one would have indicted
you for desertion; but now you are no longer master of yourself, having
enlisted for so great a king. For if "the wife does not have authority of her
own body, but the husband," how much more those living for Christ would not
themselves be masters of their own body. He who has now been despised by you,
this same one is then the one judging; think this through the whole, and the
river of fire; "For a river, he says, of fire was drawing before his face."
And that it is not possible for one delivered over to that fire to expect an
end of punishment. But the unseemly pleasures of life differ from shadows and
from dreams nothing; for before the things of the sin are completed, the
things of the pleasure are quenched; but the punishments for these have no
limit. The sweet is toward a little, but the painful is eternal. And what is
firm of the things in the world, tell me? Wealth, which often has not remained
even as far as the evening? But glory? but hear a certain righteous one
saying: "My life is lighter than a running." For just as those leap away
before they stand, so also this flies off before it has arrived. Nothing is
more precious than a soul; and not even those who have driven to the last of
folly


have been ignorant of this. For nothing is equivalent of a soul, even some
poet of those outside said. I know that you have become weaker, not wrestling
toward the wrestling of the wicked one; I know that you have stood in the
midst of the flame of the pleasures; but if you say toward the enemy, that we
do not serve your pleasures, and we do not worship the root of all your evils,
if you stretch the eye upward, the Savior will perhaps shake out the flame,
and will burn up those who threw you into the fire, and to you in the midst of
the furnace he will send a cloud and dew and a whistling breeze, so that the
fire may not even touch your reasonings and your conscience; only do not burn
yourself down. For even the strong of the cities, all arms often and engines
of those outside have not been strong to take down, but the betrayal of one or
two of the citizens dwelling inside has delivered them without labor to the
enemies. And now let none of the reasonings inside betray you, even if the
enemy brings to bear ten thousand engines from outside, he will bring them to
bear in vain.

4 You have by the grace of God many and great ones who share the pain, who
anoint, who tremble for your soul; the holy one of God Valerius, the brother
Florentius most honorable to him in all things, Porphyrius wise with the
wisdom of Christ, and many others. These lament each day for you and have not
ceased praying; and they would have obtained long ago the things they were
asking, if you had been willing even a little to release yourself from the
snares of the enemy. How then is it not strange that others even now have not
despaired of your salvation, but pray through the whole to receive back their
member, and you having once fallen are not willing to rise, but lie, all but
crying toward the enemy: Slay, strike, spare not; "Does he who falls not
rise?" the divine oracle said. But you fight this and contradict; for for one
who has fallen to despair is nothing else to say than that he who falls does
not rise. Do not, I entreat, do not wrong yourself so much; do not pour down
upon us so great a pain. I do not say now, when you do not yet complete the
twentieth year, but even if having done many things and having lived the whole
life in Christ, in extreme old age you suffered so great an outrage, not even
then is it good to despair, but to take into mind the robber justified on the
cross, those who worked about the eleventh hour, and received the wage of the
whole day. And just as it is not good for those who have fallen at the last
bound to despair, if they are sober-minded, so it is not safe to be fed by
this hope and to say that: after these things ... For the present I will enjoy
the sweet things of life, but later having toiled a little I will receive the
wages of the whole time. For I remember you having often said, when they were
advising you evil things, you frequenting the schools: "What then? even if in
this short time I end the life well, how shall I go away to him who has said:
'Do not delay to turn to the Lord, nor put off day from day.'" Recover this
thought, and fear the thief; for thus Christ calls our departure from here,
because it stands over those who do not know. Think the cares of life, those
privately, those happening in common, the fears of the rulers, the envy of the
citizens, the danger often hung even unto the last things, the toils, the
miseries, the slavish flatteries not even belonging to the earnest of the
slaves, and the fruit of these toils ending here, than which what could be
more painful? And for many it has not even happened to enjoy the things for
which they toiled, but having spent the first age in toils and dangers, when
they hoped they would receive the wages, they went away having nothing with
themselves. And not unjustly. For if one will scarcely see the king upon earth
with boldness of speech after having endured many dangers, and having
accomplished many wars, how will anyone be able to see the heavenly one,
having lived and served as soldier the whole time for another?

5 Shall I say, do you wish, also the cares at home, those of the wife, those
of the children, those of the household slaves. Painful not to have gotten
children, painful also to have gotten them; for the one is in vain to


have married, and the other to endure a bitter slavery. The little child has
fallen sick, a fear not small; he has gone away untimely, a grief beyond
consolation; and according to each age various cares on behalf of them, and
fears and many toils. A terrible thing to take a poorer wife, a terrible thing
a richer; for the one has harmed toward the substance, and the other toward
authority and freedom the husband. What need to speak of the rascalities of
the household slaves? Is this then life, O Theodore, for one soul to be
divided into so many things, to be slave to so many, to live for so many, and
never for himself. But none of these things is, O friend Theodore, among us,
and I call you yourself as witness. For when for that short time you were
willing to lift your head out of the waves, you know of how great gladness you
partook and joy. For there is not, there is not a free man except only the one
living for Christ. This one has stood higher than all the terrible things;
when he himself does not wish to wrong himself, another will never be able to
harm this one, but he is impregnable, not bitten by loss of money; for he has
learned that "We brought nothing into this world, it is clear that neither
shall we be able to carry anything out"; not caught by desire of ambition or
glory; for he has learned that "Our citizenship exists in heaven." No one
abusing grieves him, no one striking provokes; one calamity alone for a
Christian, to strike against God; but the other things, such as loss of money,
deprivation of fatherland, the danger concerning the last things, he does not
even think to be anything terrible. And what all have shuddered at, to go away
from here there, this is sweeter to that one than to live. For just as if
someone having gone up onto a high lookout should behold the sea, and those
sailing this, some being plunged under waves, others dashing against reefs,
others hastening one way and being carried another, like prisoners by the rush
of the wind, and many becoming under water, some being carried upon one plank
or upon something from the ship, and some using the hands alone instead of
ship and rudder, others floating dead, a manifold and many-faced calamity; so
also the one serving as soldier for Christ, having withdrawn himself from the
confusion of life and from the waves, sits in a safe and high place. For what
is safer and higher than to have one care, how one must please God? You have
seen, Theodore, the shipwrecks of those sailing this sea; therefore, I
entreat, flee the deep, flee the waves, and take a high place, from where it
is not possible to be caught. There is a resurrection, Theodore, there is a
judgment, a fearful tribunal awaits us having gone out from here. "We must all
stand before the bema of Christ." Gehenna has not been threatened in vain, so
many good things have not been prepared without purpose. A shadow, and more
worthless than a shadow are the affairs of life, full of many fears, and of
many dangers, and of extreme † slavery. Do not then suffer loss of both this
age and that one, it being possible to gain both, if you should wish. And that
those living for Christ gain also the things here, Paul teaches saying: "But I
spare you." And again: "And this I say toward your profit." You see then, O
Theodore, that even here the one caring for the things of the Lord is higher
than the one who has married? It is not possible having gone away there to
repent. No athlete, when he has gone out of the stadium and the theatre has
been dissolved, can wrestle again or contend. Think these things always
and you will crush the sharp sword of the wicked one through which he kills
many; and this is despair, because it cuts off from the hope those who have
been thrown down. Strong is this weapon of his and he does not hold those
caught otherwise than by binding with this bond, which if we are willing, by
the grace of God, we shall be able to break quickly.

6 I know having gone beyond the measure of the letter, but forgive; for I came
forward into this not willingly, but compelled by the pain and the
love, through which I also forced myself to write this letter, many hindering
and saying: "Cease laboring in vain and as it were sowing upon rocks." These
things many were saying to me. But I heard no one; for there is hope, I said
toward myself, God willing,


the letters will accomplish something; but if what we pray against should
happen, we shall at least gain not to be accused of silence, and we shall be
not worse than those sailing the sea, who when they see their
fellow-craftsmen, the ship having been broken by force of waves or of winds,
being carried upon a plank, having taken down sails and having let go anchors
and having gone into a boat, attempt to save men unknown, and known only from
calamity. But if those are not willing, no one would accuse of their
destruction those who attempted to save. These are the things from us; and we
believe that, by the grace of God, there will be also the things from you, and
we shall again see you distinguished in the honorable flock of Christ. May we
more quickly receive you back healthy in the true health by the prayers of
saints, O dear head. If you have any account of us and you have not altogether
cast us out of your memory, deign to write back to us; for your letter will
gladden us; and your perfection, being able to understand the things still
more greatly, will also benefit us both writing and praying. We pray that you
be strong in the Lord. Amen.
