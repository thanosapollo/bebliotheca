Through love she has been turned into a plant, and she keeps her beauty even
though her nature has changed. Narcissus was beautiful, but his suffering was
unworthy of that beauty. Hyacinth was beautiful, but be more humane than
Zephyrus, and spare a beautiful youth. If we need drink as well, I will lead
you to a spring and go before you with the pipe. Follow the songs; a wolf will
be the penalty for whoever is left behind. If midday grows harsh I will lead
the flock under a pine; from above a nightingale will sound her spring song;
they say she seeks her child and mourns like a mother. I will vie with her on
the harp, and my sheep will lie down in order, listening to the songs. Then
Pan will call Echo all the more a girl who neither speaks nor keeps silent,
always seeming near and slipping from notice, consoling her lover with a
counterfeit voice. Now swans and swallows sing overhead, wandering through the
air. Apollo is glad, crowned with laurel and holding as a plant the girl he
wanted as a maiden. Now the bees hum over the meadows; I will take many of the
combs, and milking the honey from them I will mix it into the milk. And what
is that nectar to me beside this, the drink they talk of for nothing? Now
Loves dance among the herds; a bull has bellowed in desire, and rams,
snorting, mount the lambs. Nor will I stand idle now for nothing. Idleness
alone is unbearable. I will bring my beloved and imitate the herd. Instead of
a bed some soft grass will lie beneath. I will strew on it many violets and
lilies, or rather roses, so that we may honor Aphrodite too. For she herself
could not escape Eros; the little boy is haughty and bold, and he arms himself
even against whoever he meets. He even wounds Aphrodite, not respecting the
mother who bore him, so that she may not pride herself as the only one both to
have borne him and to have escaped Eros. What then does he do? He lifts the
bow and smiles at the one who bore him, and he has set a small arrow in it.
And Adonis suddenly seemed beautiful; one would more easily have seen a
he-goat leave his yoked she-goat than Aphrodite leave Adonis. I keep silent
about the suffering, granting this to the goddess, lest I grieve her by
speaking and she show me the herd without love. Yet with Aphrodite's blood the
rose was reddened for us. Enjoying such things of the season I will pray,
together with the herd, both to see summer and that winter keep silent and
that spring shine out for us again. 5. Merchant I have seen what I prayed for,
and the thought has come to deed. The war that threatened from the clouds is
gone, the light grows young, and again the sun

is with us. My grief has dissolved with the season. Now the sea is a friend,
and it has changed from its wrath. All things make a truce with men: sky, air,
stars, the sea. For the sky, always pelting with snow, brought danger about
the houses; the stars hid their look; the air knit its brows; the sea was
shaken with the winds. Now one may see the Bear with clear eyes, our friend,
who everywhere directs the voyage. She will not bear to set with the others,
lest she overlook a wandering sailor, but she always stands announcing the
cities to those at sea. One may see great Orion too, hunting even among the
stars by his form. If I see Andromeda I pity the bonds and mourn her fortune.
But when I see Perseus raising his right hand against the sea-monster, I say
to the girl, "Take heart," and I console her in myself, and I share her joy in
her fortune. I rejoice, now seeing the Pleiades, who govern spring for us, now
the Argo, a ship in heaven, proclaiming old tales: Pelias, the fleece, Medea,
the loves, Jason. Why then do I still delay and hang back from the voyage? I
have given up, settled in one place, always spending and stripped of profit.
For what trade has given, winter has eaten away, and a housemate, ah me, no
more bearable than winter. She was always pressing, bringing some new thing
toward loss. And "I need," she says, "clothing that consoles the season." I
provided it, as I would not have wished. And she was naked again; again she
stretched the loss. And "she," she says, "so-and-so's wife shines round with
golden ornament" and "I must have the like." And I to her: "Does this too,
wife, console the season?" If I ignored her asking, "I am without honor," she
would often cry, and "there is no account of me from you." And there was need
even through the day to quarrel in the yoke, like cattle shut up together,
until my purse ran out and was gone. But now from want, now from evils I have
been freed; now there are dealings among the cities. Free. The sea will no
longer hinder my ship. Indeed the sailor too dares the open sea, and the
wayfarer sings, and shepherds pipe and the flocks dance. Now birds sing with a
varied sound, weaving a strange music, and the trees now hasten toward fruit,
and earth parades in diverse flowers, shining like stars, as if contending
with heaven. What then? I will board the merchantman; I will take heart for
the voyage. I have many sailor-partners in the affair, who besides the profit
also enjoy a varied sight. One hastens toward Sicily and sees Etna and fire
sometimes pouring, and he will see Odysseus's Charybdis. Another, having come
to Italy, sees a very great city, in which are the trophies of the inhabited
world. Others sail around the islands as if in the middle of a chorus, and
another sees Troy and laments her fortune, taking Helen into memory. But again
women: shall we again remember the evils at home? Another, arriving at Attica,
sees the Piraeus, the islands, Salamis, and the children of the Athenians and
old tales: how Xerxes threatened, how all things were set in motion, how he
who outraged land and sea wept at Athenian hands. And we shall not sail past
Cyprus and her Aphrodite; I will leave the ship, I will see the island of
Adonis, and I will console Aphrodite, the image of the suffering, the memory
of the love. And I will sing to the hunter his beauty, his strength, his
graces, the bows of the boys, the jealousy of Ares, the untimely end, the
message, the running, the wound, the blood, the rose itself, a fortunate
plant, imaging Aphrodite's suffering by its look. Then, having finished the
trade, I will be rich in goods, but until I see the little woman. 6. Adonis
This was left to you, Fortune, for a plot: not even to seek Adonis, nor to run
to him, nor to console the longing by the running, not to come upon him lying,
not to embrace him fallen, nor to call a corpse dearest. It would have been
enough to see the eyes even closed, enough for me as a sight even the silent
lips. Adonis beauty has not yet left the corpse: not blush from the face, not
flower from the cheek, not the Graces from the hair. And if I had asked the
cause of the fall, perhaps even lying he would have cried Ares to me. But what
have I been led to say? Shall I be persuaded that Adonis has fallen? He still
lives and hunts, and perhaps he seeks Aphrodite who is not there. And I delay,
and I sit in vain, my foot bound by the passion. But again I reckon Ares,
again Adonis has died. For the god is terrible; he looks grim, he gapes toward
blood, not knowing how to pity a longed-for face. O terrible perplexity. O
excess of misfortunes. Whom shall I accuse? And whom shall I blame? Fortune?
My own children? From these I came to know Adonis, from that one Ares was
armed. Now I am tried by my own arrows, now I learn my flame again, now I feel
my own birth-pang. Aphrodite these things whom Zeus begot, whom heaven brought
forth, whom nature conveyed, from whom fire and arrows and all things were
joined to desire. I became mother of Loves, I furnished bows, the little
children were winged, and nothing was impassable for them. They went upon
earth, they entered a wave, they flew through the air, they danced among stars
and were at times arrayed against gods. Nothing escaped my dominion. All
things yielded to my right hand. If anyone grieved Aphrodite I looked to the
children, I nodded; they moved the arrows, and the suffering remained. Zeus
knows, ruler of all things and obeying love alone. Poseidon knows, burning in
the midst of the sea. I did not overlook Pan; they have not held Hera in awe.
They went after Zephyrus, Boreas has been tried, and the arrow was seen
swifter than the winds. Apollo came to know a god's bow, struck by ours. What
need to speak of a man as lover and a lion's gadfly and a bull bellowing with
desire, troubled through the children? Trophies everywhere, and all things
were filled with Aphrodite. And when all things were under the dragon the
beautiful children were armed. They had the war from home and I knew it by my
own eyes; seeing from there I marveled; I suffered, I was at a loss, I
laughed, I wept that it had come to be. I was looking under the girdle, lest
someone steal it unnoticed; and I find the Loves as thieves. I yielded to the
offspring; then I praised Adonis, how beautiful he was, and I thought the hair
had been blended, the Graces to have dwelt in the eyes, the Loves to have shot
from the lips. And the cheeks, ah me, hunted with them. I longed; from there I
hated Olympus. The sight of him had even overcome gods with me, and all things
have become second to the youth. He hunted, and I marveled at the strength. He
seemed bolder than lions, leaping beyond the deer. I thought the wood Olympus,
the glens heaven. When I had him, I thought I saw the sun brighter than the
accustomed one. I loved one sight, Adonis; I longed for one hearing, the man.
No one speaking pleased me, except one saying Adonis. These things grieved
Ares and fed the envy, and the intention came to deed. I am the cause of the
suffering for you; because of me Ares too is an enemy. Having heard these
things I was struck with panic, and I became lifted by the rumor, and there
was no account of adornment for me. Running came upon me, and bare feet, and
the things at the feet overlooked. From there the suffering was more terrible
for me, and I have been struck in the heart by longing, and in the foot by the
thorn. Let this be a token of the fortune, and let my blood become nature for
the rose, to show to those after these things the image of the suffering. And
someone seeing the flower will not give Adonis over to forgetfulness, but
seeking the cause he will hear the suffering, the longing, the rumor, the
running, the wound, the blood, the change of the rose, and upon all Adonis. 7.
Phoenix I share the Greeks' joy, but I weep for the Thessalian. They have
found a savior; he has fallen to Greek dishonor and Trojan laughter. Not even
the Trojans, I think, are deaf to our sufferings. Agamemnon found well enough
where he might get allies, and he will take his justice, having shamed a
disobedient soldier. Would that this catch had come before our embassy. As it
is, our part is misfortune and words. Wise Odysseus did not persuade. Ajax was
slighted, and with him his lineage and Zeus. And I, the old man (for I cannot
say teacher), had nothing persuasive to offer on this, not gray hair, not
words, not tears. The boy's love of honor used to console me, and the seeming
that he loved glory more than a girl. Now everything is turned about, and at
the sight of her he yielded. Why did we not take her with us and show her to
Achilles? Sight is stronger than hearing for desire. So he could not hold out
once he had seen. Throwing both arms around her and weeping for pleasure, the
noble one says, "Because of you I was angry, because of you I am reconciled."
Did Chiron hand this on to you? Did I, after him? He trained the two feet for
running; he drilled him for arms and arrows and the gear of war. He taught him
to master wild beasts. Would that it had been love. I ran to speech, raised
his mind, taught him; I made the boy a soldier and an orator. "Music," I said,
"you have learned, so that you may measure your spirit, so that ... you know
how to set to rhythm toward pleasures the soul that leaps unordered, bringing
a yoke from speech. Be moderate toward outrage, and know how to bear one who
is drunken." Hearing this Peleus rejoiced, and he had good hopes of me and the
boy. He sends me out with him to Troy, and after old age I appeared a soldier,
so that I might also be a counselor, and I was enrolled in the boy's fine
deeds; he brought Telephus down, but I was in the praises; Cycnus was falling
and I enjoyed the glory. Fallen cities were counted, and Phoenix was
everything in praise and rumor. Disease held Greece and it has ceased through
us. Up to this I was fortunate and I was enrolled among the best. What came
after I weep to tell. There was strife and wonders and the first of Greece
were sick. They spoke, they threatened, they reviled. Agamemnon was in the
wrong because he would take away the γέρας prize of valor Achilles was due.
Over that Achilles suffers in his soul, and there was no end. Love that has
been wronged does not know truces, nor does longing give the mind
forgetfulness. Achilles singing as they fell well, and Priam the Greeks'
falls, the one withdrawn from the Greeks, the other luxuriating in the
occasion. To me ......... ......... drunken outrage toward the boy, Achilles'
measureless anger, disasters on this of Greeks falling one upon another,
though he threatens what he knows how to do. Yet it seemed so; and we were
ambassadors. All Greece's hope was in us. We found many devices and still
could not shake a soul held by anger and desire, but there was after all one
device for one who longs: the sight of the beloved. It did not occur to us to
learn this, not to me the old man, not to Odysseus the wise, not to Ajax the
noble. The failing of all made Agamemnon wiser toward succeeding. He sent the
girl in the guise of mourning and got what he wished. The son of Peleus could
not bear the sight, and pleasure dissolved his spirit. A woman stronger than a
teacher, a girl than a wise man, a captive than kinship, and erotic persuasion
outran the persuasion of speech and art. Henceforth let the son of Thetis
conquer the Trojans. Victory will not cover the shame. What will Peleus be to
us, once he has learned this? We have become glorious, O Chiron. Would that I
too had not been present. It is not the same to be shamed in person and to
hear these things from afar. And you now, Achilles, enjoy the captive together
with the dishonor from this.
