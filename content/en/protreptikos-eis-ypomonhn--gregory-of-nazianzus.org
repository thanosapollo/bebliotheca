* Exhortation to Patience
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

A translation from the poems of Saint Gregory the Theologian of precepts
concerning stillness and virtues. Practice stillness in words, and stillness
in deeds, and likewise in laughter and in gait; and avoid rash vehemence; for
thus the mind will remain steadfast, and will not, becoming turbulent through
vehemence, be weak and short in prudence and seeing darkly, nor will it be
overcome by gluttony, nor by boiling anger, nor by the other passions, lying
ready as prey for them; for it is fitting that the mind should rule the
passions, seated high upon a quiet throne, looking toward God. Do not,
however, be full of sluggishness in works, nor dull in words, nor filled with
hesitation in your walking, so that a good rhythm may adorn your stillness and
your bearing may appear something godlike and sacred. Guard also against the
tokens of pride: a posture with neck held high, a raised head, and a dainty
and lofty step of the feet. Let your words toward those you meet be gentle and
your greetings sweet, and toward women let there be modesty and the gaze
turned to the ground. Speak everything with consideration and give to the
voice what is useful, measuring the utterance by the need of the hearers, so
that it may be audible and neither escape the hearing of those present through
smallness, nor exceed with a greater shout. Guard that you never say anything
which you have not first considered and thought through; nor readily, and in
the midst of another's words, insert your own; for one must hear and converse
in turn, dividing time between speech and silence; learn gladly and teach
ungrudgingly, and never hide wisdom from others through envy, nor withdraw
from learning through shame. Yield to elders as to fathers, honor the servants
of God; and to those younger than yourself be a leader in wisdom and virtue.

Do not be contentious toward friends, nor a mocker against them and a jester;
and firmly refuse falsehood and deceit and insolence; and with fair speech
bear even the proud and the insolent, both gently and magnanimously. Let all
things for you hang upon God, both works and words, and refer all that is
yours to Christ, and frequently turn the soul toward God, and rest the thought
upon the power of Christ, as if in some harbor, resting in the divine light of
the Savior from all speech and action. And by day share your own prudence with
men, but with God often in the day and most of all at night; and let not much
sleep prevail over the prayers and hymns toward God; for long sleep is a rival
of death. Continue every day doing or saying some good to men; and always make
yourself a partaker of Christ who shines the divine radiance from heaven, and
let Christ be for you a continuous and unceasing gladness.

Do not loosen the tension of the soul in feasting and in the relaxations of
drinking, turning away from the delights proper to the mind, of which there is
no satiety; consider sufficient for the body what is needful, and do not
hasten to foods before the time of the meal has also come; let bread be your
meal, and let the herbs of the earth be present and the seasonal fruits from
trees; go to food with composure and not displaying rabid gluttony; be neither
a flesh-eater nor a lover of wine, unless some healing of disease should lead
you to these. But instead of the pleasures in these things choose the gladness
in divine words and hymns, and in the wisdom supplied to you by God, and let a
heavenly care always lift you toward

heaven; and let go the many cares about the body, being confident in the hopes
toward God, that he will provide you the necessities in sufficient measure,
both food for life and covering of the body and defenses against winter cold;
for of your King is all the earth and whatever grows from the earth; and he
cares exceedingly for his own servants and tends them as his sacred things and
temples. For this reason do not fear diseases excessively, nor the onset of
old age expected in time; for even disease will cease as it seems good to your
King and whenever this is good for your soul, wrapping your old age as with
wings by the divine sheltering power. Knowing these things, make the soul
strong also toward diseases, being of good courage, as some man, an excellent
contestant in the stadia, with unwavering power undergoes the labors. Do not
press the soul very much under grief, whether an impending disease weighs upon
you or some other hardship befalls, but set a noble thought against the
labors, offering thanks to God even in the midst of toilsome affairs, inasmuch
as he thinks things wiser than men, even such as it is neither possible nor
easy for men to find. Pity those who are afflicted and ask the help from God
upon men; for he will grant the grace to the friend who asks and will provide
aid to the afflicted, wishing to make his own power known to men, so that
having come to knowledge they may go up to God and enjoy the eternal
blessedness, whenever the Son of God comes restoring the good things to his
own.
