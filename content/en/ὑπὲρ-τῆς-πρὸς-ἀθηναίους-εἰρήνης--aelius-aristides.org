The day which we have all long yearned to see is this one, men of Lacedaemon,
granting us to deliberate concerning the Athenians whatever we may wish. We
must neither suffer anything ignoble nor, if one must say this, dishonor what
has been accomplished, but know how great a thing has been achieved, and that
neither are the Athenians lacking in calamities nor is anger still left to us.
I would have begged you not to think that I speak from friendship for those
men, if I did not have many tokens of goodwill toward you, and many of

:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

hatred toward them, which you all alike know. Nevertheless, I think I must
counsel you the same things I counsel myself: not to look first at what they
ought to suffer before looking at what it is noble for us to do, and not to be
ignorant that we are considering not so much the city of the Athenians as our
own. For if we close what has been done with some fitting resolution, we shall
have deliberated for ourselves and shall in truth have conquered; but if you
are led on to what some men urge, I fear that even something paradoxical may
be fitting to say. For if someone asked us for what we took up this long and
great war, we could say neither that they drove off booty from us, nor that
they cut off land, as the Argives did, nor any other such thing; nor yet that
it was from envy of their power and prosperity, but this we would least of all
concede. For what remaining reason, then? For the freedom of the Greeks, we
would say; and I think we ourselves need not even say this, but all would
agree it on our behalf. And indeed no one could even say that we had a greater
advantage against them than this. For it remained for them, from the things
they meddled in, to be hated, and for us, from the things we proclaimed. For
what purpose, then, men of Lacedaemon, have I recalled these things? For one,
that you may know that nothing is left for us, but the war has the end that
belongs to it. For just as from the beginning, when they were free, there was
nothing more to seek, so now it has been fulfilled. And if we act thus, we
shall present deeds that agree with our words, and we shall win a victory
fairest of all, advantageous to the Greeks and honorable to ourselves. But if
we destroy the city utterly, we shall seem to have made war not looking ahead
how the Greeks might become free, but from envy of the city, and sating our
own wickedness, not being displeased at their excess. And this, I suppose, is
clear to everyone as being against us.

Consider another thing besides these, even if to someone

it will appear paradoxical: the faults of the Athenians save them, and compel
us to show clemency. Why so? Because if we then thought we must be indignant
at the Athenians if they made any of the Greeks subject to themselves, it is
entirely impossible for us now to destroy them. For to deprive of freedom and
to destroy utterly are not, I suppose, similar; nor, when the lesser did not
suit those men, does the greater admit of being done by you. And indeed one
cannot even say this, that all the others had to be made autonomous and that
one had to run risks for this, but that the case of the Athenians was to be so
utterly set at nothing as to destroy them root and branch, when it is possible
to save them, if only you vote it. For if even to think it right that they
alone of all should be slaves was not in your character, then surely to wipe
them out is not even near it. And as for what they did wrong concerning the
Scionaeans and the Melians, how is it not absurd to accuse them and say that
these things happened as faults, yet to think it right to imitate them as if
they were correct, and not to reckon or see that the Athenians perhaps even
had some argument when they did these things (for they would perhaps say that
they seemed to be insulted by inferiors, and that they were not justly hated,
and that they did not persuade when they made proposals, and other such
things, speaking nothing strong or sufficient, I too say, yet they have
something with which to be shameless), but for us not even an argument is left
that we shall not have done the very things of which we ourselves have
condemned them, and which we have defined as not needing to happen among the
Greeks. For what, then, will still seem a terrible thing to have been done by
those men, with you as judges, if it will seem to you no terrible thing that
Athens has been destroyed? And I think, men of Lacedaemon, that if someone
bade you imitate the battle at Marathon or the sea-fight at Salamis of the
Athenians, it would not be right to accept it, and such offenses least of all.
But if, again, it is not even fitting for us to take the fairest of deeds from
elsewhere, but rather ourselves to be a pattern for others, then surely it is
the part of men utterly infatuated

to procure for you the worst of the things those men have done. For to me even
an excess seems to be added through both: those who do it would be
Lacedaemonians and not Athenians, and those who suffer it Athenians and not
Scionaeans, whose very name is not known to many. Then these men too, I think,
if they had conquered us, would have sought nothing more, but being ashamed at
the figure and the virtues would have stopped somewhere here. And we know that
they were in a way even ashamed about those from Pylos.

Do not, then, consider on what grounds you will punish them, but at who they
are, and who you yourselves are, and the measure of the punishment. It is
possible now for the Aeginetans to recover their own; but if we destroy the
Athenians themselves and raze the city, not even if we ourselves wish to
change our minds is anything at all left. And least of all, I thought, was it
admissible for those who blame the Athenians to propose these things. For if
they have done them justly, why do they blame them? But if they did things
that were not moderate, how is it noble for you at least to go so far in
insensibility? For I think that if you spare them, you will confirm the
accusations in deed and show what sort of men one ought to be toward the
Greeks; but if you destroy them, you will make the finest defense on their
behalf. For just as in the contests those who win carry off the name, so you,
if you surpass their facility in such things, will yourselves inherit the
infamy. Thus by going on to punish you acquit them of the charges, but by
granting pardon you convict them. And indeed this too must not escape you,
that now, in anger at what they charge, the Greeks would gladly see them
suffer anything whatever, but when time has passed and they take the matter
with calculation, they will share the pain with those men and blame you. And
there are many changes of affairs, which one must look ahead to, and think
that those who bring up against those men the sufferings of the Melians or
Scionaeans also bring up against you those of the Plataeans. But neither do
the things that belonged to the continuous war have the same pardon when done
at the end and when it is possible to have ceased, nor is it noble, I suppose,
that as many such things as possible happen, but that from

the beginning none of them should have occurred. For who of the Greeks will be
left, if because of those who perished before, those after them always suffer
this same thing? And you see even in the crown games that we neither praise
those who fight outside the lawful ways, nor, if someone accuses them, do we
allow them to use these same ways, but remaining within the established rules
they may thus prevail if they can. And indeed that the contests of the Greeks
against one another are not far from these things I have said, but would
reasonably take place for victory alone, while destruction would be owed to
the natural enemies, the barbarians: whoever knows how to be Greek not only in
words but also in mind would agree.

And I think that even if we judge the men most hateful and most hostile of
all, the things that have been done are not so small for us as still to need
remaining punishment, if one must speak about Lacedaemonians and to
Lacedaemonians. For I, so long as I saw more than three hundred triremes in
the Piraeus, and money coming in each year, so much and so much, and their
greed reaching as far as Lake Maeotis, and men enslaved, some by their power,
others in their minds, and them thinking Poseidon's empire their own and
sailing round the Peloponnese, and looking about at the things in Sicily and
Carthage and everywhere, and able to stop nowhere, but making all things their
own, I thought there was a time for both of these, hatred and fear, and that
one must chasten their arrogance and set affairs back to the ancient state.
But now what time is there for fear or envy? Or looking toward what shall we
be contentious? Have we not destroyed more than so many myriads of their
bodies in Sicily, using every manner, and have we left not a horse, not a
missile, not a ship, no form of calamity? And these other two hundred triremes
are held, and these three thousand have perished in the manner of
temple-robbers, and the rest fall each day by the plague; and all, as in a
net,

are shut in; and those many and splendid things have gone from them, flown
away as a dream. Then shall we be angry at these men? By no means. For it
belongs to men of your nature to check with arms those who stand against them,
but to handle gently those who have fallen under, and to think that using well
what is at hand has no less ambition than conquering in battle; especially if
they have become the worst of all toward the Greeks in the present time, there
was a time when none were better than these after us at least. But if you
examine rightly, the present charges will appear nowhere comparable to their
then manly deeds, both in magnitude, I mean, and in number. Now, at any rate,
they made Scione and Melos and Torone and two or three other cities
uninhabited; but then they helped save all the cities for Greece, fighting
privately against Datis, and in common with us against Xerxes and the satraps.
And now they have expelled the Aeginetans, but then they left their own city
so that nothing incurable might happen to the Greeks, and though it was
possible for them to sail away and be gone, or even to join the King against
us, they did not dare. Are there not things that plead those men off? And I am
silent about the ancient sharing of grain, and the mysteries still now
happening each year, and the receptions of those who take refuge, and
countless other benefactions.

And toward the common body of Greece such are those now envied and heavy; but
toward you privately, what sort? That they received the Heraclidae in this
city, and that they initiated Heracles and the Dioscuri first of foreigners, I
forbear to say; and yet even if, apart from a benefit, they were begging you
by these gods, there would be reason to be ashamed. But in the war against the
Messenians, when we asked, who were they who sent Tyrtaeus? Or who those who
sent Cimon at the time of the earthquake leading four thousand hoplites? Are
their things toward you of this kind, then, and ours less toward them, we who,
when the Dorians once marched against them, alone of all did not think it
right, and when they were under tyranny alone of all came to their aid? And to
Marathon we so

hastened that we became as day-runners. Is it then noble either that those who
helped us save the founders, themselves with children and women, now perish at
our hands, or that those who left their city for the freedom of the Greeks be
deprived of their city by us, or that those on behalf of whose freedom you
were fighting the tyrants, and this before they themselves displayed the
virtues I mentioned, that these same men now be destroyed with utter
destruction? And that those who then medized should have pardon from you, whom
you earlier voted to tithe, and that the Athenians were not least those who
helped persuade these things, but that those who stood with you, and with whom
it was fitting to be arrayed in the common sanctuaries of the Greeks as
freeing Greece, that for these fortune should end here? And that those who
came from the barbarians concerning earth and water, as plundering the things
of Athena, should so die at their hands, and that these two cities alone
should adorn the race of the Greeks with all the honors voted them, both ours
and theirs; but when the acropolis is being razed and the sanctuaries, and the
city utterly destroyed, and the land given I know not to what men, do you
think none of these things concerns the other gods or Athena, who is the
common foundress of both cities, and has the name of theirs? By no means, men
of Lacedaemon, let us not imitate the barbarians, nor overturn this city which
we knew as a buttress of Greece, nor pull it from under, nor cut out one of
the two eyes of Greece, but remembering that day on which they obeyed us when
we asked them not to abandon the Greeks, overlooking the King's promises, and
despising dangers and fears, now in due season repay them the favor, thinking
it one of the most shameful things, privately, if for someone the deeds of
manliness were greater than the charges, to know to pardon, but for so great a
city this justice should not be saved. Come, by Zeus, for nothing can be sworn
impossible, if the barbarian should attempt against us again from the
beginning,

and all things might happen in the long time, and we know how Greece has been
harmed, whom, tell me, shall we set beside us? To whom first shall we send?
For whom will the exhortations from us have more timely occasion? If you obey
me, you will spare both the Athenians, and you will spare yourselves first and
all Greece; and you might reasonably beg off those who are indignant,
reminding them also city by city that we shrank from no danger or hardship for
their sake, and that they have what they needed: the Corinthians Potidaea and
the colonies, the Aeginetans to recover their own, the Megarians to use the
markets and harbors of the Athenians. And indeed for the Thebans too a
sufficient favor is the things conceded at the beginning concerning Plataea;
and it is not reasonable that they force these things further, but much more
that they yield to you, and give this favor, if not ever to the Athenians, yet
to you: not to do wrong for their own sake, nor to become accursed of the
goddess and of the Greeks. For this is a pollution much more than those which
you yourselves once drove out. And having said a summary I shall be done. If
you yourselves would have met with like things everywhere had the Athenians
conquered, it is noble to do as I say; but if not, let us conquer them in
both, with arms and with clemency.
