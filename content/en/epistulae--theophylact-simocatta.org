offering its possession as attainable to some and hard to attain to others, as
if nature were grudging men in this. For if the sun's beams are equal for men,
and abundance of fire is most ready to hand for all, and the streams of
rivers, both winter-torrents and ever-flowing, are easy for all to get, why
then, you said, has nature disposed gold so sparingly for men and allotted a
contentious gift to those under the moon, through which the greatest evils
come upon men? But I pour a broad laugh upon your doctrines; for you have made
the very praise of nature a ground

of blame, and you have suffered the same as the owl; for she thinks the
conspicuous outshinings of the sun a cause of blindness. The human race
hungers for gold profitably, Sosipater; for from this both arts were brought
into life and cities were settled and ease of contracts. And if one must speak
in a word, this earthly dwelling-place would have been stripped of all good
order, if gold had not fashioned men in need of one another; for a sailor
would not have sailed, a wayfarer would not have traded, the farmers would not
have possessed a plough-ox, the sceptres of royal rule would not have been
honored, the subject people would not have been adorned with offices and
fitting things, a general would not have led the army. And if you also wish to
learn a secret argument, gold has been entrusted with the reins of virtue and
vice, and a soul's desire is tested through it, and it is a match for the
Celtic river; for it is a most true proof of bastard virtue and of vice. 11
Callistachys to Cyparisson Simichidas yesterday somewhere set fire to the
nature of the unfruitful and wild trees; for such was the sentence he voted
against the trees that bear no fruit. But the nature of the all-devouring fire
had an unbridled rush, and it suddenly destroyed the neighboring farmer's
plot. And he, throwing aside the hide and the mattock, goes toward town to get
a rhetor as ally, and a court has been assembled against Simichidas. This I
too, Cyparisson, will contrive against you, unless you order your bees to keep
away from my meadows, and you will learn not to get an unjust income from
others' labors. 12 Melpomene to Praximilla The pipe-girl Chrysogone has made
her pastimes at the Leocorion, and perhaps she even thinks she delights my
lovers. And the little whore says we take the matter very ill. But I have not
made much of what is being done; for Chrysogone's manners would test of what
sort my lovers happen to be toward us. But be for me, by the gods, a most
clear messenger of an unlying answer and say to the Laconian woman: "We owe
you very great thanks for this at least, Chrysogone; for by your ugliness our
own things will be shown more august, since even when a jackdaw has not
appeared a crow too will be counted among the comely birds." 13 Ariston to
Nicias They say the elephant is a most love-of-learning animal and a handy
pupil of human lessons; for with him the bulk of the body is not so wonderful
as the adornment of education is honored. And these things the children of the
Indians babble about; but I have marveled at Nicias as having a reasoning more
irrational than the irrational animals. For being a son of a sophist you have
kicked away your father's virtues, and you have spent the leisure of life on
dice and wrestling-grounds, and you have become a limit of noble birth for the
family. If then you wish to be called a son of Hermagoras, return at last to
that man's life; for it is a good thing for whoever has wisdom and prudence
even in old age, as it seems also to Plato. But if you take it ill to move
from the old evils and you proclaim yourself a son of Hermagoras, know that
you are becoming a tomb-robber of your father's grave; for by your vice you
insult that man's virtues. 14 Myronides to Damalus Your boy has ruined the
whole flock. Always filling the ivy-bowl with milk, the clothes-thief goes to
the plane-trees and, making a bed of leaves, has thrown himself down without a
care and embraces the luxurious life. Then taking up the pipe he puts forward
his cheer as a kind of after-dinner song, and he counterfeits the rustic laws.
And the cattle are scattered here and there and are easy to take at an attack.
Yes, and the wretch sells the dung cheap, and is not ashamed of wronging
Myronides. I was feasting Trygias yesterday splendidly; for dried figs and
locusts were my dainties. And this divine

youth has eaten up most of the figs and has somehow gulped down the locusts
(and Trygias was gaping), and after satiety he carries some portion home. Let
Clinias leave my fields at last; for it is more choiceworthy to bear evils far
off than to nourish a hidden enemy at home. 15 Atalante to Corinna I have seen
Augeas in the palaestra, Corinna; and neither speech would express the sight
nor a painter's hands imitate it. For the youth was strong, upright, shaggy on
the breast, and his eyes were a gazelle's. And the look was neither reddening
with anger nor too soft with moisture, but manly and gentle together. And the
color of the body was neither whitened toward the womanish nor shadowed toward
the darker. And the hair was waving a little with curl and was imaging the sea
turning dark-blue in an hour of calm, when it has left aside the mad and
barbarian part of the surge and folds the neighboring dry land in a soft
stream. And the cheeks were neither very red (for that is womanish) nor again
darkening the comeliness with paleness. And the nose had been turned very
augustly and was convicting much skill of the fashioning nature. And the oil
being poured on was flashing round like the sun and with the glitter of the
outshinings was making the palaestra bright like rays. I have suffered in the
soul, Corinna, and I feel the pain more sharp; for the female race is ashamed
to tell an erotic passion. 16 Gorgias to Aristides You rejoice when you
borrow, you are distressed when payment is exacted, and meeting the lenders
you are struck with fear, as if thinking you encounter some fearful and wild
beasts; and you look round the crossroads and gaze toward the stoas, longing
to run from the lenders, as those who sail in a great surge long to find a
harbor. But you also spread evil upon evil; for borrowing from some you pay
the debt to others, doing the same as those who throw themselves down a cliff
through fear of death. Since then borrowing is a many-cleft evil for men and
is harder than the mythical hydra's self-grown births, even in the phantoms in
sleep beware of borrowing; for so you would look upon the sun's rays freely
and would breathe the air above the earth very mildly. 17 Lophon to Pedias May
Leucippus not come to his season; for the plot was rearing an evil beast for
us. A court had been assembled for me and Sostratus about the bounds of the
little hill. But Leucippus was wholly corrupted in his wits and longed to see
the court golden; such a gold-loving passion held the wretch. And Sostratus,
recognizing this too, bought the victory with gold and stuffed Leucippus's
throat with the gifts. The virgin Justice has been corrupted, and gold weighs
out winning for men. For even-balanced judgment has perished; for gifts rather
than the just are trustworthy. 18 Erotylus to Hypsipyle Even palm-trees swell
with a natural love, and the male desires the female, and the male is bent
round by the love and is twined into the beloved's foliage. And if the female
is also far from the male, they take fig-wasps from the female and fasten them
on the male and by a device lead his love. If then you are not able to return
to us sooner, console the love for me through a painter, and let the painting
furnish my imagination of your sight in tablets; for even a seeming is enough
to beguile those who love too much. 19 Diogenes to Chryse I say you are a
steward of wealth, not a master of money; for such is the sentence your own
manners have brought against you. For it is not lawful for unholy souls to
share in any good. Dig the earth then and guard the gold round about,
thrice-wretched one; for the wealth is not yours, but with you it is

entrusted to be. For having emulated the vice of the Phrygian Midas you are
rich while starving, as if being strangled with golden nooses. Chloazon to
Mecon I sent wild pears yesterday, Mecon, to the desired one; and she threw
aside the woof and rose at once from the weaving, and taking the gifts she
distributed them to the pigs, and she sent the envoy away as an ill-omened
messenger. And I weep; for the love that wrongs us is fearful, forcing one to
love an unseemly girl. Fortune and the Loves are blind, the ones furnishing
the pains, the other happiness, at random and as it happens. 21 Perdicia to
Rhodope You sing unmusically and you pain, you do not delight, the lovers; for
you whimper a tragedy, not a charming song for the hearers. And the lovers
weep in distress; for your songs teach them to be temperate instead of lewd;
for you have not made the melody attractive. Spare then, by the gods, us who
are distressed; for you seem to the spectators not a pipe-girl but a mourner.
And we would all stop our ears with wax if you should practice melody; for we
would hear Sirens rather than Muses lamenting. 22 Antisthenes to Pericles
Alexander the son of Philip was in no way puffed up at his successes, but he
philosophized the blasts of fortune, not being accustomed to be baited by the
greatest praises. For this reason, seeing Darius fallen in the turn of the
war, he covered the enemy with his cloak, showing the nobility of virtue and
of fortune. From this the subject people abused Alexander, and piety was an
accusation for the king. Alexander then, being a philosopher, feared, as it
seems, the unclear thing of fortune; therefore when very many successes were
announced to him at once he said: "O Zeus, mix some misfortune too with the
goods." So most sensibly Alexander was wary of prosperities at the peak. If
then you have not known that nature is neighbor to the trophies, not long
after you will see experience as teacher, and you will have the lessons older
than the knowledge; but if you are blind of your own will, you will hit upon a
harder penalty, paying justice both of faults and of knowledge. 23 Astachyon
to Mylon Clean the field of the hemlock; for you have ruined my bees. Do not,
by the gods, furnish troubles to a farming man. You have differed in nothing
from drones for us. Why then are you harshly angry at the neighbor without
reason? If you should not stand off from the vice, all-wicked one, I would
write up your wantonness on my doors, and I would show the wrong to the
neighboring farmers, so that all may flee you as some evil hard to meet. 24
Telesilla to Lais Neither gold-miners tracking a vein of gold nor well-diggers
searching the secrets of the earth, seeking to see springs of waters, have
been so eager about their own art as I was picking the whole city to pieces,
if somewhere it was possible for me to see Agesilaus. For I have heard that
the maenad Leucippe has made a drinking-bout for him, and a violent
thunderbolt has taken up lodging with us, and the passion inflames tears for
me beyond consolation. Therefore I shall be a fellow-worker of the tragedy;
for we shall no longer see the sun rising. So I shall become more fearful than
both Medea and Phaedra. 25 Sosipater to Axiochus They say you buried your
brother recently, and that you take it very ill beyond consolation at the
passion. And how should I admire you as a philosophic man, overcome to such a
point by passion? The death babbled among us is a kind of sleep, longer than
this customary one, but very brief toward the coming day. The dead have been
sent on a long absence; our separation from them is not for long; endure the
sundering, waiting again for the union. Do not suffer something of a
body-loving soul, since even Plotinus seemed like one ashamed that he was in a
body; so this mortal tent pained the philosopher. Stop for me as far as this

the tear, legislate bounds for the grief, heal the passion with prudence,
become a physician of yourself; you have reason as the drug that takes away
grief and is without bile, forgetful of all evils. The craftsman never leads
the things made from the better to the worse. Let us leave the mortal things
to the mortals; for here the kingly thing of the soul is stained with great
spots. I would rather weep at birth than at death; for the one is a beginning
of tears, the other a dissolution of the distressing things. Ignorance renders
us very fearful, and we are wary of death not because it is an evil, but
because it has not been known to men; for of that of which someone has been
deprived of the knowledge, this he accuses most readily. Do not then become a
match for Niobe, lest you too somewhere seem to exchange the human nature for
a stone. 26 Cecropis to Dexicrates They say the Magnesian stone loves the
iron, and they relate that the stone lives so long as it is also with the
beloved. For whenever the stone is separated from its yokefellow, it is at
once made dead and puts off its own activity. Such attachments, Dexicrates,
come to be even in the soulless things. And what would I suffer, dearest,
being left so far from your being at home? I think even the speaking is harder
than the suffering. So may I pain those who have pained, and may I become a
missile for those who love; for an Aphrodisian spark is more burning than soot
and flame. 27 Theristron to Speiron We are setting off for Etna, the Sicilian
mountain, having said farewell to Attica. For we have never seen a more
unfortunate land toward increase of fruits; instead of wheat it has borne us
myrtles, instead of barley ivy. And the meal-bin is empty, and the
threshing-floor has been filled for me with famine. For this reason, the first
seeds not having sprouted for me, I would not again throw down to the
thankless one. A farmer cannot have both famine and a soldier as enemy, nor
are sailors able to fight at once against winds and thunderbolts. 28
Heraclides to Antisthenes You have not yet let up from the anger for me,
Antisthenes, but you are still harsh toward us, and you hide the distress
under a pretext of mild words, as those who keep a spark of fire in soot.
Cleanse the pain from the heart at last; for this is what our words are
ambassadors for. But if not, you will be even harsher than the sea; for she
lulls the wild thing and furnishes a humane sight to the sailors, whenever
they treat very kindly with oil her who is harsh toward the waves. 29 Lachanon
to Peganion Come to me toward the oil tomorrow; for I would go out of the town
and I shall be a diner, dearest. For I would dedicate the firstlings of the
flock to Nymphs and to Pan. For the gods are well-minded to us at last; the
ivy-bowls have been filled for me with milk, the lambs have given birth
fruitfully, the goats skip about at the successes as if rejoicing. We have
ceased fighting with poverty, a beast hard to admonish and difficult; like a
sore it is twined into those who possess it and is a custom-loving evil,
making idle, downcast, beyond consolation toward grief, quick to tilt toward
distress, sleepless, care-loving, toilsome, inventive of evils, inglorious,
easy to despise, not envied. For no one wishes to meet such an evil, not even
if he should be condemned to be sick with the madness of Orestes. Having
thrown then the things of poverty to poverty, let us change to another lot,
having exchanged the mind too with the fortune. 30 Rhodine to Calliope You
tear us to pieces among the lovers and you jeer at us with this and that,
mocking that the tense and swelling thing of my body has been made slack. And
you think your own knavery has been hidden; for you, all-defiled one, void the
things being carried in the womb unfinished, and you have made abortions more
choiceworthy than the births, choking round with sharper drugs the things
being brought to life in your belly, and you work the murders more harsh than
the Colchian Medea. An unfeeling yokefellow was teaching that bedfellow, the
ally of the contests, to be a child-killer; but you, little whore, work
countless disasters because of the comeliness of beauty. Stop then both
covering your

inhumanity and mocking our piety. For with us the birth is more humane than
the abortion. And know that Justice too is harsh against you and is not idle
at exacting penalties of child-killing. 31 Hephaestion to Thales The Median
bird, the peacock, has been allotted even the disdain of the Medes, thinking
some great and over-swollen thing upon its beauty. And even its female seems
to be worsted by this. Accordingly it raises the feather as a kind of hair and
furnishes a most conspicuous beauty to those who see, and it works a circular
shape, and it images the heavenly order, and with the eyes of the feather it
fashions the arrangement of the stars. And this is a beauty-loving habit of
Median birds; for they do not grudge their own good order, teaching painters.
But you sit on your writings and you veil your virtues and you leave the
labors unexhibited, overlooking us bereft of so great a benefit. If then some
grudging is what pains us, the attempt is unphilosophic and alien to your
profession; but if hesitation keeps the delay, you have suffered the same as a
farming man who has lent many sweats to the earth and at the peak of summer
does not reap the ears. 32 Poas to Ampelius Come, little old man, let us
lament a joint piping. The river has leapt up and has danced an evil leap for
us; it has flooded the whole field and the fearful one has sunk the new-born
things of the vines. And the greater misfortune, the defiled one does not wish
to set off from here; for it loves to linger in the little farm and has made
our field a bed. And it is worthy of tears to see; for instead of vines we
wretches farm the fishes. When the river wishes, we hunt; when it wishes, we
starve; and it thinks it is ambitious toward me with some great gift. Would
that in the summer we were in no way supplicating the clouds to grant rain to
us in drought; for so we would not have been deprived of what we possessed.
For a river by itself is burdensome; and if it should also grow rich with
rains, it is more unbridled than fire and does not check the snortings with
measures. 33 Galatea to Thetis I praise your prudence, and I admire the very
most experienced thing. For as from some Pythian tripod you told me the things
about to be, and more sharp-sighted than Lynceus you looked into the deeper
and unseen things of the affairs. Callimachus has left us; he has flown off,
being lifted by the very sharp wing of satiety; the all-defiled one has run
from us and satiety has become for him older than the desire. And you were
advising me many things: "Do not trust oaths, Galatea; nothing is easier for
those who love than oaths." For the young, drunk with erotic pleasures, are
deprived even of deliberating well, and they do and speak whatever the Loves
legislate; for they do not have the desire of the affairs as self-moved.
Distrust seems safer than trust, and toward deceit an oath is a competent
sophist. 34 Themistocles to Chrysippus Since temperance has been honored even
in the myth, come then, Chrysippus, I shall tell you a myth not unsolemn. The
birds once came to Zeus and were ambassadors that the Olympian put a leader
into their hands; for lack of rule was what pained the birds, and they were
bereft of some greatest good, leadership, and because of this they possessed
much disorder. Zeus then nodded, and the wish became a deed, and he grants a
wonderful gift to those who supplicate; for the thing given was a king's
dignity. He ordered the birds then to come to lakes and springs and to wipe
off their own filth, and he entrusted the test of the leadership to the
waters; for comeliness was what was honored with Zeus. The birds then washed,
then they run back to Zeus at once, and each was exhibiting his own
comeliness. But the jackdaw, fearing his own ugliness, adulterated the
fashioning of nature, adorning his own comeliness with a foreign adornment.
But

the owl exposed the ugliness and was showing the plastered adornment; for
recognizing her own feather she took it away as her own and was giving the
other birds a pattern that each take away his own. And the jackdaw has become
a jackdaw again. This myth, Chrysippus, speaks the truth as a waking vision,
telling us much temperance. For so even we men possess nothing here as our
own, but while living we are proud for a little of a plastered adornment, and
having died we have been deprived of what is not ours. Overlook then both
money and body, and take care of an immortal thing, the soul; for the one is
everlasting and immortal, and the others are mortal and ours for a little. 35
Myronides to Moschon I have lent the plough-ox to Tycanias; for he did not
have the other of the yoke. And Tycanias too promised to furnish me his own
bull. For I too was lacking an ox; for my wonderful bull has perished, when
that harsh plague took up lodging with the cattle. But the fine Tycanias
dissolved the agreements, and so long as the aim was being accomplished for
him he seemed to be content with what had been resolved. And I lament the
tyrant †antlen†; for we do not possess plough-oxen, and the season of the
ploughs has already somewhere even gone by. I shall therefore arm a court
against Tycanias, and I shall set the whole plot as judges. And that one will
undergo justice of the deceit, and we shall persuade the wanton to keep away
from vice, having the ruin of one man as a teacher of temperance. 36 Antigonus
to Lysistratus The Loves play with men and enslave those under the moon,
winged children, that we may be persuaded even by the painters in something.
Would that it were possible to see the enemy; for so even the Loves, striking,
would have been struck. But we have been wronged more in this very thing, that
we have not even known of what sort the enemies happen to be in nature. For
with what an irrational passion I have been twined; I desire Melanippe,
descendant of Diodorus, out of measure, having never even in a dream seen the
little woman, but only having heard from someone that she sings wonderfully.
And I have been struck in the soul, being wronged in nothing from the eyes,
which I am accustomed to suffer, Lysistratus; but now even the ears have
become eyes for me. So much has the nature of Loves been able. Whether then
she is a toad to the sight I have not known, or even whether the thing said is
some imagination I do not understand; for one witness is not competent toward
truth. But I pain in the soul all the same, and I love the one not seen, and I
think I have taken a share of Pan's madness; for for him too the desired thing
was unseen, and only a seeming had the existence of the things sought. 37
Eurybiades to Cimon You profess many things and you do few, and you have the
tongue higher than the deed. But if cleverness of words has been admired with
you, the painters are more powerful than your tongue, fashioning in the
tablets so many things as nature is not able to do. And if you also think you
delight the hearers with promises, you cheer for a little, but not long after
you distress more harshly. Since even the finest of the dreams do not cheer us
so much sleeping as they have pained us waking; for the hopes fly up together
with the sleep at once. Possess then the deeds in tune with the tongue, lest
you both become hateful to friends by lying and furnish enemies a ground of
blame as being without a share of truth. 38 Tettigon to Ortygon Why then,
thrice-wretched one, have you changed the clothing and let the partridges fly
out? Wine has become the evil for you; they say Odysseus bought even the
Cyclops's eye with wine. Therefore if you should not gather the birds,
Ortygon, I would throw myself down a cliff with you. For both for an unbridled
boy to live is hard, and for a son to appropriate a grave before the father is
even very hard to bear.

39 Thetis to Anaxarchus You cannot love both Thetis and Galatea; desire is not
sliced; for the Loves are not divided. But you would not bear a double love
either; for as the earth cannot be warmed by two suns, so one soul does not
bear a pair of erotic torches. 40 Socrates to Plato No one is wronged, but all
do wrong, each wronging himself of his own accord; for we are autocrats of
virtue and vice. Leonides has taken away your little farm; this is of the
things outside, and it has ruined your soul in nothing. Philip fines you and
appropriates the ring; you yourself have been wronged in nothing; for the
acquired thing is not ours. The barbarians have killed your son; you have not
suffered anything terrible; for you did not possess the boy as everlasting;
recently he was added to you not possessing him, and again he is not, since he
was not, but he came to be. Therefore men do wrong, they are not wronged. And
I have marveled at the Homeric Cyclops; for he said that Nobody harms one who
is being wronged, and for the shepherd the denial was the affirmation of the
truth. 41 Marathon to Peganion Having fled the civic surges and the inexorable
noises of the town I hired this field here, and I thought I had found a change
of fortune. But we have been thrown into greater evils; for sometimes I have
the rust as enemy, sometimes the locusts, at another time even the hail; and
the frost ruins the fruits as an inexorable tyrant, and I, the wretch, grant
the sweats to the winds. Alas, the unfortunate one, where should I turn?
Whenever I remember the farming toils, I embrace the things of the town;
whenever I reckon the civic turmoil, I love the country, and what is not
present is more fortunate than the present. One dissolution of the painful
things is death, whether natural or again self-chosen. Therefore hanging is
the drug for me; for for the unfortunate to loathe death is silly. 42 Pericles
to Aspasia If you seek gifts, you do not desire; for the Loves are unbribable,
and they teach those who desire to be such. If then you love, the giving
rather, not the taking, is more proper; but if you long for money and have
fabricated the desiring through trade, the tongue has convicted the mind,
selling the pleasures for gold to the one who wishes. 43 Diogenes to Demonicus
The artificial little woman, the dual-natured half-man, the thing in no way
complete beside nature, the Lydian eunuch has insulted us; for all the parts
of the body profess to have unseemly tongues. But I, according to the Homeric
Odysseus, do not feel his missiles; for the female race striking heroes is
feeble, that I may also utter something more haughty to you, imitating
Diomedes. To bark then and to be mad is fitting for eunuchs; for deprived of
the power of the hands they wish to do all things through the tongue. And the
friends accuse us of much simplicity, since we have not repaid the insulter
with penalties; for so even if an ass should kick us, we would not assemble a
court for it. And this too has been well declared to Socrates the son of
Sophroniscus. 44 Priapides to Corydon Be a diner for me tomorrow. All things
toward the wedding have been prepared for me: pulse-soup, chickpea, many dried
figs and must and honey-cake and sacrificial cakes. And do you yourself bring
the artful pipe, to sing the melodies above the swans; for you were skilled in
pastoral music-making, Corydon. For I have wished to move the bridal chamber
toward Aphrodisian pleasure, being charmed underneath by the sounds from the
trumpet. 45 Leander to Pylades The Loves are very hateful to us. I love, but
the desired one hates; and what would I do, the thrice-wretched one? The Loves
do not have an even-balanced scale; they weigh out unequal tears for men. If
then

they do wrong, let them not be called gods; but if they do not lie about the
name, let them judge straight and let them divide the pains to me according to
the just. 46 Diogenes to Aristarchus Alexander was striking Macedonia with
amazement, riding the horse Bucephalus. And they relate that the horse was not
persuaded by bits, nor was softened by tame flatteries; for the animal was
untamed and by the spirit it had been deprived of being a horse, and it was
some unapproachable evil and very hard to meet for those who encountered it.
When then it was allotted Alexander as rider, it mixed the harshness as with
gentleness, exchanging the snorting for dejection; and one could see the
unbridled one temperate. For it was not lawful to fight against Alexander. Be
then, Aristarchus, you too obedient to fortune; for the horse Bucephalus was
persuaded not by Alexander, but by fortune. 47 Poimnion to Arnon The udders of
the sheep are about to burst for me from the milk, and somehow we have been at
a loss for ivy-bowls. Therefore do you yourself give the ivy-bowls; and I
shall furnish you milk, repaying small thanks with great gifts. 48 Chrysogone
to Terpander Do not blame one who both reviles and insults; for those who
