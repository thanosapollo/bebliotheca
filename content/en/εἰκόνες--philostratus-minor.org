Let us not deprive the arts of being preserved always, thinking the older hard
to look in the face, nor, if something has been anticipated by one of the
earlier men, let us spare ourselves from emulating it according to our power,
glossing over indolence with a decent figure, but let us set ourselves upon
what has gone before; for if we hit the mark we shall do a thing worthy of
speech, and if it should happen that we fail somewhere, we shall at least
grant ourselves to appear as praising, emulating what is well done. Why then
have I struck this prelude? A description of works of painting has been
labored by my namesake and mother's father, having the tongue very Attically,
with a grace brought forward and with vigor. Wishing to go in the tracks of
this, we had necessity, before the whole attempt, also to go through some
things about painting, so that the discourse too might have its proper matter
fitting the subjects. The pursuit of painting is excellent and not in small
things, for he who will preside rightly over the art must have examined human
nature well and be able to discern tokens of characters even of those who are
silent, and what lies in the set of the cheeks, what in the mixture of the
eyes, what in the habit of the brows, and, to speak comprehensively, as many
things as tend toward the mind. Having these sufficiently he will gather all
together, and the hand will act out best the proper drama of each, and if it
should happen a madman or one angry or thoughtful or rejoicing or impetuous or
in love, and once for all it will paint what is fitting for each. Sweet also
is the deception in it and bringing no reproach; for to stand before things
that are not as if they were, and to be led by them so as to think they are,
from which there is no harm, how is that not able to charm the soul and
outside of blame? Ancient and wise men seem to me to have written much about
the symmetry in painting, as if laying down laws of the proportion of each of
the limbs, on the ground that it is not possible to hit best the movement
according to thought unless the harmony comes within the measure from nature;
for the unnatural and outside of measure does not admit the movement of a
nature that is rightly disposed. And as one looks, the art is found to have a
certain kinship toward poetry, and there is a common imagination of both. For
the poets bring the presence of gods onto their own stage and all that belongs
to bulk and solemnity and soul-leading, and painting likewise, signifying in
the picture those things which the poets have to say. And what need is there
to speak about things said illustriously by many, or, saying more, to seem to
set oneself to encomia of the matter? For these things too suffice to show
what we are eager about, that it will not be thrown away somewhere, as being
quite small; for having met with pictures of a graceful hand, in which ancient
deeds were not without the Muses, I did not think it right to pass these by in
silence. But so that the writing may not proceed for us upon one person, let
there be someone set down, toward whom one must articulate the several things,
so that thus the discourse too may have what is fitting.

The heroine who is leafy with the rush (for you see, I suppose, her under the
mountain, compact in form and dressed in dark blue), Skyros, my boy, an
island, which the divine Sophocles calls windy. And she has also a shoot of
olive in her two hands and a vine-branch, and the tower under the foothills of
the mountain: the daughters of Lycomedes are kept as maidens there together
with her who seems to have come from Thetis. For Thetis, having learned from
her father Nereus the decree of the Fates upon the child, and that it was
fated for him on both sides either to live without glory or, having become
glorious, to die very quickly, hides the child away with the daughters of
Lycomedes on Skyros, seeming to the others to be a girl, but having known the
eldest of them with a secret love, and time going forward to the season of
birth will give out Pyrrhus. But these things are not here. There is a meadow
before the tower, for the place of the island is fit to give girls abundance
of flowers, and you see how they scatter this way and that cropping the
flowers. Beauty then of all of them is beyond contrivance, but the others
simply incline toward female season, with glances of eyes looking out softly
and with the bloom of cheek and with the impulse toward each thing proving the
female very well; but this one here who tosses back her hair and is grim with
delicacy will at once be proved as to her nature and, putting off the
counterfeit that is with necessity, will show forth Achilles. For a report of
Thetis's secret having fallen among the Greeks, Diomedes is sent with Odysseus
to Skyros to prove how these things stand. And you see both, the one with the
ray of his eyes even sunk, through knavery, I think, and the always peering at
something, and the son of Tydeus sensible, but ready in mind and putting
forward the active. And behind them also the one signaling with the trumpet:
what then does he want, and what is the ethos of the painting? Odysseus being
wise and a sufficient hunter of things unclear contrives the present things
toward the proof of those hunted; for having thrown into the meadow both
baskets and as many things as are decent for girl-children for play, and a
panoply, the daughters of Lycomedes go to what is their own, but the son of
Peleus says farewell to baskets and shuttles, leaving them already to the
girls, and having rushed at the panoply is stripped from there

And Pyrrhus is no longer rustic, nor swelling in squalor, such as the youthful
boasts of herdsmen, but already a soldier. For he has stood leaning himself on
a javelin and looking off toward the ship, and his dress is a crimson cloak
gathered from the tip of the shoulder into the left hand and a white tunic
above the knee, and his eye is fierce, yet not in onset, but still in delays,
through fretting at the delay, and the mind images something of the things in
Ilium a little later. The hair now, while he is at rest, hangs over the
forehead, but when he has rushed it will be disordered, nodding with the
movements of the spirit. And the goats bounding up at large and the herds
running wild and the club thrown in the midst with a shepherd's crook have, my
boy, a story of this kind: being vexed at his mother and grandfather for the
sitting on the island, since after Achilles was dead, fearing for the child,
they made the going-out of Pyrrhus a thing sworn against, he sets himself over
goat-flocks and cattle, breaking the necks of the bulls that dishonor the
herd, which indeed are shown toward the mountain on the right. And an oracle
having fallen among the Greeks, that Troy would be takeable by no other except
the Aeacidae, Phoenix is sent to Skyros to bring up the child, and having put
in he meets him, not knowing him, himself not known, except insofar as the
delicate and sturdy of the form hinted that he was a child of Achilles. And
from there, having recognized who he was, he becomes known to Lycomedes and to
Deidameia. These things the art wishes to teach us in this short picture, and
it has been painted so as even to furnish a song to poets.

** MARSYAS.

** b.

The Phrygian has been brought down; at any rate he looks already lost through
understanding of what he will suffer, and he has trusted that he has piped
last, having grown bold unseasonably against the son of Leto, and the pipe has
been thrown from him, dishonored so as to pipe no longer, since even now he
has been proved out of tune, and he stands beside the pine, from which he
knows he will be hung, having condemned himself to this penalty, to be flayed
as a wineskin. And he looks askance at this barbarian who is whetting the edge
of the knife against him; for you see, I suppose, how his hands are toward the
whetstone and the iron, and he looks up at Marsyas, with eyes gleaming grey,
and raising a hair wild and squalid, and the redness on the cheek is of one
murderous, I think, and the brow also lies over the eye, drawn together to a
gleam and giving a certain habit to the spirit, but he has also bared the
teeth in something wild under what is about to be done to him, and I do not
know whether rejoicing or whether even as the mind swells toward the
slaughter. And Apollo has been painted resting himself upon a certain rock,
and the lyre lying on the left is still struck by the left hand falling on it
gently and, by Zeus, as if fondling it. See also the easy form of the god and
a smile blooming on the face, and the right hand lies on the lap, holding the
plectrum mildly, made idle by him who rejoices at the victory. Here also is
the river that will change its name from Marsyas. Look for me also at the herd
of Satyrs, how they have been painted mourning Marsyas, showing the wanton and
the bounding together with being pained.

** HUNTERS.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

** c.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h2
:END:

And what would you not say about these, whom the painting leads from hunting,
and gives forth to them a spring of unmixed drinkable and translucent water?
And you see, I suppose, also the grove about the spring, a work of nature, I
think, the wise one; for she is sufficient for all that she wishes, and needs
art not at all, she who has even been established as a beginning for the arts
themselves. For what is lacking toward the provision of shade? These
cultivated vines, having crept up wild, have thrown together the clusters of
the branches, binding one tree to another, and this smilax and ivy, having
spread both together and as one, furnish us this roof, dense and sweeter than
art. And the chorus of nightingales and the music-halls of the other birds
clearly bring to our tongue the words of the most honeyed Sophocles, "and
thick-feathered nightingales warble within along it," when he said it. But the
company of the hunters, pleasant and compact and still breathing the spirit in
the hunt, each doing something different, rest themselves. How, O gods, and
how sweet the clearness of the art, and how it is possible to see the fortune
of each. This improvised bed of nets, I think, put together, receives the
leaders, it is fair to say, of the hunt, and these are five, and you see the
middlemost of them, how having raised himself he has turned toward those lying
above, recounting, it seems to me, his own prize and the throwing down of one
of the beasts first, which hang from the oaks in nets, a deer, I think, and a
boar lying in them. Does he not seem to you to be lifted up and to rejoice at
the work? And they look intently at him recounting, and one of them, having
leaned himself into the bed, rests somewhere, and he himself perhaps writing
up some deed of the hunt that is his own. And on the other wing of the mess,
the one next to the middlemost, with a half-empty cup in one of the two hands,
having brought the right around over the head, seems to me to be singing the
Huntress, and the one looking at the attendant bids the cup go round. The
painter is wise and accurate in the hand; for as one looks over all, nothing
has been left out, not even of the followers; for this one, having taken a
fragment of a tree, sits equipped as he was in the running about the hunt, and
from a pouch fastened on him he is dining, and of two dogs one, having
stretched himself before him, eats, and the other, having crouched on the hind
legs, holds up the neck receiving the things thrown off toward him, and
another, having kindled a fire and put in of the vessels as many as are useful
for this, furnishes them the things for the feast in plenty, very much urging
himself on, and this wineskin has been thrown at random, to draw drink for
whoever wishes, and of two servants the one, a carver, I think, says he cuts
portions, taking care of equality in the cutting off, and the other holds
under what will receive the portions, demanding, I suppose, that they be
equal. For in hunting, at least in this, there is no difference toward
fortune.

** HERACLES OR ACHELOUS.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1

** d.

You seek perhaps what is the fellowship of a serpent, which here has stood up
large, having raised the neck, tawny-red along the back and letting down a
beard under an upright and saw-toothed crest, and looking terribly with a
fixed gaze and able to bring one to astonishment, and of a bull, a horse who
under so great a horn, having arched the neck and digging the earth at his
feet, rushes as to a charge, and of this man half-beast, for he has ox-prowed
face and a copious beard and springs of waters flooding from the chin, and the
crowd that has flowed together as to a spectacle, and the girl in the midst, a
nymph, I think, for one must think this by the adornment about her, and this
old man in a despondent form, and a youth putting off a lion-skin and having a
club in his two hands, and this heroine here, compact and, in accordance with
the story of the Arcadians' nurture, crowned with oak, Calydon, I think, these
things. And what is the story of the painting? Achelous the river, my boy,
being in love with Deianeira the daughter of Oeneus, presses the marriage, and
Persuasion is absent from the things being done, and seeming another at
another time under the forms seen he thinks to astonish Oeneus. For know that
this is he in the painting, downcast over the child Deianeira, who looks
despondently at the suitor. For she has been painted not blooming the cheek
with shame, but in great fear of what she will suffer from the unnatural of
the pairing. But the noble Heracles, as they say a by-work of the road,
willingly undertakes the contest. And these things are in delays; but look
also how they have already closed, and as many things as are in the beginnings
of the fight, of a god and an unmoved hero, let them be guessed, and the end
again: the river, having reshaped himself into ox-horned, has rushed upon
Heracles, and he, having taken with the left the right horn, uproots the other
of the temples with the club, and from there the one, giving up, already lets
go jets of blood rather than of water, and Heracles, delighting in the work,
looks toward Deianeira, and the club has been thrown to the ground for him,
and he holds out to her the horn of Achelous as a wedding-gift of the
marriage.

You play, Heracles, you play and already laugh at the contest, being in
swaddling-clothes even so, and having taken the serpents from Hera, each with
each hand, you turn not at all to the mother standing by out of her mind and
in great fear, but they have already gone slack, having lengthened their coils
to the ground and having bent the heads to the hands of the infant, showing
also something of the teeth; and these are jagged and venomous, and crests
hang to one side under death, and the eyes are not seeing, and the scale no
longer blooms with gold and crimson, nor gleams toward the turns of movement,
but is pale and livid in the tawny-red. And the form of Alcmene, as one looks
over it, seems to be recovering from the first astonishment, but she now
disbelieves what she already sees, and the astonishment did not even allow her
to lie as a woman in childbed; for you see, I suppose, how unshod and in a
single tunic, having leapt from the bed with disordered hair, having spread
out the hands, she cries, and the serving-women, as many as were present at
the birth, astonished, converse one something else with the one near. And
these in arms and the one ready with the sword bare: the ones are chosen of
the Thebans helping Amphitryon, and he, having drawn the sword at the first
message toward defense, has stood at once upon the things being done, and I do
not know whether he is astonished or whether he rejoices henceforth; for the
hand is still in readiness, but the thought of the eyes sets a bridle on the
hand, as he has not even anything he might defend, and sees the present things
as needing the forethought of an oracle. For this reason also Tiresias here
near, prophesying, I think, how great he who is now in swaddling-clothes will
be, and he has been painted inspired and panting a prophetic breath. Night
also has been painted in form, in which these things are, lighting herself
with a little torch, so that the contest of the child may not become without
witness.

** ORPHEUS.

That Orpheus the son of the Muse charmed with his music even the things that
have no share of speech, all story-makers say, and the painter also says it; a
lion then and a boar near him are hearers of Orpheus, and a deer and a hare
not leaping away from the onset of the lion, and as many as in hunting the
beast is terrible to, herd with him, easy now, themselves easy. And do not
look idly even at the birds, not the musical only, who are accustomed to sing
well in the groves, but look for me also at the screaming jackdaw and the
cawing one herself and the eagle of Zeus. He, as large as he is, having
balanced both wings from himself, looks intently at Orpheus, not even turning
to the hare that is near, and they, having shut the jaws, are wholly of the
charmer, and these wolves and lambs mixed, as they are astonished. And the
painter also makes a greater youthful boast, for having pulled trees from the
roots he brings these as hearers to Orpheus and sets them around him. A pine
then and a cypress and an alder and this poplar and as many other trees,
having thrown together their shoots like hands, stand about Orpheus and close
the theatre for him, not having needed art, so that the birds might sit on
them and he might make music under shade. And he sits putting forth a
just-downy beard flowing on the cheek, and swinging a gold-gleaming tiara on
the head, and his eye is active with delicacy and inspired, the mind always
tending toward theology. And perhaps even now he sings something, and the brow
is raised as if signaling the sense of the songs, and his dress changes bloom
toward the turns of the movements, and of the two feet the left, pressing to
the ground, holds up the cithara lying over the thigh, and the right strikes
up the rhythm, beating the floor with the sandal, and of the hands the right,
holding the plectrum fast, is stretched to the notes, with the elbow put out
and the wrist nodding inward, and the left strikes the strings with upright
fingers. But there will be a certain unreason against you, O Orpheus, and now
you charm beasts and trees, but to Thracian women you will seem out of tune,
and they will tear apart a body to which even beasts, when it spoke, furnished
kindly ears.

Who is she who raises a grim brow-ridge over the eyes, and the brow full of
thought, and holy in the hair, and the eye I do not know whether already
erotic or showing something inspired, and herself displaying the unspeakable
look of the face? This then is the token of the daughters of Helios; one must
think Medea, I suppose, the daughter of Aeetes. For the expedition of Jason
having put in at the Phasis, when it went after the golden fleece, and having
come into the city of Aeetes, the girl loves the stranger, and an unaccustomed
reasoning comes under her, and what she has suffered she does not know, but
she disorders her thoughts and wanders in the soul. And she is dressed not
active now, nor in company of the greater ones, but as even for many to see.
And the form of Jason is delicate, yet not outside of being strong, and a
bright eye lies under the habit of the brow that is proud and lifting itself
over every opponent, and he already teems with down creeping down, and the
tawny hair rides on the forehead, and as to the dress he has girt a white
tunic, having hung a lion-skin and having bound on a boot, and he has stood
leaning himself on a javelin, and the habit of the face is such as neither to
think overmuch, for he feels shame, nor to lie under, for he is confident of
the contest. And Eros makes these things his own and, having leaned himself on
the bow, stands with the two feet crossed, having turned the little torch to
the ground, since the things of the love are still in delays.

** THOSE AT PLAY.

Those at play in the court of Zeus are Eros, I think, and Ganymede, if one
must think the one by the tiara, and lead the other to recognition from the
bow and the wings. They play then with knucklebones, and the one has been
painted mockingly taunting, Eros, and shaking the fold full of the victory,
and the other of two knucklebones still, having lost even the one himself, and
sending the other on a like hope. And his cheek is downcast, and the ray of
the eye, though it is delicate, signals the of the distress as sunk. And these
three goddesses standing over them, I think, do not even need an interpreter,
for Athena is clear to one who has looked from the spot, wearing the ancestral
panoply, as poets say, and looking grey from under the helmet, and with a
manly habit reddening the cheek, and this one here again signals even in the
picture the smile-loving under the spell of the cestus, and that the third is
Hera the solemn and kingly of the form says. What then do they want, and what
is the necessity of the company for them? The Argo, leading the fifty, has put
in at the Phasis, having gone through Bosporus and the Clashing Rocks. And you
see also the river himself lying in deep reed, in a grim form, for hair is
copious for him and standing up, and a beard bristling a little, and
grey-gleaming eyes, and the massed of the stream, not poured from a pitcher,
as is the custom, but flooding from the whole, gives us to think how great it
is poured upon the Pontus. And the contest of the sailing you hear, I think,
also of poets saying the golden fleece and the Argo known to all, and songs of
Homer tell. But the sailors of the Argo are in consideration of what has
overtaken them, and the goddesses have come to supplication of Eros, asking
him to take part with them for the safety of the sailors, having gone after
Medea the daughter of Aeetes, and as a wage for the service the mother holds
out a ball, saying it has become a plaything of Zeus. Do you see also the art
in the painting? This is of gold, and a stitching on it such as to be thought
rather than seen, and twisting coils of dark blue upon itself, and having been
tossed up it will perhaps give her to liken the departing gleam to sparkles of
stars. And he no longer even sees the knucklebones, but having thrown them to
the ground he has hung upon the peplos of the mother, that she make the
promise true for him, for he will not fail the contest.

He who is about to drive over a four-horse team through the midst of the
mainland under an upright tiara and Lydian dress, Pelops, I think, a bold
charioteer, it is fair to say. For he once also steered this chariot through
the sea, Poseidon, I think, having given it, on the outermost felloe of the
wheel, running the back of the calm under an undipped axle. And a fierce eye
and a neck standing up prove the readiness of the mind, and the brow lifting
over shows that Oenomaus is despised by the youth. For he is proud in the
horses, since they are high-necked and full in the nostril and hollow in the
hoof and the eye dark-blue and ready, and hanging copious mane from dark-blue
necks, which is indeed the manner of sea-horses. And near them Hippodameia
painting the cheek with shame, and wearing a bride's dress, and looking with
such eyes as to choose rather the of the stranger. For she loves and loathes
the begetter who is proud in such first-fruits, which indeed you also see,
these heads, each hung from the gateway, and time has given a form of its own,
the time in which each of them perished. For killing the suitors of the
daughter who have come he delights in the tokens of the murder. And images
flying over them lament their own contest, hymning at the compact of the
marriage, for Pelops has compacted that the child henceforth be free of the
avenging spirit. And Myrtilus also is a witness of the compact of the two of
them. And Oenomaus is not far off, but the chariot is ready for him and the
spear is stretched over the car, to kill the youth when he has overtaken him,
and he, sacrificing to his father Ares, hastens, wild to look at and murderous
in the eye, and urges Myrtilus on. And Eros, downcast, cuts into the axle of
the chariot, giving both to think, that the girl being in love with the lover
compacts against the father, and the things about to be about the house of
Pelops from the Fates.

** PYRRHUS OR THE MYSIANS.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h1
:END:

** 10.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h2
:END:

The chorus of poets hymns the things of Eurypylus and Neoptolemus, that they
both take after the father and are of good fame in the hand according to
strength, and the painting also says these things; for fortune, having brought
together the excellence from all the earth into one city, the ones have gone
not without glory, but such as, toward many, "sons of luckless men are they
who face my might," to say, and the noble of noble prevail. The things then
about those in the winning are other, and now the spectacle is about those who
have closed. This city is beetling Ilios, according to Homer, and a wall runs
around it such as even gods not to think unworthy of their own hand, and a
ship-station on the other side, and a narrow strait of the Hellespont parting
Asia from Europe. And the plain in the midst is divided by a river, Xanthus,
and it has been painted not roaring with foam, nor such as it flooded against
the son of Peleus, but a bed for it is lotus and rush and hair of soft reed,
and it lies down rather than stands up and holds the foot on the springs for
symmetry now, wetting itself with water. The stream is a measure, and an army
on either side, of Mysians with Trojans and of Greeks from the other, the
Trojans already worn, and those with Eurypylus unworn. And you see of them how
some sit in the arms, this perhaps Eurypylus having asked, and they rejoice at
the pause, and the Mysians, high-spirited and rushing out, go, and the of the
Greeks being in a like state to the Trojans except the Myrmidons, for they are
active and ready about Pyrrhus. And as to beauty one would interpret nothing
of the two youths, since they are in arms now, yet they are large and over the
others, and the age of both equal, and active in the glances of the eyes and
not delaying.

For the eye is fierce under the helmet for each, and they nod with the
movements of the crests, and the spirit is conspicuous on them, and they are
like men who in silence breathe might, and the arms of both are ancestral, but
Eurypylus is dressed in unmarked ones and they change the gleam wherever and
however he might move, as the rainbow, and for Pyrrhus the things from
