OF THE FIRST BOOK ON POWERS
That those who later became the magistrates of the Roman constitution were
formerly priests is unknown to none of all men, when Tyrrhenus, having
migrated westward from Lydia, taught the Lydian rites to those then called
Etruscans (and they were a Sicanian people), whom it came about were renamed
Tusci from the inspection of victims; and of these we know we have made
mention at length in the first of the treatise On the Months written by us.
For king Numa, taking the insignia of the magistrates from the Tuscans,
introduced them into the constitution, just as also the hard-to-fight quality
of the arms from the Gauls. And witnesses of these things are both Capito and
Fonteius, from whom also the most learned Varro, all Romans, after whom this
Sallust, the historian, clearly teaches in the First History. So that it
remains to narrate concerning the civil powers and that they grew from a
priestly order into the civil form. Let no one therefore judge us unlike
toward the things given of old, unless perhaps one who, submitting something
discordant with logical certainty, should through envy change the praise. And
that a certain Gracchianus also wrote of these things of old, we know the
law-writers reporting; but the things written are nowhere current perhaps
themselves, and in any case time itself both brought them forth and hid them.

10 ON THE OFFICES OF THE ROMAN CONSTITUTION BOOK 1
1 As I set my hand to treat of the offices of the Roman constitution it
occurred to me that it was worth while to give the discourse a prooemium from
the oldest and most honorable of all. And this was Aeneas, who through beauty
and strength of both soul and body was thought to be a son greater than
according to men. 2 There are accomplished, therefore, from the coming of
Aeneas into Italy until the founding of Rome years nine and thirty and four
hundred according to Cato the first and Varro, the Romans; but according to
Africanus and Castor and the son of Pamphilus years 7 and 10 and 400. from


the founding until the expulsion of the rexes there ran years three and forty
and two hundred. And the years of the consuls until Caesar the first 5 or
according to some six and 60 and 400. And from Caesar until Constantine there
have passed years three hundred seventy-five; and from him until the death of
the emperor Anastasius years 224 toward seven months, from which one might
take out nine years, which Constantine happened to have reigned at sacred
Rome. There is gathered then from the founding of this fortunate city five and
ten and two hundred years toward seven months. One might therefore sum from
Aeneas until the death of the good Anastasius all the years six and forty
toward seven hundred and a thousand toward seven months, as the Hellenes think
according to all the writers of either tongue. These things having been thus
set down by us with truth, it is time to treat, as has been said, of the
offices of the constitution in our time. 3 Romulus then, having become eight
years in addition to the ten, with his brother Remus built Rome, the mother of
the kingship. And the name of their rule, which the Italians say is regium, is
as it were tyrannical; for neither is the name regium significant of a lawful
Roman kingship, as some suppose; whence it no longer after 12 the expulsion of
the rexes was styled among the Romans even though they were under kings. For
one thing is the dignity of lawful kingship and another that of tyranny and
another that of autocracy; and how, I will say in brief. A king is the first
of his own subjects, chosen by vote upon a certain seat as also a foundation,
having obtained a fortune better than the others; as Sophocles said of Ajax
that he had the seat of sea-girt Salamis. And it is proper to a king to shake
none whatever of the laws of the constitution but to keep firmly the look of
his own constitution as a kingship; and to do nothing by authority outside the
laws, but to seal with his own votes what is jointly pleasing to the best of
the constitution, showing the affection of a father and at once a leader
toward the subjects, such as God and the fitness of the time have granted us.
But not thus will the tyrant dispose those who have fallen under him, but he
will act by power irrationally whatever he even wishes, not thinking it right
even to honor laws, nor enduring to write with counsel, but being led out by
his own impulses. For the manner of a king is the law, but the law of a tyrant
is the manner. 4 For the surname of the Caesars, or at any rate of the
autokrators, is significant neither of kingship nor even of tyranny, but
rather of sole rule and of the authority of administering the tumults that
rise up against the commons toward the better and of commanding the army how
it would be necessary to fight the adversaries; for imperare is said among the
Italians for "to command," whence imperator. And that the name of autokrator
or of Caesar is not significant of kingship is clear outright by the fact that
both the consuls and after them the Caesars received the dignity of the
so-called imperatores of the surname. For neither does the rule of the Cae 14
sars appear to have used tyrannical insignia, but the purple only, going up
into the senate of the Romans and steering the forces in arms autocratically,
as I said, and for this the Romans also called them principes, as if the first
head of the whole constitution. For the name of Caesar is indicative of a
family from the first Caesar, just as of Fabii and Cornelii and Flavii and
Anicii, this having been found earlier among barbarians. For the Egyptians
from the first Pharaoh named their kings Pharaohs, and Ptolemies from the
first. This good order of the Caesars was therefore kept among the Romans
until Diocletian, who first, having set around his head a crown composed of
precious stone and having mosaicked both his dress and his feet, turned it
toward the kingly or, to speak the truth, toward the tyrannical, and measured
out the mainland and burdened it with the tributes. 5 So that Romulus was a
tyrant, first having killed his brother and the greater, and acting
irrationally


the things that fell out; for this reason he was also named Quirinus, as if
lord, even if it seems otherwise to Diogenianus the lexicographer. For neither
is Romulus, or those about him, shown to have been ignorant at that time of
the Hellenic tongue, I mean the Aeolic, as both Cato in the On Roman Antiquity
and Varro the most polymath in the Prooemia of the things written by him to
Pompeius say, Evander and the other Arcadians having once come into Italy and
sown the Aeolic tongue among the barbarians. For the etymology introduced by
the grammarians besides this has been forced with pardon; for they wish him to
have been so surnamed from Cures, a little town of the Sabines, although he
did not spring from there, but was born on the Palatine hill by the banks of
the Tiber and reared there. For tyrants love to be called lords and masters of
themselves and not kings. 16 6 And the dignity of Caesar is better than
kingship, because it also of old had in its power to give kings to the
nations. For it is hateful and alien to Roman freedom to name those who hold
power masters, but not kings, inasmuch as the name master is common to them
and to those who have acquired one runaway, but that of kings is of them and
of them alone. And it is clear outright that it is a custom for the Romans to
call those who have tyrannized dominus, as indeed Sulla and Marius, and
dominationem the tyranny; as the wicked flatterers cast down the majesty of
kings, introducing from the ignorance of slaves that they hold the first
place. And that it is true it is possible to take also from these things.
Augustus once, or perhaps Tiberius after him, having been named master by one
of the flatterers, rose up and left the assembly, thinking it unworthy, as he
said, to converse with slaves. But already earlier, the outrage having been
introduced as if in honor, the mildness of our most gentle emperor endures,
although he is moderate beyond all who have ever been kings, also to be named
master, as a good father. Not that he rejoices but rather he blushes not to
seem to admit those who think they are honoring.

CONCERNING THE INSIGNIA OF THE REX 7
And before Romulus the insignia of the kingship of the Latins were a railed
throne and the robe called among them trabea (and of what sort it was, I will
say a little later). Whence the poet of the Romans in the seventh book of the
Aeneid, describing the kingship of Latinus, makes mention of throne and
trabea. But to Romulus there was also a crown and a sceptre having an eagle on
the tip and a white ankle-length paenula, striped from the shoulders in front
as far as the feet with purple weaves (and the name of the paenula is toga, as
if 18 a covering, from tegere by correspondence; for thus the Romans call
covering) and a crimson shoe, cothurnus its name according to Cocceius. And
this form of the so-called toga was common to him, the so-called rex, and to
the subjects in peace. The trabea however happened to be the paratura of the
rex alone, as if a distinguished robe. And it was a tunic and a semicircular
wrap, which the account is that Agathocles the tyrant of Sicily first
discovered. And they named the throne solium in the local tongue, instead of
sellium by correspondence, as the Roman Asper says; or also otherwise they
called it solium, instead of solidum, as if a whole trunk, for digging out a
stout stem into the type of a box and of a seat they completed a certain
platform for kingship, in order that as in a certain case the body of the king
might be secured, from the back and from either side no joint or additional
wood tightening the seat that happened to be once for all complete and whole.
For this reason they called the king's
throne. 8 In addition to these twelve axes went before Romulus toward the
number of the vultures which he saw as he began to found the city. And later,
when Priscus Tarquinius the rex had conquered the Tuscans and Sabines in war,


there were added to the tokens of the kingship long spears, likewise twelve in
number, not having spear-points, but crests hung upon them (and the Romans
call them iubae, but the barbarians tufts, the word having been corrupted a
little); besides these vexilla, as if long spears, with weaves hung from them
(they call them flammula from the flame color), concerning which enough has
been reported in the things written by us On the Months. 20 9 Such then were
the insignia of the kingship of that time. And concerning the army in arms
Paternus the Roman in the first of the Tactica says these things in their own
words according to interpretation: "Romulus set over as decurions the
caretakers of the sacred things, naming the same also centurions of the
infantry formations. For there being three thousand infantry shield-bearers,
he set a leader of each hundred, whom the Hellenes call a hekatontarch and the
Romans a centurion, so that all the centurions were 30 and as many maniples,
as if standard-bearers. And from the army he set apart three hundred scutati
as the Romans say, instead of shield-bearers, 'for his own guard' and a little
later it will be said what a clipeatus soldier is and what a scutatus; and he
also added three hundred horsemen to the forces, handing the care of these to
a certain Celerius so called. For this reason by synecdoche the whole army was
then named Celerii. And the cavalry force being gathered in three hundreds, he
divided them by three surnames, setting Ramnites and Titii and Luceres." And
the causes of these names we have given in the things set down by us On the
Months, as has been said. 10 And it is time to say how scutum differs from
clipeum. The Romans then call scutum that which is at once strong and thin,
which the Hellenes name stiptos, instead of stout, as Aristophanes in the
Acharnians "stiptoi old men, Marathon-fighters of holm-oak." For this reason
the Romans also call scutlata the thin and close and light of the garments.
And such is the con 22 struction of the shield; for it is without weight, as
being thin, but most strong and not easily yielding to blows. And clipeum the
Romans call the thyreos from kleptein and covering the one who carries it. For
it is proper to Hellenes and to them alone to use most wheel-like shields in
war, but to barbarians thyreoi; for toward the urgency of the fight they are
accustomed to pull up the doors and use them as coverings. 11 And there is
also another kind of smaller shield, which those beyond the Ister, not being
strong enough to fight on foot, carry on the horses (the Italians call it
parma, as if the Scythians peltai). Besides these there was also for the
ancients (for not even now) ancilium, a kind of little shield, from which they
also named ancillae the women taken by the spear. For the soldier covered the
woman who happened to please him with the ancilium on the inroad so that she
might not be harmed by anyone, as being guarded for the one who saved her. For
this reason the Italians also call slaves servi from having been guarded out
of the war; but those not taken by the spear but free in fortune, yet serving
through want, famuli, because fames is named hunger. And ancilia have been
said from a Hellenic, I mean Aeolic, meaning, as if amphilea; for such are the
little peltai of the Amazons. 12 And the equipment then for the whole Roman
army was one: a bronze helmet and a ringed thorax and a broad sword, short,
hung on the left thigh, and two javelins on the right, having broad barbs, and
woven black greaves and shoes for the feet, which the Hellenes name arbylai,
but the Romans garbola and crepides; not simply somehow nor irrationally. For
in the Images Terentius surnamed Varro 24 (and the surname of Varro signifies
the manly according to the tongue of the Celts, but according to the
Phoenicians the Jew, as Herennius says) recorded that Aeneas once came into
Italy equipped thus, having seen his image, as he said, carved from white
stone at a spring in Alba. And it is more wholly true; for also the poet of
the Romans in the first of the Aeneid thus


introduced him equipped and wandering with Achates in Libya. And in peace they
put around themselves beast-skins hung from the shoulders above as far as the
shins, adorning the so-called podeones of them, and naming them glubae, as if
hides, because glubare the Romans are accustomed to call flaying. And it is a
law not for the soldiers alone when bearing arms to be equipped thus but also
for autokrators. And since the soldiers of now have emulated barbarians, and
those them, among the guards of the palace alone (and they are called among
the Romans excubitores, as if unsleeping guards, whom Tiberius Caesar first
after Romulus discovered) this form has remained from Romulus, as I said,
having its beginning from Aeneas. For it was not permitted for Romans to put
around themselves a barbarian robe; and this Tranquillus mentions in the On
Augustus. For he says that Augustus, seeing in the hippodrome some of the
Romans equipped toward the barbarian, was angry, so that in a moment those
condemned, having cast off the barbarian, were scarcely recognized by Caesar.
Such then was the army of Romulus. 13 And the so-called attensiones I
conjecture were invented for the service of the rexes toward the carrying and
bearing of the necessaries, just as nocturni toward furniture and the other
things as many as are useful toward bed; for Cicero the great in the Against
26 Verres mentions this name. They named attensi the household servants of the
rexes from attending and obeying; for attendere the Romans say for being
eager.

SECOND ADVANCE THE PATRICIANS 16
It is clear outright that Romulus chose a hundred elders in number from all
the curiae, instead of tribes, toward deliberation of the commons, whom he
himself called fathers, but the Italians patricians, instead of eupatrids; and
after the rape of the Sabines naming them conscriptos, as if co-writ 30 ten,
whence patres conscripti the magistrates of the Romans are still even now
styled, and he also numbered with the Roman army another thirty curiae and as
many centurions and three hundred other horsemen from the nation of the
Sabines, so that all were six thousand infantry-fighters, but six hundred
horsemen. And this number Marius later kept, shaping the so-called legions, as
if picked men, and the emperor Leo first, having set over the so-called
excubitores as guards of the side-exits of the palace, enlisted three hundred
only according to antiquity. 17 And the insignia for the fathers, or at any
rate the patricians, were diplakes or at any rate chlamydes reaching from the
shoulders as far as the shins, drawn up with golden pins, the color
dried-vine, distinguished through the middle with purple (they named them
laticlaviae); and the chlamydes atrabattic from the color, because the grey
according to them is named atrum; and paragodai, lance-marked tunics,
purple-edged, white through the whole, having sleeves (they call them
manicae); and such tunics the multitude knows to name paragaudae; yet the
paragaudes is an ancient tunic, distinguished for Persians and Sauromatae, as
has been said by Diogenianus the lexicographer; and white drawers, covering
the whole leg together with the feet, and a black shoe, a kind of sandal,
naked through the whole, tightening the heel with a certain short rise, but at
the tip the toes of the feet, straps from either side being drawn through to
the ankles under the arch of the foot, meeting one another toward the breast
and binding the foot, so that very little from the toes in front and from
behind the shoe showed 32 through, but the whole foot shone through the
drawer. They call it campagus from the need toward the campus, as if the
plain, still even now; for coming to be upon the plain the Romans completed
the advances of the magistrates, upon which they were equipped with such
shoes. This campagus Lepidus in the On Priests says was formerly of the
Tuscans. 18 And a going-forth for the patricians never on foot nor even on
horseback (for it seemed common), but on distinguished carriages spread on
high of the seat, four mules drawing the carriage of Corinthian bronze, carved
into a multitude of figures and types of ancient appearance. For it was not
permitted to yoke horses to the carriage except for the kings alone; for the
going-forth with horses in a carriage is triumphal. And they called the wagons
burichallia from the oxen; without which, since no load lay upon them, they
entrusted the yoke to mules.

FOR WHAT REASON THE ROMANS CALL THE INSCRIPTIONS OF THE DIGNITIES TITLES 19
Titus Tatius, leading the Sabines, as I said before, having been joined to the
Romans so united both nations, that no longer two, but one and only the
constitution of the Romans was reported. And that that man had the address
"Titus Tatius,"


diminutively they called the inscription of nobility titulus from the first
Titus and Tituses those noble from ancestors, as Persius the Roman says. 20
Such then are certain things concerning the dignities, but the eupatrids of
Rome counted first of all honor the good report from gifts; and by how much
they had more those who attached themselves to them, by so much they counted
the good reputation greater than those who had fewer. And witness is the Roman
Juvenal, having said 34 that even of consulships and triumphs and of deeds of
manhood in wars the first for the ancients was the glory from gifts. And those
who attached themselves to them they called clientes in the ancestral tongue,
instead of colientes by correspondence, as if honoring and loving them,
granting the gifts with all honor and moderation, so as to name the honors of
friends philotimiae. And no one, having obtained attachment of a eupatrid,
used another thereafter toward the comfort of the whole life, they thinking it
a shame and a departure from their own fortune if anywhere one of their
friends came to stand in need of another. And a faint trace of this was kept
among the Romans until recently. For around Ostia (and it is a city lying at
the mouths of the Tiber) the picked men of Rome sent the most attached, as a
certain fine prey, taking the foreigners putting out before the others to lead
them to themselves, the courtyard doors of the house of the greater having
been opened toward the giving of all abundance to all, no guard or doorkeeper
hindering the entrance for those in need, the eupatrids themselves with wives
and children appearing to the foreigners and thinking it right that those
never seen by them take heart. Yet such humanity also passed even to the Rome
in our time and thereafter it did not stand, the glorious among us showing the
superiority of fortune as far as themselves.

WHENCE WITH TWO NAMES AND WITH THREE NAMES AND STILL ROMANS 21
And even before Romulus one might find the kings of the country named Silvii
from Silvius Aeneas the one from Aeneas the first. For having their dwellings
in the woods and honoring the nomadic life they addressed themselves Silvii
taking pride, the kings themselves not thinking it unworthy to pasture herds
and to collect their property; whence they also call property pecuniae
according to 36 them. For this reason antiquity also knows dogs once to have
followed Evander the Tegeate. And not only upon men was the surname of Silvii
put but also upon women, as Rhea Silvia and Ilia Silvia. And after the birth
of Rome and the rape of the Sabines king Numa first was styled with two names,
being addressed Pompilius Numa; of which the one was a surname of Romans, the
other of Sabines. For neither before him would one find Romulus or anyone at
all before the joining of the Sabines to the Romans having acquired besides
the proper address that was his. 22 And then there was zeal and order for
those to take pride in the address from either race, but time going forward
introduced new surnames, now from Trojan nobility, now from the so-called
Aborigines and autochthons of the country (for we know Cethegus claiming to
draw his race from those earth-born, coming to be in the marketplace naked of
dress, only a rough toga lying about his breast, although puffed with
senatorial tokens), then also from manner or disposition, as Publius Valerius
Publicola, of which the two surnames indicated the splendor from Romans, as I
said, and Sabines, but the third showed the disposition from affection toward
