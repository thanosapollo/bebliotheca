These things, O sun of the magistri and avenger of Rome, have been labored by
me concerning Crete before the time, but they have not been given: the one,
that your achievements of the trophies may not in times be given over to
forgetfulness (for what courage does, time dims, unless speech, going before,
is stored away in certain books); but they have not been given, from fear and
shrinking and because many things have been left remaining and have been
worsted by your achievements. Receive then, even if they are lacking of the
worth, the lispings of Theodosius, and permit me to write also concerning the
overthrow and capture of Aleppo; for thus, having become more eager of your
achievements, as far as is possible, I shall become a historian. For Achilles
and Alexander of the Macedonians and those who after them commanded most
excellently have carried off much glory by the writing even until now; and the
one is sung by Homer, and they by Plutarch and others even until now and are
famed. As in a person, then, the beginning of the iambs seems toward our
emperor of old, but toward whom the head of the praises is, you will know at
once.

THE CAPTURE OF CRETE, LABORED BY THE HUMBLE DEACON THEODOSIUS FOR THE
PHILANTHROPIC AND MIGHTY EMPEROR ROMANOS

I. Old Rome, do not envy the master, having set your generals against the new;
for with empty onset and in vain Scipio plundered the whole wealth of glory;
for what strength or power was Carthage's, when standing by in a battle of ten
thousand swords he sent the disasters to her being sacked? Who was Sulla, the
bold commander, who struck the burned city of the Athenians, worsted from the
slaughter itself? Who was Caesar, running of armies in the unmingled clashes
day by day? Who was that one overpowered in a fleet and who in turn consumed
those overpowering with timber? There was no other general like the master,
even if wealth-collecting Pompey, having run out, should stand by the battles
armed as if living and give the bright double crowns of fortune. But you,
Homer, beating boastful beats, raising the small things, come, not stealing
with words, speak to us quietly with shame. For the turmoil of the ten-year
sack is small beside us now and full of blame; for knowing the houses before
the towers of Ilium from the remnants from it, having fallen together into
wonder and laughter, here we laugh at the weaving of the falsehoods, and there
again we applaud the turning of the words. But, O mixing-bowl of generals, who
mix humble deeds into lifted words, judge yourself and, having put away old
respect of persons, become as a balance of the standard. And even having died,
applaud with words the all-sending and wise generalships of the master; for
small to us is the army of Hellas, small the phalanxes, weak the commanders,
Ajax, Achilles, Odysseus, Diomedes, to whom much glory, and the crushing of
falsely-named gods, bringing the associations badly, was tearing them from the
hopes. But if it seems good to you to walk well the paths of truth or to run
past them badly, here cease from the slaughters of Ilium, singing as tragedy
the streams of the bloods of Crete.

For when the army of the master came, fenced with many swift merchant-ships,
the land of the barbarians groaned and turned away from the neighboring wet.
And the general sent, Nikephoros, knowing himself the first-worker of the
battle, set the phalanxes all under sword, set ranks, squadrons

tower-running; a shield shone, a sharpened blade, a breastplate had its
gleamings against enemies, a spear held terrible shone back slaughter. And
when he saw all saved from the waters and the greatest waves, standing in the
midst he opened his mouth toward God: Behold your army standing at the bounds
of the adversaries, Craftsman of created things; it has raised the panoply of
yours against enemies, and it puts you forward as a well-famed commander
loosing the bonds of countless tower-works. Widen their hearts unto battle,
drive from them the bitter cowardice, shame the words of the enemies'
prophets, show who Peter is and who the false-weaver, who permits to eat the
camels, who makes the barbarians initiates of fornication, a false prophet
shown by knavery. Having cried thus and taken the shield, haranguing he said
to his fellow-workers: Men generals, children, fellow-servants, friends,
sinews of Rome, faithful friends of the master (for this man has you as
children and friends, exhorting with words, not compelling with toils), you
see these sea-running places, you see many tame islands round about; they were
a dwelling of fatherly Rome and land of masters, but creeping-in hesitation
and sluggishness showed a land belonging to others, feeding enemies and
deprived of children. Let us go therefore stoutly against the barbarians, let
us take with the sword both their cities and the women and the children of the
children. Let no one fear the casts of the barbarians, let no one fear a
burning wound of battle, let no one the bitter thing, the last harm. For if
someone, having received the baptism stained with blood, is washed into the
blood of the insolent barbarians, he washes the body with the streams of the
bloods and is raised from earth a bright victory-bearer. And if he himself has
children and lots, a father behind, called master, that one will take thought
for him and for the children and for the wife; let him not fear this. Only let
him beat the shield in clashes, only let him stretch a sharp spear eagerly,
only let him appear armed in a fatherly way, only let him not insult the power
of Rome. Having winged the household-servants with these words, the man
faithful in battles, Nikephoros, sent out lions as mountain-nurtured, shaking
all Cretan evildoing, not indeed as a side-work or without a commander; for he
himself in the midst of horsemen, girt with a long-mouthed sharpened sword,
here followed striking in a strange way, and there again throwing fire as a
whip upon houses. And when all were spread plague-wise here and there instead
of wild beasts, it was then to see a pitiable lament and flight and a bitter
wonder of a contrary disaster; for a multitude of men, of women, unhappily
slaughtered, lay in one dust. There the children, hurried toward slaughter,
were falling to earth by the breaking-about of the feet; there old men bowed
with time had a slip, having fallen in the way, and the sword was dipping
crimson hair, which time had as white, dyed; there women were throwing infants
down from their arms to earth, and danger raised up against the
child-nourishers an unlovingness of offspring. Fire also was kindled, the
swords shone back, the stream of the bloods was purpling the earth and was
mixed with the Cretan places, as a new color, a three-colored vengeance.

And when some of the Cretans lay dead as a dinner of birds and taken out of
breath, and others, having fled the death with horse-speed, were being brought
within the gates, the enemy, having stepped within the gates in fear, was
snoring, choked by the flight, and the general, having run out the whole
country and having cut the one part around cuttingly into fire, and having
given the other to the sword and having razed cities, was driving at once into
a turn backward. And having come more quickly within the naval bounds he
scarcely held the army, and having fastened ships he grants them to the land,
bearing them, knowing the wet a faithless benefactress. And remaining there
the circuits of three days, he was preparing the horse and the horseman, and
having surveyed the land in an ambidextrous manner he set a multitude of a
thousand craftsmen and from the earth he rooted a tower-fenced city to guard
the turnings of armies. Homer, come even

again with shame, proclaim the Hellenic wall-makings, which the hand and cast
of Hector overturns, built spider-like for the armies. When therefore some of
the craftsmen were kindling kilns against the stones with fire-blazing
[kilns], and others were suffering turns of counter-windings, throwing rocks
into a pit with the engines, the Cretan serpents, the ridge-runners, having
run out from the ravines, were gathering into one self-killing counsel, and
they were not fortunate to engage the army (for the army was striking them as
a missile), but having attacked the craftsmen sharply, they fall to earth by
the cast of the craftsmen; for your desire as commander was persuading them to
turn the mattock into a sword. And a stream of the bloods poured out comes
into an infinite watered fullness and quenches the fiery kilns, as bitterness
having fallen into a torrent-bed; and the builder, having taken the unslaked
lime, had it crimson-like by the composition of the stones. Call, general, the
city Phoenicia; for it is made crimson by the dye of the bloods. When
therefore Nikephoros, the one holding of generals, knew what had happened (for
he was ignorant then), having sent he seized alive from the barbarians evil
serpents, five fifties, and having thrown collars onto unbending necks, he was
furnishing them fatherly honors. Crete, child-nurse of barbarians, look of
what dignities they have suddenly partaken. And when night had the seventh
portion (for the darkness of the well-minded night was prevailing), they
raised war-cries with trumpets in a strange shout and everyone holding the
shield was with a shout, foot-soldier, general, taxiarch, horseman. And when
the day of rose-colored lamp had kindled back from the dark passages, a
horse-phalanx sword-bearing was setting out, having a multitude of a
ten-thousand-man pentad. And of many foot-soldiers there was to see a panoply,
moving most excellently with tactical step, and the generals walking in the
midst as in a flock, shepherds of lambs, beast-revelers. And as rapacious
wolves rejoicing in theft, in the season of winter and the stretching-out of
frost, yoked together of the mountains of one mind, shake folds and force
dogs, so yoked the branches of your toils, horsemen, army-leaders, archers,
company-holders, were hastening to throw towers down in speed, breathing wrath
as locusts of a squall. And he who at times raises and again lowers the
light-bringing ill-colored lamps with baths dimmed the gleamings, and night
spread the robe from a dark-colored dye into all parts of earth and of the
vault. And a multitude of yours, not of armies or of wild beasts (for these
are beasts by the tearings of barbarians), had sleepless the step and the
standing. And if someone had been stolen into uncaring sleeps, standing by
there from the gate of dreams you were opening such things of the harm,
saying: Why, not fearing the general, horseman, have you fallen in the midst
of slaughter into strange places, where a rout, a whetted danger, breathes
fire-casting inflammations against us? Endure a little, having put on a
helmet; now you stand for God and for justice. Leave sleep and hold the
shield, lest the general of the battle know what has happened. Thus you were
cutting out the sluggishness of a worker, if indeed any sluggishness was
present to the household-servants.

And when day, darkness-destroying, began also to foretell light with white
tunics, and dressed the aether with bright robes, it was to behold earth
white-formed and a herald of battle of your armies to run out, and the phalanx
to scrape against a long wall, having fallen suddenly upon the adversaries.
There the general, having ordered the whole multitude of the screen-bearers to
snatch up cuttingly, the fellow-commanders of the bitter pain did not bear
what happened, but opened the doors, flew out instead of sparrows and, having
struck toward a trench, were going against the trench by a counter-rebound;
but having been struck in front they were driving out backward. When therefore
there was of flight (for a terrible stamp of trembling and cowardice and a
swift-turning turmoil), this trench of the adversaries was a tomb and an
unroofed house of disaster. For they themselves had foot-entanglers and
places, which they were preparing unto salvation, that they might learn from
unfeeling places their faith worthy

of insults. What prophet, found before the battle, would have said to the
trench-diggers of the barbarians that they were digging out a tomb, not a
trench, for their children? Invincible Crete, do not groan at drought,
receiving the children as clouds in the battle, in which the spear of Rome,
having come on as a breath and having blown, as some whirling, released the
slaughters, rainstorms of much-flowing bloods. Demosthenes, Philip has not
power; Plutarch, Caesar has not freedom of speech; Dio, Sulla is a
people-ruler in vain. Leave off, Xenophon, writing the unconquered; marvel at
Romanos and write him only. Cyzicus, lament together, you who have suffered
disasters of equal power with burned Crete; for to you old Mithridates weaves
the slaughters, and a new Romanos weaves slaughters for this one. But O
general of the whole earth (for it is fitting to say what is coming), do not
envy the household-servant applauding your victory-composed battles, but make
the tongue distinct, give freedom of speech; for the stings of daringlessness
strike us. Do not overlook the toils of Theodosius written together unto the
glory of your crown, that a hand hurried to write may walk toward a second
panoply of your army.

II. The Cretan wrestlings being thus, beauty of Rome, Romanos scepter-holder,
some were suffering badly in strange places, armed against heat and sword (for
the light-bringer was sending the flame-hot lamps like missiles to your
household-servants), and you yourself had heat and sword, the cares, through
them within in the heart. When therefore the fire of sleeplessness, burning
you, was making your starry appearance pale, those looking did not have to say
a cause how your brightness was turned to gloom, until, having groaned
toilfully from the inwards, you released flame-burnings of the torches below,
saying the matter to all the subjects; for a multitude of fatherly armies,
slipped in the like-sent toils, was striking you, but a household-servant
leading the soul was not present to you; for to all there was ignorance of the
things done. But God more quickly refashioned your affliction into good cheer,
who at once shows you the victory-bearing messenger from the one girt with a
sword in a slavely way. And when you knew the mind of the things written (for
he had a sealed letter), you did not strike the lyre, as David of old, or make
a tongue distinct instead of the lyre; but at once the phalanx of the
single-wayed (for this phalanx even without a sword turns, bends the backs and
overturns cities), this you were applauding mystically against enemies and you
have given all-night thanksgivings, having run to the dwelling of the
fellow-general. Such things you, toiling together with the household-servants;
and when the mouth of punishment had been filled (for the bitterness of the
slaughter is punishment to enemies), the general of the battle, knowing the
trench having cliffs and ravine-like places, ordered to throw rocks and earth
and timber, that the tortoises might journey easily. There the multitude of
the commands had the work spoken, but the ordered casts of the adversaries
were furnishing clouds. Air, speak, bear witness, do not hesitate, the woven
woof of your tunic, in which having been dressed you bent back harm, woven
from the unclear disasters, through which the rapacious dogs having the brow
were falling down from the battlements.

And when therefore until eight days the rainstorm of the torrent-bed was not
checked, but the quiver and missile had time, he released rams and
tortoise-arts and stone-sending slings and terrible compositions of well-bound
ladders against them. When therefore the camp-dwellers, not bearing those new
vengeances, were at a loss, Nikephoros, the household-servant longed-for by
you, arrays the crowd and again weaves punishments; for of the fallen foes he
ordered to send the heads with the slings like missiles, that the
fine-threaded breadth of aether might rejoice, defiled by the disaster of the
barbarians, and that the multitude might know of the sin, that even of the
limbs they have a placing unto punishment. They were throwing within

the heads instead of rocks, they were casting often both their fathers and the
brothers; and one having died in battle was a murderer by fatherly
murder-work; for that one, loosed from the prevailing fleshly blindness and
having learned the expedient, being hurried to fight with the
Christ-worshippers, was a punishment-bearing cast to the false-worshippers.
When therefore the city had those fleshes full of defilements, those of the
same blood by race were crying thus with the shout of the barbarians: Seeph
echeimat ischarop and the rhasan sermet midene and chaet iphesane. But the
shout was by the fatherly custom, and the slaughter found for you among
Cretans. There was then the one holding of their generalship, an old emir,
pale, full of toils, small, bald, terrible-minded, conquering the old man from
Pylos in honey-flowing words; but the words helped him nothing, even if they
were straight, to attack your power. Nevertheless he was daring (for he was a
commander), feeding himself in empty hopes, and he makes a counsel and calls
the old men like-sent in time in a sitting-together. Having sat there and
groaned from depth, he released a voice mixed with the toils: Who of you, men,
has fire in the heart burning, flaming as the phalanx of Rome burns me,
shaking my toparchy? Whose children have received the last harm from the
whetted blade? Whose field does the boiling fire coming on, the bitter
vengeance, devour out? Let that one now counsel with us, giving a best
judgment instead of the strength. But if it seems good, let us set
horsemen-men, thrice seven fifties to those who cast; then hexads of foot,
thirty thousand, all iron, dark-colored to the sight, that they may strike the
adversaries, having appeared to them from the gates beyond hope. Thus he who
before was spread out boldly was, haranguing, unhappily squeezed. And indeed
having levied and phalanxed them all, the dragon was gathering serpents from
the holes; and knowing all to walk together into roads of cowardice or
choosing slaughter, standing he was urging on the evil side-workers: Let us
hasten, horsemen-men, in the luxury of the now-appeared battle of ten thousand
swords. Let us go to the sword instead of the children, on behalf of women and
friends and fatherland. Let no one fear the cuttings of the blades; for if
someone dies, he is saved at once and will go living and receive a dwelling,
where a prophet knows to speak of winter-streams of milk, to flow from honey,
from wine. But if again, having feared, he does not die on behalf of children,
and runs past the words of the prophet, he will die from us and, having passed
from the darkness, will receive the unforeseen vengeance. Thus exhorting and
urging on to battle, he was making the road beforehand for the slaughter to
those worthy.

And the general holding, Nikephoros, did not have time to be ignorant of the
arts of the deceiver, which, dragging aside, he was weaving as turn-works; for
there was someone signaling the roads beforehand to him from those shut within
unseemly. Thus he joined together into one army-leader men, straight horsemen,
phalanx-saviors, foot-archer-holders, together with the commanders; and
standing in the midst of these well-equipped as a horseman he released most
armed words: Sinews of Rome, children, fellow-servants, friends, behold the
beast has begun again to beget devices from within against us; behold the
terrible mouth of Charybdis rages to gulp back the power of Rome; behold the
dragon in the midst of a hole breathes hissings, heralds of wrath and
slaughter; he blows the venom and forces nature; but his boldness is
cowardice. Let no one, having feared, flee backward; for wrath is to him as
far as the beats only. Let us go therefore stoutly armed; the children of
Hagar also have fleshes. Flesh is not iron or stone to them; they feel pain,
if they receive, more than Romans, they are idle, if their hands are cut, they
fall to earth, if the feet are cut. Let no one sicken with the bitter
unmanliness, let no one, having feared, not appear a branch of Rome, let no
one be eager to show himself in evils a bastard offspring and a strange
sowing. Shame, brothers, the penalty of flight; be ashamed to flee and to be
scared at the swords. For where shall salvation be found for those who have
fled, if they remain within Cretan bounds? There is not

strength of Hagar to the descendants; for God fights with us and runs with us.
Having thus whetted the swords of your army, you were making ambushes and a
feigned rout, that, stolen from within wall-works, they might take heart to
have gone out from outside. When therefore the time of counsels drew near, the
rose-colored day having the third hour, they were setting out from holes as
serpents, those having the heads shaved as a reproach. Of these there was both
a beating on the shields, and much shout and fear father of trembling and all
terrible things, but to your commanders order, silence, gravity, eagerness.
One then of generals, a branch of a well-born root, a man mighty unto the
turning of a javelin, slow fleeing and swift running down, spoke sweet words
in the midst in the army: First-general, how, having left the master, have you
not, as a cook with the vulgar art, mixing, mixed this in exhortation, that
the nectar, the sweet crown-bearer, might become a seasoning to your commands?
That one, admonishing us, permitting, stretching out the hand to all, was
doing good. Let us remember his honey-flowing sayings and the tables, then the
gifts. Remember and let us die, men, unto what is due on behalf of the race of
a most pious lord; only let the scepters of the monarchy be widened and let
Romanos alone rule all earth. And when a ten-thousand-man multitude of
barbarians rushed from the gates as a cloud of the South, threatening a roar
and showing seed beforehand, yours, having turned to a feigned flight, were
devising the Cretan-killing trap. And all having gone out a long tower and
having rushed from outside of the gates, they were undergoing the worthy
punishments of faith. For from four hiding-places crosswise a wave having been
raised and having crashed on strongly, began to beget a storm before the
battle, and of foes there was disaster, turmoil, groaning, and of your peoples
a victory-bearing joy. When therefore they were standing in the mid-space,
seeing the way out narrowed, those responsible for their own murder-work, they
were casting at once, they were being cast against more, and having the flight
at a loss (for everywhere there was a troubling harm to them) they stood into
one, the place of the burial. Who, making a tragedy, will cry out the
slaughters and the vengeance of the slaughter of each? For one of them, having
been struck in the kidneys, was suffering turnings-about, which he did not
know, and not having learned before the bendings of dancers, he was an
unwilling dancer, having received with a javelin; another, deprived of the
shorn head, lay prone spread crosswise and unhappily moving his own feet
wanted to swim in a pouring of the bloods; and another, struck in the
dried-down spine, bending, was unwillingly doing obeisance to your fleet; and
another, dead and with feet cut, forgotten, lay out of the horsemanships; and
another, having been thrust in the unsalted belly, was holding with hands the
weaving of the entrails, and before judged a stout fighter, he was an
unskilled cook, an entrails-seller. Thus the children of Hagar exchanged the
panoply into other-arted disasters, and every one dying had the vengeance, an
evil art unto destruction of life. One then of those was a Cretan
horseman-man, giant-formed, who, having taken the shield and the sword, was
driving opposite to the general of the battle, wanting to attack. But justice
did not permit this one to arrive; for Nikephoros the holder strikes him from
below the loin, in the midst of the navel, and the dust had the dead body of
the one, and the broad fleet the greatest glory of the other. Thus badly were
suffering, shut in here and there, justly by the murderers, the servants of
lawless deeds. And if one of them had been set in motion unto flight, he had
in front a toilsome sword as a fence and the general Nikephoros everywhere
casting and crying to the commanders: let us cast, men, the life-destroying
wolves. Homer, spring of the words, who have been born far from the battle,
but writing together as present, who toil much in empty things and go through
all the toils of Hellenes in vain, you come and cry with a bulk of sayings the
boast of Rome's army and a great groaning of old enemies, foes, slave-sown;
for you will have the truth as a type for us, as for the Achaeans the
digression as a beat; for there was not where someone

having fled from the battle, not even if a barbarian had a winged nature.
Xerxes, who before wanted to make earth sea, has honor by not having attacked
your power; this one did not see bare swords of Romans, he did not see your
scepter-holding, Romanos; for he would have passed by, having marveled at your
army, having taken on the defeat in you, scepter-holder.

III. He who closes, bridles, opens out, leads out the many-mouthed pourings of
the abyss, this one widens the bounds of your power, son of a lord, and
strengthens the fleet and does all things and overturns cities. And who of the
speech-writers, lord, will have strength to praise you according to worth unto
so much, even if there is another among the Stageirites, Plato, or Demosthenes
the holder of the Athenians, or he who strikes the much-famed lyre? I at
least, of your encomia in a slavely way, the speech-writer of the present day,
begin, but the stings of daringlessness prick and force the speech. But you
give the daring, open out the mouth, teach us not to be silent easily, when
there is so great a disaster to the barbarians; widen the throat with freedom
of speech; for it is narrowed, having attacked your toils. A hand is in agony,
a mind is often barred, and it shrinks and is scared and, wanting to walk in
your Christ-imitating ways, as a hare having feared, it runs past the roads.
For you, having acquired the conquering everywhere, have benefited the common,
have increased it alone; you with toils have thrust the phalanx of the
adversaries into a ravine of hard-to-pass places; you have filled the cities
not of spoils only, but you have filled them of peace and of much glory. Let a
rank of angels above rejoice, let a system of mortals below dance; not
Heracles saved Phrygians from the sea-monster, but the master the world from
murder-work; not Meleager the fatherland from the fire, but Romanos now the
whole creation. What grace of old to your children, Aloeus, to craft a great
bronze jar of Ares? For not a stepmother, but the master, having loosed a jar,
moves this against Cretan enemies; for it was not likely, sun-star of a crown,
your scepters having made bright in monarchy, that that one be narrowed and
remain in a prison, but either with him you hold the shield and defile earth
with bloods of the barbarians. And when the phalanx of Cretans was
slaughtered, as a hare fallen among hunters was taken away, earth was
delivered from many disasters, and was washed from countless defilements, and
every Scythian lay dead upon the ground, longed-for by dogs or by women and
children. Yourself, general, being ignorant of the outcomes of all these,
again were gathering the cares within as an army of the heart, until the
writing more quickly came on from subjects, filled of much joy. And having
learned all and having beaten the hands, you have given toward God such a
prayer as nature does not have to say, but perhaps, lord, that of the prophet:
crying great, Lord, my Lord, do not be silent at all; for if you should be
silent, I am one of those walking in a pit, gloomy, full of labors. Hear our
cheap wailings, and, guarding your flock saved, turn the enemies and make the
toils cease, which your army, enduring, greets. Such things you, commanding
together with household-servants even not being present, were doing, as Moses
of old the routs of Amalek by the stretching-out of the hands; and your army,
conquering in the successive battles, was lifted unto a brow. It needed then a
well-mixed drug unto chastening of the disease that had stood upon it. And
night now, the gloomy, being spread, and the army rejoicing or boasting and
hastening toward a stay or being called, every place was as on a razor
sword-bearing, hills, glens, ravines, straight valleys; for a seven-signed
fleet, having poured out from the Cretan dens, prepared beforehand, was
furnishing much cowardice to the army, having peeped suddenly and appeared
beyond hope. And night was dressing darkness in light (for a breastplate as
light and a sword and a helmet were changing the dye of evening), and the
generals, bent toward flight, were setting out covering necks with the
shields, and every one fleeing had as

a benefactress the sea itself and the hulls of the boats. For there was not
strength to fight both with night and with the barbarians and to slaughter
again, unless you yourself (a wonder and this a strange hearing), standing a
hot horseman to all from the unclear, as it seems, horsemanships, were an
exhorter and a striking-down alone and more quickly were setting in motion
from flight toward the battle those struck out of their wits by the hidden
battle. When therefore, having turned to unhoped-for forces, they were simply
stretching sharpened swords, a shield sounded, the helmet beat, the night
shone toiling together with you then and every Cretan side-worker lay dead
into blood and taken out of breath. Night then did not have the darkness as a
starting-point in so great a disaster of those of Hagar; it was rivaling the

Those then who fled this bitter battle I do not say as far as four or even
ten; so many they were of the last fortune, from which the Cretans were then

fleshes, being shut, is deprived of common foods, he who was not filled in
streams of the bloods, melted, thirsts for a drop of water. Thus badly they
have it who by an evil deed have thrust the thrice-lighted power of the
Godhead from their own heart. When therefore he was fenced within, trusting a
wall, not strength of armies, your general Nikephoros again permits with rams,
tortoises, slings to strike the wall, the bases of battlements, those toward
the wall to do all things with eagerness, to complete and to accomplish
against the adversaries. This one speaking after the manner of the
nightingale, a measure of a perceiving pentad was toiling; for a spring of
tears was flowing of the eyes, fire of smell, a sound from the ears, and the
mouth was sending out springs of words and a hand was gushing streams of many
bloods, suffering from the turning of the javelins. Thus that one, that he

heart, except he had a time in the way of the affairs, striking this one
instead of countless toils. He was then walking, he was running up and down,
weeping, exhorting and urging on the fleet; for he was ashamed of the toils of
your power † lest hesitation, having looked aside vainly through him, be
refashioned among the lawless Cretans. † And the general of sling-turners,
lord, does a thing worthy of laughter and more; for having thrown a sluggish
donkey into a sling he orders to cast a donkey living to the donkeys. And
they, standing by the weaving of the tightenings, send the wretched
air-runner. And he was preparing beforehand, was spreading feet, a rustic
donkey was going forward into air. He who before was humble in a mid-air step,
the sluggish and tortoise-footed donkey on earth, running on clouds was then
striking the Cretans. Xerxes was showing earth water, the new thing of old,

strike me out of my wits and perhaps they fame most things worthily; except
did he overpower or overturn cities, for you might say many, but such a one
when? There was not another lord of Rome of old, there was not another like
the famed master. I have read many books of many historians, Plutarch raising

the wise lyre; I did not find one bright in generalships as you, I did not
find another second commander, I did not find another Romanos a
counsel-bearer, I did not find another, not even him of Philip, whom Plutarch
has first in battles. So much have you been raised by the cooperation of God,
so much have you overpowered others in battles, so much have you struck
enemies out of their wits with a sword.

IV. Now, mighty one, light, lord of the inhabited world, your army toiling in
a standing-by with a weight of iron and with labor and with care, with much
winter and with unyielding frosts, the first dogs of the Cretans from outside,
the toparchs of earth and phalanx-leaders then (for they themselves were
dwelling cliffs and ravine-like places), having been driven out from there
with a sword, struck by an onset and by a cast of the frost and not having
given up their own times, together against us (for unto fellow-dwellers) they
rushed from the mountains unto one slaughter. As therefore the hill-nurtured
wild race of goats, of hares and of humble deer, left of food in a bitter
winter, from the soul-freezing places above goes forward to hollow and walks
in districts, seeking food and herb-like places, so the cheap children of sin
were running to the wave-receiving places of the sea, to shore shelters and
valleys. And as the greedy race among wild beasts of meats, wolves, hastening
to find a satiety of belly, go with wrath and an emptying of the hollow,
gaping a mouth stretched out before they arrive, so the tribe of the
slave-sown there, forced by your flocks, shepherd-ruler, rose up against and
whetted a sword, eager to gulp fleshes instead of foods, and wanting to drink
hot blood of Romans and first to take brightly the powers of the battle. These
barbarians had as leader Karamuntes, the slaughter-wealthy old man, the new
dragon of the old days, who had in mind to hold the reins of Cretans the whole
time of life. This one was then preparing beforehand to throw in a multitude
of a tenfold thousand unto slaughter. And when he saw all from the mountains
below, sword-bearing, horse-like, foot-drivers, standing he was exhorting and
urging on toward swords, setting phalanxes and new phalanx-leaders, fencing
the standings from outside with circles, saying such things and

crying to the barbarians: Much force there is to us, men, much disturbance and
a hurricane and toil, but there is to us more quickly the salvation, only let
us want, found with hands. Let us go toward enemies, let us not fear swords,
let us stand against them, let us not be scared at a spear, and having thrust
all into the sea, we shall set here a people-killing trophy. And all at once
said as one mouth: Let us die, if it is needed, today together of fathers, and
you give eagerness, as is possible, to all; for you are a father and a tower
of us in battles. The light tribe, winged without wings by these, rushed to
attack your fleet, an evil people, hostile, blood-gulping, an evil tribe of
slave-sown Ishmael. Such things the Cretans who had fled were doing in a
squall of so great a force, those who many times had run into battle and many
times been struck from your fleet. And what your army? Some were lying in the
sea places (for there was frost, the North blowing with ill-practicing
inroads); and those holding the ways out of the gates were striking those
within out of their wits in a standing-by. And having learned the phalanx of
the adversaries walking in hollow places, and as a beast breathing wrath, but
also cowardice, a ruling general in battle, Nikephoros, held this unbelievable
and at once arrays the whole tagma of the Thrakesians to learn the matter; for
this was strange, in so great a disaster to weave wiles, those cut out seven
times by your fleet. To these a general was a man loving the sword on behalf
of you and stretching it forward unto battle and not fearing the cutting of
the blades, who even before, having fallen upon an insatiable race with
greatest courage and strange eagerness and having given himself unto slaughter
most alone and having thrown into confusion the unhorsed barbarians. Who,
alas, having been overpowered by the rout of Macedonians and having been held
a prisoner toward a strange land, had the defiling ones as bitter masters.
This one, sent from there into a first ambush, by a love of wounds, by a
boiling-over of heart, found the common end of barbarians in the midst, having
thrown the tetrad of the limbs as leaves. For as a greatest and much-timed
wolf, being experienced and a robber and a flock-driver, hungering, sends
himself forward to the stay of the flocks with courage and tears dogs, until
he is overpowered and slaughtered by the men, who many times roared great
against him, so that commander, having fallen, a mighty one of Rome and left
alone into the midst, showed what kind of men Rome nourishes. For many having
been fenced around him, all were scared at the common of the end; for he had
the sword as a thunderbolt in hands, moving it against enemies and staining it
with slaughters, with which he was cutting out some of the adversaries, was
compelling others to flee trembling, others, persuading them to cry you God
unto battle, he was showing dead and mixed with dust. Nevertheless, having
been struck and having fallen in strange places, he furnished many toils to
the army. And indeed horsemen, foot-runners, having run, shut all those cliffs
and ravine-like places and closed them in the midst and made Cretans a food of
birds. So much have you yourself been fortunate in battle, so much have you
yourself overpowered barbarians from the inclination above and cooperation.
And Karamuntes, the ill-old in times, having seen this and having feared at
once, throws the bulk of the thought down, and uses flight on foot, but also
alone, and runs to a small hole roofed with rock, and acquires from there an
unhappy salvation. It was needed, old man, that you die before many times
rather than be shaken and slip into rocks, as a hare having fled hunters.
Having sat there until settings of sun he was sprinkling the face from the
tears, weeping the bitter wound of ill-counsel, throwing himself to
hard-to-wrestle cares. And having risen from the first night, he was driving
to the inner cliffs of the mountains in agony, an old emir, on foot, but
four-footed; for he had a force, which was strengthening his hands unto a
service of feet. And your fleet, all-greatest Romanos, having turned, was
striking walls with eagerness, rejoicing, pulling these down, throwing them to
the ground.

V. All earth of Rome and sea and city, hills, ravines, stars, water of

together with the master. Let a multitude of stars above rejoice, and let a
rank of angels dance, because the hard-to-approach beast has been slaughtered,
the terrible-formed sea-monster has been taken away against, the hard-to-hold
wound of earth has been checked. Herald, aether, this into all ground, cry,
star sun, running above, to the barbarians now the slaughter of the
barbarians. Let nations and cities and districts know the new power of the
greatest Romanos, let them learn his sleepless toils, such as the crown-bearer
had through us; let them take experience of him from the neighbors, before the
slaughterer runs up toward them. Sword-bearers of the African land, shudder,

receive by the spear of mighty Romanos, except in speed. The places of the
Delemites Belemites your missile will persuade to be called, scepter-holder.

fleshes. Chaudas, wanting to take all earth greedily, remains gaping
speechless by a disaster of Cretans. O Christ, child of the Father before
ages, what glory you have furnished to the crown-bearer. Who would groan the
tomb of Polyxena, who the capture of the Phrygians? O chorus of youths, as
many as have a care of the thoughts of Homer, do not be stolen by the words of
