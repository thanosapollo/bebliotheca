* On Saint Thecla the Martyr
:PROPERTIES:

Encomium on the holy proto-martyr and apostle Thecla.
=50.745=
The grace of the Spirit has raised up for us the yearly memorials of the
saints as fair images, and it keeps forever young the deeds that time would
send into forgetfulness. For each person, coming upon the memorial as upon a
kind of image, sees as in a mirror the beauty of the deeds, and beholds those
deeds lying within the image of memory. And I seem to myself today to behold
that blessed girl as in a mirror, standing as upon the image of the memorial,
stretching forth with one hand the crown against pleasures, with the other the
crown against dangers, and offering with one her virginity, with the other her
martyrdom, to the Master of all. She had, then, the life of virginity as well,
which to those who look closely is a great martyrdom before martyrdom.
For pleasures are certain terrible executioners of the body; or rather, more
terrible than executioners. They rack a person with bonds not made by hands.
They scrape the soul through the eyes. They bring through the ears the torch
of love of pleasure against the unyielding will. They scourge the mind with a
sharp lash. They stir up ranks upon ranks against us. When someone commands
the eyes to be blind toward beauties, pleasures pry open what was shut,
loosening it through the ears with shameless =50.746= songs. When they meet
ears closed against songs of fornication, they sport with the thoughts, having
adorned themselves lewdly. When, to speak briefly, we have beaten them on
every side while awake, they fight us as shadows while we sleep. And this
circuit of their battle-line is everlasting: it does not rise with the sun,
nor rest with the night. But if they also lay hold of youth, it becomes a
grappling of fire with fire, a joining of furnace to straw. For youth is
easily set ablaze by pleasures, being oilier for kindling, and more tender
toward the labors of chastity.
All these things made virginity a long martyrdom for the blessed virgin:
boxing with pleasures as the martyr does with beasts; wrestling with thoughts
as he does with tortures; joining battle with notions of love of pleasure as
with the constraints of executioners; yet conquering all the same that
many-shaped war which lived under her roof. For the fire of her resolve was
hotter than the fire of nature, and holy. But nature yielded to the girl;
though it plays the tyrant among others and rages toward coupling, with Thecla
it trained itself in virginity. The parents, not knowing the child's covenants
toward virginity, nor that the Master Christ had given from heaven the leading
of her as a bride =50.747=, spun out many admonitions to her about marriage.
But Paul's charm had already been keeping sober watch in the girl's ears: "The
unmarried woman cares for the things of the Lord, that she may be holy both in
body and in spirit." A crown of virginity at once. That very kind of care was
the first thing in her. For she cares a care filled with blessing; she cares a
thought freer than all freedom from care, that she may be holy both in body
and in spirit. She has nothing in common with the earth, no kinship with the
necessities of marriage: to put up with a fornicating bridegroom, to bear
empty suspicions, to be stripped of the right of useful going out, to be
called to account about food, to be envied when she adorns herself, to be spat
upon before she bears a child as if she had not yet become a wife, and after
bearing to find accusations over the births.
A girl was born? The husband is sullen because it was not male. A male was
added too? The child that was born is treated as not well formed. Both
charming in beauty? Then the anxiety over the beautiful ones is more bitter.
Weaned from milk? The struggle of rearing takes over again. When they are
well, fear that they may fall sick. When they fall sick, dread that they may
die. When they die, fear of being despised thereafter as childless. When they
do not die, the care over the living is heavier still: from where the
provision for the children's education, from where the preparation of a
marriage contract for them, from where the decency of clothing, from where a
share of servants for each, which of the goods he should allot to the greater,
how he will soothe the lesser who envies. "The unmarried woman cares for the
things of the Lord, that she may be holy both in body and in spirit." I do not
publicly denounce the nature of marriage; for this is the common providence of
the race. But I make public the care that is in marriage. I set a heavenly
care before a fleshly care. I honor the fairer above the fair. The virgin is
stronger even than the penalties against Eve. For "Your turning shall be to
your husband, and he shall rule over you" has no force for those not mastered
by men. "In pains you shall bear children" is idle for those who keep
virginity. For she who does not bear is outside the sentence of the painful
birth-pangs. Already, O virgin, you revel in the pledge of the things to come.
Already you dwell with the holiness of the resurrection.
For in the =50.748= resurrection, he says, they neither marry nor are given in
marriage. Therefore the fall of the matter is also exceeding great, since the
achievement is great too. Therefore the wantonness of her who keeps virginity
is more defiling than that of her who fornicates. For the corruption of a
virgin and of a fornicatress is not equal. The corruption of a private woman
and of a queen is not equal. The theft of a priestly vessel and of a
marketplace one is not equal. For the virgin is a priestly vessel, a purple
robe which it is not lawful for any other than the King of all to have fitted
to himself, a bride who always possesses a virgin marriage. O blessed
marriages, whose bedding is virginity. For these things were the dangers of
the blessed martyr. For she saw the beauty of the Bridegroom and was not torn
away from the sight. A mother pressed upon her, urging her toward marriage,
but she cried out to the Bridegroom in the heavens: "To you I have lifted my
eyes, who dwell in heaven." A suitor kept coming, tickling her with talk of
marriage, but she ranged herself with Christ in her own heart, saying, "My
soul has clung after you." Kinsfolk streamed together, flattering, but she
pictured Paul bearing witness: "I have betrothed you to one husband, to
present a chaste virgin to Christ." Household servants begged with tears, but
she sang in love to the Bridegroom: "Who shall separate us from the love of
Christ?" Judges frightened her with punishments, but she trampled them all
under a shouting resolve: "Rulers are not a terror to good works, but to
evil."
And since it was needful that monuments of virginity be raised for the martyr
even along the roads, some such trial befalls the girl. Released from the
judgment she went hunting after Paul, and guided by reports she even dared the
roads that led to Paul. But the devil, as it happened, was watching the girl,
and having watched her traveling he marches the suitor against her, a robber
of virginity as in a desert. And as the noble girl was finishing the road, the
suitor, a mounted wanton, ogling from behind, howled for her capture. Helpless
constraint from every side. The attacker strong, the one attacked frail. Where
in a desert can one flee for refuge? But the virgin, turning toward heaven,
toward him who is present everywhere to all who call, wailed with
lamentations: "O Lord my God, in you I have hoped." ***
