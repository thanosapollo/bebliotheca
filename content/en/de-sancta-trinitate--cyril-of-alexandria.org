And his incomprehensible generation has neither beginning nor end. And it is
without beginning because of the unchangeable; and without flux, because of
the impassible and the incorporeal. And apart from conjunction, both because
of the incorporeal again and because there is =77.1136= one God only, who has
no need of another; and without end and without cessation, both because of the
without-beginning and the timeless, and the endless, and that which is always
the same. For that which is without beginning is without end; but that which
is without end by grace is not in every way without beginning, as the angels
are. The God who always is, therefore, begets his own Word, who is perfect,
without beginning and without end, lest God should bring forth in time, he who
has a nature and existence superior to time. But

corruption and flux and increase, and clothed with a body, and possessing in
his nature the male and the female. For the male is in need of the help of the
female. But may he be gracious who is beyond all things and who lies above
every thought and comprehension.

CHAPTER 8.
The holy catholic and apostolic Church, then, teaches at once the Father and
at once his only-begotten Son, begotten of him timelessly, and without flux,
and impassibly, and incomprehensibly, as only the God of all things knows,
just as together the fire and together the light from it; and not first the
fire, and after this the light, but together. And as the light, being always
begotten from the fire, is always in it, being in no way separated from it: so
also the Son is begotten of the Father, being in no way separated from him,
but always being in him.
But the light, being begotten from the fire inseparably, and always remaining
in it, does not have a hypostasis of its own besides the fire (for it is a
natural quality of the fire); but the only-begotten Son of God, begotten of
the Father inseparably and without interval and always remaining in him, has a
hypostasis of his own besides that of the Father. He is called Word, then, and
radiance, because he has been begotten of the Father without conjunction and
impassibly and timelessly and without flux and inseparably; and Son and
impress of the Father's hypostasis, because he is perfect and enhypostatic,
and like the Father in all things, except the unbegottenness. And
only-begotten, because he alone was begotten of the Father alone, in a unique
way. For no other generation is like the generation of the Son of God; for
there is no other Son of God. For even if the Holy Spirit proceeds from the
Father, it is not by way of generation, but by way of procession. This is
another mode of existence, both incomprehensible and unknown, just as also the
generation of the Son. Therefore also all things that the Father has are his,
except the unbegottenness; which does not signify a difference of essence, nor
a rank, but a mode of existence. Just as also Adam, being unbegotten (for he
is a formation of God), and Seth, being begotten (for he is a son of Adam),
and Eve, who proceeded from the side of Adam (for she was not begotten), do
not differ from one another in nature (for they are human beings), but in the
mode of existence. For one must know that ageneton, written with one n,
=77.1137= signifies the uncreated, that is, that which has not come to be; but
agenneton, written with the two nn, declares that which has not been begotten.
According to the first sense, then, essence differs from essence. For one
essence is the uncreated, that is unoriginate, written with the one n, and
another is the originate, that is created. But according to the second
sense, essence does not differ from essence. For of every kind of living
things the first hypostasis is unbegotten, but not uncreated. For they were
created by the Creator, being brought into generation by his Word, yet they
were not begotten, there not being another of the same kind pre-existing from
whom they might be begotten. According to the first sense, then, the three
super-divine hypostases of the holy Godhead share (for they are consubstantial
and uncreated); but according to the second sense, not at all. For the Father
alone is unbegotten (for his being is not from another hypostasis); and the
Son alone is begotten (for he has been begotten of the essence of the Father
without beginning and timelessly); and the Holy Spirit alone is proceeding
from the essence of the Father; not being begotten, but proceeding; the divine
Scripture teaching thus, while the mode of the generation and of the
procession remains incomprehensible.


And this too one must know, that the name of fatherhood and of sonship and of
procession was not transferred from us to the blessed Godhead; but on the
contrary it has been imparted to us from there, as the divine Apostle says:
For this cause I bow my knees to the Father, from whom every family in heaven
and on earth. And if we say that the Father is beginning of the Son and
greater, we do not suggest that he is prior to the Son in time or in nature;
for through him he made the ages; nor in any other respect, except according
to the cause, that is, that the Son was begotten of the Father, and not the
Father of the Son; and that the Father is cause of the Son naturally; just as
we do not say that the fire comes forth from the light, but the light from the
fire. When therefore we hear that the Father is beginning and greater than the
Son, we shall understand it of the cause. And as we do not say that the fire
is of one essence, and the light of another: so it is not possible to say that
the Father is of one essence, and the Son of another, but of one and the same.
And as we say that the fire shines through the light that comes forth from it,
and we do not set the light from it as a ministerial instrument of the fire,
but rather as a natural power: so we say that the Father does all things that
he does through his own Son of the same kind; not as through a
liturgical instrument, but as through a natural and enhypostatic power. And as
we say that the fire gives light, and again we say that the light of the fire
gives light: so all things that the Father does, the Son likewise does. But
the light has not acquired a hypostasis of its own besides the fire; but the
Son is a perfect hypostasis, inseparable from the Father's hypostasis, as we
showed above. For it is impossible to find in creation =77.1140= an image that
exhibits in itself without variation the mode of the Holy Trinity. For how
shall that which is created, and composite, and flowing, and changeable, and
circumscribed, and having shape, and corruptible, clearly declare the
superessential divine essence that is freed from all these? And all creation,
it is clear, is bound by most of these, and all, according to its own nature,
is subject to corruption.

CHAPTER 9.
Likewise we believe also in one Holy Spirit, the Lord and life-giver, who
proceeds from the Father, and rests in the Son, who is worshipped and
glorified together with Father and Son, as consubstantial and co-eternal, the
Spirit of God, the upright, the princely, the fountain of life and of
sanctification, being and being named God with Father and Son, uncreated,
full, creative, almighty, working all things, all-powerful, of infinite power,
mastering all creation, not mastered; deifying, not deified; filling, not
filled; participated in, not participating; sanctifying, not sanctified;
Paraclete, as receiving the supplications of all; like the Father and the Son
in all things, proceeding from the Father, and through the Son distributed and
partaken of by all creation; and through him creating and giving essence to
all things, and sanctifying and holding them together; enhypostatic, that is,
existing in his own hypostasis, inseparable from and not departing from Father
and Son; and having all things that the Father and the Son have, except the
unbegottenness and the generation. For the Father is without cause and
unbegotten; for he is not from anyone (for he has his being from himself), nor
does he have any of the things that he has from another; but he himself rather
is beginning and cause of being and of being in such a manner naturally for
all. And the Son is from the Father by generation. And the Holy Spirit is
himself also from the Father, but not by generation, but by procession. And
that there is a difference of generation and procession, we have learned; what


the mode of the difference is, in no way. And together also is the generation
of the Son from the Father, and the procession of the Holy Spirit. All things
therefore that the Son and the Spirit have, they have from the Father; even
being itself. And if the Father is not, neither is the Son, nor the Spirit.
And if the Father has not something, neither does the Son have it, nor the
Spirit; and because of the Father, that is, because the Father is, the Son and
the Spirit are; and because of the Father the Son and the Spirit have all
things that they have, that is, because the Father has them, except the
unbegottenness and the generation, and the procession. For in these hypostatic
properties alone the three holy hypostases differ from one another; not in
essence, but being divided undividedly by the characterizing mark of their own
hypostasis. And we say that each of the three has a perfect hypostasis (lest
we recognize from three imperfect things one composite perfect nature
=77.1141=), but in three perfect hypostases one simple essence, more than
perfect and all-perfect. For everything composed of imperfect things is in
every way composite. But from perfect hypostases it is impossible that
composition should come to be. Hence we do not say the species from
hypostases, but in hypostases. And we said imperfect of those that do not
preserve the species of the thing completed out of them. For stone and wood
and iron, each

CHAPTER 10.
And one must know that it is one thing to be considered in reality; and
another, in word and in thought. Now in the case of all created things, the
division of the hypostases is considered in reality (for Peter is considered
as separated in realities from Paul); but the community and the conjunction
and the one is considered in word and in thought. For we understand with the
mind that Peter and Paul are of the same nature, and have one common nature.
For each of them is a rational mortal living being; and each is flesh ensouled
with a rational and intellective soul. This common nature, then, is considered
in word. For the hypostases are not in one another, but each is apart and
separately, that is by itself, separated, having very many things that divide
it from the other. For they stand apart also in place, and differ in time, and
are partitioned in judgment, and in strength, and in form, or shape, and in
habit, and in temperament, and in dignity, and in pursuit, and in all the
characterizing properties. And more than all, by not being in one another, but
separately. Hence also two and three human beings are said, and many. And this
can be seen also in all creation. But in the case of the holy and
superessential Trinity that is beyond all things and incomprehensible, it is
the reverse.
For there the common and the one is considered in reality, both because of the
co-eternal, and the identity of essence and of energy and of will, and the
concord of judgment, and the identity of authority and of power and of
goodness (I did not say


similarity, but identity), and the one spring of movement. For there is one
essence, one goodness, one power, one will, one energy, =77.1144= one
authority, one and the same; not three similar to one another, but one and the
same movement of the three hypostases. For each of them has toward the other
not less than toward itself, that is, that the Father, and the Son, and the
Holy Spirit are one in all things; except the unbegottenness and the
generation and the procession. And the divided is in thought. For we know one
God, and we conceive the difference only in the properties of fatherhood and
of sonship, and of procession, according to the cause and the caused, and the
perfection of the hypostasis, that is the mode of existence. For we cannot
speak of local interval, as in our case, in the uncircumscribed Godhead (for
the hypostases are in one another, not so as to be confused, but so as to hold
to one another, according to the word of the Lord, who said, I in the Father,
and the Father in me); nor a difference of will or of judgment, or of energy,
or of power, or of anything else, which generate in us the real and thorough
division. Therefore we do not say three gods, the Father and the Son and the
Holy Spirit; but rather one God, the Holy Trinity, the Son and the Spirit
being referred to one cause, not composed or coalesced according to the
contraction of Sabellius (for they are united, as we said, not so as to be
confused, but so as to hold to one another; and they have the perichoresis in
one another without any coalescence and commingling); nor standing apart, or
cut according to essence according to the division of Arius. For the Godhead
is undivided in things divided, to speak briefly; and as in three suns holding
to one another and being without interval, there is one blending and
conjunction of the light. Whenever therefore we look toward the Godhead and
the first cause, and the monarchy, and the one and the same of the Godhead,
that I may speak thus, both movement and will, and the identity of essence and
power and energy and lordship, one is what is presented to us. But when we
look toward those in whom the godhead is, or to speak more exactly, those who
are the Godhead; and those who are from there timelessly from the first cause
and with the same glory and without interval (that is, the hypostases of the
Son and of the Spirit); three are the things worshipped; one Father, the
Father and without beginning, that is without cause (for he is not from
anyone); one Son, the Son and not without beginning; that is not without cause
(for he is from the Father); but if you take beginning from time, he is also
without beginning (for he is maker of times, not under time); one Holy Spirit,
the Spirit coming forth from the Father; not filially, but by procession;
neither the Father departing from the unbegottenness, because he has begotten;
nor the Son from the generation, because he is from the unbegotten; for how
could he? nor the Spirit falling over either into Father or into Son, because
he has proceeded, and because he is God. For the property is unmoved. Or how
would a property remain if it were moved and falling over? For if the Father
is Son, he is not Father in the proper sense; for there is one Father in the
proper sense. And if the Son is Father, he is not Son in the proper sense; for
there is one Son in the proper sense, and one Holy Spirit. And one must know
that we do not say the Father is =77.1145= from anyone, but we say he is
Father of the Son.
And we do not say the Son is without cause, nor Father; but we say he is from
the Father, and Son of the Father. And the Holy Spirit we say is both from the
Father, and we name Spirit of the Father. But we do not say the Spirit is from
the Son, yet we name him Spirit of the Son (For if anyone has not the Spirit
of Christ, says the divine Apostle); and we confess that he has been
manifested and imparted to us through the Son. For he breathed, it says, and
said to his disciples: Receive Holy Spirit. Just as from the sun there is both
the ray and the illumination (for he himself is the fountain of the ray and of
the illumination), and through the


ray the illumination is imparted to us, and this is what enlightens us, and is
participated in by us. But we do not say the Son is of the Spirit, nor yet
from the Spirit.

CHAPTER 11.
One must therefore think that each of the things said of God does not signify
what he is according to essence, but either declares what he is not, or some
relation toward one of the things set over against him, or one of the things
that attend upon the nature, or an energy, or something indicative of being
itself. The more proper, then, of all the names said of God seems to be the
being [perhaps He Who Is]. For it has gathered into itself the whole of being,
as some sea of essence, infinite and indefinite. And second, the name God,
which signifies also the creative (for theinai is to make; and theis, the one
who made), but more clearly it is indicative of the seeing energy. For God is
a seer even of the things not yet come to be, as lying before his sight. For
he beheld all things before their generation, conceiving them timelessly; and
each, according to his willing thought, which is timeless (which is
predestination and image and pattern), also comes to be at the time that was
predestined. The former, then, is indicative of being itself, and not of what
he is; and the second, of energy. And the without-beginning, and uncreated,
and incorporeal, and such things, declare what he is not; and the good and
just and holy and such things attend upon the divine nature, but do not
declare the essence itself. And Lord, king, master, and such things, declare a
relation toward the things set over against them; for he is called Lord and
King of those who are lorded over and ruled.

CHAPTER 12.
And since we find very many things said more corporeally in the divine
Scripture concerning God in a symbolic way, one must know that, being human
beings as we are, and clothed with this thick flesh, it is impossible to think
or to speak the divine and high =77.1148= and immaterial energies of the
Godhead, unless we use images and types and symbols according to ourselves.
Whatever things, then, have been said more corporeally concerning God, are
said symbolically; but they have some higher meaning. For the Divine is simple
and without shape. Eyes of God, then, and eyelids and sight, let us understand
as his power that oversees all things, and the unerring of his knowledge, from
the fact that among us more complete knowledge and assurance come about
through this sense. And ears and hearing, his being propitious and receptive
of our petition. For we too become well-disposed toward those who make
supplications through this sense, bending the ear to them more genuinely. And
mouth and speech, that which indicates his will, from the fact that among us
the thoughts of the heart are signified through mouth and speech. And eating
and drinking, our running together toward his will. For we too, through the
sense of taste, fill up the necessary appetite of nature. And smell, that
which accepts our thought toward him and our goodwill, from the fact that
among us the acceptance of fragrance comes about through this sense. And face,
the indication and manifestation of him through his works, from the fact that
our manifestation comes about through a face. And hands, that which
accomplishes his energy. For we too accomplish the things that are needed and

especially the more honorable through our own hands. And right hand, his help
upon the auspicious things, from the fact that we too rather use the right
hand in the more honorable things and those that need most strength. And
touch, his more exact discernment and collection even of the very fine and
hidden things, from the fact that among us those who are touched cannot hide
anything in themselves. And feet and walking, the coming and presence toward
help of those in need, or defense against enemies, or some other action; from
the fact that

CHAPTER 13.
The uncreated, however, and uncircumscribed and superessential, and such
things, are common to Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit. For this reason they
are also called properties of the divine nature; of the divine nature, because
they are natu =77.1149= ral, and not hypostatic, belonging with equal honor to
the three hypostases of the Godhead; and properties, as being wholly unshared
by every other nature except the divine essence alone, being contemplated
neither in human beings nor in angels at all. And it is called both essence,
and nature, and form, [the one and the same; and essence, as having being in
itself; and nature,] as causing the hypostases to grow; and form, as gathering
the whole of the things formed under it, that is of the characters. And the
things contained are called both individuals and hypostases and persons. And
individuals, because none of them admits cutting or division. For though the
human essence, that is to say humanity, is divided into the human beings
according to sense, none of them can be cut into other human beings, rational
and mortal living beings, receptive of mind and of knowledge; and according to
this account, all those in part are individuals. And hypostases, as standing
and remaining under the nature, this being universal and considered in
thought; while the particular things are and have being in reality.
And persons, because each of them has its own character unshared with the
rest. Therefore in the Godhead we dogmatize one essence, one nature, one form;
and three individuals, three hypostases, three persons, known by corresponding
characters, or properties. And the Son is called impress of the Father's
hypostasis, not because he himself is the property of the Father (for who who
has partaken of reason would suppose this), but because insofar as the Father
is Father, he is characterized and known from having a Son. And because the
Son, having in himself all things of the Father, remaining Son, shows him from
whom he is, just as his natural unvarying image. Therefore he says: He who has
seen me has seen the Father. And the God without beginning, being mind by
nature, is a fountain of a Word of like nature. And being Father, he is
generative of a consubstantial Son. For this reason the same is both Word of
God and Son of the Father.

CHAPTER 14.


This Son and Word of God and Father, through whom the Father created all
creation, as through his own wisdom and power (for he himself is the wisdom
and power of the Father); this one, for our sake, by the good pleasure of him
who begot him, became man, being in the Father, and having come to be with us
(for he is uncircumscribed); and he assumed flesh, soul, mind, reason, will,
energy, all things that belong to a human being according to nature; not
remaining inexperienced even of those passions introduced upon us after the
first sin, which blame does not reach; but even these he accepted through
great condescension; in order that he might recast all humanity toward the
better; and to speak simply, he became a perfect human being, not according to
fantasy or seeming, but truly and =77.1152= manifestly existing as a human
being, according to us together and above us. According to us, because,
conceived in the womb of a woman, formed living in her, carried as long as and
as the law of nature granted, he was born in the manner of an infant. Above
us, because the conception was of the Holy Spirit without conjunction, apart
from fleshly pleasure, without any passionate thought; the compacting of the
body from all-pure virgin bloods, the birth without pangs did not violate the
virginity of her who bore him. For she remained a virgin even after the birth,
as she was also before the birth and before the conception, she who through
all her life is ever-virgin, both undefiled and unstained. For it was fitting
that she who bore God should be such. According to us, because he took up an
earthy and corruptible body, composed of the four humors, made living by a
tripartite soul, governed by innate heat and natural powers; subject to
hunger, and thirst, and the other blameless weaknesses of nature.
Above us, because he was not a mere human being, but human being together and
God. For he did not accomplish his own becoming-man by being changed from
being God into becoming a human being, but remaining God what he was, he
became a human being, what he was not; not changing into a divine nature the
human nature which he assumed; but, it remaining in its own bounds, deifying
it through the extreme union. For as fire, being united wholly to iron, sets
it on fire, yet does not make it depart from the nature of iron (for this
remains iron even after the firing, and it both is and is called fired iron);
in such a way the Son and Word of God and God, being united to the mortal
nature, deified it, yet did not make it depart from being a mortal nature. For
this remains a mortal nature even after the deification, and it both is called
and is a deified mortal nature; deified flesh with an intellective, rational,
willing, energetic soul.

CHAPTER 15.
And since sin is bastard and contrary to nature (for it comes in upon us from
outside when we are not sober), the God Word reasonably did not assume this.
Again, since he deified the whole assumed nature, the deified nature has
justly remained inaccessible and untouched in every way by sin. But neither
was there in Christ the wavering choice. For Christ was possessed of choice as
self-determining even according to the human, because he was also rational
(for every rational thing is self-determining; and every self-determining
thing is possessed of choice); but of counsel and consideration and judgment
and choice as we have them he had need nowhere; just as neither, again, of
sobriety. For he was God according to both; the one by nature, the other by
deification.
Wherefore also according to this account he was above all sin. For sinning
comes about for us either from a precarious


choice, or even against choice from inattention. Or rather even inattention
itself is sin, or at any rate a beginning of sin. For this very reason we are
also exacted penalties for involuntary sins. But from where the cause has
fled, what entrance did the caused have there =77.1153=? Choice simply, then,
is an advance toward what is to be chosen, that is a self-determining rational
impulse toward the willed, which belongs by nature to every rational thing.
Choice is said again, the choosing before another, which comes about when
counsel has gone before; then judgment, after which follows the disposition
toward what was judged from counsel, and it is called gnomic judgment; after
the judgment, the choice now being declared, or the selection of what seemed
good, succeeds. Such a choice no one of sound mind would say is in Christ. For
of what counsel was he in need, or of what of the things from counsel, the
wonderful counsellor, the wisdom of God and Father? or how would the choice
that is in us be in him who has pre-chosen the good, as it has been witnessed?
For Isaiah also cried out beforehand, that before he knows, or chooses evil
things, he will choose the good, that is, this one would not know evil things
at all, nor would he choose them; but the good he will choose from the first
beginning. And again: Before the child knows good or evil, he disobeys
wickedness, to choose the good. That is, before all knowledge the child in
extreme infancy, being disobedient and uninclined toward the evil, will
appropriate the good; and the before he knows has been said according to the
appearance. For when was the child not knowing, in whom all the treasures of
wisdom and of knowledge are hidden, being manifested at corresponding times as
far as was needed?
Such also is it that Jesus advanced in wisdom and grace with the stature,
according to the appearance, that is. For there was increase of the stature,
the body growing naturally; and of wisdom and of grace, of the very first and
highest, not increase, but timely manifestation. Therefore Christ, being God
united to the human essence according to hypostasis, was good as God. And he
had also as a human being, self-determiningly, the good that is according to
nature. For as evil and sin are contrary to nature, so indeed the good and
virtue are according to nature. For this reason sin also received such a name,
as a missing of the good according to nature.

CHAPTER 16.
The angels, then, both are called and are hard to move toward evil, being
helped by attendance upon God and by the continuous illumination from

And it was energizing the government of the body; but the God Word was its
governor, it following him self-determiningly and even greatly desiring the
fulfillment of his divine will by the excess of holiness. Wherefore also the
Lord, praying to his own Father at the =77.1156= time of the passion as a
human being, was saying: Not as I will, but as you; let your will be done. For
this word also belongs to the soul of the Lord, which knew one will of the God
Word united to it and of the Father. The Master, then, had the things of the
slaves, because of the slaves; but above the slaves. For he was Master, even
if he took a slave's form. He hungered in truth, as we, he thirsted, he bore
drowsiness, he was weary, he was grieving, weeping, fearing, agonizing, but
willingly, when he himself, willing, was granting to his own humanity to do
what is its own blamelessly. This is above us. Again he was eating, he was
drinking,


he was sleeping, he was conversing, just as we, but altogether without sin.
This is above us. For in which of us, in the natural and unreproached things,
would the blameless be found throughout? He was suffering for us, he was in
pain at the nailing and stretching on the cross; at last, he died, but the
passion was voluntary for him, and the pain, and the dying. The flesh was
suffering, the soul was in pain naturally because of the conjunction (for from
it is the sensation to the flesh); yet the godhead that was in both was
undivided

CHAPTER 17
The Lord, having suffered and died and been buried, shook off the corruption
and the things of the corruption; and having prepared his own body toward
incorruption, and having taken up his own soul by authority as God, he rose on
the third day. For I lay down my soul, he says, that I may take it again. I
have authority to lay it down, and I have authority to take it again. And if
after the resurrection the Lord ate, yet he did this according to economy,
presenting to his own disciples that it was he himself who had risen, who was
formerly with them. And when I say according to economy, I drive out the
fantasy. For he ate truly; but he administered the food in a new manner, as he
himself knows.
For this reason he also was showing types of wounds; the things that had come
to be, yet were not, the one who can do all things making present. Since
indeed that body, having become above all need, all resistance, was admitting
neither foods nor touchings. Wherefore we also say that the Lord is not now
according to flesh, as having put away the things of the flesh; desire of
food, sleep, labor, and such things; besides these, also the resistant. For
his body, through the putting off of the corruption, was completed as without
need, and impassible, and untouchable, and immortal; yet not also uncreated,
nor again uncircumscribed. For these things belong to Godhead alone; and that
I may speak in sum, as much as the human of Christ was receptive of divine
boasts, it partook of them from the Word himself through the union, through
which it is also august and worshipful. But as much as it was not receptive,
it did not partake, in order that from this too we may know both the
difference of the natures of Christ, and the oneness of the person.

CHAPTER 19.
Hence he who said a theandric energy did not introduce confusion to the
natural energies; but he presented their union to the extreme, and that the
human energy has been deified. For as in Christ through the union the human
nature had been deified; so indeed also the will had been deified together,
and the energy. Wherefore also when he was working wonders, the humanlike
things were yoked together with the godlike energizings. And from the
humanlike energizings, reason that is and touch, and such things, the divine
signs were going forth. Just as when cutting iron has been fired, and is
cutting and burning at the same time, both the cutting comes to be with the
burning, and the burning is inseparable from the cutting. And the running
together of the energies brings in the unitary for them, not according to
nature, but according to union. He therefore who said a theandric energy,
through the singular utterance made clear that the two energies are one by the
coming together and that they have not been divided in reality. And in saying
theandric, that is divine and manly, he showed the difference of the energies
by nature even after the union. And otherwise, the name of energy declaring
two things (for natural energy is the going forth of the power dwelling in the
nature toward =77.1160= fulfillment of some work, which has its being in the
energizing, just as the power in being of such a nature and being able.
Energy is said again, also the fulfillment of the work, the thing energized
itself, the completion of the thing, the action itself); this second sense of
energy, then, one must fit to the theandric. For Christ being God-man, every
action of his also is God-manlike and theandric. And to speak thus does not
bring confusion to the energies, but makes clear that the one energizing is
one and the same; both in a godlike way and in a humanlike way; neither the
divine things as God; for he is a human being; nor the human things as a
human being; for he is God. Likewise also he who said one incarnate nature of
the God Word did not dogmatize the Lord of one nature. For by adding incarnate
he took away such a suspicion. For other is the nature of the Word, that is
the divine; and other again that of the flesh, that is the humanlike, by
synecdoche.

CHAPTER 20.
Other is the will of the divine nature, and other of the humanlike. And we say
will the willing, the willing power itself, which is natural will. The will,
then, of the human nature, the Lord, having assumed it and deified it,
preserved what it was according to nature, human. And wishing to present this
clearly, having entered into a house in the parts of Tyre and Sidon, he wished
no one to know, and he was not able to be hidden. For having wished to be
hidden economically he was not able; teaching us through the things themselves
that the all-powerful did not reject the weak will of the human nature, but
appropriated even this essentially, having taken up our whole nature with all
the things according to nature. Will, then, is said

primarily, the willing, which is natural will, as has been made clear. Will is
said also the thing willed itself; which in human beings is hypostatic, not
the same belonging to the whole nature. For to one of the human beings,
perhaps, it has been willed to live privately; and to another, to speak
freely; and to one, to work the land; and to another, to sail; and to another
again something else according to judgment. And often to the same one man the
willed is different and at another time another. And such a hypostatic thing
is called gnomic will. Therefore the Lord, since he has been confessed of two
natures, is believed to have acquired also two natural wills, both divine and
human; that is two willing powers differing from one another by the account of
the nature. And since he has been known of one hypostasis, it is unsafe to say
of him different gnomic wills. For the thing willed is one, because also the
one willing is one, his humanlike willing being moved toward its own willed
thing; when this was willed to his godlike will, and as far as it was willed.

CHAPTER 21.
And judgment being said in two ways; for judgment is said also the simply
inclination toward the willed, as coming about ratio =77.1161= nally and with
knowledge in the rational. Judgment is said also the disposition about
something from prior counsel and judgment; it is not safe to say either
judgment or gnomic will of Christ according to the second sense of judgment;
because ignorance is brought in together with these. For even if the Lord took
the nature that is ignorant, yet he imparted to it of the divine boasts. And
the soul of the Lord was having all wisdom, divine together indeed and human;
all knowledge of the things that have come to be, of the things that are, of
the things that will be; not by nature, not by grace; but through the union
according to hypostasis toward him who alone is wise and who knows all things
even before their generation from before the ages. Of what counsel, then, was
this in need? of what of the things after the counsel, she who knows all
things, as we said. And if it has been written that the Lord, being hungry,
came to a fig tree not having fruit, if perhaps he would find something on it,
and he did not find except leaves; such a shaping of the word is according to
suspicion, as perhaps he who saw his coming to the fig tree would have
suspected, not however knowing the account of the economy. For to whom even of
ordinary people would the obvious have been unknown, since it was not the
season of figs? But in order that he who is able in all things might show in
season that he has not only a beneficent power, but also a punitive one; and
that whatever he would will, by word alone he will dry up those who will
crucify him; the hunger, and the coming to the fig tree, the wise and
philanthropic one sets as going before such a showing. For this very reason
also the hunger contrary to custom, it being morning, as it had been willed.
Therefore the arrival at the fig tree, likewise also the inquiry about Lazarus
where that one had been laid when he died, and if anything similar, not of
ignorance, but of economy and fitting condescension. According to the second
sense, then, of judgment and of choice, we would say neither judgment nor
choice of Christ. But according to the first, we say that this or that has
been done also according to the judgment of God, that is, the thing done was
willed by God and acceptable; as if we should say that David has reigned
according to the judgment of God. And the Word of God is possessed of choice;
and the Holy Spirit has been called possessed of choice. One must therefore
examine the word in all things, if it declares more than one, and with
clearness advance the accounts toward the things declared. For great is the
deceit from homonymy, and this is the first mode in the sophisms.


CHAPTER 22.
Thus also the name God and the name human being sometimes signifies nature,
and sometimes hypostasis. And godhead likewise and humanity always nature
alone, but never also hypostasis. And Christ declares a composite hypostasis,
of both godhead and humanity. And the anointing was the godhead, and the
humanity, the =77.1164= anointed. And one must understand anointing as the
perichoresis of the whole of the chrism into the whole of the anointed. For
thus that which has received the anointing would be in truth wholly christ.
For that which has been anointed on the surface is christ in part, but not
wholly; hence not christ in the proper sense. Wherefore kings and high
priests, being anointed with the oil, and receiving the surface anointing, are
not christs in the proper sense. And if the grace goes into depth through the
anointing, not again christs in the proper sense. For they are by grace and
perishable. The Lord alone, then, is christ in the proper sense, as whole
throughout and not by grace; and by the union according to hypostasis also
eternally christ.

CHAPTER 23.

the hypostasis, as, Son of God, and impress of his hypostasis; and, Angel of
great counsel, wonderful counsellor; and the like. And some, the perichoresis
of the hypostases in one another, as, I in the Father, and the Father in me;
and the undeparting establishment, as, Word, wisdom, and power, and radiance.

likewise; and the power in him who is able, and the radiance in the light is

CHAPTER 24.
And of the things in the union, there are three modes. For when we make the
account from the better, we say deification of the flesh, and being made Word
and exaltation, and such things; showing the wealth that accrued to the flesh
from the union and growing-together toward the most high God Word. And when
from the lesser, we say incarnation of the Word, becoming-man, emptying,
poverty, humiliation. For these and such things are said of the Word and God
from the mixing toward the human. And when from both together, we allege
union, communion, anointing, growing-together, conformation, and such things.
Through this third mode, then, the two modes spoken before are said. For
through the union it is made clear what each had from the joining and
perichoresis of that which subsists together with it. For through the union
according to hypostasis, the flesh is said to have been deified, and to have
become God, and co-God with the Word, and the God Word to have been incarnate,
and to have become a human being, and to be called a creature, and to be
called last, not as the two natures having been changed into one composite
nature; for it is impossible that in one nature the contrary natural things
should come to be at once; but as the two natures having been united according
to hypostasis, and having the perichoresis into one another unconfused and
unchangeable.
And the perichoresis has come to be not from the flesh, but from the godhead.
For it is impossible that the flesh should permeate through the godhead; but
the divine nature, having once permeated through the flesh, gave also to the
flesh the ineffable perichoresis toward it, which indeed we call union.
And one must know that in the first and second mode of that in the union, the
reverse is considered. For when we make the account concerning the flesh, we
say deification and being made Word, and exaltation and anointing. For these
things are from the godhead, but are considered around the flesh. And when
concerning the Word, emptying, incarnation, becoming-man, humiliation, and
such things, which are, as we said, from the flesh, but are said of the Word
and God. For he himself underwent these things willingly.

CHAPTER 26.
And of the things after the resurrection some are God-fitting, as, Baptizing

the days, until the

CHAPTER 27.
One must know, then, that the name of godhead and of humanity is presentative
of the essences or natures; but God and human being is also ranged upon the
nature; whenever we say, God is an incomprehensible essence, and that God is
one. And it is taken also of the hypostases, as the more particular receiving
the name of the more universal. As when the Scripture says: Therefore God,
your God, has anointed you. For see, it declared the Father and the Son. And
as when it says, There was a certain human being in the land of Ausitis. For
it declared Job alone. In the case, then, of our Lord Jesus Christ, since we
know two natures, but one hypostasis composite of both; whenever we consider
the natures, we call them godhead and humanity; and when the hypostasis
composed of the natures, sometimes from both together we name him Christ, and
God and human being at once and God incarnate. And sometimes from one of the
parts, God only and Son of God, and human being only and son of man. And
sometimes from the high things only, and sometimes from the low things only.
For he is one who exists similarly both as that and as this; being the one
always; and having become the other later through love of humanity. Saying
godhead, then, we do not name of it the properties of the humanity. For we do
not say godhead passible or created; nor do we predicate of the flesh, that is
of the humanity, the properties of the godhead. For we do not say flesh, that
is humanity, uncreated. But in the case of the hypostasis, whether we name
this from both together, or from one of the parts, we set upon it the
properties of both natures. For Christ, which is both together, is called both
God and human being, created and uncreated, and passible and impassible. And
when from one of the parts he is named also Son of God and God, he receives
the properties of the nature that subsists together, that is of the flesh, God
being named passible, and Lord of glory crucified; not insofar as God, but
insofar as also a human being the same. And when he is named human being and
son of man, he receives the properties and boasts of the divine essence, a
child before the ages and a human being without beginning, not insofar as
child and human being, but insofar as being God before the ages, he became in
the last times a child. And this is the mode of the exchange, each nature
giving to the other its own things, through the identity of the hypostasis


and their perichoresis into one another. According to this we can say
concerning Christ: This is our God, he appeared upon the earth, and was
conversant with human beings; and, This human being is uncreated and
impassible and uncircumscribed.

CHAPTER 28.
The Son and Word of God, then, became man, in order that through himself and
in himself he might renew that according to the image and according to the
likeness; and might teach us also the virtuous way of life, having made this
easy of access for us through himself; and by the communion of life might free
from corruption the corrupted nature of human beings, having become
firstfruits of our resurrection, and might redeem us from the tyranny of the
devil, having called us to the knowledge of God. The worship of the demons,
then, has ceased, the creation has been sanctified by the divine blood, altars
and temples of idols have been thrown down, knowledge of God has been planted,
Trinity the consubstantial, the one true God is believed and worshipped,
virtues are in citizenship, a firm hope of resurrection has been given through
the resurrection of Christ, the demons shudder at the human beings formerly
subject; and what is wonderful, that all these things have been accomplished
through a cross, and passions, and death; into all the earth the Gospel of the
knowledge of God has been proclaimed, not turning the adversaries by war and
arms and camps, but a few naked, poor and unlettered, being persecuted,
maltreated, being put to death, proclaiming him crucified in flesh and dead,
overcame the many and powerful and rich and wise; for the all-powerful power
of the crucified was strengthening them. The formerly most fearful death has
been defeated, and is now preferred to the sweetest life. These are the
accomplishments of the presence of Christ, these the marks of his power. For
he did not, as through Moses, save one people from Egypt and from the slavery
of Pharaoh by dividing a sea; but rather he delivered all humanity from
corruption of death and from the bitter tyrant of sin; not leading by force
toward virtue and piety, not covering with earth, not burning with fire, and
commanding those who sin to be stoned; but by meekness and long-suffering
persuading human beings to choose these things.
But, O Word of God, and wisdom, and power, what would we, the resourceless and
weak, give you in return for all these things? For all things are yours and
you ask from us nothing, except to be saved. For you yourself both give even
this, and know grace for those who receive through unspeakable goodness. Grace
to you, who have given the being, and have granted the well-being; and from
this, when we had fallen aside, have led us back again toward this through
your ineffable condescension. To you, then, belongs all glory, honor and
worship, with your Father without beginning, and your all-holy and good and
life-giving Spirit, now and always and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
