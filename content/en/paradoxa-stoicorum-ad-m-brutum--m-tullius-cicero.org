* Introduction

* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

I have often noticed, Brutus, that Cato, your uncle, when he was giving his
opinion in the Senate, would take up grave topics from philosophy, remote from
this practice of the courts and of public life, and yet by speaking would
bring it about that they seemed plausible even to the people.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And that is a greater thing for him than for you or for me, because we make
more use of that philosophy which has given birth to fluency of speech,
and in which things are said that do not differ much from popular opinion.
Cato, however, a finished Stoic in my judgment, both holds views that are not
much approved among the crowd, and belongs to that school which follows no
bloom of speech and does not expand an argument: with little questions like
pinpricks he makes his point.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

But nothing is so incredible that speaking cannot make it plausible, nothing
so rough, so uncultivated, that it does not take on splendor by oratory and as
it were be polished. Thinking this, I have done something even bolder than the
man I am speaking of. For Cato, at least on greatness of soul, on
self-command, on death, on every praise of virtue, on the immortal gods, on
love of country, is accustomed to speak in the Stoic manner, with the
ornaments of oratory applied. I have, in sport, cast into commonplaces those
very things which the Stoics scarcely approve in the schools and at leisure.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

And because they are astonishing and contrary to the opinion of all, and are
called paradoxes even by the Stoics themselves, I wanted to try whether they
could be brought out into the light, that is, into the Forum, and so spoken
that they would win assent, or whether learned speech was one thing and
popular speech another. I wrote these topics the more willingly because those
things they call paradoxes seem to me to be especially Socratic and by far the
truest.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

You will receive, then, this little work, finished by lamplight in these
already shortening nights, since that gift of longer vigils has already
appeared under your name, and you will taste the kind of exercises I have been
used to, when I transfer to this oratorical manner of ours the things that are
treated in the schools as theses. I do not ask you to enter this work as a
credit: it is not such that it could be set on the citadel, like that Minerva
of Phidias, and yet it should be plain that it came from the same workshop.

** Paradox I
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1
:END:

** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h2
:END:

That only the honorable is good.

I am afraid that this speech may seem to some of you drawn from the
disputations of Socratic men, not from my own sense. Still I will say what I
think, and I will say it more briefly than so great a matter can be said.
Never, by Hercules, have I counted among goods, or among things to be sought,
those men's money, or their splendid houses, or their resources, or their
commands, or those pleasures to which they are most bound. For I saw that,
overflowing with these things, they still most of all desired what they
already had in abundance. The thirst of desire is never filled or sated; and
those who have these things are tortured not only by the craving to increase
them, but also by the fear of losing them.

** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h3
:END:

In this I often miss the prudence of the most self-controlled of men, our
ancestors, who thought these weak and changeable limbs of money should be
called by the name of goods, when by fact and by deeds they had judged far
otherwise. Can a good be a good to a bad man, or can anyone in an abundance of
goods himself not be good? And yet we see that all those things are of such a
kind that the wicked have them and the upright lack them.

** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h4
:END:

For this reason, let anyone laugh who wishes: true reason will still have more
weight with me than the opinion of the crowd. I will never say that a man has
lost his goods if he has lost cattle or furniture, and I will often praise
that wise man Bias, I think, who is numbered among the Seven. When the enemy
had taken his country, Priene, and the rest were fleeing in such a way that
they carried off much of their property, and someone warned him to do the
same, "I am doing so," he said; "for I carry all that is mine with me."

** Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h5
:END:

He did not even count as his own these playthings of fortune, which we even
call goods. What, then, someone will ask, is a good? If that which is done
rightly and honorably and with virtue is truly said to be done well, then that
which is right and honorable and with virtue is, I think, the only good.

** Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h6
:END:

But these things can seem more tedious when they are argued more slowly. They
have been made clear by the life and deeds of the greatest men, these things
which seem to be argued in words more finely than is enough. For I ask you
whether those who left us this commonwealth so splendidly founded seem to have
had any thought of silver for greed, or of pleasant places for delight, or of
furniture for luxury, or of banquets for pleasures. Set before your eyes each
of the kings.

** Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h7
:END:

Do you wish to start from Romulus? Do you wish, after the free state, from
those very men who set it free? By what steps, after all, did Romulus climb to
heaven? By those things which these people call goods, or by his deeds and
virtues? What? Do we think that the earthen bowls and clay urns of Numa
Pompilius were less welcome to the immortal gods than the rich bowls of the
Salii? I pass over the rest; for they are all equals among themselves except
the Proud.

** Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h8
:END:

If someone should ask Brutus what he did in freeing his country, if someone
likewise should ask the rest who shared that plan what they looked to, what
they followed: would anyone come forward to whom pleasure, to whom riches, to
whom, in short, anything besides the duty of a brave and great man seemed to
have been set as an aim? What drove Gaius Mucius to the killing of Porsenna
with no hope of his own safety? What force held Cocles alone on the bridge
against all the enemy's troops? What sent the father Decius, what sent the
son, with life devoted, into the armed ranks of the enemy? What did the
self-command of Gaius Fabricius aim at, what the spare living of Manius
Curius? What the two bulwarks of the Punic War, Gnaeus and Publius Scipio, who
thought the coming of the Carthaginians should be blocked with their own
bodies? What the elder Africanus? What the younger? What Cato, set between
their ages? What countless others? (for we have examples at home in plenty):
did they think that anything in life was to be sought for themselves except
what seemed praiseworthy and splendid?

** Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h9
:END:

Let those mockers of this speech and this view come, then, and let them now
judge for themselves whether they would rather be like one of those men who
abound in marble houses gleaming with ivory and gold, in statues, in pictures,
in chased gold and silver, in Corinthian works, or like Gaius Fabricius, who
had none of these things and wished to have none.

** Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h10
:END:

And these things, indeed, which are shifted now this way, now that, they are
easily brought to deny that they are among goods. That other point they hold
tightly and defend with care, that pleasure is the highest good: which to me
seems the voice of cattle, not of men. You, when a god, or nature, the mother,
so to speak, of all things, has given you a mind, than which nothing is more
excellent or more divine, will you so cast yourself down and prostrate
yourself that you think there is no difference between you and some
four-footed beast? Is anything a good that does not make the one who possesses
it better?

** Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h11
:END:

For as each man most shares in the good, so he is most praiseworthy, and there
is no good of which he who has it cannot honestly boast. What of these is in
pleasure? Does it make a man better or more praiseworthy? Or does anyone, in
getting pleasures, lift himself up by boasting and proclamation? And yet if
pleasure, which is defended by the patronage of a great many, is not to be
counted among goods, and the greater it is, the more it moves the mind from
its seat and standing, then surely to live well and happily is nothing other
than to live honorably and rightly.

:PROPERTIES:

** Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h2
:END:

That virtue is sufficient for happiness.

Where virtue is, nothing is wanting for a happy life.

I never thought Marcus Regulus wretched or unlucky or miserable. For it was
not the greatness of his soul that was tortured by the Carthaginians, not his
dignity, not his good faith, not his constancy, not any virtue, not, in short,
the mind itself, which, with the guard of so many virtues and so great a
company, when his body was taken, could not itself be taken. We have seen
Gaius Marius, who in prosperity seemed to me one among fortunate men, in
adversity one among the greatest, than which nothing can be happier for a
mortal.

** Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h3
:END:

You do not know, you madman, you do not know what strength virtue has for men.
You use the name of virtue only, and you do not know what the thing itself can
do. No one can fail to be most happy who is wholly dependent on himself and
who places all that is his in himself alone. He whose every hope and plan and
thought hangs on fortune can have nothing certain, nothing that he can be sure
will remain his for a single day. Terrify that man, if you find one, with
those threats of death or exile. As for me, whatever happens in so ungrateful
a state will have come upon me not even refusing it, let alone resisting it.
For what have I toiled at, or what have I done, or in what have my cares and
thoughts kept watch, if I have produced nothing of such a kind, attained
nothing, so as to be in that state which neither the rashness of fortune nor
the injury of enemies could shake? Do you threaten me with death,

** Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h4
:END:

that I must leave the company of men altogether, or with exile, that I must
migrate from the wicked? Death is terrible to those for whom everything is
extinguished with life, not to those whose praise cannot die. Exile, moreover,
is terrible to those for whom the place of dwelling is as it were fenced
round, not to those who hold the whole world to be one city. Miseries press
you, all distresses press you, who think yourself happy, who think yourself
flourishing. Desires torment you. You are tortured day and night, you for whom
what there is is not enough and who fear that even that itself will not last.
The conscience of your crimes goads you. Fear of courts and laws drains you.
Wherever you have looked, your injuries meet you like Furies, and they do not
let you sigh freely.

** Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h5
:END:

For this reason, as no one who is wicked and foolish and idle can be well off,
so no good and wise and brave man can be miserable. Nor, indeed, can the life
of one whose virtue and manners are to be praised itself fail to be
praiseworthy, nor, further, is a life that is to be praised a life to be fled;
but it would be to be fled if it were miserable. For this reason whatever is
praiseworthy ought to seem both happy and flourishing and to be sought.

** Paradox III
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

** Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h2
:END:

That faults and right actions are equal.

"It is a small thing," he says. But the fault is great. For sins are not to be
measured by the outcomes of things, but by the vices of men. That in which one
sins can be greater or less than another; the sinning itself, however you turn
it, is one. Whether a steersman wrecks a ship of gold or a ship of chaff, in
the thing there is some difference, in the steersman's ignorance none. Desire
has slipped with an unknown woman: the pain reaches fewer than if he had been
wanton with some well-born and noble girl; yet he has sinned no less, if
indeed to sin is as it were to cross the lines, and when you have done that,
the fault has been committed. How far you go once you have crossed does
nothing to increase the fault of crossing. No one, certainly, is allowed to
sin; and what is not allowed is held by this one point, if it is shown that it
is not allowed. If that can never become greater or less, since the sin is in
this, that it was not allowed, which is always one and the same, the sins that
arise from it must be equal.

** Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h3
:END:

And if the virtues are equal among themselves, the vices too must be equal.
And that the virtues are equal, and that a good man cannot become better, nor
a temperate man more temperate, nor a brave man braver, nor a wise man wiser,
can be seen most easily. Will you call a man good who has returned a deposit
of ten pounds of gold with no witness, when he could have kept it with
impunity, if the same man has not done the same in ten thousand? Or temperate,
who has restrained himself in one desire and poured himself out in another?

** Section 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h4
:END:

Virtue is one, agreeing with reason and lasting constancy. Nothing can be
added here to make it more virtue, nothing taken away so that the name of
virtue is left. For if good deeds are right deeds, and nothing is righter than
right, then certainly nothing better than good can be found either. It
follows, then, that the vices too are equal, if indeed perversities of the
mind are rightly called vices. And since the virtues are equal, right deeds,
since they proceed from the virtues, ought to be equal, and likewise sins,
since they flow from vices, must be equal.

** Section 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h5
:END:

"You take those points from the philosophers," he says. I was afraid you would
say from the pimps. "Socrates used to argue in that way." Well told, by
Hercules; for it has been handed down to memory that that man was learned and
wise. Still I ask you, since we are contending with words, not with fists: are
we to ask what porters and day-laborers have thought, or what the most learned
men have thought? Especially since no view can be found not only truer but
even more useful for the life of men. For what force more keeps men from all
wickedness than if they have felt that there is no distinction in offenses?
that they sin equally if they lay hands on private men and if on magistrates?
that whatever house they have brought shame into, the stain of lust is the
same?

** Section 24
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h6
:END:

"Is there then no difference" (for someone will say this) "between killing a
father and killing a slave?" If you put those things bare, I could not easily
judge of what kind they are. If to take a father's life is in itself a crime,
the people of Saguntum, who preferred their parents to die free rather than
live as slaves, were parricides. Therefore a parent's life can sometimes be
taken without crime, and a slave's often cannot be taken without injustice. It
is the cause, then, not the nature, that distinguishes; and since, whichever
it has joined, that becomes the heavier, if it is joined to both, they must
become equal.

** Section 25
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h7
:END:

This, however, does make a difference: that in killing a slave, if it is done
unjustly, one sins once; in violating a father's life many sins are committed.
He is violated who begot, he who fed, he who taught, he who placed him in a
seat and a house and in the commonwealth. He exceeds by the number of sins and
is therefore worthy of a greater penalty. But in life we ought to look not at
what penalty belongs to each sin, but at how much is allowed to each. Whatever
one ought not to do we must think a crime, whatever is not allowed, a
wickedness. Even in the smallest things? Even so, if indeed we cannot fix a
measure of things, we can keep a measure of minds.

** Section 26
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h8
:END:

If an actor has moved a little off the beat, or if a verse has been delivered
one syllable too short or too long, he is hissed off, driven off. In life,
which ought to be more measured than every gesture, more fitted than every
verse, will you say that you have sinned in a syllable? I do not listen to a
poet in trifles: shall I listen, in the partnership of life, to a citizen
measuring out his sins with his fingers? If you like, let them be shorter: how
can they seem lighter? Since whatever is sinned is sinned by a disturbance of
reason and order, and once reason and order have been disturbed nothing can be
added by which it could seem that one can sin more.

** Section 27
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h2
:END:

That every fool is mad.

That all fools are mad.

I do not think you a fool, as often, nor a knave, as always, but mad * * *

* * * unconquered in the things needful for life * *
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He can be. Will the mind of the wise man, fenced as with walls by greatness of
counsel, by endurance of human things, by contempt of fortune, in short by all
the virtues, be conquered and taken by storm, he who cannot even be driven
from a city? For what is a city? Every gathering, even of wild and savage men?
Every multitude even of runaways and robbers collected into one place? You
will certainly say no. That, then, was not a city at that time, when the laws
had no force in it, when the courts lay fallen, when ancestral custom had
died, when the magistrates had been driven out by the sword and the name of
the Senate was not in the state. That concourse of brigands, and a banditry
set up in the Forum under your lead, and the remnants of the conspiracy turned
from Catiline's furies to your crime and madness, was not a city.

** Section 28
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h1
:END:

And so I was not driven from a city, which did not exist. I was summoned into
a city, when there was a consul in the state, who then had been none, there
was a Senate, which then had died, there was a free agreement of the people,
there was a recovered memory of law and equity, which are the bonds of a city.
And see how I despised those weapons of your banditry. I always held that the
wicked injury thrown and sent against me by you was thrown; I never thought it
had reached me, unless perhaps, when you were tearing down walls or carrying
criminal torches onto roofs, you thought that something of mine was falling or
burning.

** Section 29
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h2
:END:

Nothing is mine, or anyone's, that can be taken away, that can be snatched,
that can be lost. If you had snatched from me the divine consciousness of my
mind, that by my cares, my watches, my counsels the republic stood, though you
were most unwilling; if you had wiped out the immortal memory of this
everlasting service; much more still, if you had snatched from me that mind
from which these counsels flowed, then I would confess that I had received an
injury. But if you neither did these things nor could do them, your injury
gave me a glorious return, not a calamitous departure. Therefore I was always
a citizen, and then most of all, when the Senate was commending my safety to
foreign nations as that of an excellent citizen. You are not one even now,
unless perhaps the same man can be both an enemy and a citizen. Or do you
distinguish a citizen from an enemy by nature and place, not by mind and
deeds?

** Section 30
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h3
:END:

You made a slaughter in the Forum, you held the temples with armed robbers,
you set fire to private houses and sacred buildings. Why is Spartacus an
enemy, if you are a citizen? And can you be a citizen, you on whose account
the city at one time did not exist? And you call me by your name, when all
think that at my departure the republic went into exile? Will you never, you
most mindless of men, look around you? Will you never consider what you do,
nor what you say? Do you not know that exile is the penalty of crimes, and
that that journey of mine was undertaken for the most splendid things I had
done?

** Section 31
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h4
:END:

All the criminal and impious, whose leader you profess yourself to be, whom
the laws wish to be visited with exile, are exiles, even if they have not
left their country. Or when all the laws order you to be an exile, will you
not be an exile? Is he not to be called an exile who has gone armed?
Your dagger was seized before the Senate. Who has killed a man? You have
killed very many. Who has made a fire? The temple of the Nymphs burned down by
your hand. Who has occupied temples? You pitched a camp in the Forum.

** Section 32
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h5
:END:

But why do I bring forward the common laws, by all of which you are an exile?
Your closest friend carried a special bill about you, that if you had gone
into the secret rites of the Good Goddess you should be an exile: and you are
even accustomed to boast that you did it. How then, driven into exile by so
many laws, do you not shudder at the name of exile? "I am at Rome," he says.
And indeed you were in the secret place. A man will not, then, hold the right
of a place wherever he is, if by the laws he ought not to be there.

** Paradox V
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h1
:END:

** Section 33
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h2
:END:

That only the wise man is free and every fool a slave.

That all the wise are free and all fools slaves.

Let this man indeed be praised as a commander, or even be called one, or be
thought worthy of this name. How, or whom, after all, will this man command as
a free man, who cannot command his own desires? Let him first bridle his
lusts, scorn pleasures, hold his anger, check his greed, drive off the other
stains of the mind. Then let him begin to command others, when he himself has
ceased to obey the most wicked of masters, disgrace and shame. While he obeys
them, he is not to be counted a commander, nor even a free man at all. For
this has been finely used by the most learned, whose authority I would not use
if I had to hold this speech among some rustics; but since I speak among the
most prudent, to whom these things are not unheard, why should I pretend that
if I have put any work into these studies I have wasted it? It has been said,
then, by the most learned men that no one is free except the wise man.

** Section 34
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h3
:END:

For what is freedom? The power of living as you wish. Who then lives as he
wishes, except the man who lives rightly, who rejoices in duty, whose way of
living has been considered and planned? Who does not even obey the laws
from fear, but follows and cultivates them because he judges that to be most
wholesome; who says nothing, does nothing, thinks nothing, in short, except
willingly and freely; whose every counsel and every thing he does proceeds
from himself and is referred to the same, and there is no thing that has more
force with him than his own will and judgment; to whom even Fortune herself,
who is said to have the greatest force, yields, if, as the wise poet said,
"she is shaped for each man by his own character." This, then, befalls the
wise man alone, that he does nothing unwilling, nothing in pain, nothing under
compulsion.

** Section 35
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h4
:END:

And although this must be argued at greater length, this at least
is both short and to be granted, that unless a man is so affected, no one is
free. All the wicked, then, are slaves; and this is not so unexpected and
astonishing in the fact as in the saying. For they do not say that they are
slaves as chattels are, which have been made the property of masters by bond
or some civil right, but if slavery is, as it is, the obedience of a broken
and cast-down mind, lacking its own judgment, who would deny that all the
fickle, all the greedy, all the wicked, in short, are slaves?

** Section 36
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h5
:END:

Do I count that man free, whom a woman commands? On whom she lays laws,
prescribes, orders, forbids what she likes? Who can deny her nothing when she
commands, who dares refuse nothing? She asks, it must be given; she calls, one
must come; she throws him out, one must go; she threatens, one must be
terrified. I think that man should be called not only a slave, but the vilest
of slaves, even if he was born in the most distinguished household. And in
equal folly are those whom statues, whom pictures, whom chased silver, whom
Corinthian works, whom magnificent buildings delight too much. "But we are
leaders of the state," he says. You are not even leaders of your
fellow-slaves.

** Section 37
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h6
:END:

But as in a great household there are some slaves who are, as they think, more
elegant, and yet slaves, as the stewards, while those who handle those things,
who wipe, who oil, who sweep, who sprinkle, do not hold the most honorable
place in slavery, so in the state those who have given themselves to the
desires of those things hold almost the lowest place of slavery itself. "I
have waged great wars," he says; "I have been set over great commands and
provinces." Then bear a mind worthy of praise. A painting of Aetion holds you
stupefied, or some statue of Polyclitus. I leave aside where you took it from,
how you have it: when I see you gazing, admiring, raising shouts, I judge you
a slave of all follies.

** Section 38
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h7
:END:

"Are those things not charming, then?" They are; for we too have trained eyes.
But, I beg you, let those things be counted graceful so as not to be chains of
men, but amusements of boys. For what do you think? If Lucius Mummius saw one
of those men handling a Corinthian chamber-pot with the greatest eagerness,
when he himself had scorned all Corinth, would he think him an excellent
citizen or a careful steward? Let Manius Curius come back to life, or one of
those in whose villa and house there was nothing splendid, nothing ornate,
except themselves, and let him see someone who has used the highest favors of
the people catching little-bearded mullets from a fishpond and handling them
and boasting of his stock of morays: would he not judge this man so much a
slave that he would not think him worthy even in a household of any greater
business?

** Section 39
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h8
:END:

Or is the slavery of those in doubt, who from desire of a little store refuse
no condition of the hardest slavery? What injustice does the hope of an
inheritance not take on in serving? What nod of a rich childless old man does
he not watch? He speaks to please, he does whatever has been ordered, he
follows, he sits by, he gives gifts. What of these is of a free man?

** Section 40
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h9
:END:

What, in short, is not of a lazy slave? What of that desire which seems more
liberal, of office, of command, of provinces: how hard a mistress it is, how
imperious, how violent! It forced those who thought themselves most
distinguished to serve Cethegus, a man not of the best repute, to send gifts,
to come to his house by night, to beg, in short to supplicate. What is
slavery, if this can be thought freedom? What? When the domination of desire
has gone out and another master has arisen from the conscience of sins, fear,
how wretched is that, how hard a slavery! One must serve young men a little
too talkative. All who seem to know something are feared as masters. And the
judge, how great a domination he has, with what fear he strikes the guilty! Or
is not all fear slavery?

** Section 41
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h10
:END:

What force, then, has that speech of Lucius Crassus, a most eloquent man, more
copious than wise: "Snatch us from slavery"? What is that slavery for so
famous and so noble a man? All timidity of a weakened and low and broken mind
is slavery. "Do not allow us to be slaves to anyone." Does he wish to be
restored to freedom? Not at all; for what does he add? "Except to you all." He
wishes to change his master, not to be free. "To whom we both can and ought."
We, indeed, if we are of a high and lofty mind, built up with virtues, neither
ought nor can. You may say that you can, since indeed you can; do not say that
you ought, since no one ought anything except what it is shameful not to pay
back. But so much for this. Let him see how he can be a commander, when reason
and truth itself convict him of not even being free.

That only the wise man is rich.

That only the wise man is rich.

What is that so insolent display in recounting your money? Are you the only
rich man? Immortal gods! Am I not to rejoice that I have heard something and
learned something? The only rich man? What if not even rich? What if even
poor? For whom do we understand as rich, or in what man do we place this word?
I think in him whose possession is so great that he is easily content for
living in a free manner, who seeks nothing, aims at nothing, wishes nothing
more.

** Section 43
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h3
:END:

Your mind ought to judge itself rich, not the talk of men, nor your
possessions. He thinks nothing is wanting to him, he cares for nothing more,
he is sated or even content with money: I grant it, he is rich. But if because
of greed for money you think no gain shameful, when for that order of yours
none can even be honorable; if every day you cheat, deceive, demand, bargain,
carry off, snatch; if you strip allies, plunder the treasury; if you do not
even wait for the wills of friends and yourself substitute them: are these the
signs of a man who has abundance, or of a man in want?

** Section 44
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h4
:END:

It is the mind of a man that is usually called rich, not the money-chest.
However full that may be, while I see you empty I will not think you rich. For
from this, how much is enough for each, men measure the quantity of riches. A
man has a daughter: there is need of money; two, of more; more daughters, of
still more. If, as they say of Danaus, there were fifty daughters, so many
dowries ask for great money. For to what each needs, as I said before, the
measure of riches is fitted. How then shall I call rich a man who has not
several daughters but countless desires, which in a short time can drain the
greatest stores, when he himself feels that he is in want?

** Section 45
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h5
:END:

Many have heard you when you said that no one was rich except the man who
could keep an army from his own revenues, which the Roman people, with such
great taxes, has now for a long time scarcely been able to do. On this
proposition, then, you will never be rich before so much is returned to you
from your possessions that with it you can maintain six legions and great
supports of horse and foot. You already confess, then, that you are not rich,
you who lack so much to fill up what you long for. And so you have never borne
that poverty of yours, or rather that need and beggary, in the dark.

** Section 46

For as we understand that those who seek a living honorably by making trades,
by giving work, by taking public contracts, have need of gain, so whoever sees
in your house equally associated gangs of accusers and of informers; who sees
guilty and moneyed defendants, with you as the same agent, working at the

short, everything for sale, edict, decree, another's vote and one's own,

he has need of gain? And who that has need of gain has anyone ever truly
called rich?

** Section 47
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h7
:END:

For the fruit of riches is in plenty, and plenty is declared by satiety of
things and abundance. And since you will never reach that, you will never be
rich at all. And since you scorn my money, and rightly (for by the crowd's
opinion it is moderate, by yours it is nothing, by mine it is modest), I will
be silent about myself and speak of the thing.

** Section 48
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h8
:END:

If we had to rate and value the thing, which, after all, should we value more,
the money of Pyrrhus, which he was offering to Fabricius, or the self-command
of Fabricius, who refused that money? The gold of the Samnites, or the answer
of Manius Curius? The inheritance of Lucius Paulus, or the generosity of
Africanus, who granted his own share of that inheritance to his brother
Quintus Maximus? These things, surely, which belong to the highest virtues,
are to be valued more than those which belong to money. Who then, if indeed
each man is to be held richest as he possesses what is worth most, would doubt
that riches are in virtue? Since no possession, no mass of gold and silver, is
to be valued more than virtue.

** Section 49
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h9
:END:

Immortal gods! Men do not understand how great a revenue thrift is. For I come
now to the spenders, and I leave that money-maker. He takes six hundred
thousand sesterces from his estates, I a hundred thousand from mine. For him,
making gilded roofs in his villas and marble floors and infinitely craving
statues, pictures, furniture, and clothing, that yield is scanty not only for
expense but even for interest. From my thin revenue, when the expenses of
desire have been taken off, something will even overflow. Which then is
richer, the one who lacks or the one who has a surplus? Who is in want or who
abounds? Whose possession, the greater it is, the more it requires to maintain
itself, or that which supports itself by its own strength?

** Section 50
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h10
:END:

But why do I speak of myself, who by the fault of manners and of the times
perhaps myself also wander somewhat in the error of this age? Was Manius
Manilius, in the memory of our fathers (that we may not always talk of
Curius and Luscinus), poor, after all? For he had a little house in the
Carinae and a farm in the Labican district. Are we then richer, who have more?
Would that we were! But the measure of money is bounded not by the rating of
the census, but by living and by culture.

** Section 51
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h11
:END:

Not to be greedy is money; not to be a buyer is revenue; to be content with
one's own things is the greatest and surest riches. For if those shrewd
valuers of things value meadows and certain lots highly, because that kind of
possession can least, as it were, be harmed, at what rate is virtue to be
valued, which can neither be snatched nor stolen, is lost neither by shipwreck
nor by fire, and is not changed by the disturbance of storms or of times!

** Section 52
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h12
:END:

Those who are endowed with it are the only rich. For they alone possess things
both fruitful and everlasting, and they alone, which is the proper mark of
riches, are content with their own things, think what there is is enough, aim
at nothing, lack nothing, feel that nothing is wanting to them, seek nothing.
The wicked and the greedy, however, since they have possessions uncertain and
set in chance, and always aim at more, and not one of them has yet been found
for whom what he had was enough, are to be thought not only not well supplied
and rich, but even destitute and poor.
