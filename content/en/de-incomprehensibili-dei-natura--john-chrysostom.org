Of our father among the saints Jo. archbishop of Constantinople, Chrysostom,
concerning the incomprehensible, the bishop being absent, first discourse
against the Anomoeans. What is this? The shepherd is not present, and the
flock stands with all good order. This too is the shepherd's achievement, that
the flocks show all diligence not only when he is present but also when he is
absent. For among the irrational creatures, when the one who leads them out to
pasture is not there, the sheep must remain inside the folds, or, if they poke
out of the pen without the one who shepherds, they undergo much wandering; but
here there is nothing of that sort, but even though the shepherd is not
present, you have come to the accustomed pastures with much good order.
Rather, the shepherd is present too, if not in the flesh, yet in disposition,
if not by the presence of the body, yet by the good order of the flock. For
this I am the more struck and I bless him, that he was able to deposit such
diligence in you. For we admire a general most of all when even in his absence
the armies keep good order. This Paul also sought in the disciples, saying:
"So that, my beloved, as you have always obeyed, not as in my presence only,
but much more in my absence ..." Why: "Much more in the absence"? Because when
the one who shepherds is present, even if a wolf comes upon the flock, he is
easily driven from the sheep; but when he is not present the flocks must stand
in greater agony, there being no one to wall them. And besides this, when he
is present he shares with them the wages for the diligence, but when he is not
present he leaves their achievement to appear naked. These things the teacher
also, being absent, discourses to you, and wherever he may now be, he imagines
you and your assembly, and he does not so much see those who are with him
there and present as he now sees you who are absent. I know his love, boiling
and fired and hot and unrestrainable, which he has rooted at much depth of
mind and which he tends with much diligence. For he knows clearly that this is
the head of all goods and root and spring and mother, and when this is not, we
have no benefit from the rest. For this is the image of the Lord's disciples,
the character of the servants of God, the mark of the apostles: "For in this
all will know," he says, "that you are my disciples." In what? Tell: is it in
raising the dead or cleansing lepers or driving out demons? No, he says, but
passing by all those: "In this all will know," he says, "that you are my
disciples, if you love one another." For those are gifts of grace from above
alone, but this is also an achievement of human diligence. And the noble man
is not so accustomed to be characterized by the gifts given from above as by
the achievements of his own labors. Therefore Christ says his own disciples
are known not from the signs but from love. For when this is present no part
of philosophy is lacking to the one who has it, but he has virtue whole and
complete and finished, just as when it is absent he is barren of all goods.
Therefore Paul too lifts it and exalts it in speech; or rather, whatever he
may say, he does not yet reach its worth. For what could become equal to this,
which holds together the prophets and the whole law, and without which neither
faith nor knowledge nor acquaintance with mysteries nor martyrdom itself nor
anything else will be able to save the one who has it? For: "If I give my
body," he says, "to be burned, but have not love, I am benefited nothing." And
elsewhere again, indicating that it is greater than all and the peak of goods,
he said: "But whether prophecies

will be abolished, or tongues will cease, or knowledge will be abolished; but
faith, hope, love remain, these three; and greater than all is love." But the
speech about love has brought in for us no chance inquiry. For that prophecy
should be abolished and tongues cease is no hardship; for these gifts, having
furnished us their own use for a season, even when they have ceased will not
be able to harm the word at all; see at any rate that now there is no
prophecy, nor gift of tongues, and the word of piety has been hindered in
nothing; but that knowledge should cease, this is the thing sought. For having
said: "Whether prophecies will be abolished, or tongues will cease," he added:
"or knowledge will be abolished." If then knowledge is going to cease, our
affairs will advance not toward the better but toward the worse; for without
this we shall utterly lose even being men. "For fear God," it says, "and keep
his commandments, because this is every man." If then this is man, to fear
God, and this comes from knowledge, and knowledge is going to be abolished, as
Paul says, then we shall utterly perish, there being no knowledge, and all
that is ours will be gone, and we shall be disposed no better than the
irrational creatures, but much worse. For in this we have the advantage of
them, since in all the other bodily things we are beaten by them from a great
surplus. What then is it that Paul says, and concerning what, that "Knowledge
will be abolished"? He says this not concerning the complete but concerning
the partial, calling abolition the advance toward the better, so that the
partial, being abolished, is no longer partial but perfect. For just as the
age of the child is abolished, not by the substance being made to vanish, but
by the age increasing and coming out into a perfect man, so also it happens in
the case of knowledge. This small one, he says, would no longer be small,
having become great through what follows. This is the "is abolished," and this
he made clearer to us through what follows. For lest, having heard that it is
abolished, you suppose it is a complete dissolution, but some increase and
addition toward the better, having said "is abolished," he added: "For we know
in part and we prophesy in part; but when the perfect comes, then that which
is in part will be abolished," so that it is no longer in part but perfect. So
that its imperfect is abolished, so that it is no longer imperfect but
perfect. This abolition then is a filling and an addition toward the greater.
And consider for me Paul's understanding. For he did not say: We know a part,
but: "We know in part," we hold a part of the part. Perhaps you desire to hear
also how much part we hold and how much has been left, and whether we hold the
more or the less. Therefore, that you may learn that you hold the less, and
not simply the less, but the hundredth or the ten-thousandth, hear what
follows; or rather, before I read you the apostolic voice, I will tell an
example able to set before you, as far as it is possible from an example, how
much has been left and how much it is that we now hold. How much then is the
interval between the knowledge that is going to be given us and the present
one now? As much as the interval of a perfect man toward a suckling child; for
so great is the superiority of the knowledge to come toward the present. And
that this is true and that that is so much greater than this, let Paul himself
say again. For having said: "We know in part" and wishing to show from how
much a part and that we now hold the least, he added: "When I was an infant, I
spoke as an infant, I thought as an infant, I reasoned as an infant; but when
I have become a man, I have abolished the things of the infant," comparing the
present knowledge with the condition of an infant, and the knowledge to come
with that of a perfect man. And he did not say: When I was a child, for a
twelve-year-old also is called a child, but: "When I was an infant," showing
us the one still at the breast and still fed with milk and suckling. For that
Scripture calls this one an infant, hear the psalm saying: "O Lord our Lord,
how wonderful is your name in all the

earth, because your magnificence was lifted up above the heavens; out of the
mouth of infants and sucklings you have prepared praise." Do you see that
everywhere he calls the infant the suckling? Then, foreseeing in the spirit
the shamelessness of the men to come, he was not satisfied with this example
only, but by a second and a third he made this sure for us. For just as Moses,
being sent to the Jews, took proof of three signs, so that if they disbelieve
the first they may hear the voice of the second, and if they despise this too,
being ashamed at the third they may receive the prophet, so this man also sets
three examples: one that of the child, saying: "When I was an infant, I
thought as an infant," a second that of the mirror, and a third that of the
riddle. For having said: "When I was an infant," he added: "But we see now
through a mirror in a riddle." See also a second example of the present
weakness and of knowledge being imperfect; a third again the "in a riddle."
For the child sees many things and hears and speaks, but nothing clear does he
either see or hear or speak; and he thinks, but nothing articulated. So I too
know many things, but I do not understand their manner. For that God is
everywhere I know, and that he is altogether everywhere; but the how I do not
know. That he is without beginning and eternal I know; but the how I do not
know. For reasoning does not admit knowing how it is possible for a substance
to be that has being neither from itself nor from another. I know that he
generated a Son, but the how I am ignorant of; I know that the Spirit is from
him, but the how from him I do not understand. I eat foods, but how they are
divided into phlegm, into blood, into humor, into bile, I am ignorant. These
things which we see every day as we eat we are ignorant of, and we busy
ourselves with the substance of God? Where then are those who say they have
received the whole of knowledge, who have fallen into the pit of ignorance?
For those who say they have received the whole according to the present set
themselves barren of complete knowledge for the future. For I who say that I
know in part, even if I say that this knowledge is abolished, travel toward
the better and more perfect, the partial being abolished and becoming more
perfect; but that one who says he has complete and whole and perfect
knowledge, then confessing that it is abolished in the future, shows himself
barren of knowledge, this being abolished and another more perfect not being
brought in, if according to them this is the perfect. Do you see how,
contending here to have the whole, they will have neither what is here, and
there they will cast themselves out of the whole? So great an evil it is not
to remain inside the bounds which God set us from the beginning. So also Adam,
in hope of more honor, fell even from what he had; so also it happens with the
money-lovers; many often, desiring greater things, have lost even the present;
so also these, expecting to have the whole here, have fallen even from the
part. Therefore I exhort you to flee their madness; for I for my part say it
is the last madness to contend to know what God is in substance. And that you
may learn that this is of last madness, I will make it plain to you from the
prophets; for the prophets appear not only ignorant of what he is in
substance, but they are also at a loss concerning his wisdom, how great it is;
and yet it is not the substance from the wisdom, but the wisdom from the
substance. And when the prophets cannot even grasp this with exactness, of how
great madness would it be to suppose one can subject the substance itself to
one's own reasonings? Let us hear then what the prophet says concerning it:
"Your knowledge was made wonderful from me." Or rather let us bring the word
higher: "I will confess to you, that you were wonderfully made in a fearful
way." What is "in a fearful way"? Many things we admire only, but not with
fear, such as beauty of columns, painting of walls, flowers of bodies; we
admire again the open sea and the boundless deep, but with fear, when we stoop
toward the depth. So then also the prophet, having stooped toward the
boundless and yawning sea of the wisdom of God and grown dizzy,

having admired with much fear, withdrew crying and saying: "I will confess to
you that you were wonderfully made in a fearful way; wonderful are your
works." And again: "Your knowledge was made wonderful from me, it was
strengthened, I shall not be able toward it." Do you see the good sense of a
servant? "I thank you," he says, "for this, that I have an incomprehensible
Master," not speaking here concerning substance; for that, as agreed to be
incomprehensible, he passed by; but concerning God's being present everywhere
he says these things, showing that he does not even know this itself, how he
is present everywhere. For that he speaks concerning these things, hear what
follows: "If I go up into heaven, you are there; if I go down into Hades, you
are present." Did you see how he is present everywhere? But the prophet does
not know, but grows dizzy and is at a loss and is troubled merely taking it
into mind? How then is it not of last folly for those so far from that man's
grace to busy themselves with the substance of God itself? And yet this is the
prophet who says: "The unclear and the hidden things of your wisdom you made
plain to me." But nevertheless, having learned the unclear and the hidden
things of his wisdom, he says concerning this itself that it is unapproachable
and incomprehensible: "For great is the Lord," he says, "and great is his
strength and of his understanding there is no number," that is, there is no
grasping. What do you say? The wisdom is incomprehensible to the prophet and
the substance is comprehensible to us? is this madness not plain? His
greatness has no limit and you circumscribe his substance? These things Isaiah
also, philosophizing, said: "His generation who will narrate?" He did not say:
who narrates, but: "Who will narrate" and he shut off the future. And David
says: "Your knowledge was made wonderful from me"; but Isaiah says not from
himself, but that this narration is shut off from all human nature. But let us
see whether perhaps Paul knew, as having enjoyed greater grace; he himself is
the one who says: "We know in part and we prophesy in part." And not here
only, but elsewhere also, discoursing not concerning substance but concerning
wisdom, and himself concerning that which appears in providence, not the
universal I mean, according to which he provides for angels and archangels and
the powers above, but examining that part of providence according to which he
provides for men on earth, and again a part of this itself. For he does not
even examine this whole, according to which he makes the sun rise, according
to which he breathes souls, according to which he forms bodies, according to
which he feeds men on earth, according to which he holds the world together,
according to which he gives the yearly foods; but leaving all these and
examining a small part of his providence, according to which he cast out the
Jews and received those from the nations, and toward this small part itself,
as toward a boundless sea having grown dizzy and seen a yawning depth, he
leapt away at once and cried out greatly saying: "O depth of wealth and wisdom
and knowledge of God, how unsearchable are his judgments." He did not say:
incomprehensible, but "unsearchable." And if it cannot be searched, much more
is it impossible to be grasped. "And his ways untraceable." His ways are
untraceable and he himself comprehensible? Tell me. And why do I speak
concerning the ways? The prizes laid up for us are incomprehensible: "For eye
has not seen them, nor ear heard, nor have they gone up upon a man's heart,
the things God prepared for those who love him." And his gift is unspeakable:
"Thanks be to God," he says, "for his unspeakable gift" and "his peace
surpasses every mind." What do you say? His judgments unsearchable, his ways
untraceable, the peace surpasses every mind, the gift unspeakable, what God
prepared for those who love him has not gone up upon a man's heart, the
greatness has no limit, the understanding has no number, all things
incomprehensible, and he alone comprehensible? And what excess of madness
would this not have? Hold the heretic; do not let him withdraw. Say: what does
Paul say? "We know in part." He does not speak concerning the substance, but
concerning the economies. Most of all: even if the word were concerning the
economies, from more

abundance for us even so the victory-prizes; for if the economies are
incomprehensible, much more he himself; but that here he does not speak
concerning the economies, but concerning God himself, hear what follows. For
having said: "We know in part and we prophesy in part," he added: "Now I know
in part, but then I shall know fully even as I also was known fully." By whom
then was he known fully, by God or by the economies? By God, clearly; this one
then he also knows in part. And he said "in part," not because he knows one
thing of his substance and is ignorant of another, for God is simple, but
because that God is he knows, but what he is in substance he is ignorant of;
that he is wise he understands, but how much he is wise he is ignorant of;
that he is great he is not ignorant of, but how or what his greatness is, this
he does not know; that he is present everywhere he understands, but the how he
does not understand; that he provides and holds together all things and keeps
them with exactness he is not ignorant of, but the manner according to which
he does these things he is ignorant of. Therefore he said: "We know in part
and we prophesy in part." But come, if it seems good, leaving Paul and the
prophets, let us go up into the heavens, lest perhaps there some are who know
what God is in substance. For most of all, even if they are found knowing,
there is nothing common toward us; for great is the interval of angels and
men; yet nevertheless, that from abundance you may learn that not even there
does any created power know this, let us hear the angels. What then? Do they
discourse there concerning this substance and inquire among themselves? By no
means. But what? They glorify, they worship, they send up continually the
victory and mystical odes with much shuddering; and some say: "Glory in the
highest to God"; and the Seraphim: "Holy, holy, holy" and they turn away their
eyes, not even able to bear the condescension of God; and the Cherubim:
"Blessed is his glory from his place," not that there is a place about God,
let it not be, but as we might say speaking humanly: wherever he is or however
he is, if indeed even this is safe to say concerning God; for we have a human
tongue. Did you see how much fear above, how much contempt below? Those
glorify, these busy themselves; those speak well, these meddle; those hide
their faces, these contend to gaze shamelessly into the unspeakable glory. Who
would not groan, who would not lament them for this derangement and last
folly? I wished then to lead the word further, but since I have now first come
down into these wrestling-matches, I think it is profitable for you to be
satisfied for the present with what has been said, so that the multitude of
what is going to be said, coming on with much rush, may not sweep away the
memory of these things too; and at any rate, if God permits, we shall work
this subject henceforth for a long time. For I too long ago was in labor to
speak these words to you, but I was about to and I delayed, because I saw many
of those sick with these things listening to us with pleasure, and not wishing
to scare away the hunt, I held my tongue for the present from these
wrestling-matches, so that after holding them exactly I might then strip; but
since through the grace of God I heard them exhorting and pressing us to enter
these contests, I stripped with confidence henceforth and took the weapons
that pull down reasonings and every height lifting itself against the
knowledge of God. And I took these weapons not that I may strike the
opponents, but that I may raise those who lie; for this is the power of these
weapons, they know how to strike those who contend, and to heal with much care
those who hear with good sense; they do not give wounds, but they heal
diseases. Let us not then be savage toward those, nor put forward anger, but
let us discourse with them with gentleness; for nothing is stronger than
gentleness and meekness. Therefore Paul also with much diligence commanded us
to hold to this matter saying: "And a servant of the Lord must not fight, but
be mild toward all." He did not say toward

the brothers only, but toward all. And again: "Let your gentleness be known,"
he did not say: to the brothers, but "to all men." For what, he says, is the
benefit, if you love those who love you? If then their friendships harm and
drag toward communion of impiety, even if they are those who generated you,
leap away; even if it is an eye, dig it out: "For if," he says, "your right
eye scandalizes you, cut it out," not speaking concerning a body; for how? For
if he were speaking concerning the nature of the body, the charge would pass
to the craftsman of nature; and otherwise one ought not to dig out one; for
even if the left is left, it scandalizes the one who has it likewise. But that
you may learn that the word is not concerning an eye, he added the right,
showing that even if you have someone as a right eye, cast this one out too
and cut him from friendship toward you, if he scandalizes you. For what
benefit is it to have an eye for the ruin of the rest of the body? If then, as
I said, the friendships harm, let us flee and leap away; but if they wrong us
in nothing toward the word of piety, let us drag and draw those to ourselves;
but if you neither benefit him and you receive the harm from him, gain
remaining unharmed through the cutting, and flee their friendships, if they
harm; only flee, do not fight, nor make war. So also Paul exhorts saying: "If
possible, as far as it is from you, being at peace with all men." You are a
servant of the God of peace; that one, casting out demons and working ten
thousand goods, when they called him possessed, did not send a thunderbolt,
did not crush those who abused, did not burn the tongue so shameless and
thankless, though able to do all these things, but he beat off the accusation
only saying: "I do not have a demon, but I honor the one who sent me." And
when the servant of the high priest also struck him, what does he say? "If I
spoke badly, bear witness concerning the bad; but if well, why do you beat
me?" The Master of the angels apologizes and gives accounts to a household
servant; there is no need of longer words. Only turn these sayings in the mind
and study them continually and say: "If I spoke badly, bear witness concerning
the bad; but if well, why do you beat me?" And consider the one who speaks and
toward whom he says it and for what reason, and these words will be for you
some divine charm and continual, and they will be able to settle every
inflammation; consider the dignity of the one insulted, the cheapness of the
one who insulted, the excess of the insult. For he did not only abuse, but
also struck; and he did not strike simply, but also slapped; and nothing is
more dishonorable than this blow; but nevertheless he bore all, that from
abundance you may learn to be temperate. Let us philosophize these things not
only here, but also when there is occasion let us remember them. You praised
what was said, but show me the praise in the works. For the wrestler also
trains in the palaestra, that in the contests he may show the benefit of the
training there; you too then, of the hearing here, when anger comes on, show
the gain and say this saying continually: "If I spoke badly, bear witness
concerning the bad; but if well, why do you beat me?" Write this on the mind;
therefore I continually remind you of these words, so that all that has been
said may be put into your soul, so that the memory may remain inerasable and
the benefit from the memory. For if we have these sayings written on the
breadth of our mind, no one is so stony and thankless and senseless as ever to
be carried out toward anger; for instead of every bit and every rein, these
sayings will be able to hold our tongue when it is carried beyond measure and
what is fitting, and to settle the mind when it swells, and to make it be
moderate continually, and to settle in us whole the peace of which may it be
that we enjoy throughout, by grace and philanthropy of our Lord Jesus Christ,
with whom to the Father glory together with the Holy Spirit, now and always
and unto the ages of the ages. Amen. 2 Of the same, before

many days having spoken against the Anomoeans, then against the Jews, then
having been silent because of presence of bishops and many memorials of
martyrs that took place, now again against the Anomoeans concerning the
incomprehensible, second discourse. Come then, let us strip again toward the
unbelieving Anomoeans; and if they are vexed at being called unbelievers, let
them flee the thing, and I hide the name; let them stand off from the
unbelieving mind, and I stand off from the reproachful appellation. But if
they themselves, dishonoring the faith through the works and shaming
themselves, do not sink, for what reason are they displeased at us who through
words charge them with what they themselves show through works? For recently,
when we went down into the stadium of these words, as indeed you remember, and
took hold of the same wrestling-matches, the contests toward the Jews at once
succeeded us, and it was not safe to pass by our own members that had become
sick. For the words against the Anomoeans always have a season; but then, if
we had not anticipated and at once snatched from the Jewish pyre those of our
brothers who were sick and diseased with the Jewish things, there would have
been no benefit left to us from the exhortation, the sin concerning the fast
having already advanced for them. And after the contests toward those, again
there succeeded a presence of many spiritual fathers arrived here from many
places, and not even then was it well-timed, while all those were flowing like
some rivers into this spiritual sea, for our word to be stretched out; and
after their departure successive and continual memorials of martyrs met us,
and one ought not to despise the blessing of those athletes. And I say and
enumerate these things, lest you suppose that the delay of the contests toward
those came to us from some hesitation and slackness. Now at any rate, since we
have been released henceforth from the fight toward the Jews, and the fathers
have returned to their own fatherlands, and we have enjoyed the blessing of
the martyrs sufficiently, come then henceforth let us loose the long labor of
our hearing. For I know well that each of you is in labor no less than I who
speak to hear the words concerning these things; and the cause is, our city is
Christ-loving from above, and you have succeeded to this ancestral
inheritance, never to overlook the doctrines of piety being adulterated. From
where is this clear? Some came down from Judea once in the time of your
ancestors, muddying the pure doctrines of the apostolic teaching, and ordering
them to be circumcised and to keep the law of Moses. Those then inhabiting
your city did not bear this innovation in silence; but like some noble pups
seeing wolves coming in and destroying the whole flock, so leaping upon those
they did not stand off sooner, scaring them from everywhere and driving them,
until they arranged that doctrines be sent by the apostles everywhere in the
inhabited world walling off both those and all after them from such an assault
against the faithful. From where then must we begin the words toward those?
From where else but from the accusation concerning unbelief? For they do and
busy themselves with everything so as to thrust faith out of the mind of the
hearers, than which what greater charge of impiety could there be? For when
God declares something, one must receive what was said faithfully, not busy
oneself rashly. Let whoever wishes of them call me unbelieving, but I am not
vexed. Why? Because through the works I show the appellation. And why do I
say: Let him call me unbelieving? let him call me also a fool in Christ? And
at this again I exult as at a crown; and I share this appellation with Paul.
For that one says: "We are fools because of Christ." This folly is more
prudent than all wisdom. For what the outside wisdom was not able to find,
these things the folly according to Christ accomplished; this drove away the
darkness of the inhabited world, this brought back the light of knowledge. And
what is folly according to

Christ? When we settle our own reasonings raging unseasonably, when we make
our mind empty and void of the outside education, so that, when it is
necessary to receive the things of Christ, we furnish it idle and swept for
reception of the divine words. For when God declares something that must not
be busied over, one must receive it by faith. For in such things to busy
oneself with the causes and to demand the accounts and to seek the manner
belongs to a most rash and reckless soul. And this again I will try to set
from the Scriptures themselves. There was a certain Zacharias, a wonderful and
great man, honored with high-priesthood, entrusted by God with the presidency
of the whole people; this Zacharias, having entered into the holy of holies,
into the very inaccessible things which it was then lawful for that one alone
of all men to see, consider how he was a counterweight of the whole multitude,
and offering prayers for the whole multitude to God and making the Master
propitious to the household servants, being as it were some mediator of God
and men, he saw an angel standing within, and, since the sight struck the man,
that one says: "Do not fear, Zacharias; your petition has been heard and you
will beget a son." And what sequence is this? He was exhorting for the people,
he was begging for the sins, he was asking pardon for fellow-servants, and he
says: "Do not fear; for your petition has been heard," and he makes the proof
of its being heard that the child John will be born to him. And very
reasonably. For since he was exhorting for the sins of the people, and he was
going to beget a son crying: "See the lamb of God who takes away the sin of
the world," reasonably he says: "Your petition has been heard and you will
beget a son." What then did that one? For the thing sought is this, that to
busy oneself with manners of divine oracles is unpardonable, but one must
receive the declarations by faith. He looked to his own age, to the grayness,
to the body that had become weak; he looked to the barrenness of the woman and
he disbelieved and sought to learn the manner and said: "According to what
shall I know this?" How, he says, will this be? See, I have grown old and
become gray, and my wife is barren, advanced in her days; the age is untimely,
the nature useless; how do the things promised have reason? I the sower am
weak; the field is barren. Does he not seem to some to be worthy of pardon,
seeking sequence of things and seeming to say something reasonable? But to God
he did not seem to be worthy. And very reasonably. For when God declares, one
must not move reasonings, nor put forward sequence of things, nor necessity of
nature, nor anything else of such things; for the power of the declaration is
above all these, being cut by no hindrance. What are you doing, man? God
promises, and you flee to age and put forward old age? Is old age stronger
than the promise of God? Is nature more powerful than the craftsman of nature?
Do you not know that his words are strong works? His word set the heaven; his
word brought forth the creation; his word made angels, and you doubt
concerning a birth? For these things the angel was vexed, and he did not
pardon even because of the priesthood; rather through it he was punished more.
For the one who had more than the others in account of honor ought also to
have the advantage of the others concerning the faith. And what was the manner
of the punishment? "See, you will be silent, and not able to speak." Your
tongue, he says, which served toward the unbelief of the words, this will also
receive the punishment upon the unbelief: "See, you will be silent and not
speaking, until these things happen." Consider philanthropy of a Master. You
disbelieve me, he says; now receive the punishment, and when I furnish the
proof through the things, then I loose the anger; when you learn that you were
justly punished, then I release you from the punishment. Let the Anomoeans
hear how God is vexed when he is busied over. And if that one, having
disbelieved the mortal birth, is punished, you who busy yourself with the
unspeakable and the one above, how will you escape punishment? Tell me.

That man did not declare, but wished to learn, and he did not obtain pardon;
but you, even insisting that you know the things unseen and incomprehensible
to all, what defense will you have? And what punishment will you not draw upon
yourself? But let the words concerning generation wait the fitting seasons;
for the present let us walk by a road upon the former subject, whose remnants
we left recently, attempting to pull out the destructive root, the mother of
all the evils and from where these doctrines sprouted for them. And what is
the root of all the evils? Believe me, shuddering holds me as I am about to
utter it; for I hesitate to bring out through tongue what those practice in
the mind continually. What then is the root of these evils? A man has dared to
say that I know God as God himself knows himself. Do these things then need
refutation, these proof? Is not the mere utterance of the words enough to show
all their impiety? For this is some madness, clear derangement unpardonable, a
newer manner of impiety; no one ever dared either to cast such a thing into
mind or to bring it forward through the tongue. Consider, wretched and
miserable one, who you are and whom you busy yourself with; being a man you
meddle with God? For the bare names suffice to show the excess of the folly, a
man being earth and ash, flesh and blood, grass and flower of grass, shadow
and smoke and vanity, and if anything is weaker and cheaper than these. And do
not suppose what is said to be an accusation of nature; for I do not say these
things, but the prophets philosophize these, not dishonoring our race, but
settling the puff of the mindless; not cheapening our nature, but humbling the
madness of those who rave. For if, though those have said so many and such
things, some nevertheless appeared who surpassed the devil's boastfulness, if
none of these had been said, where would they not have kicked out in madness?
Tell me. They have the drug laid on and they still inflame; and where would
they not have been puffed with bulk and madness, if those had not declared so
many and such things concerning human nature? Hear at any rate what the just
patriarch says concerning himself: "And I am earth and ash." He was
discoursing with God, and the freedom of speech did not lift him; this itself
then, this persuaded him to be moderate. But these, not even being worthy of
that man's shadow, suppose themselves to be greater than the angels
themselves, which is a proof of last madness. God, tell me, you busy yourself
with, the one without beginning, the unchangeable, the bodiless, the
incorruptible, the one present everywhere, and surpassing all things and being
above all creation. Hear what the prophets philosophize concerning him and
fear: "He who looks upon the earth and makes it tremble." He only saw and he
shook so great and such an earth. "He who touches the mountains, and they
smoke ... he who shakes what is under heaven from foundations, and its pillars
are shaken; he who threatens the sea and dries it; he who says to the abyss:
You shall be made desolate." "The sea saw and fled; the Jordan withdrew
backward; the mountains skipped like rams and the hills like lambs of sheep."
All creation is shaken, fears, trembles; but these alone despise, overlook,
cheapen their own salvation; for I would not say the Master of all. Recently
then we were making them temperate from the powers above, angels, archangels,
the Cherubim, the Seraphim; but now from the unfeeling creation, and not even
from here are they put to shame. Do you not see this heaven, how beautiful,
how great, how it is crowned with the varied chorus of stars? How much time
has it lasted? For five thousand and more years henceforth it has stood, and
the multitude of time has not brought old age upon it; but just as a young and
vigorous body has the season of its age blooming and in its prime, so also
heaven has kept the beauty which it obtained from the beginning and has become
nothing weaker by time. But

this one, the beautiful, the great, the bright, the starred, the lasting, the

the prophet, not even able to find an example of the ease, said that he made
the earth "as nothing." For since the sizes and the beauties of the things
seen did not suffice to set forth the power of the craftsman, but fell short
by much measure of the size and the strength of him who made them all, the
prophets found another manner, through which according to their own power they
were able to indicate to us something more concerning the power of God. What
then is this? Not to set forward the sizes of the creatures only, but also to
say the manner of the crafting, so that from both, both from the size of the
things that have come to be and from the ease according to the crafting, we
may be able to take some thought worthy according to our power concerning the
power of God. Do not then examine the size of the things that have come to be
only, but also the ease of him who made. And this is shown not upon earth
only, but also upon the nature of men itself. For now he says: "He who holds
the circle of the earth, and those who dwell in it as locusts," and now he
says: "All the nations as a drop from a bucket before him." Do not pass the
word simply, but unfold well what was said and examine; reckon all the
nations, Syrians, Cilicians, Cappadocians, Bithynians, those who inhabit the
Euxine sea, Thrace, Macedonia, all Greece, those in the islands, those in
Italy, those beyond the inhabited world according to us, those in the British
islands, Sauromatians, Indians, those who inhabit the land of the Persians,
the other boundless races and tribes whose names we do not even know; but all
these nations "... as a drop," he says, "from a bucket before him." How much a
part are you of this drop, tell me, that you busy yourself with the God before
whom "all the nations as a drop from a bucket"? And why must one speak
concerning heaven and earth and sea and nature of men? Let us go up above
heaven by the word and come upon the angels. For you know this surely, that of
this visible creation one angel alone is a counterweight; or rather even much
more honorable. For if the whole world would not be worthy of a just man, as
Paul says: "... of whom this world was not worthy," much more it would never
become worthy of an angel; for angels are much greater than the just. But
nevertheless there are ten thousand ten thousands of angels above and a
thousand thousands of archangels and thrones and lordships and beginnings and
authorities and boundless peoples of bodiless powers and unspeakable tribes,
and all these powers he made with such ease as no word is able to set forth.
For it sufficed him to will toward all things, and just as for us the wishing
furnishes no toil, so neither for him the crafting of so many and such powers.
And the prophet, showing this, said: "All things whatsoever he willed he made
in heaven and on earth." Do you see that not toward the crafting of the things
on earth only, but also toward the creation of the powers above, his will
alone sufficed? Hearing these things then do you not lament yourself, tell me,
and bury yourself toward

such a pitch of madness having been lifted, that him whom one must only
glorify and worship, this one you contend to busy yourself with and meddle
with as one of the cheapest things? Therefore Paul also, filled with much
wisdom, seeing the incomparable superiority of God and the cheapness of human
nature, being vexed toward those who busy themselves with his economies and
being displeased with much intensity, said: "Nay but, O man, who are you who
answer back to God?" Who are you? Consider your nature first; for there is no
name to find able to set forth your nothingness. But you will say that I am a
man honored with freedom. But you were honored not that you may use the
freedom toward contradiction, but that you may use the honor toward obedience
of him who honored. God honored you not that you may insult him, but that you
may glorify; and he insults who busies himself with his substance. For if not
to busy oneself with his promises is to glorify him, to meddle and search
through not only the declarations but also the one who declared is to
dishonor. And that not to busy oneself with his promises is to glorify him,
hear Paul saying concerning Abraham and that man's obedience and faith in all
things: "For he considered," he says, "his own body as good as dead, and the
deadness of Sarah's womb; but toward the promise of God he did not waver in
unbelief, but was empowered in faith." The nature and the age, he says, were
casting into despair, but the faith stretched under good hopes. But he was
empowered in faith giving glory to God and being fully persuaded that what he
has promised he is able also to do. Do you see that the one who is fully
persuaded concerning whatever God declares gives glory to God? If then the one
who believes him gives glory to God, the one who disbelieves him turns his
dishonor upon his own head. "Who are you who answer back to God?" Then wishing
to show how great the interval is of man and God, he showed not as much as one
ought, yet from the example given it is possible to take a thought of a much
greater difference. For what does he say? "Shall the thing formed say to him
who formed: why did you make me thus? Or does the potter not have authority of
the clay from the same lump to make one vessel unto honor, and one unto
dishonor?" What do you say? Must I thus be subject to God as the clay to the
potter? Yes, he says; for so great is the interval of man and God as of clay
and potter; or rather not even so great is the interval, but even much more.
For of clay and potter there is one substance, as also it has been said in
Job: "But I let alone those who inhabit clay houses, from which we ourselves
also are from the same clay." And if man appears better and more seemly than
that, the difference the change of nature has not made, but the wisdom of the
craftsman; since you have stood apart from clay in nothing. And if you
disbelieve, let the coffins and the chests persuade you; for having gone away
to the tombs of the ancestors you will know that these things are so. Of clay
then and potter there is no interval; but of God and of the substance of men
so great is the interval as not even word is able to set forth, nor thought
has room to measure. As therefore the clay follows the hands of the potter,
wherever that one draws and leads around, so you too according to the clay be
voiceless, whenever God wishes to economy something. For Paul has not said
these things taking away our authority, let it not be, nor harming the
self-determined, but from much abundance stopping our audacity. And if it
seems good, let us see this too. What then did those wish to learn, and he
stopped them so intensely? Were they busying themselves with substance? By no
means; for no one ever dared this, but the much less, they were seeking
economies of God; why is so-and-so punished and why is so-and-so pitied; why
is so-and-so released from punishment, and the other is in terrible things;
and one obtained pardon, and the other did not obtain. For these and such
things they were seeking. From where is this clear? From the things above
these; for having said: "So then whom he wills he pities, and whom he wills

he hardens." You will say then to me: Why does he still blame? For who has
stood against his will? Then he added: "Nay but, O man, who are you who answer
back to God?" Paul then stops those who seek to busy themselves with
economies. Then to those not even these things are permitted; but you,
meddling with the blessed substance itself that economies all things, do you
not think yourself worthy of ten thousand thunderbolts? and how are these
things not of last madness? Hear the prophet saying, or rather God through
that one: "If I am a father, where is my glory? and if I am Lord, where is my
fear?" For the one who fears does not busy himself, but worships, does not
meddle, but speaks well and glorifies. Let the powers above also teach you
these things and the blessed Paul; for he does not rebuke these things in
others, and himself is not so disposed. Hear at any rate what he says to the
Philippians, showing that he has partial recognition, as also writing to the
Corinthians he said that "We know in part" and of the whole not yet, and now
he has taken hold: "Brothers, I do not reckon myself to have grasped." What is
clearer than this voice? More brightly than a trumpet he cried, teaching the
whole inhabited world to love and to be content with the given measure of
knowledge and not to suppose they have received the whole now. What do you
say? Tell me: you have Christ speaking in yourself, and you say that "I do not
reckon myself to have grasped"? For this very reason, he says, I said it,
because I have Christ speaking in myself; he himself taught me these things.
So that these too, if they were not completely barren of the Spirit's help and
had not thrust all his energy from their own soul, would not, when Paul says:
"I do not reckon myself to have grasped," have supposed themselves to have
taken hold of the whole. And from where is it clear, he says, that he says
these things concerning faith and knowledge and doctrines, and not concerning
life and conduct; that I reckon myself imperfect in life and conduct? Most of
all he made this clear saying: "I have fought the good fight, I have finished
the course, I have kept the faith; henceforth there is laid up for me the
crown of righteousness." And the one who is going to receive the crown and has
finished the course would not have said: "I do not reckon myself to have
grasped." And otherwise the things to be done and not to be done are unclear
to none of men, but clear and known to all, both barbarians and Persians and
every race of men. And that I may make what I say even clearer, I will read
the sequence of the passage itself. For having said: "See the dogs, see the
evil workers" and having discoursed much concerning those who introduce Jewish
doctrines unseasonably, he added saying: "What things were gains to me, these
I have reckoned loss because of Christ; but rather I will also reckon all
things loss, that I may be found not having righteousness that is from law,
but the righteousness from God, that through faith of Jesus Christ." Then he
says of what sort of faith: "... to know him and the power of his resurrection
and the communion of his sufferings." What is "power of his resurrection"? A
new manner of resurrection, he says, was shown. For many dead also before that
one often rose, but thus not even one. For all the others, having risen,
returned again to the earth and, being released for a time from the tyranny of
death, were led again under that one's rule; but the master's body, having
risen, did not return to the earth, but went up into the heavens and dissolved
all the enemy's tyranny and raised up with itself the whole inhabited world
and now sits on the royal throne. Paul then, considering all these things and
showing that no reasoning will be able to set forth so many and such wonders,
but faith alone can teach and make them clear, said upon the faith: "... to
know the power of his resurrection." For if reasoning cannot set forth
resurrection simply, for this is greater than human nature and the sequence of
things, the resurrection that differs so much from the other resurrections,
what reasoning will be able

to set forth? There is none; but we need faith only, if we are going to be
persuaded that a mortal body both rose and came into immortal life, having no
limit nor end; which then also elsewhere signifying he said: "Christ having
been raised dies no more, death no longer lords it over him," so that the
wonder is double, both the rising and the rising thus. Therefore he said upon
the faith: "... to know the power of his resurrection." And if it is not
possible to find the resurrection by reasonings, much more the generation
above. And Paul, discoursing concerning these things and speaking concerning
the cross and the passion, entrusted those too to the power of faith; then
having moved this whole word, going forward he said: "Brothers, I do not
reckon myself to have grasped." He did not say: I do not reckon myself to
know, but "to have grasped"; he testified to himself neither complete
ignorance nor complete knowledge. For to say: "I do not reckon to have
grasped" is of one making clear that he still stands on the road and walks and
goes further, but has not at all taken hold of the end. This he also exhorts
others saying thus: "As many as are perfect, let us think this; and if you
think anything otherwise, God will also reveal this to you." Not reasoning, he
said, will teach, but God will reveal. Do you see that the word is not
concerning life and conduct, but concerning doctrines and faith? For conduct
and life do not need revelation, but doctrines and knowledge. And elsewhere
also making this same thing clear he said: "If anyone seems to know something,
he has not yet known anything." He did not say simply: he has known nothing,
but "as one must know"; for he has knowledge, but not exact and finished. And
that you may learn that this is true, let us not discourse at all concerning
the things above, but if you wish let us bring the word down toward the
visible creation. Do you not see this heaven? That it preserves an image of a
vault we know, and this itself not from reasonings, but having learned from
the divine Scripture; and that it encompasses all the earth, this too we know,
having heard likewise from that; but what it is in substance we are ignorant
of. And if someone insists and contends, let him say what heaven is in
substance; is it frozen crystal? is it a condensed cloud? is it thicker air?
But no one would have anything to say clearly. Do you still need proof, tell
me, toward learning the madness of those who say they know God? You cannot say
the nature of the heaven seen every day, and you profess to know with
exactness the substance of the invisible God? And who is so senseless as not
to condemn the last madness of those who say these things? For these things
then I exhort all of you, as those who have fallen into a phrenitic disease
and are raving, to try according to your power to heal, discoursing with them
with mildness and gentleness; for from madness this doctrine was born for
them, and much is the puff of their mind; and the inflaming of wounds does not
even bear an application of a hand, nor endure a rougher touch. Therefore the
wise of the physicians wipe such sores with some soft sponge. Since then these
too have a wound in the soul inflaming, drawing as with some tender sponge
mild and drinkable water, thus pouring all that has been said, let us try to
settle their puff and to take down all the bulk; and if they insult, and if
they kick, and if they spit, and if they do anything whatever, do not leave
the healing, beloved. For those who heal a deranged man must undergo many such
things; but nevertheless not even so must one stand off, but for these very
things most of all one must count them miserable and weep, because such is the
form of their sickness. These things I say toward the stronger and
unassailable and able to receive no harm from their association; but if
someone is weaker, let him flee their companies, let him leap away from the
assemblies, so that the pretext of friendship may not become an occasion of
impiety. So also Paul does, himself mixing with

the sick and saying: "I became to the Jews as a Jew, to the lawless as
lawless," but the disciples and those more weakly disposed he leads away,
exhorting thus and teaching: "Evil associations corrupt good habits." And
again: "Come out from their midst and be marked off, says the Lord." For the
physician, if he comes toward the one who is ill, has often benefited both
that one and himself; but the weaker has harmed both himself and the one who
is sick, mixing with those who are diseased; for he will be able to benefit
that one in nothing, and he himself will draw much harm from the sickness. And
what those who look toward the ones with eye-disease suffer, drawing something
of that sickness, this also those who mix with these blasphemers undergo, if
they are weaker, drawing to themselves a great part of the impiety. Therefore,
lest we harm ourselves the greatest things, let us flee their companies, only
praying and exhorting the philanthropic God, who wishes all men to be saved
and to come into recognition of the truth, to release them from the error and
the snare of the devil, and to bring them back upon the light of knowledge,
the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, with the life-making and all-holy
Spirit, to whom the glory and the might, now and always, and unto the ages of
the ages. Amen. 3 Of the same concerning the incomprehensible and that not

harming their labors and by the roughness of the root and the heaviness of the
shade destroying the tame of the plants, cut it out with much diligence. And
often also a wind coming out from somewhere takes part with them in the cut,
and falling upon the foliage of the tree and shaking it with much intensity,
both broke it and threw it to the earth, and lightened the more of the toil
for them. Since then we too are cutting out a wild and untamed tree, the
heresy of the Anomoeans, let us exhort God to send us the grace of the Spirit,
so that, striking more intensely than every wind, it may pull up the heresy by
the roots, lightening the more of the toil for us. For already somewhere also
land that has lain fallow and has not enjoyed farming hands has often thrown

so also the soul of the Anomoeans, having been made desert and not having

wild and untamed heresy. For this tree Paul did not plant, Apollos did not

brighter; but he always remains upon his own glory, neither increased by

according to their own power, harvest the benefit from the doxology; and those

their much madness and mindlessness. For boastfulness is not to say that the
craftsman is above the grasp of all generated things, but to say that him who
is incomprehensible to the powers above it is possible for those who crawl on
the ground and stand so far from those to circumscribe and encompass by the
weakness of their own reasonings. I, if I do not show this which I promised,
would be just to undergo a charge of boastfulness; but you, if after I show
him being incomprehensible to the powers above, still insisting and you
contend to know him, of how many pits would you be worthy, and of how many

turned the word again upon the prayer; for often this sequence of the prayer

dwelling unapproachable light, whom no one of men has seen nor is able to see,
to whom honor and might unto the ages. Amen." These are not mine, but Paul's
words; but you learn for me the reverence of his soul and fear rooted. For
having remembered God, he did not bear to go out toward the sequence of the
teaching sooner, until he paid the debt owed him, shutting the word into
doxology. For if "memory of a just man is with praises," much more memory of

beginning a letter often, whenever he remembers God, he does not go forward

deliver us from the present evil age according to the will of God and Father,

said that "I was praying to be anathema from Christ for my brothers, my

and the lawgiving and the services and the promises; from whom the Christ
according to flesh, who is God over all, blessed unto the ages. Amen." For
just as to the Father, so also to the only-begotten having paid the doxology

"That all may honor the Son, as they honor the Father." And, that you may
learn that this prayer itself will furnish us the proof, come let us bring it
into the midst. "The king of those who reign," he says, "and lord of those who
lord, who alone has immortality, dwelling unapproachable light." Here stand
and ask the heretic what this ever is, "who dwells unapproachable light," and
attend to the exactness

of Paul. He did not say: being unapproachable light, but: "dwelling
unapproachable light," that you may learn that if the house is unapproachable,
how much more the God who dwells it. And he said these things not that you may
suspect a house and a place about God, but that from much abundance you may
learn the incomprehensible. But he did not even say dwelling incomprehensible
light, but unapproachable, which is much greater than the incomprehensible.

not grasped by those who seek it; but unapproachable is that which does not
even bear search at the beginning, nor can anyone become near it. For example
a sea is called incomprehensible, into which the divers letting themselves
down and being carried toward much depth are unable to find the limit; but
that is called unapproachable which cannot even at the beginning be sought,
nor searched. What would you say toward these things? But to men, he says,
incomprehensible, but not to angels, nor to the powers above. Are you then an
angel, tell me, and do you belong to the chorus of the bodiless powers? Are
you not a man and of the same substance as I? Or have you even forgotten the
nature? For let us grant it is unapproachable to men only, and yet this is not
added, nor did Paul say: dwelling unapproachable light to men, but not
unapproachable to angels; yet nevertheless, if you wish, let us grant it by
concession; are you yourself not a man? What then if it is not unapproachable
even to angels? What is this toward you who contend and busy yourself and say
that that substance is comprehensible to human nature? And that you may learn
that he is unapproachable not to men only, but also to the powers above, hear

common, but is proper to him and lordly, and present to none of the others
thus as to that one. And that what I say may become clearer, I will say it
more broadly again. The name son is present also to men, is present also to
Christ, but to us by catachresis, to that one properly; but the only-begotten
is of him only and is present to none of the others even by catachresis.
Therefore, so that from the appellation present to none but to him only you
may also reckon the other one present to the many to be lordly of him,
therefore first he said: "The only-begotten" and then: "Son." And if these do
not even suffice you, he says, I say also another third, thick and human, but
able even to lead those who crawl on the ground up into the thought of the
glory of the only-begotten. And what is this? "Who is into the bosom of the
Father." The saying is thick, but able to show the genuineness, if we take it
in a God-befitting way. For just as hearing a throne and the sitting from the
right, you do not suppose a throne, nor a place and circumscription, but from
the appellation of the throne and the communion according to the sitting, the
unchanged and equal of the honor, so also hearing the bosom, do not reckon
there is a bosom, nor a place, but from the appellation of the bosom take the
nearness toward him who generated and the freedom of speech; for to dwell in
the bosom makes much clearer to us than the sitting from the right the
nearness toward him who generated and sets it forth. For neither would the
Father have borne to have the Son in the bosom, if he were not of the same
substance, nor would that one have endured, being of a lesser nature, to dwell
in the paternal bosom. As Son then and only-begotten and spending time in the
paternal bosom he knows all the things of the Father

exactly; therefore the evangelist also needed these words, so that he may set
forth the exact knowledge of the Son concerning the Father. For the word was
concerning knowledge; since if this is not, for what reason has the bosom been
taken? For if God is not a body, as indeed he is not, nor does it make clear
the genuineness and the nearness toward him who generated, the saying has been
thrown in simply and in vain, fulfilling no need for us. But it has not been
thrown in simply, let it not be; for the Spirit speaks nothing in vain, but it
makes clear the nearness of the Son toward the Father. For since the
evangelist declared a great thing, that not even the creation above sees him,
that is knows him with exactness, wishing to set a trustworthy teacher of the
matter, he has set these words, so that both as to a Son and as to an
only-begotten and as to one spending time in the paternal bosom you may
believe concerning all things and doubt nothing henceforth. And if someone
would not contend, nor wish to be shameless, I say this is also indicative of
the eternal. For just as from that saying spoken toward Moses: "I am the one
who is," we take the eternal, so also from this saying that says: "Who is in
the bosoms of the Father," it is possible to take the being eternally in the
bosoms of the Father. That then the substance of God is incomprehensible to
all creation has been proved to us through all these; it remains then
henceforth to show that the Son and the Holy Spirit only understand him with
all exactness. But having postponed this to another discourse, lest by the
multitude we bury the memory of what has been said, let us turn the word again
upon the accustomed exhortation. And what was the accustomed exhortation for
us? To hold to intense prayer with a sober mind and a waking soul. For having
discoursed concerning these things recently also, I saw all readily obeying.
It is then out of place to charge those who are slack, and not to praise those
who achieve. I wish then to praise you today and to pay you thanks for that
obedience. And the thanks will be, if we teach you for what reason that prayer
happens before the others and why ever the deacon orders the possessed and
those held by an evil madness to be brought in then and to bend the heads? For
what reason then does he do this? The energy of the demons is an evil and
grievous chain, a chain more powerful than every iron. As therefore when a
judge has a going-forth and is about to sit in front upon a high bench, the
jailers, having led out all those who inhabit the prison from the dwelling,
sit them before the lattices and the hangings of the court, squalid, filthy,
long-haired, wrapped in rags, so also the fathers legislated, when Christ is
about to sit in front as upon a high bench and appear upon the mysteries
themselves, that the possessed be brought in as some prisoners, not that they
may undergo accounts of the things they have offended, as those bound there,
nor that they may undergo punishment and vengeance, but that, the people and
the whole city being present inside, common supplications concerning them may
happen, all with one soul asking the common Master for them and exhorting him
to pity with an intense cry. Then then we charged those who leave such a
prayer and spend time outside at that season; but now I wish to charge those
who remain inside, not because they remain inside, but because remaining they
are disposed no better than those who spend time outside, discoursing with one
another at that most shuddering season. What are you doing, man? You see so
many prisoners from your brothers standing near, and you discourse concerning
affairs that belong in nothing? Is the sight alone not sufficient to strike
you and lead you into sympathy? Your brother is in bonds, and you in
slackness? and what pardon will you have, tell me, being so unsympathetic, so
inhuman, so raw? Do you not fear lest sometime while you are discoursing,
being slack, making light, some demon leaping out from there

finding the soul idle and swept, may enter with much fearlessness, having
found the house un-doored? For ought not all in common to send forth springs
of tears at that hour and the eyes of all to be seen wet and laments and
wailings to happen from the whole Church? After the communion of the
mysteries, after the enjoyment of the bath, after having covenanted with
Christ, that wolf was able to snatch the lambs from the flock and to hold them
with himself, and you seeing the misfortune do not weep? and where are these
things worthy of defense? You do not wish to suffer with the brother? even for
yourself at least fear and stand up. If you saw the neighbor's house being set
on fire, tell me, even if the neighbor was most hostile of all, would you not
have run upon quenching the pyre, fearing lest the fire walking by a road may
also touch your own doorways? Reckon this also upon the possessed; for the
energy of the demons is some fire and a grievous burning. Consider then for me
lest the demon walking by a road also occupy your soul beforehand, and when
you see him present, with much diligence take refuge with the Master, so that
the demon, seeing your hot and awakened soul, may reckon your mind henceforth
untrodden for him. For if he sees you yawning and being slack, as into a
desert lodging he will enter in quickly; but if stretched and awakened and
hanging from the heavens themselves, he will not even dare to look against
henceforth. So that even if you despise the brothers, yet spare yourself at
least and wall off the entrance against your soul to the evil demon. And
nothing is so accustomed to wall off his assault against us as prayer and
intense petition. For this itself also, the deacon exhorting all and saying:
"Let us stand upright well," has not been legislated simply nor in vain, but
that we may set upright the reasonings being dragged on the ground, that,
having cast out the faintness that happens to us from the affairs of life, we
may be able to set our soul upright before God. And that this is true and the
word is stretched not toward the body but toward the soul, ordering this to
set it upright, let us hear also Paul having used this saying in this manner.
For writing toward men who had fallen and had given up toward the bringing on
of the terrible things he said: "Set upright the hands that have been let go
and the knees that have been paralyzed." What then would we say? that he
speaks concerning hands and knees of the body? By no means; for he does not
discourse to runner men, nor to pancratiasts, but through these words he
exhorted to raise the strength of the reasonings within that had been cast
down beforehand by the temptations. Consider near whom you have stood, with
whom you are going to call God, with the Cherubim; reckon your fellow-dancers
and this will suffice you unto sobriety, when you consider that, being wrapped
in a body and entwined with flesh, you have been counted worthy to hymn with
the bodiless powers the common Master of all. Let no one then dissolved in
eagerness share in those holy and mystical hymns; let no one have reasonings
of life at that season, but having banished all the earthly things of the mind
and having transferred his whole self toward heaven, as standing near the
throne of glory itself and flying with the Seraphim, thus let him send up the
all-holy hymn to the God of glory and greatness. Therefore we are also ordered
to stand well at that season. For to stand well is nothing else but to stand
thus, as it is fitting for a man being to stand before God, with shuddering
and trembling, with a sober and awakened soul. For that this saying also is
stretched toward the soul, Paul again made this itself clear saying: "Stand
thus in the Lord, beloved." For just as the archer, if he is going to send the
arrows well-aimed, first takes care of his own standing and having set himself
opposite the mark with exactness

takes to the release of the arrows, so you too, being about to shoot the evil
head of the devil, first take care of the standing of the reasonings, so that,
having fixed some upright and unhindered standing of yourself, you may send
the arrows well-aimed against that one. And concerning the prayer these
things; but since besides the slackness according to the prayers the devil
also invented some other thing full of much despondency, this assault of his
too must be walled off. What then is it that that evil demon worked ill?
Seeing you thus as one body bound together and nailed to what is said with
much exactness, he did not dare to let down some of his own deacons who would
lead you away from the hearing through counsel and exhortation; for he knew
that none of you will bear those who counsel these things; but mixing some
robbers and purse-cutters in the multitude, he arranged that gold, which they
had bound on, be stolen often from many of those gathered here; and this
happened often and upon many here. Therefore, lest this also happen, nor the
loss of the money quench in time the eagerness concerning the hearing, many
suffering this, I exhort and counsel all of you, that no one carrying gold
enter here, so that your diligence concerning the hearing may not become a
provision for those of the ill-working, nor the pleasure that happens to you
from the spending of time here be dimmed by the theft of the money. For the
devil arranged this, not that he may make you poorer, but that the destruction
of the money, having cast you into much unpleasantness, may lead you away from
the eagerness concerning the hearing. Since he also stripped Job of all the
money, not that he may make him poorer, but that he may strip him of piety.
For what is eager to that one is not to take away money, for he knows this is
nothing, but that through the taking away of the money he may cast into sin of
soul; and if he is not able to do this, he will reckon he has achieved nothing
ever. Knowing then his mind, beloved, whenever he takes away your gold, either
through robbers, or through some other pretext, glorify the Master, and you
will have gained more and you will give the enemy a double blow, both that you
were not displeased and that you gave thanks. For if he sees that the loss of
the money humbles you and persuades you to be displeased against the Master,
he will never stand off from working this; but if he beholds that you not only
do not blaspheme the God who made you, but also thank him upon each of the
terrible things that happen, he will stand off from bringing on the
temptations, knowing that the trial of the terrible things becomes a subject
of thanksgiving for you, and works the crowns brighter for you and the prizes
more. Which then also happened upon Job. For since, having taken away his
money and having struck the body, he saw him become thankful, he no longer
dared to come forward, but having undergone a shameful and unpardonable defeat
he went away, having worked the athlete of God brighter. These things then we
too knowing, let us fear one thing only, the sin, but let us bear the others
nobly, whether loss of money, or disease of bodies, or circumstances of
affairs, or spite, or slander, or anything else whatever of terrible is
brought upon us; for the nature of these things will not only not harm us, but
will also be able to benefit the greatest things, if we bear them with
thanksgiving, and will work more wages for us. You see at any rate also Job,
after having bound on all the crowns of endurance and manliness, having
received back all the things lost double. But you will receive back not double
and triple, but a hundredfold all things, if you bear nobly, and you will
inherit eternal life of which may it be that we all obtain, by grace and
philanthropy of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom the glory now and always and
unto the ages of the ages. Amen.

5 Of the same concerning the incomprehensible, fifth discourse. Whenever
someone is about to take hold of a longer subject and needing many words and
being finished not in one day and two and three, but in many more, I think it
is necessary for such a one not to set all the teaching at once nor under one
upon the mind of the hearers, but cutting the whole into many parts to make
this load of the word light and easy to take by the breaking into bits. For
tongue and hearing and each of the sense-organs have measures and canons and
bounds laid for us, and if someone ever attempts to overstep these bounds he
also falls from the power that is. For what is sweeter than light, tell me?
And what more pleasant than a ray? but nevertheless this sweet and pleasant,
whenever it associates beyond our measure, becomes burdensome to the eyes and
heavy. Therefore God also legislated night to be after the day, so that,
having taken the eyes that have toiled, it may settle the eyelids, put the
pupils to sleep, rest our seeing power that has grown weary and prepare it
more suitable toward the contemplation of the day to come. Therefore also
waking and sleep, being opposite to one another, from the due measure likewise
become most pleasant to one another, and calling the light sweet, we likewise
call also sleep sweet that leads us away from the light. Thus the lack of
measure is everywhere heavy and burdensome, and the due measure sweet and
useful to us and mild. For this then we too, having a fourth or even a fifth
day henceforth, from which we move the word concerning the incomprehensible,
have not even today prepared to finish it but, having said the things due in
measure toward your love, we have known again to rest your mind. Where then
did we leave the word recently? for it is necessary to take it up from there
again, since there is some one sequence of teaching. We said then that the son
of thunder said that "No one has seen God ever; the only-begotten Son, who is
in the bosoms of the Father, he himself narrated." Today it is necessary to
learn where the only-begotten Son of God himself narrated this. "He answered,"
he says, "to the Jews and said: Not that anyone has seen the Father except he
who is from God, this one has seen the Father." Seeing again here he means the
knowledge. And he did not say simply that "No one knows the Father" and was
silent, lest someone suppose this is said concerning men only, but wishing to
show that neither angels, nor archangels, nor the powers above, he made this
clear by the addition. For having said: "Not that anyone has seen the Father
ever," he added: "except he who is from God, this one has seen the Father."
For if he had said simply: No one, many of those who heard perhaps would have
supposed it had been said concerning our nature only; but now having said "No
one" and having added: "except the Son," by the addition of the only-begotten
he shut off all creation. What then, he says, also the Holy Spirit? By no
means; for this is not even a part of the creation; and the "No one," this
always lies toward distinction of the creation only; and neither, if it is
said concerning the Father, is the Son thrust out, nor, if concerning the Son,
does it cast out the Spirit. And that I may already make this itself plain
from here, that the "no one" has been said not toward setting aside of the
Spirit, but toward distinction of the creation concerning this knowledge
itself, which he says is of the Son only, let us hear what Paul discoursing
says to Corinthians. What then does he say? "For who knows the things of the
man, except the spirit of the man that is in him? So also the things of God no
one knows, except the Spirit of God." As therefore here the "no one" being
said does not cast out the Son, so also upon Christ the "no one" having been
set does not thrust out the Holy Spirit. From where it is clear that what has
been said is true. For if saying that "No one

has seen the Father, except he who is from God," he thrust out the Spirit,
Paul said superfluously that as a man knows his own things, so also the Holy
Spirit understands the things of God with exactness. So also the "one" has
been said; for it has the same force as this and power. And consider: "One
God," he says, "the Father from whom are all things and one Lord Jesus Christ
through whom are all things." For if the Father being said to be one God casts
the Son out of the godhead and the Son being said to be one Lord casts the
Father out of the lordship; but indeed the being said that "one Lord Jesus
Christ" does not cast the Father out of the lordship. Therefore neither does
the being said that one God is the Father cast the Son out of the godhead. And
if they should say again that therefore the Father is said to be one God,
because the Son is God, but not such a God as the Father, it follows to say
that, from the premises they themselves set, for we would not say it, that
therefore the Son is said to be one Lord, because the Father is Lord but not
such a Lord as the Son. And if this is impious, neither would the former have
reason; but just as the "one Lord" does not thrust the Father out of the exact
lordship nor set it around upon the Son only, so neither does the "one God"
cast the Son out of the true and genuine godhead, nor show it of the Father
only. For that the Son is God and such a God as the Father, remaining however
Son, is clear from the addition itself. For if this name God were of the
Father only and was not able to make clear to us another hypostasis, but that
unbegotten and first only, as being proper to it only and recognitive, the
addition of the Father lies superfluously; for it sufficed to say "one God"
and we would recognize who was the one said, but since the name God is common
of Father and Son and having said: "one God" it did not make clear whom Paul
means, therefore he needed the addition of the Father, so that he may make
clear that he speaks concerning the first and unbegotten hypostasis, as the
appellation of God not sufficing to show it, since this is common to him
toward the Son. For of the names some are common, and some proper; common, so
that they may show the unchanged of the substance, and proper, so that they
may characterize the property of the hypostases. The "Father" then and "Son"
is proper of each hypostasis, but the "God" and "Lord" common. Since then he
has set a common name, the "one God," he needed also the distinguishing, so
that you may recognize whom he means, so that we may not fall into the madness
of Sabellius. For that neither the name "God" is greater than "Lord," nor the
"Lord" less than "God," is clear from there. In all the Old the Father is
continually called Lord: "The Lord your God," it says, "the Lord is one," and
again: "You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve," and
again: "Great is our Lord and great is his strength and of his understanding
there is no number," and again: "Let them know that your name is Lord; you
alone are most high upon all the earth." And yet if it were less than God and
unworthy of that substance, it ought not to have been said: "Let them know
that your name is Lord." Again if God were greater than Lord and more august,
the according to them lesser Son ought not to be called from the name fitting
the Father, which was also only proper of that one. But these things are not,
they are not. For neither is the Son lesser than the Father, nor is the name
Lord cheaper than God. Therefore both upon Father and upon the Son Scripture
uses these appellations without difference. As therefore you heard the Father
called Lord, come let us show you also the Son being called God. "Behold the
virgin will have in womb and will beget a son and they will call his name
Emmanuel, which is being interpreted: God with us." Did you see how also to
the Father a name is Lord and to the Son a name is God? For just as there he
says: "Let them know that your name is Lord," so also here he says: "They will
call his name Emmanuel." And again: "A child was generated to us, a son also
was given to us and his name is called messenger of great counsel, mighty God,

ruler." And consider for me understanding of prophets and spiritual wisdom.
For lest, having said God simply, they be supposed to speak concerning the
Father, they remember the economy first; for the Father was not generated
through a virgin, nor did he become a child. Again another prophet says
somehow thus concerning him: "This is our God, another will not be reckoned
toward him." And concerning whom does he say this? perhaps concerning the
Father? By no means; for hear how this one also remembered the economy; for
having said: "This is our God, another will not be reckoned toward him," he
added: "He found every way of knowledge and gave it to Jacob his child and to
Israel the one loved by him. After these things he was seen upon the earth and
associated with men." And Paul: "From whom the Christ, according to flesh," he
says, "who is God over all, blessed unto the ages, amen," and again: "Every
fornicator or greedy one has no inheritance in the kingdom of the Christ and
God," and again: "According to the appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus
Christ." And John also thus calls him saying: "In the beginning was the Word
and the Word was toward God and the Word was God." Yes, he says, but show
that, where Scripture arranging him with the Father calls the Father Lord. And
I show not this only, but that it both calls the Father Lord and the Son Lord
and that it calls the Father God and that the Son God, setting both names
together. Where then is this to be found? Christ discoursing sometime to the
Jews says: "What seems to you concerning the Christ? whose son is he?" And
they say to him: "of David." He says to them: "How then does David in spirit
call him Lord saying: The Lord said to my Lord: sit from my right?" See "Lord"
and "Lord." Do you wish to learn where Scripture calls God and God the Father
and the Son setting them together? hear also the prophet David and the apostle
Paul making this itself clear to us: "Your throne, O God, unto the age of the
age, a rod of uprightness is the rod of your kingdom. You loved righteousness,
and hated lawlessness; therefore God, your God, anointed you, oil of gladness
beyond your partakers." And Paul again brought this testimony saying that
"Toward his angels he says: who makes his angels spirits; but toward the Son:
your throne, O God, unto the age of the age." For what reason then here, he
says, did he call the Father God, and the Son Lord? Not simply nor in vain did
he do this here, but since the word was toward Greeks diseased with
polytheism. Therefore, lest they say that charging us as saying many gods and
many lords, you yourself are caught by these charges saying gods, and not God;
therefore condescending to their weakness, he calls the Son by another name
having the same power. And that this is true, let us read from above the
passage itself, and you will know clearly that what is said is not our guess:
"And concerning the idol-sacrifices we know that we all have knowledge.
Knowledge puffs, but love builds. Concerning the eating then of the
idol-sacrifices we know that an idol is nothing in a world and that there is
no other God except one." Do you see that stretching toward those he says
these things, those who suppose there are many gods? For even if there are
many called gods and many lords, whether in heaven, or upon earth, again he
fights toward those, as indeed there are many gods and many lords, that is
called, but for us one God the Father, from whom are all things, and one Lord
Jesus Christ, through whom are all things. Therefore he also added the "one,"
lest those suppose polytheism to be introduced again, and he called the Father
one God, not casting the Son out of the godhead, as indeed also the Son one
Lord, not casting the Father out of the lordship, but correcting their
weakness, and wishing to give them no handle. This at any rate also became a
cause of the Son of God not being made known clearly nor manifestly, but dimly
somehow and rarely through the

prophets to the Jews. For having just been released from the polytheist error,
if they again heard God and God, they would have returned toward the same
disease. Therefore up and down continually the prophets say that "One God and
besides him there is not," not setting aside the Son, let it not be, but
wishing to heal their weakness and to persuade them for the present to be
released from the suspicion of the many gods and not being. When then you hear
the "one" and "no one," and as many such things, do not lessen the glory of

except the Father, nor does anyone recognize the Father, except the Son." So

this one has seen the Father." For together he said that he knows him with
exactness and he has set the cause through which he knows. And what is the
cause? The being from him; and of the being from him again a proof is the
knowing him with exactness. For therefore he knows him clearly, because he is
from him, and a sign of the being from him is the knowing him clearly. For a
substance would not be able to know well a substance that surpasses, even if
the interval is little. Hear at any rate what the prophet says concerning the
angels, and concerning human nature, that the difference of this and that is
little. For having said: "What is man, that you remember him, or son of man,
that you reckon him?" he added: "You lessened him a little something beside
angels." But nevertheless even if the interval is little, since there is
altogether some interval, we do not know substance of angels with exactness,
even if we philosophize ten thousand things, we are not able to find. And why
do I speak of angels, where we do not even know well the substance of our own
soul, or rather not even howsoever? And if those contend to know, ask what the
soul ever is in substance; is it air, or spirit, or wind, or fire? But they
will say none of these; for all these are bodies, but that is bodiless. Then
they do not know angels, nor their own souls, but they contend to know with
exactness the Master and craftsman of all. And what could become worse than
this folly? And why do I speak what sort the substance is, the soul? How it is
of us in the body, not even this is it possible to say. For what would someone
have to say? that it is stretched out with the bulk of the body? but this is
irrational; for this is proper of bodies. And that upon a soul this is not is
clear from here: often even of the hands and of the feet having been cut out,
that remains whole, having been mutilated in nothing from the maiming of the
body. But it is not in the whole body, but has been taken together in some
part? Then it is necessary that the remaining parts become dead; for the
soulless is altogether dead. But not even this is it possible to say, but that
it is in our body we know, but the how it is we do not know. For this God also
shut off the knowledge of this from much abundance stopping us and holding us,
and persuading us to remain below and not to busy ourselves with the things
above us, nor to meddle. But lest we construct such things from reasonings,
come let us bring the word again upon the Scripture. "Not that anyone," he
says, "has seen the Father except he who is from God, this one has seen the
Father." And what is this? he says; for this saying has not yet testified
exact knowledge to him. But that the creation does not know him he made clear
saying: "Not that anyone has seen the Father" and that the Son knows him, this
too again he made clear having added: "... except he who is from God, this one
has seen the Father"; that however he knows him exactly, and thus as he
himself himself, this has not yet been proved. For it is possible, he says,
neither the creation to know him clearly, nor the

Son, but more clearly than that, yet not even this one to have the exact
grasp. For that he sees him which is and knows he said, but that he knows him
exactly, and thus as he himself himself, he has not yet made this clear. Do
you wish then that we confirm this too from the Scriptures, and from the voice
of Christ itself? Therefore let us hear what he says toward the Jews: "As the
Father knows me, I also know the Father." What more exact than this knowledge
do you wish henceforth? Ask the one who contradicts: does the Father know the
Son exactly, and has he all his knowledge exactly, and does nothing of the
things according to the Son escape him, but is the acquaintance finished? Yes,
he says. Therefore, when you hear that the Son also knows him thus as he
himself the Son, seek nothing still more, the exactness of the knowledge thus
being equalized. For elsewhere also indicating this itself he said: "No one
knows the Son, except the Father, nor does anyone recognize the Father, except
the Son and to whom the Son wishes to reveal." And he reveals, not as much as
he himself knows, but as much as we have room. For if Paul does this, much
more Christ; for that one also says to his own disciples: "I was not able to
speak to you as to spiritual, but as to fleshly; as infants in Christ I gave
you milk to drink, not food; for you were not yet able." But this, he says, he
said to Corinthians only. What then, if we show him knowing also some other
things, which no one of men has learned already, and he went away alone
knowing them of all men? And where is this to be found? In the letter toward
the Corinthians; for this is the one who says that "I heard unspeakable words,
which it is not permitted a man to speak." But nevertheless even this one
himself, having then also heard unspeakable words, which it is not permitted a
man to speak, has partial knowledge and much lacking of that to come. For he
himself who said these things has also said those, that "We know in part and
we prophesy in part" and: "When I was an infant, I spoke as an infant, I
thought as an infant, I reasoned as an infant" and: "Now I see through a
mirror in a riddle, but then face toward face." So that from these all their
crookedness has been convicted for us; for when the substance itself is
unknown, not that it is, but what it is, it would be of last madness even to
set some name to it. And yet even if it were clear and known, not even so was
it safe for us to set an appellation from home and from ourselves upon the
substance of the Master. For if Paul did not dare to set names upon the powers
above, but having said that "He seated the Christ above every beginning and
authority and power and every name that is named, not only in this age, but
also in the one to come," and having taught us that there are some names of
the powers which we will know then, he did not take heart to set others
himself instead of those, nor to busy himself with those themselves. Of what
pardon would they be worthy or of what defense who dare these things upon the
substance of the Master? And when the substance itself is also unknown, one
must turn away from them as those who rave. For that God is unbegotten is
clear; but that this is the name of his substance no prophet said, no apostle
hinted, no evangelist. And very reasonably; for those ignorant of the
substance itself, how were they going to say a name of it? And why do I speak
concerning the divine Scriptures, where so great is the conspicuousness of the
absurdity and the excess of the lawlessness that not even the Greeks who have
wandered from the truth ever attempted to say any such thing? For no one even
of those dared to set out a definition of divine substance and to encompass it
with one name. And why do I speak of divine substance, where philosophizing
concerning the nature of the bodiless things they did not even set out a
finished definition of this, but some dim explanation and a sketch rather than
a definition? But what is their wise word? Do you not then know what you
worship? he says. Most of all one ought not even to answer toward this, so
great a proof having happened from the Scriptures that it is impossible to
know what God is in substance; but since we do not speak toward enmity, but so
as

to correct them, come let us show that not the being ignorant of what God is
in substance, but the contending to know, this is to be ignorant of God. For
tell me, if of two men contending toward one another to know the size of
heaven, the one says that a human eye is not able to grasp it, and the other
contends it is possible, going over measuring it all with the span of the
hand, whom would we say knows size of heaven, the one contending to know how
many spans it has or the one confessing to be ignorant? Then upon heaven the
one yielding to the size, this one will be the one who rather knows the size,
and upon God will we not even use this reverence? and how is this not of last
derangement? For that we are required to know so much only that God is, not to
busy ourselves with his substance, hear what Paul says: "For the one who comes
forward to God must believe that he is." And again the prophet charging
someone with impiety does not charge this, that he does not know what God is,
but that he does not know that God is. "For a fool said," he says, "in his
heart, there is no God." As therefore what makes that one impious is not the
being ignorant of what God is in substance, but the being ignorant that God
is; so it suffices toward piety to know that God is. But they have also some
other practiced word. What then is this? "It has been said," he says, "God is
spirit." Is this then indicative of his substance, tell me? and who will bear
these things, even having come howsoever upon the doors of the divine
Scriptures? For according to this word God will also be fire; for just as it
has been written that "God is spirit," so it has been written that "Our God is
a consuming fire," and again: "A spring of living water." And he will not be
spirit and spring and fire only, but also soul and wind and human mind and
other things much more absurd than these; for one must not go through all
things in the word, nor imitate their madness. For this spirit makes many
things clear, such as our soul, as also Paul says: "Give over such a one to
Satan, so that the spirit may be saved" and wind, as the prophet says: "With a
violent spirit you will crush them." And the spiritual gift is also called
thus: "For the spirit itself co-testifies to our spirit," he says, and again:
"I will pray with the spirit, and I will pray also with the mind." And the
anger is also called thus. For Isaiah also says: "Were you not the one
practicing with your hard spirit to take them away?" And the help from God is
also called spirit: "For a spirit before our face is Christ Lord." All these
things then according to those God will be for us and he will be compounded
from so many. But lest we talk nonsense, bringing into the midst the things
that do not even need contradiction, come, having dissolved the word toward
them here, let us turn the whole upon the prayer, by as much as they are more
impious, by so much more exhorting for them and begging them sometime to stand
off from the madness. For this is acceptable before our Savior God. "... who
wishes all men to be saved and to come into recognition of truth." Let us not
then leave off making the supplications for them. For prayer is a great
weapon, an unfailing treasure, wealth never spent, an un-waved harbor, a
subject of calm, and of ten thousand goods it is root and spring and mother,
and more powerful than the kingdom itself. Often then of him who wears the
diadem himself fevering and lying upon the bed and burning, physicians have
stood by, spear-bearers, attendants, generals, and neither art of physicians,
nor presence of friends, nor service of household servants, nor abundance of
drugs, nor costliness of preparation, nor surplus of money, nor anything else
of the human things is able to console the sickness that lies on; but if
someone having freedom of speech toward God enters and only touches the body
and makes a pure prayer for him, he has put to flight all the sickness; and
what the wealth and the multitude of those serving and the science of
experience and the bulk of the kingdom were not able, this was able of one
often poor and beggared

prayer. And I mean prayer, not this bare one and full of slackness, but that
which happens with intensity, with an aching soul, with a crushed mind. For
this is the one that goes up toward heaven, and just as the waters, as long as
they are carried upon a level place and enjoy much spaciousness, do not rise
toward height, but whenever hands of channel-makers from below having squeezed
them press them being straitened, they are hurled toward the height sharper
than every arrow, so also the human mind, as long as it enjoys much
fearlessness, is poured out and flows apart, but whenever a circumstance of
affairs straitens it from below, being well pressed, it sends up pure and
vigorous prayers toward the height. And that you may learn that those that
happen with affliction would especially be able to be heard, hear what the
prophet says: "Toward the Lord in my being afflicted I cried and he heard me."
Let us then heat the conscience, let us afflict the soul with the memory of
the sins, let us afflict, not that we may straiten, but that we may prepare to
be heard, that we may make it sober and wake and touch the heavens themselves.
Nothing so puts slackness and making-light to flight as ache and affliction
gathering the mind from everywhere and turning it toward itself. The one
afflicted thus and praying is able after the prayer to settle much pleasure
into his own soul. And just as a running-together of clouds at the beginning
makes the air gloomy, but whenever it has sent successive snows, having put
off all the rain, it works the air calm and bright, so also despondency, as
long as it is twisted together inside, darkens our reasoning, but whenever
through the words of the prayer and the tears with those it is emptied and
breathes out to the outside, it puts much brightness into the soul, the succor
of God as some ray having been sent into the mind of the one who prays. But
what is the cold word of the many? I am without freedom of speech, he says, I
am full of shame, I am not even able to open the mouth. This reverence is
satanic, these are coverings of slackness; for the devil wishes to shut off
for you the doors of the approach toward God. Are you without freedom of
speech? Great freedom of speech, great benefit is this itself, the reckoning
oneself to be without freedom of speech, just as shame and last condemnation
is the reckoning to have freedom of speech. For even if you have many
achievements and are conscious to yourself of nothing evil, but you reckon you
have freedom of speech, you have fallen from all the prayer; and even if you
bear ten thousand loads of sins upon the conscience, and only persuade
yourself of this, that you are last of all, you will have much freedom of
speech toward God. And yet this is not even humility, the being a sinner
reckoning oneself to be a sinner. For humility is when someone, being
conscious to himself of many and great things, imagines nothing great
concerning himself; when someone being according to Paul and able to say: "I
am conscious to myself of nothing," says again: "But I have not been justified
in this" and again: "Christ Jesus came to save sinners of whom I am first."
This is humility, the high from achievements humbling oneself from mind. But
nevertheless God through his unspeakable philanthropy not only those who
humble themselves, but also those who with good sense say their sins he
approaches and receives and becomes propitious and well-minded to those so
disposed. And that you may learn how great a good it is to imagine nothing
great concerning oneself, make two chariots by the word, yoke righteousness
and madness, and sin with humility, and you will see the yoke of the sin
taking the lead of the righteousness, not from its own power, but from the
strength of the yokefellow humility, and that one again being lessened, not
from the weakness of the righteousness, but from the weight and the bulk of
the madness. For just as the humility through its exceeding height conquers
the heaviness of the sin and arrives to go up toward God, so also the madness
through the much weight of it

and the bulk is able even to prevail over the lightest righteousness and to
drag it down easily. And that this yoke is quicker than that, remember the
Pharisee and the tax-collector. The Pharisee yoked righteousness and madness,
saying thus: "I thank you, O God, that I am not as the rest of men,
plunderers, greedy, nor as this tax-collector." O the madness. All the nature
of men did not sate his madness, but he also leapt upon the tax-collector
standing near with much madness. What then did that one? He did not beat off
the reproaches, he did not ache upon the accusation, but he received what was
said with good sense; and the enemy's arrow became for him a drug and a
healing, and the reproach an encomium, and the accusation a crown. So great a
good is humility, so great a gain, not to be bitten by the abuses from others,
nor to be made savage toward the insults of the neighbors. For it is possible
even from them to harvest some great and noble good, which then also happened
upon the tax-collector. For having received the reproaches, he put off the
sins, and having said: "Be propitious to me the sinner," he went down
justified beyond that one. And words prevailed over works and sayings
conquered deeds. For the one put forward righteousness and fasting and tithes;
but this one said bare sayings and put off all the sins. For God did not hear
the words only, but also saw the mind with which he put these forward, and
having found it humbled and crushed, he pitied and was philanthropic. And I
say these things not that we may sin, but that we may humble ourselves. For if
a tax-collector man, the last form of vice, not having humbled himself, but
having been of good sense only, having said the sins and having confessed what
he was, drew such goodwill from God, how much help will those who have
achieved great goods, but reckon nothing great concerning themselves, draw?
Therefore I exhort and beg and entreat to confess to God continually. For I do
not lead you into a theater of your fellow-servants, nor do I force you to
uncover the sins to men; unfold the conscience before God and show him the
wounds and ask the drugs from him; show to the one who does not reproach, but
heals; for even if you are silent, that one knows all things. Say then, that
you may gain; say, that having put off all the sins here you may go away there
clean and outside the offenses and be released from that unbearable
publishing. The three children spent time in a furnace and gave the soul for
the confession toward the Master; but nevertheless after so many and such
achievements they say: "There is not for us to open the mouth; shame and
reproach we have become to your servants and to those who worship you." Why
then do you open a mouth? That we may say this itself, he says, that there is
not for us to open the mouth and by this itself we may draw the Master. Power
of prayer quenched power of fire, bridled anger of lions, loosed wars, stopped
fights, took away storms, drove demons, opened gates of heaven, cut through
bonds of death, put diseases to flight, beat off spites, stood cities being
shaken, and simply prayer took away all the terrible things, both the blows
being carried from above and the plots from men. And I mean prayer again, not
that lying simply upon the mouth, but that going up from the depth of the
mind. For just as of the trees as many as send the roots toward the depth,
even if they receive ten thousand assaults of winds, are not broken nor pulled
up, the roots being squeezed with exactness by the depth of the earth, so also
prayers sent up from below from the depth of the mind, rooted with safety,
stretch toward height and are turned aside by no assault of reasoning.
Therefore the prophet also says: "From depths I cried to you, Lord." I say
these things not that you may praise only, but that you may also show upon the
works. For if to speak out toward men one's own misfortunes and to

tragedize the evils that have overtaken brings some consolation to those who
ache, as some breathing-through happening through the word, much more, if you
communicate toward your Master the affections of your own soul, you will
receive some leading of soul and much consolation. For a man often is even
weighed down by the one wailing and lamenting toward him and beats him off and
thrusts him away; but God not so, but he both approaches and draws, and if you
spend the day communicating to him your misfortunes, then he loves you more
and nods to your supplications. This itself at any rate Christ showing said:
"Come toward me all who toil and are loaded and I will rest you." He calls
then, let us not disobey; he draws, let us not leap away; and if we have ten
thousand sins, then let us run forward more; for he also calls such: "For I
did not come," he says, "to call just but sinners unto repentance," and here
those loaded, those who have toiled, those who have grown weary from weight of
the sins. For he is called God of consolation and God of mercies; since this
is a continual work for him, to console and exhort those who ache and those
who are afflicted, even if they have ten thousand sins. Only let us give
ourselves, only let us run toward him, and let us not stand off and through
the trial itself we will know the truth of what has been said, and nothing of
the things that are will be able to pain us, if we have intense prayer and
made exact; for whatever comes on, we will easily beat it off through this.
And what wonder if power of prayer knows how to loose human circumstances,
where it even quenches nature of sins and makes it vanish easily? Therefore,
so that we may both finish the present life with ease and put off as many sins
as we have rubbed on ourselves, and stand with freedom of speech upon the
bench of Christ, let us continually prepare this drug for ourselves,
compounding it from tears and diligence and sitting-by and endurance; for thus
we will both enjoy continual health, and we will obtain the goods to come of
which may it be that we all obtain, by grace and philanthropy of our Lord
Jesus Christ, with whom to the Father glory, with the Holy Spirit, now and
always, and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
