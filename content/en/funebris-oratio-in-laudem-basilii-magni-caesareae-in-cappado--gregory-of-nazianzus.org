left to those who wished them, theaters, public gatherings, drinking parties.
21.2 For I think nothing is honorable that does not lead toward virtue, nor
make better those who are eager about it. Others then have certain other
appellations, either from a father or from home, from their own pursuits or
actions; but for us the great thing and name was both to be and to be called
Christians; 21.3 in which we took more pride than Gyges in the turn of the
ring, if it was not a myth, from which he became tyrant of the Lydians; or
Midas once in the gold, through which he perished, having obtained his prayer
and acquired all as gold, this other Phrygian myth. 21.4 For why should I

that there was also a clan about us not ignoble, being educated and led under

Lydian chariot, his course and manner; from which it was ours to be notable

even as far as beyond the borders, as has become clear from the many who
recount these things. For our educators were among as many as Athens; and we
among as many as the educators, being heard together and spoken of together,
and a pair not nameless, both being among them and being heard of. 22.3
Nothing such were the Orestes and the Pylades to them; nothing the Molionidae,
the wonder of the Homeric tablet, whom a sharing of misfortunes made known,
and driving a chariot well, dividing in the same place reins and whips. 22.4
But I escaped my own notice, being drawn into praises of myself, I who never
even from another accepted this; and it is no wonder if here too I enjoyed
something of his friendship, as of the living toward virtue, so of the
departed toward good report. But let the speech be brought back again to the
turning-post for us. 23.1 Who was so gray in understanding even before
grayness? since by this even Solomon defines old age. And who so venerable
either to old or to young, not only of those in the same time with us, but
even of those who had far anticipated us? Who less needed words because of his
manner? Who more partook of speech even with the

manner? 23.2 What form of education did he not go through? Rather, which not
with excess as if it alone? Thus going through all, as no one through one; and
thus to the peak in each, as through none of the others; for zeal ran together
with natural gift, from which sciences and arts have their strength. 23.3
Least needing speed of nature because of tension, least needing tension
because of speed; and so taking both together and bringing them into one, that
it was unclear by which of these he was more wonderful. 23.4 Who so great in
rhetoric, that which breathes the might of fire, even if his character was not
according to rhetoricians? Who in grammar, which hellenizes the tongue and
gathers history and presides over meters and legislates for poems? Who in
philosophy, the truly high and walking above, as much as is practical and
contemplative, and as much as has to do with logical proofs and oppositions
and the wrestlings, which they name dialectic; so that it was easier to go
through labyrinths than to escape the nets of his arguments, if there should
be need of this? 23.5 And of astronomy and geometry and the proportion of
numbers taking so much as not to be shaken by the clever about these things,
he spat out the superfluous as useless to those who wish to be pious; so that
one may rather wonder at what was chosen than at what was passed over, or
rather at what was passed over than at what was chosen. 23.6 For medicine,
both the weakness of the body and the nursing of the sick, being a fruit of
philosophy and love of toil, made necessary for him; starting from which he
arrived at a habit of the art; and of this, not as much as has to do with the
appearing and lying below, but as much as is dogmatic and philosophical. But
what are these, even though they happen to be so great, toward the education
in the man's character? 23.7 Nonsense to those who have tried the man is that
Minos and that Rhadamanthys whom Greeks thought worthy of asphodel meadows and
Elysian fields, having come to a fantasy of our Paradise, from the Mosaic
books I think and ours, even if they differed somewhat about the naming,
indicating this in other names. 24.1 These things then were thus, and the
cargo was full of education, as far at least as is reachable for human nature;
for what is beyond Gadeira is not passable; and there remained a return and a
more complete life and a laying hold of the things hoped for by us and agreed.
The day of departure was present and all that belongs to departure; farewell
speeches, escorts, recallings, wailings, embracings, tears. 24.2 For nothing
is so painful to anyone as for those who are fellow-feeders there to be cut
from Athens and from one another. Then indeed there comes to be a spectacle
pitiable and worthy of history. Standing around us the chorus of companions
and agemates, and of some even teachers, they said they would not let us go
whatever should happen, entreating, forcing, persuading; for what did they not
say? and what not do, of the things likely for those in pain? 24.3 Here I will
accuse myself of something, and I will accuse that divine and unassailable
soul, even if it is daring. For he, having spoken the causes of the contention
about the return, was shown stronger than those holding him; and by force, yet
he was allowed the departure; but I was left behind at Athens; softened in
part, for the truth will be said, and in part betrayed by him, who was
persuaded to let go one who was not letting go and to yield to those dragging.
24.4 A thing not believed before it happened; for it becomes as a cutting of
one body into two and a death of both, or a unyoking of calves reared together
and yoked together, moaning mournfully over one another and not bearing the
alienation. 24.5 Yet the loss was not longer for me; for I could not endure
any more to be seen pitiable and to give account to all of the separation; but
after I had remained at Athens a time not long, longing makes me the Homeric
horse; and having broken the bonds of those holding me I stamp over the plains
and was carried toward my fellow-feeder.

25.1 When then we had returned, having granted small things to the world and
the stage and as much as to pay off the longing of the many; for we ourselves
had nothing theatrical or display-like; most quickly we became our own and we
pass into men from the unbearded, stepping more manfully toward philosophy;
not still with one another, for envy did not allow it, yet with one another in
longing. 25.2 For the city of the Caesareans holds him, as a kind of second
founder and city-guardian; then certain departures, since it did not have us,
take him of the necessary things, and not off the mark of the philosophy set
before us. But reverence of parents and care of old age and a rising of
misfortunes, holding me, led me away from the man; not well perhaps nor
justly, yet it did lead me away. 25.3 And I consider whether from there too
all the unevenness and difficulty about my life fell upon me, and the not easy
path toward philosophy nor worthy of the desire and the purpose. Let our
things then be led wherever it is dear to God; and may they be led better by
his intercessions. 25.4 But him the many-turned philanthropy of God and the
economy about our race, having made him known through many things in the
middle and always showing him more brilliant, sets forth as a lamp of the
Church, conspicuous and celebrated, having enrolled him for a time in the
sacred seats of the presbytery, and through the one city of the Caesareans
torching for the whole inhabited world. 25.5 And in what manner? Not
improvising the rank nor washing and making wise at once, according to the
many of those now reaching after presidency; but by order and law of spiritual
ascent having thought him worthy of the honor. 26.1 For I do not praise the
disorder and unseemliness among us, sometimes even in the case of those
presiding on platforms; for I will not dare to accuse all, nor is it just. I
praise the sailor's law, which first putting the oar into the hands of the one
now helmsman and from there bringing him to the prow and entrusting the things
in front, thus seats him at the tillers, after the much beaten sea and the
observation of the winds; as it is also in warlike things: soldier, taxiarch,
general. This order is best and most profitable for those ruled. 26.2 And ours
would have been worth much, if it were so. But now the holiest rank of all
among us risks being the most ridiculous of all. For the presidency is not
from virtue rather than from villainy, nor are the seats of the more worthy
but of the more powerful. 26.3 Samuel among prophets, who sees the things in
front; but also Saul, the rejected. Rehoboam among kings, the son of Solomon;
but also Jeroboam, the slave and apostate. And there is no physician nor
painter who has not first considered the natures of ailments, or mixed many
colors or given form; but the president is found easily without having toiled,
and fresh in the dignity, sown and put forth at once, as the myth makes the
Giants. 26.4 We mold the saints on the same day, and we bid them be wise,
those who have been made wise in nothing, nor have brought anything in before
the rank, except the wishing. And one is content with the lower place and
stands humbly, he who is worthy of the high, having practiced much in the
divine words and legislated much for the flesh into subjection of spirit; 26.5
but the other sits in front solemnly and raises the brow against the better,
and does not tremble at the seats, nor shudder at the sight looking down on
the continent man below; but together with the power he also supposes himself
wiser, knowing badly and having been robbed of thinking by the authority. 27.1
But not so the great and great Basil; but as of all the other things, so also
of the order about these things he is set as a type for the many. For first
reading the sacred books to the people, the expounder of these, and not
disdaining this order of the platform, thus in a seat of presbyters, thus
among bishops he praises the Lord, not having stolen the authority nor seized
it, nor having pursued the

honor, but having been pursued by the honor, nor having received a human
grace, but one from God and divine. 27.2 Let the account of the presidency
then wait; but let us linger a little on that of the lower seat. For how this
too nearly ran past me, lying in the middle of the things said! 28.1 There
came to be a difference toward the man with the previous leader of the Church;
from where and how it is better to be silent, yet it came to be; a man not
ignoble in the other things and wonderful in piety, as the persecution then
showed, and the resistance toward it, yet having suffered something human
toward him; 28.2 for Momus touches not only the many, but even the best, so
that only God's would be the wholly unfailing and uncaptured by passions. 28.3
There is moved then against him as much of the Church as is select and wiser,
if indeed wiser than the many are those who have separated themselves from the
world and dedicated their life to God; I mean our Nazirites and those most
eager about such things; who, having made it a terrible thing to overlook
their own strength insulted and thrust away, dare a most dangerous thing; they
think of a revolt and a rending of the great and unfactious body of the
Church, cutting off also not a small portion of the people, as much as of
those below and as much as of those in dignity. 28.4 And this was easiest from
three of the strongest things. For the man was venerable, as I do not know if
any other of the philosophers among us, and able to furnish courage, if he
wished, to the body; and the city held the one grieving under suspicion from
the disturbance about the appointment, as having received the presidency not
lawfully nor canonically rather than tyrannically; and there were present some
of the western high priests, drawing toward themselves as much of the Church
as was orthodox. 29.1 What then did that noble man and disciple of the
Peaceful One? For he could neither strain against the insulters or the
zealots, nor was it of himself to fight, or to tear apart the body of the
Church, otherwise too being warred upon and lying dangerously under the then
domination of the heretics. 29.2 And at the same time having used us as
counselors about this and genuine exhorters, a fugitive from here he withdraws
with us toward Pontus, and presides over the retreats there; both establishing
something worthy of memory for them and embracing the desert with Elijah and
John, the very philosophers, thinking this more profitable for him than to
devise something about the present things unworthy of his own philosophy and
to destroy in a squall the steering of the reasonings in calm. 29.3 And
although the withdrawal was thus philosophical and wonderful, we shall find
the return better and more wonderful; for it was thus. 30.1 While we were in
these things, suddenly there stands over us a cloud full of hail and hissing
destruction, wiping out every church against which it burst and as much as it
overtook; a king most gold-loving and most Christ-hating, and sick with these
two greatest things, greed and blasphemy; 30.2 the persecutor after the
persecutor, and after the apostate not an apostate, yet nothing better for
Christians, or rather for the most pious and purest part of Christians and
worshiper of the Trinity, which alone I call piety and saving glory. 30.3 For
we do not weigh out divinity, nor alienate from itself the one and
unapproachable nature by outlandish alienations, nor do we heal evil with
evil, loosing the godless contraction of Sabellius by a more impious division
and cutting; which Arius having fallen sick with, he of the name of madness,
shook and corrupted the much of the Church, neither honoring the Father, and
dishonoring the things from him through the unequal ranks of divinity. 30.4
But we know one glory of the Father, the equal honor of the Only-begotten; and
one of the Son,

that of the Spirit. And whatever of the three we set below, we think we pull
down the whole; worshiping and knowing three in the properties, but one in the
divinity. 30.5 Of which that man thinking nothing nor being able to look
above, but being brought low by those leading him, dared to bring low with
himself even the nature of divinity; and he becomes an evil creature, bringing
down the lordship into slavery and setting with the creation the uncreated and
beyond-time nature. 31.1 He then so thinking also campaigns against us with
such impiety; for this is to be supposed nothing other than a barbarian raid,
pulling down not walls and cities and houses, nor any of the small and
handmade and again set up, but dragging down the souls themselves. 31.2 And
there invades with him also an army worthy, the evil leaders of the Churches,
the bitter tetrarchs of the inhabited world under him; who already having some
of the Churches, and attacking another, and hoping for another from the king's
inclination and hand, the one drawing, the other threatening, came even to
overthrow ours, having nothing of all things to take courage in so much as the
smallness of soul of those mentioned, and the inexperience of the one then
presiding over us and the ailments in us. 31.3 The contest then was great; and
the eagerness of most not ignoble, but the array weak, not having the champion
in front and the craftsman defender in power of word and Spirit. What then
that noble and great-souled soul and truly Christ-loving? 31.4 He did not even
need many words toward being present and fighting with us; but at once he saw
us as ambassadors, for the contest was common to both as having been put
forward for the word, and he was defeated by the embassy; and having divided
most well with himself and most philosophically with the reasonings of the
Spirit, that another time is of smallness of soul, if one must even suffer
such a thing, that of security; and another of long-suffering, that of
necessity; 31.5 at once he rises from Pontus with us and is jealous for the
truth in danger and becomes a volunteer ally and bearing himself he gives
himself to the mother Church. 32.1 Was he then thus eager, but has he
contended less than the eagerness? Or does he contend manfully, but not
wisely? Or in an educated way, but without danger? Or all these things
perfectly and beyond speech, but was there left in himself some remnant of
smallness of soul? By no means. 32.2 But at once he reconciles all, counsels,
arrays himself; he looses the stakes and stumbling blocks in the middle, and
those in which they took courage and campaigned against us; he takes on one,
holds another, beats off another; he becomes for some a strong wall and
palisade, for others an axe cutting a rock, or a fire in thorns, as the divine
scripture says, easily consuming the brushwood-like and insulters of the
divinity. 32.3 And if even Barnabas, the one saying and writing these things,
contended with Paul, thanks to Paul who chose and made him a fellow worker of
the contest. 33.1 They then so went away unsuccessful, and the evil badly then
first shamed and defeated, and having learned not easily to despise
Cappadocians, even if of all men; of whom nothing is so their own as the
unbroken of the faith and the faithful and genuine toward the Trinity; from
which both the being united and the being strong are theirs, things they help
being helped, or rather much better and stronger. 33.2 And for him a second
work and eagerness comes to be, to tend the president, to loose the suspicion,
to persuade all men that the things in which he had been grieved were a trial
of the evil one and a wrestling of one envying the concords toward the good;
and that he himself knew laws of ready obedience and spiritual order. 33.3 For
this 33.3 he was present, he was wise, he obeyed, he admonished, he was all
things to him, a good counselor, a right-hand supporter, of the divine things

an expounder, of the things to be done a guide, a staff of old age, a stay of
faith, of those within the most faithful, of those without the most practical;
in a word, so great toward goodwill as he was formerly thought toward enmity.
33.4 From there there remained for him even the power of the Church, even if
he had the second things of the seat; for bringing in the goodwill, he
received the authority in return; and there was a wonderful agreement and
intertwining of being able. The one led the people, the other the leader; and
he was as a kind of lion-keeper, taming by art the one in power; 33.5 for he
needed it, having been set newly upon the seat, and still breathing something
of the worldly matter, and not yet fitted in the things of the Spirit, and
much surge boiling around and the enemies of the Church lying upon it, of the
one leading by the hand and underpropping. For this he both loved the
alliance, and supposed that he himself ruled when that man ruled. 34.1 And of
the man's care and presidency about the Church, there are many other marks
too; frankness toward rulers both the others and the most powerful of the
city; loosing of differences not distrusted, but stamped by his voice, using
the manner as a law; 34.2 presidencies of those in need, the more part
spiritual, and not a few also bodily; for this too often bears toward a soul
enslaved through goodwill; feedings of the poor, lodgings of strangers, cares
of virgins; legislations of monks, both written and unwritten; arrangements of
prayers, good orders of the platform, the other things by which the true man
of God and stationed with God would benefit a people; but one, which is both
greatest and most notable. 34.3 There was a famine, and of those ever
remembered the most harsh. And the city was laboring, and help was from
nowhere, nor any drug of the distress. For the seacoasts bear such lacks not
harshly, giving the things from themselves and receiving the things from the
sea; but for us inlanders even the surplus is useless, and the lacking
uninventable, not having how either we may dispose something of the things
that are or bring in the things that are not. 34.4 And what is harshest in
such things, the insensibility and greed of those who have. For they watch the
seasons and make merchandise of the lack and farm the misfortunes; neither
hearing that he who has mercy on the poor lends to the Lord; nor that he who
holds grain is publicly cursed; nor any other of the things either promised to
the humane or threatened to the inhumane. 34.5 But they are greedier than they
ought and think badly; shutting their own bowels to those, and the bowels of
God to themselves, of which even needing more they do not know it than others
of them. These things the grain-buyers and grain-hucksters, neither
reverencing kinship, nor thankful toward the divine, from whom the having is
theirs, while others are pressed. 35.1 But he was not able to rain bread from
heaven through prayer, and to feed in a desert a fugitive people, nor to make
food well up from bottoms unspent, filled by emptying, which is even a
paradox, that he might feed a woman feeding in return for hospitality; nor to
feast thousands with five loaves, of which even the remnants of many tables
are another power. 35.2 For these things were of Moses and Elijah and of my
God, from whom even to those was the being able to do these things; and
perhaps also of those times and of the then condition, since the signs are for
the unbelieving, not for those who believe. But the things that follow these
and bear to the same, these he both thought and accomplished with the same
faith; 35.3 for by word having opened the storehouses of those who have and by
exhortations, he does the thing of the scripture, he breaks food for the
hungry and fills the poor with bread and nourishes them in famine and fills
hungry souls with good things. And in what manner? For this too is not small
as an addition. 35.4 For having gathered in the same place the wounded of
famine, and some even breathing a little, men and women,

infants, old men, every pitiable age, collecting every form of food, whatever
happens to be a help of famine, and having set out cauldrons full of pulse and
of the pickled relish among us that also feeds the poor; 35.5 then imitating
the ministry of Christ, who even girding himself with a towel did not disdain
to wash the feet of the disciples, and using his own children, or rather
fellow slaves, as fellow workers toward this, he tended the bodies of those in
need, and he tended the souls, intertwining with the need the thing of honor
and making them easier from both sides. 36.1 Such was the new grain-giver for
us, and a second Joseph; except that we have even something more to say. For
the one makes merchandise of the famine and buys Egypt with the humane, having
disposed the time of plenty into that of the famine and being arranged toward
this by the dreams of others; but he was good freely and an unmercenary helper
of the grain-lack, looking to one thing, to acquire the humane by the humane
and to hit upon the good things from there through the grain-measuring here.
36.2 These things with the food of the word and the more complete benefaction
and distribution, the truly heavenly and high; if indeed the bread of Angels
is word, by which souls are fed and given to drink, hungering for God and
seeking food not flowing nor going away, but always remaining; 36.3 of which
he was grain-giver, and very rich, being the poorest of those we know and most
resourceless, not healing a famine of breads nor a thirst of water, but a
longing of word truly life-giving and nourishing, and leading the one well fed
into increase of spiritual stature. 37.1 From these then and such things, (for
why must one linger saying all things?) when the one of the name of piety had
just been transferred and had sweetly expired in his hands, he is led up to
the high seat of the episcopate; not without toil nor without envy and
wrestling both of those presiding over the fatherland and of the most wicked
of the city arrayed with them. 37.2 Yet the Holy Spirit had to conquer; and
indeed he conquers with much surplus. For he moves from beyond the borders
those who would anoint, men known for piety and zealots, and with these the
new Abraham and our Patriarch, I mean my father, about whom even something
wonderful happens. 37.3 For not only having failed in the multitude of years,
but also worn by disease, and being at the last breaths, he dares the road, to
help the vote and having taken courage in the Spirit. And I will utter
something brief, a dead man having been put in as into some tomb, the litter,
he comes back young, strong, looking above, having been strengthened from the
hand and the anointing, and it is not much to say even from the head of the
one anointed. 37.4 Let this be added to the ancient narratives, that toil
grants health, and eagerness raises the dead, and old age anointed with the
Spirit leaps. 38.1 And having been thought worthy of the presidency thus, as
those who have become such, and have obtained such grace, and are likely so
supposed, he shamed in none of the things following either his own philosophy,
or the hopes of those who had trusted; 38.2 but always surpassing himself by
so much as before this he was shown surpassing the others, thinking about
these things most well and most philosophically. For he held it to be the
virtue of a private man, not to be evil, or even somehow good; but the vice of
a ruler and president, and especially such a rule, not to excel the many by
much, nor always to appear better, nor to measure the virtue with the dignity
and the seat; 38.3 for it is scarcely possible for the extreme to hit the
middle, and by the surplus of virtue to draw the many toward the moderate; or
rather, that I may philosophize better about these things, which I behold in
the case of the Savior, and I think each of the wiser too, when he came to be
with us, having been formed what is above us and ours, this I reckon to have
happened here too. 38.4 For that one advanced, it says, as in stature, so

also in wisdom and grace; not by these things taking increase, for what could
become more complete than the complete from the beginning? but by these things
being little by little unveiled and shown forth. And the virtue of the man, I
think, then took not an addition, but a greater working, using the authority
as more matter. 39.1 First he makes that clear to all, that it was not a work
of human grace for him, but a gift of God, the thing given; and ours too will
make it clear. For how he philosophized with me philosophizing about that
time! For when all the others thought that I would both run up to what had
happened and be overjoyed, which perhaps even another would have suffered, and
would share the rule rather than be a fellow dynast, and judging this by the
friendship; 39.2 since I, fleeing the burdensome, and indeed in all things, if
anyone else, and at the same time the enviable of the time, otherwise too of
those around him still in labor and being troubled, remained at home, having
bridled the longing by force, he blames, yet he pardons. 39.3 And after this
when I stood by, but did not receive the honor of the seat, for the same
cause, nor the preference of the presbyters, he neither blamed me, and he
praised besides, doing well, having chosen rather to be accused of pride by a

81.1 Come then standing around me all his chorus, as many as of the platform
and as many as of those below, as many as of ours and as many as of those
outside, work the good report with me, one recounting and seeking some other
of his fair things, 81.2 those of the seats the legislator, those of the
commonwealth the city-founder, those of the people the good order, those about
words the educator, the virgins the bride-leader, those under a yoke the
temperer, those of the desert the wing-giver, those of the mixing the judge,
those of simplicity the guide, those of contemplation the theologian, 81.3
those in good cheer the bridle, those in misfortunes the consolation, the
staff the grayness, the education the youth, the poverty the provider, the
plenty the steward; 81.4 and widows seem to me that they will praise the
patron, and orphans the father, and poor the poor-lover, and the
stranger-lover the strangers, and brothers the brother-lover, the sick the
physician, whichever disease and healing you wish, the healthy the guardian of
the health, all the one who became all things to all that he might gain all or
more.

82.1 These things for you from us, O Basil, of the tongue once sweetest to you
and of equal honor and of like age. If they are near the worth, this is your
grace; for taking courage in you, I set up the speech about you; and if far
and by much beside the hope, what must one suffer even worn by old age and
disease and by longing for you? 82.2 Yet even what is according to power is
dear to God. And may you look over us from above, O divine and sacred head,
and the thorn of the flesh given to us from God, our education, either stop by
your own intercessions or persuade to bear stoutly; and may you lead through
all life for us toward the most profitable. 82.3 And if we should be
transferred, may you receive us from there too in your own tents, that living
together with one another and looking over together the holy and blessed
Trinity, more purely and more completely, of which now we have moderately
received the appearances, we may stand here of the desire, and may take this
as the return of the things for which we have fought and been fought. 82.4 For
you then this speech from us; and who will praise us after you when we leave
life? Even if we should furnish something worthy of praise to the words, in
Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom the glory unto the ages; Amen.
