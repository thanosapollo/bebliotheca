* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I know that most writers have so dedicated the life of Marcus and of
Verus to letters and to history that they have given their readers
Verus first to be made known, following not the order of ruling

but of living. I, however, because Marcus began to rule first, then
Verus, who died while Marcus still lived, have thought that Marcus
should be celebrated first, and Verus thereafter.

Therefore Lucius Ceionius Aelius

Commodus Verus Antoninus, who by Hadrian's will was called Aelius, and
by his connection with Antoninus was called Verus and Antoninus, is
placed neither among the good princes nor among the bad.

It is agreed that he did not bristle with vices, nor abound in virtues,
and that he then lived not in a principate of his own and free, but
under Marcus in an empire of like and equal

majesty, from whose way of life he differed by the wantonness of his
manners and by the excess of a too free

life. For he was of simple character, and one who could disguise
nothing.

His natural father was Lucius Helius Verus, who, adopted by Hadrian,
was first called Caesar and, being set in

that same station, died. His grandfathers and great-grandfathers and

likewise most of his ancestors were of consular rank. Lucius was born
at Rome in his father's praetorship on the eighteenth day before the
Kalends of January,

the same day as Nero, who gained the mastery of affairs. His paternal
origin was for the most part from Etruria, his maternal from Faventia.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Born of this stock, when his father had been adopted by Hadrian he
passed into the Aelian family, and when his father the Caesar had died

he remained in Hadrian's family, and by him was given to Aurelius to be
adopted, when that man had wished Pius to be his son and Marcus

his grandson, providing well enough for posterity, and indeed on this
condition, that Verus should receive Pius' daughter. She was given to
Marcus for this reason, that Verus still seemed unequal

in age, as we have set forth in the life of Marcus; but he married
Lucilla, the daughter of Marcus. He was brought up in the

Tiberian house. He heard Scaurinus the Latin grammarian, the son of
Scaurus, who had been Hadrian's grammarian; of the Greeks, Telephus

and Hephaestion,

Harpocration;

the rhetors Apollonius, Caninius Celer, and Herodes Atticus; the Latin
Cornelius Fronto; the philosophers

Apollonius and Sextus. All these he loved uniquely, and was loved by
them in turn, and yet he was not

gifted for letters. In boyhood he loved to make verses, afterwards
orations. And he is said to have been a better orator than poet, or
rather, to speak more truly,

a worse poet than rhetor. Nor are there wanting those who say that he
was helped by the talent of his friends, and that those very writings,
such as they are, were written for him by others; seeing that he is
said always to have had with him many eloquent and learned men.

He had Nicomedes as tutor. He was eager

and overly cheerful, and most fittingly suited to all pleasures, games,
and jests.

After his seventh year he was transferred into the Aurelian family and
was formed by Marcus' manners and authority. He loved hunting, the
wrestling-grounds, and all

the exercises of youth, and he was a private person in the imperial
house for twenty-three years.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

On the day Verus received the toga of manhood, Antoninus Pius, on the
occasion on which he was dedicating his father's temple,

was generous to the people. And Verus sat in the middle between Pius
and Marcus,

when as quaestor he gave a show

to the people. After the quaestorship he was at once made consul with

Sextius

Lateranus. After some years had passed he was made consul a second time
with his brother Marcus; but for a long time he was also

a private person and lacked that distinction with which Marcus was
adorned.

For he neither

sat in the senate before his quaestorship, nor on a journey rode with
his father but with the prefect of the praetorian guard, nor was any
other name of honor joined to him than that he was called the son of
Augustus.

He was devoted also to the circus, no less than to the gladiatorial
show. Though he was shaken by such great wanderings of pleasure and
luxury, he seems to have been kept by Antoninus for this reason, that
his father had ordered him to pass into Pius' adoption in such a way
that Pius should call him grandson. To him, as far as

it seems, he showed good faith, not love; yet Antoninus Pius loved the
simplicity of his nature and the purity

of his living, and urged him to imitate his

brother also. When Pius was dead, Marcus conferred everything upon him,
even the sharing of imperial power being granted, and made him his
partner, although the senate had conferred the empire on him alone.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

When the empire had therefore been given and tribunician power granted,
after the consulship

and the honor also conferred, he ordered him to be called Verus,
transferring his own name to him,

whereas before he was called Commodus. Lucius indeed,

returning the favor to Marcus, if he undertook anything,

obeyed

as a legate obeys a proconsul or a governor an emperor. For at the very
first he spoke to the soldiers for both of them, and for the harmony of
the empire

he conducted himself gravely and after Marcus' manners.

But when he set out for Syria

he was defamed not only for the license of a freer life but also for
adulteries and for loves of

young men; seeing that he is said to have been of such luxury that even
after he returned

from Syria he set up a cook-shop at home, to which he would turn aside
after Marcus' banquet,

with persons of every shameful kind

waiting on him. He is said also to have diced through the whole night,
having taken up that vice in Syria, and to have been so far a rival of
the vices of Gaius and of Nero and of Vitellius that he would wander at
night through taverns and brothels with his head covered by a common
traveler's hood and revel with his drinking companions, pick quarrels,
hiding who he was, and often come back beaten, with a livid face, and
be recognized in the taverns when

he was hiding himself. He would also throw very large coins into the
cook-shops

with which he might break the cups. He loved charioteers too,

favoring the Green. He also more often had fights of gladiators at his
banquet, drawing dinners out into the night and falling asleep on the
dining-couch, so that he was lifted with the coverlets

and carried into the bedchamber. He was of very little sleep, of very
easy digestion.

But Marcus, knowing all these things well,

dissembled them

from that shame 2, so as not to rebuke his brother. And a banquet of
his of this sort is reported as most famous, at which first twelve are
said to have reclined, although the saying about the number of guests
is most well known
/seven make a banquet,

but nine a brawl,/
handsome boys who waited on each being given as gifts, the carvers also
given, and dishes to each and every one, and living animals given,
whether of tame or of wild birds or of four-footed beasts,

whose meats had been served, cups also given to each for each drink, of
murra and of Alexandrian crystal, as often as they drank; golden and
silver cups given too, and jeweled ones, crowns even given with golden
ribbons set among them and with flowers out of season,

golden vessels also given with unguents in the form

of alabaster flasks, and vehicles given with she-mules and muleteers
with silver harness, that they might so return from the banquet.

And the whole banquet is said to have been valued at

six million sesterces. After Marcus heard of this banquet he is said to
have groaned and

to have grieved for the public fate. After the banquet they played

at dice until daylight. And these things indeed were after the Parthian
war, to which Marcus is said to have sent him, lest he should either
sin in the city before the eyes of all, or that he might learn thrift
by travel, or that he might return more amended by the fear of war, or
that he might know that he was

emperor. But how much he profited, both the rest of his life and this
dinner which we have told have shown.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

He had so great a care of the circus-games that he often both sent and

received letters from the province for the sake of the circus-games;
lastly even when present and sitting with Marcus he suffered many
insults from the Blues, because

he favored most shamefully against them. For he had also made a golden
image of the Green horse Volucer, which he carried with him.

For this horse indeed he would put raisins and pine-nuts in the manger
in place of barley; he would order him to be brought to himself in the
Tiberian house covered with cloaks dyed with purple; when he

died he made him a tomb on the Vatican. In this horse's honor they
first began to demand gold pieces or prizes

for horses. And that horse was in so great honor that a peck of gold
pieces was often demanded for him by the people of the Greens.

Marcus escorted him as far as Capua when he set out for the Parthian
war; and when from there he was stuffing himself through everyone's
villas, he fell sick of a disease at Canusium

and lay ill, and his brother hastened there to

see him.

Many slothful and sordid things in his life are uncovered even in time
of war.

For when the legate had been killed, the legions cut down, the Syrians
thinking of revolt, and the East was being laid waste, he was hunting
in Apulia and sailing among concerts and songs at Corinth and Athens,
and through each of the maritime cities of Asia and Pamphylia

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

and those more famous of Cilicia he lingered in pleasures. After he
came to Antioch, he himself indeed gave himself to luxury, but the
generals finished the Parthian war, Statius Priscus and Avidius Cassius
and Martius Verus, through four years, so that they reached Babylon and
Media

and claimed Armenia. And he himself received the names Armeniacus,
Parthicus, and Medicus, which were also

conferred on Marcus while he stayed at Rome. And through four years
Verus spent the winter at Laodicea, the summer

at Daphne, the rest at Antioch. He was a laughing-stock to all the
Syrians, of whom many jests spoken against him in the theatre

are still extant. He always admitted home-born slaves into the
dining-room at the Saturnalia

and on feast days. Yet he set out for the Euphrates, following the
urging of his companions.

He also returned to Ephesus, that he might receive his wife Lucilla,
sent by her father Marcus, and most of all for this reason, that Marcus
might not come with her into Syria and learn of his scandals.

For Marcus had told the senate that he would bring his daughter into
Syria.

When the war was indeed finished he gave kingdoms to kings, but the
provinces

to his companions to govern. From there he returned to Rome for a
triumph unwillingly, because he was leaving Syria as if it were his own
kingdom, and he triumphed equally with his brother, receiving from the
senate the names which he had received in the army.

It is said besides that in Syria he laid aside his beard at the bidding
of a common mistress; whence many things were said against him by the
Syrians.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

It was his fate that through those provinces by which he returned all
the way to Rome he seemed to bring the plague with him.

And the pestilence is said to have arisen in Babylonia, where from the
temple of Apollo, from a golden casket which a soldier had by chance
cut into, a pestilent breath escaped, and from there filled the
Parthians and the world.

But this was not by the fault of Lucius Verus but of Cassius, by whom
Seleucia, which had received our soldiers as friends,

was stormed against good faith. And this among others even Quadratus,
the writer of the Parthian war, clears, after blaming the people of
Seleucia, who had first broken faith.

Verus had this reverence for Marcus, that he shared with his brother
the names

which had been conferred on him, on the day

of the triumph, which they celebrated equally. Returned from the
Parthian war, Verus showed less respect toward his brother; for he both
indulged his freedmen more dishonorably and arranged many things
without his brother.

To these things was added that, as if he were bringing some kings to
the triumph, so he brought actors out of Syria, of whom the chief was
Maximinus, whom he named by the name of Paris.

Besides he built a most notorious villa on the Via Clodia, in which for
many days he himself raved in huge luxury with his freedmen and with
unequal friends,

in whose presence

there was no

reverence, and he asked Marcus, who came, that he might show his
brother the venerable and imitable holiness of his own manners; and
staying five days in the same villa he gave his attention to continuous
hearings, while his brother was either dining

or preparing banquets. He had also the actor Agrippus, whose surname
was of Memphis, whom he had also brought from Syria as if a Parthian
trophy,

whom he named Apolaustus. He had brought with him also lyre-girls and
flute-players and actors and mimic buffoons and jugglers and all kinds
of slaves, on whose pleasure Syria and Alexandria are fed, so that he
seemed to have finished not a Parthian war but an actors' war.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

And that this difference 1 of life and many other things had made
quarrels between Marcus and Verus, open truth did not show, but hidden
rumor had sown.

But this was the chief point: that when Marcus had sent a certain Libo,
his cousin, as legate into Syria, and that man carried himself more
insolently than a modest senator, saying that he would write to his
brother if he should by chance

doubt of anything,

and Verus, being present, could not bear it, and he had suddenly died
of a disease, with signs of poison almost appearing, it seemed to some,
yet not to Marcus, that he had been killed by Verus' fraud; which thing
increased the rumor of quarrels.

Freedmen had much power with Verus, as we have said in the life of
Marcus, Geminas and Agaclytus, to whom he gave Libo's wife against
Marcus'

will; lastly, when the wedding was celebrated by Verus,

Marcus was not present at the banquet. Verus had other wicked freedmen
too, Coeden and Eclectus

and the rest, all of whom Marcus after Verus' death cast off under a
show of honor,

keeping Eclectus, who afterwards killed his son Commodus.

To the German war, because Marcus did not wish either to send Lucius
without himself to the war or to leave him in the city because of
luxury, they set out together and came to Aquileia, and against
Lucius' will they crossed the Alps,

when Verus had only hunted and banqueted at Aquileia,

but Marcus had looked to everything.

Concerning that war, what was done

through envoys of the barbarians seeking peace, what

through our generals, has been discussed most fully in

the life of Marcus. And when the war in Pannonia had been settled they
returned to Aquileia at Lucius' urging,

and because Lucius longed for the city's

pleasures, they hastened

to the city. But not far from Altinum Lucius was suddenly seized in the
carriage by the disease which they call apoplexy; taken down from the
carriage, his blood drawn, he was brought to Altinum, and when he had
lived three days without speech, he died at Altinum.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

There was talk that he had also committed incest with his mother-in-law
Faustina. And he is said to have been destroyed by the treachery of his
mother-in-law Faustina, with oysters sprinkled with poison, for this
reason, that he had betrayed to the daughter the intimacy

which he had had with the mother. Although that story too which is set
in the life of Marcus, being abhorrent

from the life of such a man, arose, since many assign the crime of his
death even to his wife, and for this reason, that Verus had indulged
Fabia too much, whose power his wife

Lucilla

could not bear. There was indeed so great a familiarity between Lucius
and his sister Fabia that

rumor also claimed this, that they had entered a plan to take Marcus

from life, and when this had been betrayed to Marcus through the
freedman Agaclytus, Lucius was forestalled

by Faustina,

lest he should forestall.

He was handsome in body, of a gracious countenance, with a beard let
down almost in the barbarian fashion, tall, and by a brow drawn more
closely toward the eyebrows

venerable. He is said indeed to have had so great a care of his yellow
hair that he would sprinkle gold-filings on his head, that the hair,
being more lit, might grow more yellow.

He was rather halting in speech, most greedy of dice, of a life always
luxurious, and in most things a Nero, except for

cruelty and mockeries. Among the rest of his apparatus of luxury he had
a crystal cup named Volucer from the name of that horse which he loved,
which went beyond the measure of a human drink.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He lived forty-two years; he reigned with his brother eleven years; and
his body was borne into Hadrian's tomb, in which his natural father the
Caesar is also buried.

A story is known, which the life of Marcus does not admit, that Marcus,
when he had cut a piece of sow's womb with a knife poisoned on one
side, offered Verus the part smeared with poison.

But this

it is impious to think of Marcus, although both the thoughts and the
deeds of Verus deserve it. Which we shall not leave in the middle but,
having wholly cleared and refuted it, we spit it out, since even now
after Marcus, except for Your Clemency, Diocletian Augustus, not even
flattery seems to have been able to invent such an emperor.
