OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA ON THE HOLY SPIRIT AGAINST THE MACEDONIANS THE
PNEUMATOMACHI
Perhaps it is not even proper to answer the vain among words; for the wise
precept of Solomon seems to tend to this, the one that commands not to answer
a fool according to his folly. But since there is a danger that through our
silence falsehood may prevail over the truth and this putrefying gangrene of
heresy, taking much pasture against the truth, may mutilate the sound word of
the faith, it appeared necessary to me not to answer according to their folly
those who put forward such things against piety, but for the correction of the
wicked suppositions. For the proverbial precept seems to me to enjoin not
silence, but correction of those who are being foolish, that the answers must
not run along with the folly of the suppositions, but rather overturn their
mindless and deceived opinions about the doctrines. What then is it that they
bring against us? They accuse of impiety those who have magnificent
suppositions about the Holy Spirit; and whatever we, following the doctrines
of the fathers, confess about the Spirit, taking these according to what seems
good to them, they furnish themselves a starting-point against us for an
indictment of impiety. For when we confess that the Holy Spirit has been
ranked with the Father and the Son so that there is no variation in any of the
things piously thought and named concerning the divine nature, except that the
Holy Spirit is contemplated as peculiar in hypostasis, because he is from God
3,=1.90= and is of Christ, as it is written; being confused neither according
to the unbegotten with the Father nor according to the only-begotten with the
Son, but being contemplated by himself in certain distinctive properties, in
all other things, as I said, having the conjunction and the invariable
identity, as we confess; the opponents say that he has been alienated from the
Father and the Son as regards natural communion and that because of the
difference of nature he has been ranked below and lessened in all things, in
power and glory and dignity and once for all in all the names and thoughts
spoken in a manner befitting God; and for this reason they say he is without
share in glory, and unworthy of equal honor with the Father and Son; and that
he shares in power only so much as is sufficient for him toward certain
assigned and partial activities, and that he stands altogether outside the
creative strength. And when this supposition prevails among them, it is
constructed by them as a consequence that the Spirit has in himself none of
the things piously said and thought concerning the divine nature. What then is
our word? We will answer nothing new nor from ourselves to those who challenge
us with such things, but we will make use of the testimony of the divine
scripture concerning the Holy Spirit, through which we have learned that the
Holy Spirit is and is called divine. If then they too agree with this and do
not oppose the God-inspired voices, let those who are ready for war against us
say, for what reason they fight not against the scripture but against us? For
we too say nothing other than this. And confessing that <the Spirit is> of
divine nature we recognize no difference, either from the teaching of the
scriptures or from common notions, in this very thing; so that the divine and
transcendent nature would be divided against itself through some inten
3,=1.91= sity or remission, differing from itself by the more and the less.
For since it is believed to be simple and uniform and incomposite, and no
plaiting and composition from dissimilars is contemplated about it, for this
reason, whenever once we have conceived a divine


nature in the soul, we have accepted along with this name the perfection in
every thought befitting God; for the divine has perfection in every account
according to the good. But if it should fall short in something and lag behind
perfection in any respect, according to the deficiency the account of godhead
also will limp, so that it is no longer divine in that part either to be or to
be called. For how could one fit this appellation to the imperfect and
deficient and needing addition from elsewhere? And it is possible to confirm
the argument also through bodily examples. For the nature of fire equally in
all the parts that complete it furnishes the heating sensation to those who
touch it; and it is not that one part of the flame has heat stretched beyond,
and another lowered; but as long as it is fire, it has throughout an undivided
union with itself by identity of activity. But if something should be cooled
in some part, it will no longer be named fire according to the cooled part,
the name also being changed along with the alteration of the heating activity
toward the opposite. In the same way also of water and of air and of all
things that underlie elementally there is one and the same account upon each,
not admitting excess or diminution. For water cannot be said more or less; for
as long as it is equally wet, the appellation of water is true of it; but if
it should be changed toward the opposite quality, the name also has altogether
been altered upon it. And of air the soft and upward-tending and light is
equally contemplated in all the parts; but the dense and heavy and flowing
toward earth escapes even being named air. Thus also the divine nature, as
long as through every thought piously contemplated about it 3,=1.92= it has
perfection, will make the appellation true by the perfection of the good; but
if something of the things stretching toward the account of perfection should
be withdrawn, the name of godhead will be false in that part and will not fit
the underlying thing; for it is equal or even more impossible to fit the
surname of water to a dry body and to address as fire that which is cooled in
quality and to call the solid and resistant air, and to name divine that in
which the notion of perfection is not understood along with it. If then truly
and not as far as a name the Holy Spirit has been addressed as divine by the
scripture and by our fathers, what argument is still left for those who stand
against the glory of the Spirit? For if he is divine, he is altogether also
good and powerful and wise and glorious and eternal and all whatever names of
such a kind there are that raise our suppositions toward the magnificent. That
he does not have these by participation the simplicity of the underlying thing
testifies, as if one were to suppose him to be one thing by his own nature,
and to become another by the presence of the things said; for these things are
proper to those who have obtained a composite nature. But that the Holy Spirit
is simple has been agreed equally by all, and the one who contradicts is not.
If then the account of his nature is simple, he does not have the good as
acquired, but whatever he is, he is goodness, wisdom, power, sanctification,
righteousness, eternity, incorruption, all the high and surpassing names; from
what thought then do those who do not fear the hard condemnation of blasphemy
against the Spirit construct such a one to be without glory? For they clearly
put this forward, that one must not believe him to be glorifiable, I do not
know by what reasoning they judge it advantageous not to confess that which is
by nature glorious as being what it is. For neither is that sufficient for
them as a defense, that because he has been delivered third in order by the
Lord to the disciples, for this reason he has been alienated from the thought
befitting God. For in those in whom the activity according to the good has no
diminution 3,=1.93= or variation, how is it reasonable to think the order
according to number a sign of some diminution, of variation according to
nature? Just as if someone seeing the flame divided among three lamps; and let
us suppose the cause of the


third light to be the first flame, kindling the end through the middle by
transmission, then should construct that the heat is in excess in the first
flame, but in the next has been ranked below and has the variation toward the
less, and that the third is no longer even called fire, even if it burns and
shines similarly and works all the things of fire; but if nothing hinders the
third lamp from being fire, even if it shines forth from a preceding flame,
what is the wisdom of those who think it pious for this reason to set aside
the dignity of the Holy Spirit, because he was numbered after Father and Son
by the divine voice? For if something of the thoughts befitting God is lacking
among the things contemplated upon the nature of the Spirit, they testify well
to him the inglorious; but if through all things the greatness of the dignity
of the Holy Spirit is understood, why are they petty about the confession of
the glory? Just as if someone saying that a certain one is a man should no
longer think it safe to confess along with this upon him the rational or
mortal or whatever else is said about man, and for this reason should overturn
again what he gave; for if he is not rational, he is not a man at all; but if
this has been granted, how is what is thought conjunctly with the man doubted?
Thus then if the one who says divine about the Spirit speaks truly, neither
does the one who defines this to be honorable and glorious and good and
powerful lie; for with the notion of divinity all such thoughts enter in
together; so that of the two it is necessary that it be the one or the other,
either not even to say divine, or not to withdraw any of the thoughts
befitting God from the divinity. For this reason the two must altogether be
grasped with one another, both the divine nature with the 3,=1.94= fitting
supposition and the pious notions about the divine and transcendent nature.
Since then it has been said that the Spirit is of the divine nature and it has
been said well, and every magnificent notion is indicated together with this
name as has been said, the one who has granted that has by the power confessed
the remaining things, that he is also glorious and powerful and whatever
carries the meaning toward the better. For it does not have a nature that
these things not be confessed upon the Spirit because of the incongruity of
the things distinguished against such names; for the one who does not grant
glorious will grant inglorious; and the one who sets aside the powerful upon
him will agree with the opposite; likewise also concerning the honorable and
the good; and if he should not accept the things toward the better, he will
altogether confess the opposites. But if this is dreadful and beyond all
absurdity and blasphemy, it is clear that those who are pious will agree with
the more significant names and thoughts about the Holy Spirit and will say
that these are, which indeed we have often said, honorable, powerful,
glorious, good, and whatever else of the things stretching toward piety is
said. And these things are not present to the Spirit imperfectly nor having a
limited quantity of the beautiful, but corresponding with the appellations to
the infinite; for he is not honorable up to a point, then something else is
thought besides the honorable, but always such. And if you should reckon
behind the ages and if you should look toward what is next, in nothing will
you find the lacking toward honor or glory or power so as either to be
increased by addition or lessened through subtraction. Therefore if he is
perfect throughout, he admits diminution in nothing. For in whatever the
perfection about such a supposition is diminished, in that it will give room
to the more dishonorable of the suppositions; for that which is not perfectly
honorable is supposed to share in some part in the opposite. But if even to
take this as far as a thought is of the last madness, it is well altogether to
testify to him the perfection in the goods as unbounded and uncircumscribed
and in no part lessened. 3,=1.95= If then he is such, let the argument next
examine the equal also concerning the Son and likewise concerning the Father.
Does he not confess the perfection of the honor both upon this one and upon
that one? I think all who have mind will agree with what has been said. If
then the honor of the Father is perfect, and that of the Son also perfect, and
the perfection of the honor has been testified also to the Holy Spirit, why do
the new dogmatists legislate for us that we must not


confess upon him toward Father and Son the equal in honor? For we, following
the things examined, are able neither to say nor to think that what needs no
addition toward completion is more dishonorable than some other. For where the
argument does not discover the exceeding because of the unlacking and perfect,
in what it will grasp his diminution, I do not see. But those who forbid the
equal in honor altogether dogmatize the more dishonorable. And they will turn
aside all the pious suppositions about the Spirit in the same way according to
the same sequence through the diminution according to comparison toward the
opposite, not co-testifying the perfect in goodness nor in power nor in any
other of the things piously said about him. But if, declining the obviousness
of the impiety, they confess the perfect in every thought said according to
the good, let the wise say how of perfect there is perfect or more perfect or
more imperfect; for as long as the account of perfection is fitted, this
reasoning admits neither the more nor the less in the notion of the perfect.
If then they agree that the Holy Spirit is perfect through all things, and it
has been confessed in addition that it is pious that both of the Father and of
the Son there is the perfect in every good, what is the argument according to
which they judge it reasonable again to take away what they have given; for to
take away the equal in honor is a demonstration of not thinking 3,=1.96= him
to share in the perfection. And this very honor upon the divine nature, what
then do they think it is, of which they wish the Spirit to be without share?
Do they say this which also men grant to men, both serving with word and with
figure, showing the subject in the going-forth and whatever such things by the
vain custom of life come to be by the account of honor? All which things have
been constituted with the choice of those who work these things; of whom, if
by hypothesis they have not chosen, none of men has from nature any
starting-point toward being more honorable than the rest, all being recognized
similarly according to the same measures of nature. And the argument is clear
and having no doubt; for the one who today because of the rule over which he
stands seems to the many to be honorable we shall find next becoming one of
those who honor, the rule having been transferred to another. Do they then
invent some such form of honor also upon the divine nature, so that when we
wish he has the honorable, but when we have ceased from honoring the divine
honor ends together with our choice? Or is it ridiculous and impious at once
to think such things? For not through us does the divine become more honorable
than itself, but it always is in the same way, able to pass over neither
toward the worse nor toward the better; for the one it does not admit, the
other it does not have. In what manner then will you honor the divine? How
will you exalt the highest? How will you glorify that which is above all
glory? How will you praise the incomprehensible? If all the nations are as a
drop from a bucket, as Isaiah says, if all the life of men, fitted together,
should send up a like-voiced glory, what addition will the grace of the drop
be to that which is glorious according to nature? The heavens tell the glory
of God and are thought small heralds of the dignity; Because his magnificence
has been lifted up, not as far as the heavens, but above the heavens, which
have been encompassed by a small part of the godhead, the span tropically
3,=1.97= named. And shall a man, this perishable and short-lived living thing,
well likened to grass, being today and tomorrow not going to be, have believed
that he honors the divine nature worthily? It is like as if someone, having
kindled a thin fiber from tow, should suppose that he grants some addition to
the glitterings of the sun through the spark. Tell me, saying what will you
honor, if indeed you should wish at all to honor the Holy Spirit? That he is
altogether immortal, that he is unturning and unalterable and always beautiful
and without need of grace from elsewhere, that he works all things in all as
he wills, holy, leading, straight, just, true, searching the depths of God,
proceeding from the Father, being received from the Son, and if anything such.
What then by these and


such things do you grant? Do you say the things that belong, or do you honor
through the things that do not belong? For if you testify the things that do
not belong, the grace is vain and bringing nothing more; for the one who says
the bitter sweet has himself lied, but he has not praised the blameworthy. But
if you say the things that are, he is altogether such by nature both of the
one who confesses and of the one who does not; for the apostle says that If we
are unfaithful, he remains faithful. What then does the shrinking of the soul
wish, and the magnanimity <of those> who are ambitious concerning the Father,
and who grant to the Son to obtain the equal things, but concerning the Spirit
are petty with the grace, if indeed it has been shown that the honor that
belongs properly to the divine nature is not filled from our choice, but
exists together fittingly? For the ingratitude of the thankless is convicted
through such a judgment of theirs; but the Spirit is by his own nature
honorable, glorious, powerful, all the high things of the thoughts, even if
these do not wish it. Yes, he says. But we have been taught by the scripture
that the Father is creator; likewise we have also learned that through the
3,=1.98= Son all things have been generated; but the word has taught us
nothing such about the Spirit. And how is it reasonable to bring the Holy
Spirit into equal honor with the one who through the creation has displayed so
great a magnitude <of the> power? What then shall we answer to this? That they
have spoken vain things in their hearts who think that the Spirit is not
always with the Father and the Son, but at certain seasons is sometimes
contemplated by himself, and sometimes grasped conjunctly. For if without the
Spirit the heaven and the earth and all the creation came to be through the
Son alone from the Father, let those who say these things say, what was the
Holy Spirit doing then, when the Father with the Son was working the creation?
Was he occupied with some other works and for this reason did not take part in
the creation of all things? And what peculiar work of the Spirit do they have
to show, when the creation was being constituted? Or is it foolish and
mindless even to invent another creation besides the one that has subsistence
from the Father through the Son. But he was not occupied, yet by some
slackness and ease and shrinking from toils he was separated from the zeal
about the creation. But may the grace of the Spirit itself be gracious to us
upon this vanity of the words. For following in track the absurdity of those
who dogmatize such things we have unwillingly soiled the argument with their
notions as with some mire. For the pious thought has this manner: neither is
the Father ever thought without the Son nor is the Son grasped apart from the
Holy Spirit. For as it is impossible to go up toward the Father unless one has
been exalted through the Son, so it is impossible to say Lord Jesus except in
the Holy Spirit; therefore consequently and conjunctly the Father and the Son
and the Holy Spirit are always recognized with one another in the perfect
Trinity, and before all creation and before all ages and before every
graspable thought the Father is always Father and in the 3,=1.99= Father is
the Son and with the Son the Holy Spirit. If then these are inseparably with
one another, what is the vanity of those who attempt at some season to
separate the uncut and to divide the inseparable, so as to dare to say: The
Father alone made all things through the Son alone, the Holy Spirit in the
season of the creation either not being present or not working. For if he was
not present, let them say where he was, God having taken hold of all things,
if they invent some standing-place peculiar to the Spirit, so as to be by
himself separately according to the time of the creation; but if he was
present, how was he inactive? By not being able to do something or by not
wishing? Voluntarily being left behind or being driven by some more violent
necessity? For if from choice he embraced the idleness, he does not altogether
admit the working even in any other thing, and according to them the one who
says that he works all things in all as he wills is false. But if there is in
him the impulse toward working, but some overlying authority hinders the
purpose, let them say the cause of the one who hinders these things. By envy
of the


glory from the works, so that the wonder of the achievements may not also pass
over to another, or by not trusting the cooperation, as if his working would
be for the ruin of the whole? For altogether these wise men will also make
clear to us the causes of such suppositions. But if neither envy touches the
divine nature nor is some failure invented about the unfailing nature, what
does the pettiness of the thoughts wish for them, the one that marks off the
power of the Spirit from the creative cause, when it is necessary, with the
higher of the notions, to let go the low and human of the thoughts, and to
take a reasoning worthy of the height of the things sought; that neither did
the God over all, needing some cooperation, make all things through the Son,
nor does the only-begotten God, having the power less than the purpose, work
all things 3,=1.100= in the Holy Spirit. But the Father is a fountain of
power, and the Son is power of the Father, and the Spirit of power is the Holy
Spirit; and all the creation, as much as is perceptible and as much as is
bodiless, is a finished work of the divine power. And since no toil is
supposed in the constitution of the things about the divine nature (for
together with choosing what must come to be, at once the purpose becomes
substance), one would reasonably address all the nature that has subsistence
through the creation as a movement of will and an impulse of purpose and a
transmission of power, beginning from the Father and going forth through the
Son and being completed in the Holy Spirit. These things we, thinking in an
unprofessional way and according to the manner customary to us, do not accept
these wise things of those who bring objections, believing and confessing that
in every deed and thought, both cosmic and supercosmic and in the things in
time and in the things before the ages, the Holy Spirit is grasped with Father
and Son, being left behind neither in will nor in activity nor in any other of
the things piously thought according to the good; and for this reason, outside
the difference according to order and hypostasis, we grasp the varied in
nothing, but we say that he is numbered third in sequence after Father and
Son, and third also in the order of the tradition; but in all the remaining
things confessing the inseparable conjunction in nature and in honor and in
godhead and glory and magnificence and in the authority over all and in the
pious confession. But concerning service and worship and whatever such things
the wise among themselves put forward being petty, we say those things, that
the Holy Spirit is higher than all the things that come to be among us from
human choice; and our worship is lower than the honor owed and, if human
custom has anything else honorable, it is somewhere below the dignity of the
Spirit; for that which is by nature unmeasured is greater than those who from
a small and circumscribed and cheap power bring gifts 3,=1.101= to him
according to power. These things then we say toward those who agree with the
more pious supposition about the Holy Spirit, that he is both divine and of
divine nature. But if someone should set aside this voice and the thought
indicated together with the name of divinity, and should say that which is
carried about by the many toward the taking-down of the greatness of the
Spirit, that he is not of those who make, but of those who have been made, and
that it is proper to think him not of the divine but of the created nature, we
will answer this argument: that we have not learned to number those who are
thus among Christians. For just as one would not address as a man the
uncompleted embryo, but as able, if indeed it were completed, to come forth
into the generation of a man, but as long as it is in the incomplete it is
something else and not a man; so the argument does not know as a Christian the
one who has not received through the whole of the mystery the true formation
of piety. For it is possible also to hear Jews confessing God and our God. The
Lord also agrees with them in the gospel, that they think no other God than
the Father of the only-begotten, Whom you, he says, say that he is our God.
Must one then name the Jews Christians, because they too to worship the one
who is worshiped by us


confess? I know also Manichees carrying about the name of Christ. What then?
Since the name worshiped by us is revered among these, shall we for this
reason number them too among Christians? Thus also the one who confesses the
Father and accepts the Son, but sets aside the greatness of the Spirit, has
denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever and lies against the surname
of the Christian. The apostle commands the man of God to be complete. And
complete upon the common man would be that which has been filled out in every
account of the nature; for he must be rational [too], receptive of mind and
knowledge, 3,=1.102= sharing in life, upright in figure, able to laugh,
broad-nailed; but if someone should name a man, yet should not have the said
signs of the nature to furnish upon this one, he has honored in vain with the
address. Thus also the Christian is characterized by the faith into Father and
Son and Holy Spirit; this is the form of the one who has been formed according
to the mystery of the truth. But if the form should happen to be disposed
otherwise, I will not recognize the nature of the unformed; there is a
confusion of the character and an alienation from the form according to nature
and a variation of the recognizing signs of humanity, whenever the Holy Spirit
is not taken along with the faith. For the word of Ecclesiastes is true; that
this one is not a living man, but Bones, as that one says, in the belly of her
who is with child. For how will he confess Christ who does not think together
with the anointed the anointing? This one, he says, God anointed in the Holy
Spirit. Let those then who take down the glory of the Spirit and rank him
together with the subject nature tell us of what the anointing is a symbol. Is
it not of the kingship? What then? Have they not believed the only-begotten to
be by nature king? Those at least who have not once for all been wrapped in
heart with the Jewish veil will not contradict. If then the Son is by nature
king, and the anointing is a symbol of kingship, what does the argument
indicate to you through the sequence? That the anointing is not something
alien to the one who is king according to nature, nor has the Spirit been
ranked with the holy Trinity as something foreign and of another tribe. For
the Son is king; and the Holy Spirit is a living and substantial and
hypostatic kingship, by which, having been anointed, the only-begotten is
Christ and king of the things that are. If then the Father is king, and the
only-begotten is king, and the Holy Spirit is kingship, there is altogether
one account of the kingship upon the Trinity. And the notion of the anointing
hints through unspeakable things that there is no interval between the Son
3,=1.103= and the Holy Spirit; for as between the surface of the body and the
anointing of the oil nothing middle is thought, neither the argument nor the
sense, so undivided is the conjunction of the Son toward the Holy Spirit, so
that for the one who is going to touch him through faith it is necessary first
to encounter the myrrh through the touch; for there is not some part that is
naked of the Holy Spirit. For this reason the confession of the lordship of
the Son comes to be in the Holy Spirit for those who grasp, the Spirit meeting
beforehand from all sides those who approach through faith. If then the Son is
by nature king, and the Holy Spirit is a dignity of kingship, with which the
Son is anointed, what alienation of the kingship according to nature toward
itself is invented? Then let us also consider this: the kingship is altogether
recognized in the rule of the subjects. What then is the subject of the
kinging nature? The argument altogether grasps the ages and the things in
these; For your kingship, it says, is a kingship of all the ages. And saying
ages it includes from the containing all the creation constituted in them,
both the visible and the invisible; for in them all things were created
through the maker of the ages. If then the kingship is always thought with the
king, and the subject nature is confessed to be something other besides the
ruling, what is the absurdity of those who fight with themselves, who add the
anointing to the one who is king according to nature as a dignity, but bring
down this very thing into the subject rank as of the dignity


inferior? For if it is of the subjects according to nature, how is it fitted
to the dignity of kingship of the only-begotten, becoming an anointing of
kingship? But if its leading is shown through being taken along into the
magnificence of the kingship, what is the necessity of being taken down
altogether into a private and slavish lowliness, being numbered with the
serving creation? For it is not possible for the two saying things about him
3,=1.104= to speak the true in each, that he is both leading and subject. For
if he leads, he is not lorded over; but if he serves, he is no longer grasped
with the kinging nature. For as men with men and angels with angels and all
things are recognized with those of the same nature, so it is necessary that
the Holy Spirit be confessed with one of the two, either with the lording or
with the bowing nature. For the argument recognizes nothing middle of these,
so that some property of nature newly made on the border of the created and
the uncreated should be thought to be between these, so as both to share in
both and to be perfectly neither. For it is not even possible to think some
mixing of the opposites and a plaiting of the created toward the uncreated
being blended together and two of the opposites being mixed into one
hypostasis; so that that which is molded through this strange mixing is not
only composite, but also has the composition from dissimilars and not agreeing
according to time; for that which has the hypostasis through creation is
altogether later than that which subsists uncreatedly. If then they say that
the nature of the Spirit has been mixed toward each, then they also invent
some mixing of the older toward the younger; and according to them there will
be something older than itself and again something later than itself, so as
both to have the more ancient with the uncreated and the younger with the
created. Since then this does not have a nature, it is altogether necessary to
say that the one of these is true upon the Spirit, which is the uncreated. And
let us consider also that, how much absurdity it has. All the things thought
in the creation having, according to this very thing, the having had being
through creation, the equal honor, what is the cause that separates the Spirit
from the rest into being ranked with the Father and the Son? For it is found
from the sequence of the argument that that which is contemplated together
with the uncreated nature is not from the creation; or if it should be from
there, it is able to have no more than the kindred creation nor to be fitted
to the overlying nature. 3,=1.105= But if they should say that he is both
created and able above the creation, again the created nature itself is found
at variance with itself and being divided toward the ruling and the subject,
so that the one benefits, and the other is benefited, and the one sanctifies,
