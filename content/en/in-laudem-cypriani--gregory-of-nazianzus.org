ORATION XXIV.
On the holy hieromartyr Cyprian, after returning from the country one day
after the commemoration.
A. Cyprian almost escaped us; O the =35.1172= loss! And you endured it, you
who more than all men admire the man, and honor him with the yearly honors and
festivals; Cyprian, of whom even those forgetful of other things must remember
what is necessary; if indeed the best things most of all ought to be
remembered, and those of which the remembering is both holy and profitable at
once. But let us pay the debt with interest, if we should appear so well
furnished, and not be altogether in want and poor. And if we are even
exceedingly poor, I know that he will forgive us both the delay and the
poverty, since the man is in all things magnanimous and a philosopher; only
let us give thanks that he did not escape us. And let us give thanks; for it
is worthy. And we must begin thus: that we have returned to you for a good
thing, and by the good measures of God, who both defines and directs all
things in weight and measure, from quiet to the word, from the love of martyrs
to martyrs, from bodily rest to the spiritual feast.
B. We longed for you, O children, and we were longed for in return by equal
measures; for I am persuaded. See a father's goodwill: I both speak my own and
bear witness to yours, and having been separated from one another so far as to
make the longing known and to test it by the absence, as painters do the
pictures, we have come together again. How great a spark of memory is even a
brief familiarity of friends, for those who are loving in manner, and who
imitate the philanthropy of God! And how should we not, being disciples of
Christ, who emptied himself for us even to a slave's form, and gathered to
himself those who were strangers to the heavenly things, both hold to and
cling to one another, and keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace,
which is the mystery of the law and the prophets, or rather the sum?
C. This then is one thing of the benefaction and the first, that we ran to one
another as quickly as possible and embraced. For zeal does not bear delay; and
a whole life is one day to those who suffer from longing. And second, which
=35.1173= is also the greatest, that we did not run after the feast, nor were
left behind from the mystagogy of the martyrs, and from the delight and
refreshment that comes to us from there. For I confess that in other things I
am more sluggish than anyone; and I have shaken off every longing, from the
time I enlisted with Christ, and nothing takes me of all that is pleasant and
much-sought to others; not wealth that is dragged below and overturned, not
pleasures of the belly, and satiety the father of insolence, not clothing soft
and flowing about, not the brilliances and graces of stones, not hearing that
bewitches, not smell that unmans, not the applauses of peoples and of theaters
that drive mad, which we long ago yielded to those who want them; not all the
offspring of that first tasting of ours, from which we perished; but I even
condemn much folly in those who allow themselves to be mastered by these
things, and to destroy the nobility of the soul by the pettiness about them,
and who attach themselves to things that flee as if they stood. But of that I
am exceedingly insatiable and I cling to it, and I accept myself in the
passion: I take delight in the honors of martyrs, and I exult in the blood of
athletes; and of others are the contests and

the victory, but mine the crowns. So much do I snatch beforehand the good
order, and so do I make the achievements my own!
D. All martyrs then must be celebrated, and for all we must readily open
tongue and hearing and mind, both speaking something about them eagerly and
hearing, and thinking all things less than their contest. For so it is: though
there are many things for us toward guidance to the better, and many the
trainings toward virtue, word, law, prophets, apostles, the very sufferings of
Christ, of the first martyr who went up upon the cross, and gathered me with
him, that he might nail my sin, and triumph over the serpent, and sanctify the
wood, and conquer pleasure, and save Adam again, and call back the fallen
image; though there are so many and such things for us, no less for our
pedagogy are the martyrs, rational whole-burnt offerings, perfect victims,
acceptable offerings, proclamations of the truth, pillories of the lie,
fulfillment of the law, at least of that understood spiritually, dissolution
of error, driving-out of wickedness, flood of sin, purification of the world.
E. But you, Cyprian, the thing and name most precious to me, more than
according to the other martyrs (for there is no envy among martyrs toward
martyrs); =35.1176= both am I especially overcome by your virtue, and I am
lightened by the memory, and I become as one possessed by pleasure, and in a
certain way I am present with the martyrdom, and I share the contest, and I am
wholly transferred toward you; perhaps through the kinship of words, in which
you so far surpassed the others as the rational surpass the irrational nature
(for somehow a charm comes in together with those joined by something, no less
in the remaining things than in those of blood-kinship); perhaps through the
suddenness and paradox of the change, which is greater than word and example.
For sweet is the sun after a cloud, by which it was till then covered; sweeter
is the spring, because it is after the gloom of winter; more pleasant is
smiling calm, and the sea spread out, and playing upon the shores after the
standing of the winds, and the laboring waves.
F. This Cyprian, O men (that those who know may become more pleasant by the
reminder, and those who do not know may learn the fairest of our narrations,
and the common ambition of Christians), this is that man, once the great name
of the Carthaginians, but now of the whole inhabited world, conspicuous in
wealth, and looked at for power, and known by race (if indeed the greatest
proof of nobility is a share in the senate and a presidency), the flower of
youth, the statue of nature, the power of words, both those according to
philosophy and as many as belong to the rest of education, and of these
whichever part you will; so that the variety rather than the peak in each was
admired, or rather the distinction in each of the polymathy about all things;
or, that I may divide more clearly, some things by the variety, some by the
peak, and some by both, but in all things he surpassed all.
G. Of the words then the words also are witnesses, which many and splendid
that man laid down for us, when by the philanthropy of God he transferred the
education, of him who makes all things and recasts them toward the better, and
by the word he made unreason bow; but from here I do not know how I should use
the word and what I should become; how I may not stretch the speech long, and
altogether outside the season, remembering all the things of Cyprian; and how
I may not greatly damage those present by the things left silent. That I may
therefore walk a middle way of the season and of the longing of those who
hear, thus it seems to me it must be done: =35.1177= to leave the other things
to those who know, to teach thoroughly those who do not know, if there are
any, that both may be benefited alike, both those who teach his things and
those who learn (since even to remember

the man is a sanctification, and the word is greatest toward exhortation of
virtue); but of one or two of his things to make mention briefly, and of these
as many as it is not possible even for one who wishes to pass by.
H. And I will remember the former life, and what way of salvation came to be
for him, and what the calling, and the transfer toward the better. That at
least is exceedingly ignoble and small-souled, to think that the athlete is
insulted by the memory of the more awkward things. For thus even Paul the
great would not be praiseworthy to us, and Matthew the tax-collector among the
worst, and Cyprian himself; the one remembering his former persecutions, and
the transfer of the zeal, that from the parallel he might more glorify the
benefactor; the other adding the Tax-collector to himself in the numbering of
the disciples, as some other honorable mark; and the other even by a long
speech pillorying his former wickedness, that he might bear this also as fruit
to God, the confession, and become a way of better hope for many of those
turning from wickedness. What then is the wickedness, and consider how great
and of what magnitude: He was a servant of daemons, he who later was a
disciple of Christ; and a most bitter persecutor, he the great contestant of
the truth; and in word and deed troubling our way, through the power in both,
he who after this was strongest in both for Christians. How great an evil,
even sorcery added to these, the most well-known of his things! How much more
terrible even insatiability of body, which can drive mad even those wise in
other things, and forces them to think worse, as an insolent colt, snatching
away the reasoning!
I. And the word has come to us upon the very head of the matter. And let no
one, looking toward the first things of Cyprian, give himself to pleasures;
but let him be made sober by the last. There was a certain virgin of the
well-born and orderly. =35.1180= Hear, virgins, and exult together, or rather
also as many of those under the yoke as are sober and lovers of sobriety; for
the narration is a common ornament to both. And the virgin was exceedingly
beautiful in form; let the divine David sing along with us to her, saying, All
the glory of the daughter of the king is within, a genuine bride of Christ, a
stored beauty, a living statue, an inviolable offering, an unenterable
precinct, a garden enclosed, a fountain sealed (for let Solomon also sing
something along), kept for Christ alone. Of this one the great Cyprian was
captured, I know not from where and how, of her who was in all things secure
and orderly. For greedy eyes touch even the untouchable, the most ready of
organs and most insatiable. And he was not only captured, but he also made
trial. O the folly, if he hoped to plunder this one, or rather the
shamelessness of him who both dares such things and persuades to dare! That
one even from the beginning slipped into paradise against the first formed;
and he stands among angels to demand Job; and at the last he dares against the
Master himself, who would dissolve him and put him to death, and he brings a
trial to the untried (since he saw the appearing of God as a second Adam), as
if he would wrestle this one down too. For he did not know that he would fall
upon divinity, having run toward humanity. What wonder then, if through
Cyprian also he makes trial of the holy soul, and of the untouched body?
J. Yet he made trial, and he uses a procurer, not some old little woman of
those suited to these things, but some one of the daemons who love bodies and
love pleasures; since the apostate and envious powers are swift toward the
service of such things, seeking many partners of the fall. And the wage of the
procuring, both sacrifices and libations, and the intimacy through bloods and
the savor of fat; for such wages had to be for those who grant such things.
But she, when she perceived the evil, and knew the plot (for the pure and
godlike souls are swifter toward hunting the one at work, even if he is most
sophistic and varied in the attempt), what does she do, and what

does she counter-craft against the craftsman of wickedness? Despairing of all
other things, she flees for refuge to God, and she makes her own bridegroom
her champion against the hated longing, who both rescued Susanna, and saved
Thecla; the one from bitter elders, the other from a tyrant suitor, and a more
tyrannical =35.1181= mother. Who is this? Christ, who both rebukes spirits,
and lightens those being baptized, and walks the sea on foot, and gives a
legion of spirits to the deep; and he rescues a just man from a pit, set forth
as food for lions, and having conquered the beasts by stretching out of hands;
and he rescues a fugitive prophet swallowed by a sea-monster, who even in the
bowels preserved the faith; and he saves Assyrian children in flame, having
cooled the pyre by an angel, and having yoked the fourth to the three.
K. Proclaiming these things and more than these, and beseeching the Virgin
Mary to help a virgin in danger, she puts forward the medicine of fasting and
of lying on the ground; at once wasting the beauty as a plotter, that she
might withdraw the matter of the flame, and consume the fuel of the passions;
and at once propitiating God through the faith and through the humility; for
by nothing of all things is God so served as by hardship, and by tears the
philanthropy is given in return. You long for the rest of the narration, I
know it. For you are in agony for the virgin, and for the lover no less, lest
the longing ended in evil for both. But take courage. For the longing becomes
a sponsor of faith; and the lover, betrothing a virgin to himself, is
betrothed by Christ; and the flame of the longings is quenched, but that of
the truth is kindled. How and in what way? Here for me is the sweetest of the
narration. The virgin conquers, the daemon is conquered. The tempter comes to
the lover, he reports the defeat; he is despised. He is vexed at the contempt;
he punishes the despiser. And what is the punishment? He takes up house in
him; in the former servant, that evil may be struck out by evil, and rage
become a remedy of rage. From the virgin he is beaten off, as some engine from
a wall strong and noble, a fugitive of word and of prayer; but with the sender
he wrestles, (O the wonder!) turning back again toward the one who threw, and
choking him, as some second Saul.
L. What then does the foolish lover and sober possessed man? He seeks the
loosing of the evil, he finds it; for everything that is pressed is
resourceful. =35.1184= What is the loosing? He flees for refuge =35.1184= to
the God of the virgin, as Saul to the lyre of David and the strikings; he
comes to her Shepherd; he is cleansed, as of the longing through the blow, so
of the wicked spirit through the faith toward Christ; he transfers the
longing; for long being disbelieved and sent away; for the thing seemed to be
of the unbelievable and marvelous, that Cyprian should ever be numbered among
Christians, even if all men were; but he is transferred then, and the proof of
the change is clear: He sets forth publicly the books of sorcery; he triumphs
over the weakness of the wicked treasure; he proclaims the folly, from them he
raises a bright flame, he consumes by fire the long deceit, which did not help
against one flame of flesh; he stands away from the daemons, he is made
intimate with God. O the grace! How great a one finds God by a wicked longing
and spirit, he becomes a sacred sheep of the sacred flock, and as I heard from
someone, even a temple-keeper, having begged much, that he might philosophize
the lowly toward cleansing of the former arrogance. Then a shepherd, and of
shepherds the strongest and most proven. For he does not preside over the
Church of the Carthaginians only, nor over Africa made famous from him and
through him even until now, but also over all the west, and almost even over
the east itself, both southern and northern lot, as far as that man came by
the wonder. Thus Cyprian becomes ours.

M. These things the God of signs and of wonders; these things, he who led
Joseph into Egypt as a bought slave through the spite of brothers, and tested
him in a woman, and glorified him in the giving of grain, and made him wise in
dreams, that he might be trusted in a strange land, and be honored by Pharaoh,
and become father of many myriads, for whose sake Egypt is tortured, the sea
is cut, bread is rained, the sun stands, the land of the promise is allotted.
For wisdom knows from afar to lay down the hypotheses of great things, and
through the contrary to manage the contrary, that it may even more be wondered
at. These things also of the fair things of Cyprian suffice toward a measure
of most perfect eulogy. But now the things remaining are so many and such,
=35.1185= that even if none of the things said before belonged to him toward
praise, it would suffice by the things that follow to conquer all. For that I
may cut short the things in the middle, the contempt of money, the dissolution
of pride, the pedagogy of the body and purity antagonist of the former
impulses, the philosophic about clothing, the about meetings both high and
philanthropic at once, so as to be equally far from cheapness and from
arrogance; the lying on the ground, the vigils, which, though he was a late
learner of such things, he far surpassed those who had gone before; the
ambition about words, from which he educated every character, and cleansed
lack of education of doctrines, and adorned the lives of men, and the divinity
of the original and royal Trinity being cut, and by some even being smeared
together, he led back to the ancient, remaining in the bounds of pious union
and co-numbering; that I may gather these things because of the lack of
measure, I will dissolve the word together with the dissolution of the life.
N. Decius was raging against us, and he invented all forms of punishments, and
some of the terrible things were already present, and some were about to be;
and he makes an equal contest both to take Christians, and to outdo the
persecutors before him, or rather, either all Christians, or Cyprian alone,
both to take and to set beside himself. For by how much he knew the man to
differ in piety and in glory, by so much he also saw the victory more
conspicuous and more splendid for himself, if he should master this one. For
in that way it would be to master Christians only, but thus even philosophy
itself, and words. And to take away the tongue first, then to lead away
voiceless and irrational those supported by it, he thought to be of the best
generalship; not rightly, nor piously thinking these things, yet not
altogether unreasonably, at least toward his own will and attempt; and the
deed showed it. For when he had shaken off every assault and trial both
manfully and nobly, as some seashore rock the onsets of waves, at last he is
condemned by him to exile; the noble man did not love the thing according to
himself, nor was he content being saved; nor did he think the dishonor more a
safety for the body than the quiet a danger of soul, and the overlooking of
the others who were in danger by the season, there not being one to train
them, and to give them courage toward the contest. For even a word is able not
a little toward an addition of courage for those stripping for the stadium of
virtue. =35.1188=
O. For this reason he was absent in the body, but present in the spirit, and
he contended together with those who were contesting; and he was not able to
help with the tongue, but he helps with the letter. How? He becomes an
anointer from beyond the border, writing the hortatory things, and composing
the piety in words, and that man almost alone makes more martyrs from letters,
than all who were present by themselves for those then toiling. For he
persuades them not to bring fatherland, not race, not substance, not power,
not any other of the things laid on the ground, before the truth, and the
prizes of virtue stored from there, for those who run risks on behalf of the
good. And that this is the best of businesses, to buy with a little blood the
kingdom of the heavens, and to receive in exchange eternity of glory for the
temporary goods. For there is one fatherland for the high, the Jerusalem that
is thought, not those

divided here by small bounds, and exchanging many inhabitants; and one
splendor of race, the keeping of the image, and assimilation toward the
archetype, as far as is reachable for those bound of flesh, and able to
receive a brief outflow of the fair; and one power, the mastery over the
wicked one, and the unconquerable and unvanquished of the soul in the contests
on behalf of piety, when wickedness contends toward virtue, and world toward
world, the one being loosed toward the one that stands, and a bitter president
of games toward noble contestants, and Beliar arrays himself toward Christ.
Through these things he was persuading them to despise swords, and to think
the fire cold, and to consider the wildest of beasts tame, and to take famine
as the highest luxury, and to pass by tears of their own, and dirges, and
wailings, as baits of the wicked one, and hindrances of the divine journey.
For these things are of manly and noble souls, and of sober reasoning. And the
example from near is he himself who says and writes these things, having
considered all things dung, that he might gain Christ.
P. Thus thinking, Cyprian, and thus arming with the words toward the contest,
was producing many athletes. And what wage of these does he receive? =35.1189=
How lavish and ambitious! He becomes a martyr upon all those whom he sent
before, having his head cut by the sword, and upon the many tortures he sets
this as the crown. Thus he is brought to Christ, thus he is transferred toward
Christ, Cyprian great in impiety, and greater in piety, the great both
persecutor and crown-wearer, marvelous no less for the change than for the
virtue. For it is not so great to preserve a type of good, as to cut a new
path of godliness. For the one is of habit, the other of good counsel; and the
one of things belonging to many, but of the other the examples are few.
Q. But what sort of thing is that too of his wonders? Let us still endure a
little with the word, that we may gratify the athlete. Such then was the man's
way of life, and such the manner of the contest. And when he dissolves the
life (if it is lawful to say this, and not to name his thing a departure
toward God, or a fulfillment of longing, or a loosing of bonds, or a sundering
of a burden), something is worked as a wonder here too worthy of the things
that went before; the name of Cyprian was great among all, and not among
Christians only, but also among those arrayed on the opposite side to us (for
the fair is equally venerable to all); but the body was unseen, and the
treasure was with some little woman of those warm toward piety, and this for a
long time; I do not know whether God was honoring the god-loving woman, and
for this reason clinging to the martyr, or whether he was exercising our
longing, if we would not bear being damaged, and being deprived of the relics
of the saints. And when the God of the martyrs did not endure to make the good
of all the private thing of someone, nor to damage the common by the grace
toward that woman, he makes the body public through a revelation, and
depositing this honor too with some woman of the worthy; that women also might
be sanctified, as they both formerly bore Christ and announced him to the
disciples after the resurrection from the dead, so now also Cyprian, the one
having shown him, the other having handed over the common benefit. This is the
last of his fair things. Thus he who is worthy not to be hidden comes into the
midst, and he is not permitted to philosophize his own theft, since he is
better and higher even than the honors upon the bodies. =35.1192=
R. The things from us then are so many, and I do not know that one must say
more. For not even if we should stretch a long speech would we say anything
worthy of the things belonging to him, and of those which each has supposed
about the man. And these things we went through, so far as to pay as a duty
the honor owed to him; but the remaining things must be added from you
yourselves, that you also may offer something to the martyr, the destruction
of the daemons, the dissolution of diseases, the foreknowledge of the future;
all which even the dust of Cyprian can do with the faith, as those who have
made trial know, and who have

sent the wonder even as far as us, and will hand it over to the time to come.
Or rather bring in beforehand the things greater than these, and such as it is
likely for those who genuinely honor that man, emptying of body, ascent of
soul, flight from wickedness, increase of virtue; the virgins the
unfleshliness, the women the good order of virtue rather than of body, the
young the manliness against the passions, the old the good counsel, those in
power the good law, those in generalship the gentleness, those in words the
reasonable; let me say something also of our own, the priests the mystagogy,
those of the people the obedience, those in mourning the consolation, those in
fair weather the fear, the rich the sharing, the poor the thanksgiving; all
the array against the wicked and bitter persecutor, that he may not strike
appearing, may not shoot hidden, may not war as darkness, may not play as an
angel of light, and steal toward the pit of destruction.
S. It is terrible to be captured by eyes, and to be wounded by tongue, and to
be baited by hearing, and to be burned through boiling anger, and to be
brought down by taste, and to be softened by touch, and to use the weapons of
salvation as weapons of death; when one ought, having fenced with the shield
of the faith, to stand toward the methods of the Wicked one; and having
conquered with Christ, and having contested with the martyrs, to hear that
great voice: Come, you blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom promised to
you, where is the dwelling of all who are gladdened, and of those dancing an
undissolved dance; where is a sound of those keeping festival, =35.1193= and a
voice of gladness, and a more perfect and purer illumination of divinity, of
which now the enjoyment is in riddles and shadows. By these Cyprian rejoices
being honored more than by all the other things together; these things he both
philosophized to the life when present, and when absent he commands to all
through our voice; which do not in any way dishonor, if at all the endurance
of that man concerns you, and his contests concerning the truth, and me who am
ambassador of these things. These are the first-fruits of my words to you, O
divine and sacred head; this to you both a prize of the words and of the
contest; not an Olympic wild-olive, nor Delphic apples, playthings, nor
Isthmian pine, nor Nemean celery, by which unfortunate youths were honored;
but a word, the most intimate of all things to the servants of the Word; and
if it is even worthy of your contests and words, the gift of the Word. But may
you look upon us from above, gracious, and may you lead through our word and
life, and may you shepherd this sacred flock, or shepherd it with us, both
directing the other things as far as possible toward the best, and sending
away the heavy wolves, the hunters of syllables and of words; and granting us
a more perfect and more splendid illumination of the holy Trinity, of which
you are now a stander-by, which we worship, which we glorify, with which we
live as fellow-citizens, worshipping Father in Son, Son in Holy Spirit, to
which may we also later stand by, sincere and without stumbling, of which may
we also partake, perfect perfectly, in Christ himself our Lord, to whom be all
glory, honor and power unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
