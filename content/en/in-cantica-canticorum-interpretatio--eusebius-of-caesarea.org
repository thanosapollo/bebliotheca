Interpretation of the Song of Songs by Eusebius Pamphili.
It is called Song of Songs for this reason: after the other songs comes
this one, and after this one no other is still to be expected. And by
"another" I do not mean a book, but what is signified from the book. And
what it means is of this kind. All divine Scripture prophesies concerning
the descent of the Word to us and his incarnate presence. For this is the
will of God, and this is the foremost thing for the prophets and for all
divine writing to announce. As an accompaniment to the divine narrative
they also make discourses concerning the nations of Babylon, of Damascus,
of Moab, and the rest; and through each writing they rebuke those who
sin, and they urge those already overtaken to repent, threatening the
coming judgment of fire. The other Scriptures, then, foretell, as Moses
does, saying: The Lord God will raise up a prophet from among our
brothers like me. And Isaiah: Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear
a son. And Micah: In the last days the mountain of the Lord shall be
manifest. And each one foretells in another way. And even while these
were speaking, according to the grace of the Spirit, the promised Lord
was still being =3.531= awaited; for this reason many also announced him,
and when he came those who prophesied ceased, and all these are songs
singing of the coming Savior.
The Song of Songs is not as something that prophesies, nor as something
that signifies beforehand; rather, the one whom the others announced in
advance, this book shows as already come and already having taken flesh,
and it rightly sings a wedding song over the union of the Word and the
flesh. And the other Scriptures, while speaking about the Savior, also
announce other things in between. But this book sings only the union of
the Word with the body. And the other Scriptures, because of the things
that take place in between, have also words of wrath and anger and the
threat of fears; but this book, since it signifies nothing else than the
presence of the Word alone, has only words of gladness and joy and
delight. When the bridegroom is present, it is fitting that all rejoice
and that no one mourn, as the Lord himself has said. For he himself,
being present, bore our weaknesses, and turned our sorrows into joy.
Just as, then, after the economy of the Savior we no longer expect a
prophet, so after the things signified in the Song of Songs there is
nothing newer to study or to expect as a further signification. For the
things signified in the Song are also found spoken at intervals in the
prophets and in the other Scriptures.
Again, just as, when John points to the Lamb, the law and the prophets
are until John, so the things signified in the Song of Songs are the end
of all that is signified in divine Scripture. For what else after the
Savior must one expect except re =3.532= surrection and judgment? For this
reason the Cataphrygians, who introduce prophets after the Lord, go
astray and have been condemned as heretics. It is Song of Songs, then,
because after what is said nothing else is to be expected. And just as
after the law there were the holy things, and after the holy of holies
there was no longer an inner place, so after the Song of Songs one is no
longer to expect a newer, more inward promise. For the Word once became
flesh, and he has completed the work.
The whole book is full of these dialogues: of the ancient people toward
the Word, and of the human race as a whole toward the Word, and of the
church from the nations toward him, and of the Word toward her, and
again toward the human race; then of those from the nations toward
Jerusalem, and of Jerusalem concerning those from the nations, and
dialogues concerning herself; then also of the ministering angels toward
the people called into the preaching. Of the ancient people, then,
asking him to come down and be united to the flesh, these words: "Let
him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for your breasts are better
than wine." And again through these words it urges him: "New with old,
my kinsman, I have kept for you." And there is also a common voice of
all toward the Word, that he may take up our body: "Who would give you
to me, my kinsman, nursing =3.533= at my mother's breasts? Finding you
outside, I would kiss you, and indeed you would not despise me. I will
take you, I will bring you into my mother's house, and into the chamber
of her who conceived me." For it was asking him to take up the body not
from outside, but from the womb from which he was born, even if not as
we are from man and woman; for as the Fashioner he took a body for
himself from the Virgin alone, yet still from a human being: for the
Theotokos Mary is a human being. And of the Word toward those who asked,
already taking up the flesh from the Virgin, these words: "I have come
into my garden, my sister bride; I have gathered myrrh with my spices. I
have eaten my bread with my honey; I have drunk my wine with my milk."
For entering into his own garden, the creation, he took for himself the
body from the Virgin and became man: a mortal body, then, but with
fragrance; for it has been united hypostatically to the all-holy Word.
And if as man he allowed the body to eat milk, yet he also filled it
with the wine of his own perfection; for just as he ate milk, so he also
works in it and in all that is his own. Then the Word, having now taken
the body, first calls the ancient people, as those first entrusted with
the oracles, through these words: "Rise, come, my companion, my fair
one, my dove, for behold, the winter has passed, the rain has gone, it
has departed of itself; =3.534= the flowers have appeared in the land, the
time of pruning has come, the voice of the turtledove has been heard in
our land, the fig tree has put forth its unripe figs, our vines are in
blossom."
He reminds them that the first people received until a time the shadow
of the teaching in the law, and that the turtledove has given its voice.
And after calling Jerusalem, the Word then also calls those from the
nations through these words. For there is one Lord of both Jews and
nations. "Come from Lebanon, bride, come from Lebanon; you shall come
and pass through from the beginning of faith, from the head of Senir and
Hermon, from the dens of lions, from the mountains of leopards." For he
calls her into faith from idolatry and a beastlike way of life. Called,
then, from the nations and already pricked at heart, she says these
things to the Word: "Tell me, you whom my soul has loved. Where do you
pasture, where do you rest at noon, lest I become as one who veils
herself beside the flocks of your companions." For having experience of
change into many things, she asked to be established now and made firm,
lest she suffer the same again. The Word, being entreated, then teaches
her through these words: "If you do not know yourself, O fairest among
women, go forth in the tracks of the flocks, and pasture your kids by
the tents of the shepherds." For no one can know God without first
knowing himself. For the beginning of piety toward God is perception;
and perception is not the outward kind, but the understanding of the
soul with faith.
Jerusalem, seeing the calling of the =3.535= nations taking place in
uncircumcision of faith, says in wonder: "What will you see," she says,
"in the Shunammite, who comes as choirs of camps?" For they were coming
not from one nation but from every nation, leaving their own camps, and
having broken their swords, and now thinking thoughts of peace. The
church from the nations, seeing Jerusalem as if finding fault, defends
herself, that her calling is not idle. "I am black and beautiful,
daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of
Solomon. Do not look at me because I am darkened, because the sun has
looked askance at me. The sons of my mother fought in me; they made me
a keeper in the vineyards; my own vineyard I have not kept." For if, she
says, I am black, yet I am under the same sun; and if I am uncircumcised,
yet I have renounced idols; and I have gone out from my mother's house,
just as Abraham also did, for I am of that faith. And for this reason I
ask, "Bring me into the house of wine; set love upon me; strengthen me
with ointments, pile apples about me, for I am wounded with love." Do
not attend to the uncircumcision, but receive me in love, O Law and
prophets. And I am foul-smelling in my wildness, but do you train me,
and establish me with your fragrance. For I have been wounded with love,
and for this reason: "I have adjured you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the
powers and by the strengths of the field, if you raise and awaken love
until it should wish." I adjure you, O fathers, not by the powers, for
that is not permitted, but in the powers of paradise, that look =3.536= ing
toward that one, as toward Adam, you may receive me in this way; for
there too there is no circumcision, and now you are renewing me together
with Adam. The Word, seeing the faith from the nations and accepting it,
says: "My companion is beautiful as good pleasure, fair as Jerusalem, a
wonder as ordered ranks." For those from the nations must be none other
than Jerusalem, that there may be one people; and this happens when,
along with honoring the law, we believe in Christ. For the God of the
law and of the Gospels is one; and he who does not become as Jerusalem
does not become a companion of God. Jerusalem, seeing both the faith of
the nations and the loving-kindness of the Word, says: "I went down into
a garden of nuts to see in the fruits of the torrent, whether the vine
had blossomed, whether the pomegranates had blossomed; there I will give
you my breasts; my soul did not know; it set me as the chariots of
Aminadab."
I came into a garden of the walnut, that is, the Scriptures, which like
walnuts are hard on the outside, but spiritual within. And I saw that I
must be yoked, as in chariots, by the calling from the nations. When
both peoples had thus been yoked, the preaching was then sent
everywhere, and the angels become ministers of this, saying: "Go out and
see in King Solomon, in the crown with which his mother crowned him, on
the day of his wedding, and on the day of the gladness of his heart."
For he who goes out from wickedness will see also the loving-kindness of
the Word toward us through the taking up of the body, =3.537= then he has
also accomplished the victory over death, through which he has filled
all things with gladness. Such are the things of the dialogues in the
Song of Songs; from which, taking a starting point, one can from the
book itself join like things to like according to the sense.
And the book has 280 verses.
