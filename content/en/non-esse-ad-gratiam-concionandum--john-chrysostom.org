* That One Ought Not to Preach for Favor
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

That it is dangerous both for those who speak and for those who hear to preach
for favor, and that it is useful and the greatest justice to accuse one's own
sins.
=50.653=
αʹ. We have, I think, sufficiently taken hold of you the other day, and have
given a deeper wound; therefore it is necessary to heal today, and to apply
milder medicines. For this is the best manner of healing, not only to cut but
also to bind up the sores; this is an admirable law of teaching, not only to
rebuke but also to exhort and to console. So also Paul commanded: Reprove,
rebuke, exhort. If one exhorts throughout, he makes the hearers more remiss;
if he only rebukes, he makes them harsher; for being unable to bear the burden
of continual reproofs, they leap away at once. Therefore the manner of
teaching must be some varied thing. Since then on the former day the word bit
each man's mind more sharply, we need a milder teaching today, and upon the
pains that came from the reproofs it is necessary to drop, as oil, the
gentleness of the word, having first reminded you of those very reproofs. We
read to you the other day the law of Paul laid down concerning the communion
of the mysteries for all the initiated. And the law was this; for nothing
prevents us from reading it again even now: But let a man examine himself, and
so let him eat of the bread, and drink of the cup. The initiated know what is
said, and what the bread is, and what the cup. For he who eats and drinks, he
says, unworthily of the Lord, shall be guilty of the body and blood of the
Lord. This law we read to you; and we also explained the sense of the saying.
We said what it is, He shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord: that
he will pay the same penalty, as great a punishment as those who crucified
Christ will undergo. For just as those, he says, the slayers, became guilty of
the blood, so also these who partake of the mysteries unworthily; for this is,
He shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. The saying seemed to
have much excess, and the threat to be unbearable; we added also a reasoning
from an example that has much sequence. For just as, I said, whether one tears
the royal purple, or =50.654= defiles it with mire, he has equally insulted
the king who put it on; so also here both those who slew the Master's body and
those who receive it with an unclean mind equally outrage the royal garment.
The Jews then tore it by the cross, but he also defiles it who receives it
with an unclean soul; so that even if the acts of lawlessness differ, the
insult is equal. This took hold of many, this troubled many; it bit the
conscience of the hearers, or rather not of the hearers only, but also of me
who speak before you; for the teaching is common, the wounds are common;
therefore I also apply common medicines. This became a work of the
philanthropy of God, that both the speaker and the hearers should be subject
to the same laws, share the same nature, each alike be liable when he
transgresses. Why? That he may make the rebuke with measure, that he may
become forbearing toward those who sin, that remembering his own weakness he
may not make the reproof unbearable. For this reason God did not bring down
angels from heaven and set them as teachers over the nature of men, lest
because of the superiority of their nature, and their ignorance of human
weakness,


they should make their rebukes against us more unsparing; but he gave mortal
men as teachers and priests, men clothed with weakness, so that this very
thing, that both the speaker and the hearers are liable to the same things,
might become a bridle to the speaker's tongue, not allowing the accusations to
be made beyond measure. And that this is true, Paul himself, who laid down
that law, taught us this cause also, speaking thus: For every priest taken
from among men is appointed on behalf of men, being able to bear moderately
with the ignorant and those who are led astray. Whence and why? Since he
himself also is clothed with weakness. Do you see that weakness becomes the
ground of sympathy, and the kinship of nature does not allow the one who
rebukes ever to fall into excess, even if he is very contentious? And for what
reason did I say these things? =50.655= That you may not say, You, being clean
of sins, delivered from the pain that belongs to rebuke, with much authority
bring a deeper cut upon us. For I first feel the pain, since I myself also am
liable to the sins. For we are all under penalties; and, No one shall boast
that he has a pure heart. So that it was not philosophizing over others'
evils, nor from any inhumanity, but from much care that I made those reproofs.
For in the case of those who heal bodies, the one who inflicts the cut takes
no sensation of the cut, and the one being cut, he alone is torn with the
pains; but in the case of those who heal souls it is not so, unless perhaps I
am mistaken in judging the things of the rest from myself, but the speaker
himself first suffers pain when he rebukes others. For we do not so grieve
when we are reproved by others as when we reprove others for sins to which we
ourselves are liable. For the speaker's conscience immediately lays hold, and
the dignity of teaching that he wears, falling into the same sins as the
disciples, and needing the same reproofs, makes the pain more bitter for the
speaker.
βʹ. And I do not now lament these things simply, but because many, not bearing
the weight of what was said, after the dismissal came forward and were
displeased, were angry, saying, You drive us from the holy table, and you
scare us away from communion. For this reason I was compelled to say these
things, that you may learn that I do not drive away, but rather gather; I do
not scare away nor shut out, but I draw you more by the reproofs. For the fear
of the punishment spoken of, falling upon the conscience of those who sin as
fire upon wax, both dissolves and melts our offenses, sitting continually, and
making the mind clean and gleaming, implants in us greater confidence; and
from the confidence and eagerness there would be more toward continually
partaking of the ineffable and fearful mysteries. And just as he who gives
bitter medicines to those with a bad stomach, and purging the wicked humors,
awakens the appetite that has been cast down, and prepares them to take the
accustomed food with greater eagerness; so also he who speaks bitter words,
and purges the wicked reasonings of the mind, and putting off the heavy burden
of sins, gives the conscience room to breathe, and prepares it to taste the
Master's body with much pleasure. Therefore one must not be displeased at what
was said, but accept and praise it. But if some are more weakly disposed, and
cannot bear this defense of ours, I would say this to them, that I am not
expounding to you laws of my own, but I am reading letters that have come down
from heaven; and it is necessary that the one entrusted with this ministry
either say with confidence all that is contained, seeking everywhere what is
profitable, not what is pleasant to the hearers; or, shrinking from the
hearers' hatred, throw away both his own and their salvation through this
untimely favor. For that it is exceedingly dangerous both for the speaker and
for the hearers the


hiding of any of the divine laws, and that teachers =50.656= are judged liable
for murders, when they do not expound all the ordinances of God without
reserve, I will again furnish Paul to you as witness of these things. For this
reason I continually take refuge for all things in that holy soul, because
Paul's words are certain useful and divine laws. For it is not Paul who
speaks, but Christ who moves that man's soul; for through him he utters all
that that man said. What then does Paul say? Having called those who dwell in
Ephesus, and having spoken a last discourse to them, since he was about
thereafter to depart from them, teaching those set over them that, just as
those who shed the blood of the disciples, so those who do not tell them what
is profitable are liable to chastisement and punishment, he speaks somehow
thus: I am clean from the blood of all. Why? For I did not shrink from
declaring to you all the counsel of God; as if he had shrunk from declaring,
he would not have been clean from the blood, but would have been judged as a
manslayer, and very reasonably. For the manslayer destroys the body only, but
he who preaches for favor, and makes the hearers more remiss, destroys the
soul; and that one delivers to the present death, but this one destroys the
soul and sends it on to the immortal chastisements and punishments. Does Paul
alone then say this? By no means, but even before Paul God again hints this
same thing through the prophet, speaking thus: I have given you as a watchman
to the house of Israel. What is, A watchman? A watchman is called the one who,
when the armies sit below, has occupied a high and hilly place and from there
watches the enemies coming on, and announces the approach to those sitting
below, and rouses them to the array of war, so that the enemy may not fall
upon them unguarded and slay them with much ease. Since then we who walk on
the ground do not see many of the terrible things coming upon us, the grace of
God has arranged that the holy prophets, standing as on a high place of
prophecy, should announce to us from afar the wrath of God about to come upon
us, so that, recovering ourselves through repentance and raising up our fallen
soul, we may beat back from afar the God-sent blow. For this reason he says: I
have given you as a watchman to the house of Israel; that you may announce
beforehand the disaster about to come, as that man does the enemies. And he
lays no small punishment on the one who does not foretell the wrath of God.
What punishment then is this? The souls of those who perish, he says, I will
require from your hand. Who then is so savage and inhuman and without sympathy
as to accuse the one who speaks and continually discourses about the wrath of
God, when he is about to undergo so great a punishment if he should be silent?
That then it is not profitable for us who speak to be silent about such
things, both the prophet and the apostle have sufficiently taught us; and that
it is not profitable for you who hear either, is clear from this. For if I,
being silent, were hiding the sins by the silence, each would be right to be
displeased and justly angry, when I am not silent; but if even when we are
silent now, the offenses must altogether appear there =50.657=, what benefit
could come from the silence? But there is no benefit, rather extreme harm. For
if I speak now, I lead to repentance and compunction of mind; but if I am
silent, now we shall not remember the things we have offended in, nor shall we
repent; but there we shall see them before our eyes, naked and laid bare, and
we shall lament in vain and to no purpose.
γʹ. Since then it is altogether necessary, either there or here, to be pricked
in conscience over our sins, it is better here, and not there. Whence is this
clear? From the prophetic words, from the evangelical. For the prophet says,
In Hades who shall confess to you? not that we do not confess, but that we do
this in vain. And Christ also taught this same thing through a parable. For
there was a certain Lazarus


who was poor, he says, ulcerated on every side, having an incurable disease;
and another certain rich man would not share even the crumbs with the poor
man. And why must I go through the whole parable? for you know the whole
history, the cruelty of the rich man, how he did not share his table with the
poor man, that man's poverty and the hunger with which he wrestled
continually. But these things were here; and when each had died, he sees that
poor man in the bosom of Abraham. And what does he say: Father Abraham, send
him, that he may drip with the tip of his finger on my tongue, that I may rest
from the pain. Did you see the requital? he did not share crumbs with him, he
does not receive a drop of water. For with the measure, he says, you measure,
it shall be measured to you. What then does Abraham say? Child, you received
your good things, and Lazarus his evil things; and now this man is comforted
here, but you are in pain. But the point sought is this, that they are pricked
over their sins and are changed and become better from Gehenna, yet they gain
nothing toward consolation of the flame; for Father, send him to my house, he
says, that he may testify to my kinsmen, and they may not come to this place.
Himself having failed of the benefit, he thereafter procures salvation for
others. Did you see how cruel he was before this, how philanthropic he became
afterward? While living he passed by Lazarus lying before his eyes, but now he
even cares for kinsmen who are absent; and then, being in abundance, he was
not bent toward the pitiable sight of the poor man, but now, being in pains
and in inexorable necessities, he cares for those belonging to him, and asks
that the one who will announce these things to them be sent. Do you see how he
has become philanthropic and gentle and sympathetic? What then? did he gain
anything from the repentance? was there any profit for him from the
compunction? By no means; for the repentance was untimely. The theater has
been dissolved, the wrestling-pits have gone, it was no longer the time of the
contests; therefore I exhort, and I beg and I entreat, here one must wail and
lament over one's sins. Let the words grieve us here, and let not the deeds
terrify us there; let the word bite us here, and let not the venomous worm
bite us there; let the rebuke burn us here, and let not the Gehenna of fire
burn us there. Those who lament here it follows will be comforted there; those
who live in luxury here and laugh and are without feeling about their sins
must of necessity there lamen =50.658= t and wail, and gnash their teeth. This
word is not mine, but of him who will then judge us; for Blessed, he says, are
those who mourn, for they shall be comforted; Woe to you who laugh, for you
shall weep. How much better then to exchange a temporary compunction and
wailing for immortal goods and a pleasure that has no end, than, having
laughed this short and temporary life here, to go away there to be punished
immortally? But you are ashamed and you blush to speak out the sins? Even if
it were necessary to say these things before men and to make a show of them,
even so one ought not to be ashamed; for shame is the sinning, not the
confessing of what has been sinned; but now there is not even necessity to
confess with witnesses present. Let the examination of the offenses take place
in the reasonings of the conscience; let the court be without witness; let God
alone see you confessing, God who does not reproach the sins, but looses the
sins from the confession. But even so you hesitate and draw back? I too know
that the conscience cannot bear the memory of its own offenses. For if we only
come to the recollection of the things we have offended in, like some unbroken
and hard-mouthed colt, the mind rears. But hold it, bridle it, stroke it with
the hand, make it gentle, persuade it that if it does not confess now, it will
confess there, where the punishment is greater, where the making an example is
larger. Here the court is without witness, and you who have sinned judge
yourself; but there all things will be brought into the midst of the theater
of the world, if we do not first


blot them out here. Are you ashamed to confess sins? be ashamed to commit
sins. But we, when we do them, dare them boldly and shamelessly; and when we
must confess, then we are ashamed and hesitate, though we ought to do this
with eagerness. For it is not shame to accuse sins, but justice and virtue; if
it were not justice and virtue, God would not have set a reward for it. For
that confession has rewards, hear what he says: Speak you your lawless deeds
first, that you may be justified. Who is ashamed of a work from which he
becomes just? who is ashamed to confess sins, that he may loose sins? Does he
command confession for this, that he may punish? not that he may punish, but
that he may forgive.
δʹ. For in the courts outside, after the confession there is punishment. For
this reason the psalm also, suspecting this very thing, lest someone, fearing
punishment after confession, should deny the sins, says: Confess to the Lord,
for he is good, for his mercy is forever. Does he not know your sins, if you
do not confess? What then do you gain more from not confessing? can you escape
notice? Even if you do not speak, he has seen; but if you speak, he forgets.
For behold I am God who blots out your lawless deeds, and I will not remember.
Do you see? I will not remember, he says; for this is of philanthropy; but you
=50.659= remember, that you may receive a ground of sobriety. Paul, having
heard these things, of sins which God did not remember, himself remembered
continually, speaking thus: I am not sufficient to be called an apostle,
because I persecuted the Church; and, Christ came into the world to save
sinners, of whom I am first. He did not say, I was, but, I am. The forgiveness
of the sins came to be with God, and the memory of the forgiven sins was not
made to vanish with Paul; what the Master blotted out, he himself made a show
of. For you heard the prophet saying, And I will not remember; but you
remember. God calls him a vessel of election, and this man calls himself first
of sinners. And if he did not forget the forgiven sins, consider how he
remembered the benefits of God. And why do I say that memory of sins does not
put to shame? memory of achievements does not so make us splendid as memory of
sins; or rather the memory of achievements not only does not make us splendid,
but even fills us with shame and condemnation; but the memory of sins fills us
with confidence and much justice. Who says this? The Pharisee and the
publican. For the one who spoke the sins went down justified; but the one who
spoke the achievements went down having become less than the publican. Do you
see how great the harm of remembering achievements, how great the benefit of
not having forgotten sins? And very reasonably. For he who remembers
achievements is lifted into madness, looks down on the rest of men, which is
what the Pharisee suffered; for he would not have come to such great
boastfulness, he would not have said, I am not as the rest of men, if he had
not remembered his fasting and his tithe; but the memory of sins brings down
the mind, persuades it to be humble-minded, and through humility draws the
goodwill of God. Hear then how Christ also commands us to send achievements
into forgetfulness. When you have done all these things, say that We are
unprofitable servants. You say that you are an unprofitable servant, I do not
make you unprofitable; if you confess your own cheapness, I make you splendid
and I crown you. Do you see through how many things it has been shown to us
that the memory of sins has profit, and the memory of achievements has harm,
and again the contrary, that punishment comes from forgetfulness of sins, but
benefit from forgetfulness of our achievements? Do you wish to learn also from
another place that to remember sins is a very great achievement? Hear Job. For
just as he prided himself on the other things, so also on the confession of
sins, speaking thus: And if, having sinned willingly, I hid it because of the
multitude of my people, so as not to declare my


sins. And what he says is this: never did an assembly of fellow-servants lead
me into shame. For what benefit from the ignorance of men, when the judge
knows all? and what harm from these knowing the offenses, when that one wishes
to release from the judgment? Even if all judge, but the judge votes
acquittal, I have no account of their vote; and even if all praise and wonder,
but that one condemns me, again I have no benefit from their judgment. For
everywhere one must look to that one, and do the same in the case of sins that
we do =50.660= in the spending of money. Immediately upon rising from the bed,
before throwing ourselves into the marketplace, or taking in hand any private
or public affairs, calling the servant we demand an account of what has been
spent, that we may know what has been spent badly, and what for what was
needed, and how much is left; and if we know that what is left is little, we
invent by every means occasions of income, lest we be destroyed by famine
unawares. This then let us do also in the case of our actions. Calling our
conscience let us make an account with it of words, of deeds, of thoughts; let
us examine what has been spent for what was needed, and what to our harm; what
word was spent badly, on revilings, on shameful speech, on insults; what
thought moved the eye to wantonness; what reasoning was brought out to our
harm into a work, either through hands, or through tongue, or through the eyes
themselves; and let us be eager to stand off from the untimely spending, and
instead of what was once spent badly, to lay up another income, instead of the
words brought forward in vain, prayers, instead of the glances that came to be
wantonly, alms, fasts. For if we are going to spend untimely, and to lay up
nothing, nor to treasure up any good for ourselves, having been brought down
to extreme poverty, we shall unawares send ourselves on to the immortal
punishment of the fire. Of money then we are accustomed to make the account at
dawn, but of actions after dinner and the evening itself, lying on the bed,
with no one troubling, no one making a tumult, let us demand of ourselves the
accounts of all that has been done and said by us during the day; and if we
see something that has been sinned, let us punish the conscience, let us
rebuke the mind, let us prick the reasoning so sharply that, remembering the
evening's blow, we may no longer dare, when we have risen, to lead ourselves
to the same pit of sin.
εʹ. For that this time is more suitable for such an accounting, hear the
prophet saying: What you say in your hearts, be pricked on your beds. Many
things we do during the day not as we wish; and friends provoke, and servants
make us savage, and a wife grieves, and a child causes pain, and a crowd of
worldly and public affairs stands around; and we cannot then see clearly, nor
even how we are tripped. But having been delivered from all these, and having
come to be by ourselves in the evening, and enjoying much quiet, let us
assemble the court on the bed, that through such a judgment we may have God
propitious. But if we sin every day, and strike our soul, and never perceive
it, just as those who receive continual wounds, then despise them, draw upon
themselves fevers and an unbearable death; so also we from this continual
=50.661= insensibility draw upon ourselves inexorable punishment. I know that
what has been said is burdensome, but it has much profit. We have a gentle
Master; he wishes only to take hold of a pretext, and at once he displays all
his philanthropy. For if, sinning and remaining unpunished, we did not become
worse, he would even have remitted the chastisement for us; but he knows this
clearly, that not to be punished when we sin harms us no less than the sins
themselves. For this reason he lays on the punishment, not demanding a penalty
of the things that have gone, but the


things to come, correcting them. And that you may learn that this is true,
hear what he says to Moses: Let me alone, and being wrathful I will wipe them
out. Let me alone, he was saying, not because Moses was holding him; for he
had uttered nothing to him, but stood by in silence; but wishing to give him a
pretext for the supplication on their behalf. For since they had sinned things
worthy of punishment, and of inexorable punishment, but he did not wish to
punish them, but to act philanthropically, and this made them more remiss, he
arranged both, so that neither should the punishment be brought on, nor should
those become more remiss from going unpunished, having learned that not
according to their own desert, but according to the advocacy of Moses they
escaped the Master's wrath. This we also often do, and when servants have
offended things worthy of punishment, wishing neither to punish them, nor to
release them from the fear that comes from punishment, we command friends to
snatch them from our hands, so that both the fear may remain flourishing in
them, and they may escape the blows from us. This God also did; and that it is
true is clear from the words themselves: Let me alone, he says, and being
wrathful. And yet when we wish to punish and no one lets us, then we are
wrathful; but he says, Let me alone and I will be wrathful, that you may learn
that wrath is not a passion about God, but the punishment that comes upon us
is called by this name. When therefore you hear Moses saying, If you forgive
them the sin, forgive, be struck with wonder at the Master before the servant,
because he himself furnished him the occasions of this philanthropy. And not
here only did he do this, but also to Jere =50.662= miah, and to Ezekiel he
says this same thing: Run about and see in the ways of Jerusalem, if there is
anyone doing judgment and justice; I will be propitious to them. Did you see
philanthropy? From the virtue of one, many even of the wicked share in the
enjoyment; but by the wickedness of the many, even if there is one who
succeeds in the midst of a large people, he is not carried down with them; but
one man living rightly will be able to snatch a whole people from the wrath of
God, but a whole city corrupted will not be able to drag the one who lives
well to its own chastisement and punishment and to pull him down. And this is
clear from Noah; for when all were perishing he alone was saved; and it is
plain from Moses; for he alone was able to intercede for so great a people.
And I have even another greater proof of the philanthropy of God to tell. For
when he does not find living men who have confidence able to beg off those who
have sinned, he takes refuge in those who have died, and through those he says
he forgives the sins, as indeed he says to Hezekiah: I will shield this city
for my sake, and for the sake of David my servant, who has already died.
Knowing these things then, that God moves and manages all things so as to
deliver us from chastisement and punishment, let us furnish him many
occasions, confessing, repenting, weeping, praying, forgiving anger toward
those near us, correcting the poverty of our neighbors, being sober in
prayers, displaying humility, remembering our sins continually. For it is not
enough to say, I am a sinner, but one must also remember the offenses
according to their kind. For just as fire falling upon thorns easily makes
them vanish, so also a reasoning that continually turns over with itself the
things that have been offended in easily destroys them and sinks them. And may
God who passes over lawless deeds, who takes away injustices, both deliver us
from the sins, and count us worthy of the kingdom of the heavens, by the grace
and philanthropy of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom to the
Father be glory, together with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the
ages of ages. Amen.
