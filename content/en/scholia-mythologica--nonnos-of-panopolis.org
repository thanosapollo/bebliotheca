COLLECTION AND EXPLANATION OF THE STORIES WHICH GREGORY AMONG THE SAINTS
MENTIONED IN THE FIRST INVECTIVE ORATION AGAINST JULIAN The invective oration
is a censure of the things done by Julian. The invective differs from censure
in that censure proceeds through the encomiastic headings, such as family,
upbringing, deeds, comparison; but the invective through deeds alone, and if
it happens also through comparison. And it was called invective from a
metaphor of the pillar; and a pillar is either stone or bronze in an elongated
square shape, on which the outrage of the one pilloried has been inscribed. As
the Athenians wrote Arthmios the Zeleian on a pillar, the one corrupting the
Greeks with money so that they might rather obey the Persian, dishonoring both
him and all his family, having given in the letters authority to whoever
wished to kill him, having inscribed also the cause on the pillar, which was a
censure for Arthmios himself: Because, it says, he brought the gold from the
Medes into the Peloponnese. And one must know that often also the benefactions
of benefactors were written on pillars, as the benefaction of Leucon, the
ruler of the Bosporus, was written in Athens. And this is the hypothesis, but
concerning the stories we will make mention as far as possible in the briefest
terms. 4.1 This is the first story: Let Empedocleses and Aristaeuses and
Empedotimuses and Trophoniuses play these things among those people. This
Empedocles was Sicilian by race, Pythagorean in philosophy. And wanting to be
vainglorious, as that he had been consecrated and had become airborne into the
heavens, he threw himself into the fire given forth by Aetna. And this fire is
called The craters of Hephaestus. And he himself perished by the fire, but
God, wanting to make public his vainglory, made his sandal be spat out sound
from the fire. And thus it was known that he had not become airborne, but was
burned worthily of his own vainglory. And those around Trophonius and
Empedotimus and Aristaeus were from Boeotia, a city of Lebadeia, prophets by
their arts. And these too, wanting to be vainglorious and to show that they
had been taken up, threw themselves into certain underwater caves so as to
have died and their remains not be found. And these have died; and it was
known that they died there because the oracle appeared about the place. And
there is a proverb that says You have consulted at Trophonius's. And it is
said of those who are pale and never laugh. For everyone going down into that
oracle came up unlaughing and pale continually. And this was a refutation of
these very men, that having grown pale and never having laughed in the
underwater place they died. And Trophonius and Agamedes his brother were
deemed worthy to give oracles because they built from their own money the
sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi. 4.2 The second is the story concerning Proteus.
This Proteus was Thracian by race; and he had sons Molos and Telegonus. These,
being pirates, Heracles coming killed, as he was going away after the cattle
of Geryon. This Proteus then, being despondent because of the loss of the
children, and having thrown himself into the sea, the gods pitying
immortalized. And he became a marine daemon, and he comes and dwells on the
Pharian island. This one is said also to spend his time with the seals. This
one also took Helen of Alexander when he came from Greece, and gave him the
image of Helen. And later, when Menelaus after the Trojan war learned that
Helen was in Egypt and came to Proteus, he gave her to him. And this Proteus
is said to metamorphose toward those who encounter him, and sometimes to
appear of such a sort, and sometimes


of such a sort. 4.3 The third is the story concerning Heracles and the pyre.
Much talk babbles of Heracles, that being son of Zeus and Alcmene, and being a
hero, he accomplished the twelve labors. But the story now in this place about
the pyre is this. This Heracles had a wife named Deianeira, whom he took from
Oeneus, having become a rival in love to Achelous the river, from whom he also
begot Hyllus. This Deianeira then, together with taking her and carrying her
off to his own fatherland, Nessus, one of the Centaurs, seeing her, fell in
love and at a certain river wished to have intercourse with her. Heracles
learning shoots Nessus with an arrow. And dying Nessus gives of his own blood
to Deianeira, suggesting and deceiving that This blood will be for you a
love-charm of Heracles, so that, he says, if you learn that he loves another,
you may anoint from this blood the garment of Heracles, and you transfer him
to desire of yourself. This blood then Deianeira had. When then Heracles had
fallen in love with Iole the daughter of Eurytus and taken her and sent her
through Lichas as a captive to Deianeira, Deianeira was moved to thought and
to jealousy, and wanting to transfer the love to herself, she anoints the
tunic of Heracles with the blood of Nessus, and gives it to Heracles to put
on. But the blood was man-slaying. And when Heracles had put it on, the tunic
kindled and burned up Heracles. And he, burning and having thrown himself into
the nearby river, made the water hot. From which thereafter Thermopylae came
to be, between Thessaly and Phocis. 4.4 The fourth is the story concerning the
butchery of Pelops. And it is this. Tantalus was king of Phrygia. This one
begets Pelops. To this Tantalus once the gods were entertained as guests.
Taking then Pelops his own son Tantalus slaughters and butchers and boils, and
sets a feast before the gods. And of these meats Demeter putting forth a hand
took and ate from the shoulder. But the other gods pitying Tantalus, and
wondering that he thought little of his own child, put the meats together, and
complete Pelops sound. But it was lacking that bit of flesh which Demeter ate
from the shoulder. Adding then something of ivory at the shoulder, they filled
Pelops sound. Thus thereafter all the race of the Pelopidae was from this
distinguished, all having in their shoulders the ivory place. 4.5 The fifth is
the story of the cutting of the Phrygians. And this is of this sort. In
Phrygia they worshipped the mother of the gods Rhea above every god.
Performing then the rites for her the Phrygians used to cut themselves with
knives, not wanting to kill, but only to make bleed. And this they did being
soothed by pipes, so that they might bear being struck without pain. And after
the blows they went to unclean mixings, of women I mean, not of men. 4.6 The
sixth is the story concerning Mithras. Mithras then is thought among the
Persians to be the sun, and they sacrifice to him and perform certain rites
unto him. No one then can be initiated unto him unless first he should pass
through the steps of the punishments. And the steps of punishments are, in
number eighty, having a descent and an ascent. For they are punished first the
lighter ones, then the more drastic, then still more the more drastic. And
then thus after passing through all the punishments, then the one being
initiated is initiated. And the punishments are passing through fire, through
frost, through hunger and thirst, through much journeying, through seafaring
and simply through all such things. 4.7 The seventh is the story concerning
the killing of strangers among the Taurians. And it is this. Taurians are a
nation about Scythia. Among these the daughter of Agamemnon, Iphigeneia, was
transferred by Artemis, in theabout to be sacrificed for the Greeks at Aulis,
for whom the goddess substituting a deer gave it to sacrifice. This Iphigeneia
then being among the Taurians, so as not to be recognized by those coming as
guests who she is, used to permit them to be sacrificed to Artemis. And this
was the


killing of strangers which the divine Gregory mentions. And later Orestes,
being terrified by the Erinyes, and coming among the Taurians, and being
seized as a stranger for destruction, and being brought as to a priestess to
Iphigeneia, was recognized through the ivory shoulder and was released from
the sacrifice. Thus thereafter Orestes taking his own sister, I mean
Iphigeneia, brings her to Greece. 4.8 The eighth is the story of the sacrifice
of the royal girl upon Troy. And it is the one concerning Polyxena. Polyxena
is daughter of Priam, whom Achilles wanting to marry, entered into Troy, and
by a certain ambush is shot by Paris in the temple of Apollo, and dies. In
later time then to Pyrrhus his son having become a man he appeared in a dream
that Polyxena must be sacrificed to him, through whom he perished. And
Pyrrhus, being in Troy, and taking her after the victory, slaughters her upon
the tomb of Achilles, his own father. 4.9 The ninth is the story concerning
Menoeceus. Menoeceus is son of Creon, king of Thebes. When then his own
fatherland of Thebes was being warred upon by the seven generals, Tiresias
prophesied, being a seer, that he would immediately stop the war and there
would be victory for them, if someone of the royal race of the autochthons,
those given up from the teeth of the dragon, should give himself to
destruction. Learning this Menoeceus, and wanting to free the city from the
siege, gave himself to destruction apart from his father Creon. =4.10= The
tenth is the story about the daughters of Scedasus. And it is this. The
Lacedaemonians in the Peloponnesian war after conquering the Athenians by
force wished also to war against their own allies. And setting out against the
Thebans, before approaching the city, being at Leuctra (and this is a city of
Thebes) joining battle they fared ill, Epaminondas general of the Thebans
having distinguished himself. And the disaster came to the Lacedaemonians at
Leuctra from a cause of this sort. Scedasus, being a Leuctrian, had three
daughters. And certain of the Lacedaemonians long ago being guests with them
outraged the girls by intercourse. Fearing then the Lacedaemonians lest they
make public the wantonness to their own father, they destroy them. And
Scedasus returning to his own city Leuctra, and learning what had happened,
cursed all Lacedaemonians at the very tomb of his daughters to fare ill.
Wherefore also the ill fortune overtook the Lacedaemonians at Leuctra. =4.11=
The eleventh is the story concerning the Laconian ephebes. And it is this. The
Lacedaemonians wanting their own citizens and children to be enduring and
brave, used to train them with scourgings, and to the one enduring still more
a prize was given. =4.12= The twelfth is the story the blood upon the altar.
And it is the one concerning Artemis. This goddess being a virgin and
temperate, rejoiced to be honored with the bloods of the killing of strangers.
=4.13= The thirteenth is the story about the hemlock of Socrates. And it is
this. Socrates was Athenian by race, a philosopher in education. This man as
introducing new daimonia into the state Anytus and Meletus accused. He was
condemned then, as corrupting the young, to die having drunk the hemlock; and
having drunk he died. =4.14= The fourteenth is the story concerning the leg of
Epictetus. Epictetus was a philosopher. This one was bound by a tyrant †of
Macedon as to the leg. And later the tyrant wished to loose him. He asked him
then thus: Do you wish, O Epictetus, that I loose you? And he answered, What
then, am I bound? as of his soul, forsooth, not being bound. It is said then
that the human being is not the body but the soul. =4.15= The fifteenth is the
story concerning Anaxarchus. And it is this. Anaxarchus was a philosopher.
This one being held by Archelaus the tyrant and thrown into a mortar, was
pounded with a wooden pestle. And to be pounded is to be struck in the manner
of ptisane, whence also ptisane from being pounded. And being pounded he said
this: Pound, pound the sack of Anaxarchus, for you do not pound Anaxarchus,
hinting as a philosopher forsooth that one should make no account of the body.
=4.16= The sixteenth is


the story the leap of Cleombrotus. And it is this. Cleombrotus Ambraciot by
race (and this is a city of old Epirus). This one encountering the Phaedo, the
dialogue of Plato about the soul, and learning that the separation from the
body is better for the soul, threw himself from the wall and died, so that
forsooth his soul going out might be separated from the body. =4.17= The
seventeenth is the story concerning the Pythagorean beans. And it is this.
Pythagoreans a race of philosophers from Pythagoras the Samian. These used to
learn the doctrines of philosophy through riddles. And there was handed down
among the others also this riddle, Not to eat beans. That is, not betraying
the just to be bribed with money. For the judges at Athens of old used beans
instead of pebbles toward condemnation in the courts. He says then that from
the beans from the court one must not eat. =4.18= The eighteenth is the story
concerning the death of Theano the Pythagorean. And it is this. This woman was
held by a tyrant unto saying the secrets of the fatherland. This woman, having
bitten off the tongue, spat it at the tyrant, not wanting, even being
compelled, to speak out, the organ of the voice having been taken away. =4.19=
The nineteenth is the story concerning Epaminondas and Scipio. And it is this.
Epaminondas was Theban by race, a very great general, having been general in
the Leuctrian war, having become a pupil of Philolaus the Pythagorean. This
one making war was very continent both from foods and from sexual pleasures
and every luxury, wherefore also he set up very great trophies against the
Lacedaemonians. And Scipio himself also a general of the Romans, similar in
diet and character to Epaminondas. He too distinguished himself in the war
against the Carthaginians, who from the victory over the nation took the
surname and was called Scipio Africanus. And Carthaginians are the Africans.
=4.20= The twentieth is the story concerning the unwashed-of-feet and the
ground-sleepers. And it is this. In Homer the poet Selli a nation of
Dodonaeans. From these priests of the oak were put forward. And in this oak
there was an oracle of Zeus, the so-called Dodonaean oak. These priests then,
as being attendants of a god keeping themselves pure, neither bathed nor slept
in a bed, but on the ground, as the poet says in this verse: ... and about you
the Selli dwell as interpreters, unwashed of feet, sleeping on the ground.
=4.21= The twenty-first is the story concerning the greed of Solon. And it is
this. Solon was lawgiver of the Athenians. This one being asked to legislate
the laws, demanded of the citizens an oath that they remain a decade and are
content with the laws set by him, and thereafter, if they should not be
pleasing to them, then they dissolve them. And this he did so that for a while
he might accustom them to remain for a little time in the decrees. For he knew
that if they taste of the legislation, thereafter they will hold to it hard to
change. Taking then Solon the oath from the Athenians and having legislated
for them, he went out from Athens, owing to spend the decade outside. Going
about then the cities he came also to Lydia to Croesus the king. Whom coming
Croesus wanting to move to wonder, sent to his treasures, showing him how
much-gold Croesus. And returning from the treasures Solon Croesus asked whom
he would think more fortunate than all humans, supposing that he would say him
before all. But Solon answered Tellus the Athenian. For Solon defined not
wealth and distinction as happiness, but the one who lived without passion,
and died having distinguished himself. Now then he calls Solon greedy, as
reaching after seeing a greater wealth than the Lydian gold. And wealth too is
a certain happiness concerning the externals. And the discourse knows three
happinesses, the passionlessness in soul, the health in body, the extravagance
concerning the externals. Solon then ought to have added that, If also in soul
you were best, the wealth too is sufficient for you toward the happiness
concerning the externals. =4.22= The twenty-second is the story concerning the
love of beauty of Socrates. Socrates, being a philosopher, used to go about
the marketplace, and the


lads he used to urge toward philosophy, and especially those he thought to be
fit for philosophy the very beautiful lads. For they say the well-formed are
also well-natured. There was then an opinion among Athenians that Socrates was
a lover of boys because of choosing the well-formed. And Plato is found both
in the Theaetetus and everywhere saying thus from Socrates, that Beautiful is
Theaetetus and Beautiful is Charmides. Openly then in the discourses Plato
furnishes an implication, that Socrates was suspected to be such because of
such, so to speak, counter-questions, that, You are beautiful, but not in the
look but in the soul. And it was suspected that he also consorts with
Alcibiades according to the shameful love. But those speaking about Socrates
say that he consorted with all not according to the passionate love but
according to the inspired. =4.23= The twenty-third is the story concerning the
greediness of Plato. And it is this. Plato the philosopher hearing that
Dionysius the tyrant of Sicily was of great nature, sailed to him. And the
interpreters now of Plato say that he sailed for two causes, both that he
might inquire into the fire given forth in Aetna, and that he might persuade
Dionysius to philosophize. But the blessed Gregory says that for this Plato
went away to Sicily, because of the extravagance of the lunches of Dionysius.
And he makes as evidence even his being sol d. And he was sold in truth, being
given by Dionysius to Pollis the Lacedaemonian admiral then spending time
there. Then Pollis coming to Aegina sells him for fifty minae to Anniceris.
=4.24= The twenty-fourth is the story concerning the dainty-eating of
Xenocrates. And it is this. Xenocrates and Aristotle pupils of Plato, who also
succeeded to his school. And Xenocrates made a Stoic sect, and Aristotle the
Peripatetic. And it is said that Xenocrates was so temperate that even living
in luxury he was not moved toward sexual pleasures because of thus, he says,
arranging the passions to obey reason. The blessed Gregory then accuses him as
a glutton and dainty-eater and as a philosopher declining nothing from eating.
=4.25= The twenty-fifth is the story concerning the glibness of Diogenes. And
it is this. Diogenes was a Cynic philosopher. And he was called Cynic from a
metaphor of the land dogs. For just as these dogs have something both guarding
and distinguishing (for they distinguish the household from the strangers, and
they guard the household), so also this one imitated the distinguishing and
the guarding. And he used to guard the doctrines of philosophy, and he
distinguished those fit and unfit for philosophy. This Diogenes then, being
such, was reasonably free of speech. He used then to refute both rulers and
notable men and every human forsooth for the sake of the beautiful. And
refuting, he used to refute more wittily and more ridiculously. Looking then
to this the blessed Gregory says that Diogenes was rather ridiculous and
deceptive than a philosopher. And glibness is the deception proceeding
according to wittiness. =4.26= The twenty-sixth is the story concerning the
one dwelling in the jar. And it is this being able to be explained in many
ways; for this too is about Diogenes. It is said then that in winter this one
used to dwell in a jar in the evening because of the frost, and by day he used
to go out because of the sun. Wherefore also once as he was warming himself,
Alexander the Macedonian stood over him, shading him from being warmed, and
saying: Speak, Diogenes, what do you wish I should grant you? And he said:
Grant me this, to stand a little out of my way so that I may be warmed. So
either this is the story, or the one that happened under the Ptolemies. And
the one that happened under the Ptolemies is this. A certain Sotates an
Alexandrian philosopher was standing in a certain place in the sun picking
lice. This man from above from a certain lookout Ptolemy seeing, came down so
that he might take him into the palaces. But Sotates, seeing him, went into a
fragment of a jar lying there, and hid himself from Ptolemy. And later some
slandered this man as unfortunate. And others say that Ptolemy himself also
uttered this iambic: I want a drop of Fortune rather than a jar of wits,
hinting that that jar is full of wits


because of Sotates. And yet because of his being unfortunate, the multitude of
wits benefited him nothing. And the story is this, but the syntax thus
wantsAnd of the glibness of Diogenes, by which he makes the strangers stand
aside, so that it may be that Diogenes through the glibness used to make those
producing the shadow for him withdraw (and he says to the tyrants instead of,
by the tyrannical words, making those who encounter him withdraw). So either,
the one concerning the glibness and the jar being one, the syntax is bound
together, or, the stories being two, one must construe the glibness of
Diogenes with the by which. And that they call tyrants the bold words even the
divine man Gregory himself said, as from tragedy. For especially the
tragedians have used such a trope. And the, the cheap breads to the
sesame-cakes is for him a parody. There is such a thing said among tragedians,
or rather among comedians, that through the sweeter breads they make cheap the
cheap breads. The divine Gregory then mocks using the parody, that, he says,
through the glibness that sweetens in speaking he used to make cheap those who
happened by, refuting them. And according to my judgment, I have considered
the story concerning the glibness and the jar one. =4.27= The twenty-seventh
is the story concerning Epicurus. Epicurus became a philosopher who opined
that God does not provide for the affairs here. And this one set as the end of
every good pleasure. And the interpreters now of the philosophers say that he
set as end pleasure, not the unclean but the most natural condition. And this
one too the philosophers reject both as not saying Providence, and that he
sets as end pleasure and not the good, the first and only. And this is God.
=4.28= The twenty-eighth is the story concerning Crates. And it is this.
Crates was Theban of Boeotia by race. This one wanting to philosophize the
Cynic philosophy, taking his possessions, threw them to the people,
proclaiming thus: Crates releases the things of Crates, so that the things of
Crates may not overpower Crates. And he let his lands become sheep-grazed.
=4.29= The twenty-ninth is the story concerning the one who spoke about the
little cloak. And it is this. Again a Cynic philosopher, either Antisthenes or
Zeno (for it is disputed), this one sailing, and a storm happening, and a
shipwreck coming to be, bearing thankfully the poverty that would be for him,
uttered toward Fortune: Well done, O Fortune, I acknowledge thanks to you,
that you have reduced my substance as far as the little cloak. As clear that
some of the things from his substance had been loaded on the ship. =4.30= The
thirtieth is the story concerning Antisthenes. And it is this obvious from the
text itself. For this Antisthenes was a Cynic philosopher who, being struck
and hit in the face, taking a paper and writing onto the paper the one who
struck, glued it onto his own forehead and thus used to walk about. =4.31= The
thirty-first is the story about the philosopher who stood all day in the sun.
And it is this. When Rome was being warred upon by barbarians in the time of
the emperor, the philosopher standing the whole day under the sun prayed. And
fire fell from heaven and burned up the barbarians and the philosopher
himself. =4.32= The thirty-second is the story about Potidaea. Potidaea is a
city lying in Thrace. In this Potidaea a Potidaean philosopher stood in winter
time all night praying. And so airborne, he says, he became in soul, as not to
perceive the frost of winter. And he calls ecstasy the philosopher standing
out of himself, and, as I said, becoming airborne in soul. 20standing he calls
here not the civil war but his standing praying. =4.33= The thirty-third is
the story concerning the Arcadian question. And it is this. Homer the poet
being about Arcadia (and this is a country of the Peloponnese) encountered
fishermen picking lice, and asked them thus: Men from Arcadia, fishermen, have
we then something? And the sense of the


question is this: O Arcadian men, fishermen by art, did we then hunt
something? And the fishermen answered this verse: those we took we left, and
those we did not take we carry. And the sense of this verse is of this sort,
that, The lice which we hunted, we killed here, and those we were not able to
hunt, we carry in the clothes. And Homer did not understand, as they say, this
verse, and having been there a little, he died from grief. And they say there
is also an oracle that was prophesied to Homer, that when a question should be
put to him, and he should not find it, then he will die. =4.34= The
thirty-fourth is the story concerning Aristotle and the Euripus. And it is
this. Aristotle the philosopher became a pupil of Plato. Even if especially
giving himself to the physical part of philosophy, he was eager to say the
causes and the natures of all the bodies after the heavenly, such as earth,
sea, air, aether, animals, plants, rain, snow, earthquakes, comets, simply
all. This one then having investigated the nature of all things, wished also
to give a physical account of the nature of the Euripus. And not being able to
grasp it, he threw himself in this part of the sea, uttering this: Since
Aristotle did not take the Euripus, let Euripus have Aristotle. And thus
Aristotle died. And the Euripus is a place of the sea between the island of
Boeotia and Attica, which turns seven times a day. And it turns by the water
in this part fleeing under and as it were being sucked back, and again
belching forth and the water being filled again as it was. And this happens,
as I said, seven times a day. And they have died is from common instead of
Homer and Aristotle, the one because of the Arcadian question, the other
because of the Euripus. =4.35= The thirty-fifth is the story concerning the
well of Cleanthes. And it is this. Cleanthes was himself also one of the Cynic
philosophers. This one standing in a certain well, used to draw water for
those passing by, and giving to drink, he used to take bread from them and
eat. And this one too is wondered at among the philosophers as enduring.
=4.36= The thirty-sixth is the story concerning the strap of Anaxagoras. And
it is this. =4.37= The thirty-seventh is the story concerning the gloom of
Heraclitus. And it is this. Heraclitus and Democritus not having come to be in
the same time, but being physicists, similarly mocked the succession of the
world, the one laughing, the other weeping. Democritus used to laugh
continually at the affairs, and Heraclitus used to weep. And Democritus was an
Abderite, and Heraclitus an Ephesian. =4.38= The thirty-eighth is the story
concerning the phalloi. And it is this. Holding a feast for Dionysus the
Greeks used to honor him with phalloi. And a phallos is from red hide a shape
of a man's private part. And they used to put this around themselves both on
the necks and on the middle thighs, and they used to dance out, honoring
Dionysus from this. And they used to hold this honor for Dionysus for a cause
of this sort. From Semele the daughter of Cadmus Dionysus is begotten. And she
being struck by thunderbolt, was sought by Dionysus. And as this one was
wandering about and seeking, a child so called Polyhymnus encountered
Dionysus, and promised to show him the mother, if he would love him as a boy.
And Dionysus promised this. Polyhymnus says to him that Semele is in Lerna.
Then Dionysus going into the sea so that he might cross into Lerna, Polyhymnus
also followed with him. And Dionysus, as a god, was saved, but Polyhymnus
died. And Dionysus being grieved that his lover died, toward his honor having
hewn a wooden private part from fig wood, he used always to hold it toward
memory, as I said, of Polyhymnus. For this cause they honor Dionysus with the
phalloi. Phalloi then are all, and ithyphalloi those bound straight upon the
thighs. =4.39= The thirty-ninth is the story concerning the black-rumped and
the rump-less. And these are various, and the one concerning the rump-less is
this. Peirithous going down into Hades in order to snatch Persephone, the one
of Pluto, was bound below beside certain rocks.


Heracles then going down and wanting to take Peirithous, drew him up, but his
rumps remained attached to the rock. Peirithous then was called rump-less. And
the one concerning the black-rumped is this. Two certain brothers displaying
every injustice throughout all the land were called Cercopes, having obtained
the surname from the sharpness of their deeds. For the one of them was called
Passalus, and the other Aclemon, as Dios the commentator says. And these their
mother, Memnonis by name, having seen working many terrible things throughout
the land, said to them, Not to encounter a black-rumped one. And once as
Heracles was sleeping under a tree and his weapons were leaning on the plant,
these approaching wished to put their hands to the weapons. And immediately
Heracles perceiving, taking them, and binding them head-down on a wood carried
them. And then those remembered the command of their own mother, hanging
having seen the rump of Heracles black from the thickness of the hairs. And
speaking this very thing to one another, they attached much laughter to
Heracles. And immediately he redeemed them from the bonds and released them.
=4.40= The fortieth is the story concerning the solemn Pan. And it is this.
Penelope became wife of Odysseus. As this one was wandering the wandering
which he wandered after the Trojan war, many certain ones came to woo
Penelope. And she from day to day put it off wanting to be married to no one
because of waiting for Odysseus. All then having fenced themselves had
intercourse with her, and having conceived, she bore Pan. Wherefore also he is
called Pan, because he was sown from all. And they made this one a god. And
this is Pan the goat-footed. =4.41= The forty-first is the story concerning
Bouthoinas. And it is this. Heracles is the one called Bouthoinas. And he was
called for a cause of this sort. Heracles was passing the Dryopian land,
carrying also his son Hyllus. Then as Hyllus was hungry and asking for food,
Heracles asked a certain man ploughing called Theiodamas for bread. And he did
not give, but even insulted. Then Heracles taking one of the ploughing oxen
slaughtered it, and both he himself and his son feasted. And he was called for
this cause Bouthoinas, since he feasted on the whole ox. Hence there came to
be for Heracles the war against the Dryopes. For Theiodamas going up into the
city, and saying that An enemy came into the country (speaking about
Heracles), they went out against him. And Heracles conquered all. =4.42= The
forty-second is the story concerning the Three-evening one. And it is this.
Heracles is said to have been conceived and born through three evenings, and
they mythologize certain such things about him. And similarly they say this
same Heracles in one night mixed with all the daughters of Thestius, and all
begot children from him, so that also through these things they both made him
a god and named this his thirteenth labor. =4.43= The forty-third is the story
about Julian being called Eidolianos and Kausitauros. And it is this. Since
Julian transferred the name of the Christians to Galileans, now the divine
Gregory wants to mock and to say that It is permitted also for us to call
Julian from the names about him. He says then, Let us call him Eidolianos as
an idolater, and Pisaean as honoring the Zeus in Pisa. And Zeus was honored in
the city Pisa (and Pisa is a city of Elis). He says then one must call Julian
Pisaean just as Zeus, and Adonaean because of Adonis. And Julian is said to
have reverenced Adonis. And this Adonis was loved by Aphrodite, being a human.
And he was murdered by Ares because Ares was a rival in love of Aphrodite. And
Kausitauros, he says, one must call Julian because of sacrificing whole bulls
and burning them to gods. =4.44= The forty-fourth is the story concerning the
vixen. And it is this. The fox is called kerdo. Hence also kerdaie wickedness
and craftiness. For this animal the fox is crafty and wicked. And Minos was
thought a just human, as son


of Zeus. What then the divine Gregory says is this, that Julian used to hide
his own wickedness through a plastered character, being unjust in a just
pretext. =4.45= The forty-fifth is the story about Melampus and Proteus. And
it is this. This Proteus is the one of Thrace, whose sons Heracles killed.
This one then was also a seer, and having become a marine daemon, and dwelling
Pharos; wherefore also Pharos was called the island of Proteus. He used to
metamorphose toward those approaching so as not to be asked to speak oracles
and foreknowledges. And similarly also Melampus was a seer and hierophant.
This one revealed the sacred things in Egypt to the Greeks, which Greeks
imitated. And this one too was of those who metamorphose, as a seer. For these
seers had, he says, so great a power, as to metamorphose into whatever they
should wish so as not to be known by those conversing. =4.46= The forty-sixth
is the story concerning the Aetnaean fire. And it is this. Sicily is a very
great island lying about Italy. In this there is a city Catane so called. And
above Catane there is a mountain from which much and continual fire is given
forth. And the mountain is called Aetna, and they are called also craters of
Hephaestus. And this fire is when it overflows so much as even to be carried
river-wise, and to flow as water with the fiery matter given forth. And this
fire is also something divine. For it is said once that a certain father with
a son was found on this mountain, and suddenly the fire flowed river-wise, and
it became impassable for them to pass. And the child took the father and
carried him. And the fire blushing at the deed of the son was checked from the
inflow. And the child and the father passed unharmed. =4.47= The forty-seventh
is the story the punishment in Mithras. And it is this. The Persians think
Mithras to be the sun, and to this one they sacrifice many sacrifices, and
they are initiated in certain rites from him. And no one can be initiated in
the rites of Mithras unless he should pass through all the punishments, and
show himself someone passionless and holy. And the punishments are said to be
eighty which by a step the one about to be initiated must pass, such as, first
to swim through much water for many days, then to throw himself into fire,
then to live in a desert and to go without food, and certain other things
until, as we said, he should pass the eighty punishments. =4.48= The
forty-eighth is the story the inhumanity of Echetus and Phalaris. And it is
this. Echetus was tyrant of Epirus. This one being most savage used to invent
all sorts of penalties and places of punishment. To this one those wanting to
punish unsparingly used to send, as also the poet says about him: To Echetus
the king, destroyer of all mortals. And Phalaris is someone of this sort, and
this one became an inventor of newer places of punishment. And he was Sicilian
by race. This one gratifying Dionysius the tyrant, being most savage and
punitive, invented a bronze bull in which one had to throw those being
punished and fire to be kindled, so that, being inside and burning and crying
out, the voice running through the mouth of the bull, the bull being burned
might seem to accomplish a bellowing. And Dionysius seeing the inhumanity of
this, threw this very one into the bull, and having burned him killed him.
=4.49= The forty-ninth is the story concerning the Hydra. And it is this. A
beast is mythologized about Lerna fifty-headed, and others nine-headed; and
the heads to be of snakes. Which beast Heracles coming with Hyllus his
attendant killed. And this too is mythologized, that, as one head of it was
cut, two used to grow beside, so as to become a hundred heads. And Hyllus
learning this, used to bring fire, and used to burn the head being cut. And
thus they were able to kill all. =4.50= The fiftieth is the story concerning
the Pataran Chimera. And it is this. Patara a city of Lycia. And it is said
that in this there is a mountain, in which mountain there is a beast, which in
front is a lion, and behind a dragon, and in the middle was a goat; from which
goat fire was given forth, and it used to harm those passing. And later
Bellerophon coming


having the horse Pegasus killed the Chimera. =4.51= The fifty-first is the
story concerning Cerberus. And it is this. It is mythologized that there is a
certain dog having three heads who is called Three-headed. And this one, he
says, guards the gates of Hades. And those going down he fawns on and
flatters, and those going up he bites and does not allow to go up. He says
then that Heracles, when he went down to snatch with Peirithous Persephone,
going up killed Cerberus. =4.52= The fifty-second is the story concerning
Scylla. And it is this. There is mythologized in the Tyrrhenian sea a beast,
which is a very beautiful woman as far as the navel, and from here and from
here heads of dogs have grown upon her six; and the rest of her body
snake-like. Which, he says, beast used to eat those sailing past these seas
unsparingly and beast-like. =4.53= The fifty-third is the story concerning
Charybdis. And it is this. About this Sicilian and Tyrrhenian sea there is a
certain place, in which the sea becomes ebb and surf. And the water stands
apart so that the bottom of the sea in this part, if possible, is seen. And
ebb is as it were a certain drinking-up and sucking back; and the water sinks
down in certain hollows of the rocks and again bursts out. Which is also
called surf. And this place was dangerous for those sailing and full of death.
For the ship used to become wholly underwater in this place, if it happened,
as the ship was sailing, that ebb and surf came to be. About these Homer: From
here Scylla, and on the other side divine Charybdis. And he called Charybdis
an opposite comparing it to Scylla. =4.54= The fifty-fourth is the story about
the dog-skin cap of Hades. And it is this. Homer the poet introduces Zeus
producing a certain invisibility and disappearance about the Greeks so as not
to be seen. He says then more mythically, that he put around them the dog-skin
cap of Hades so as not to be seen. And the kynee is a helmet of Hades that is
of Pluto. =4.55= The fifty-fifth is the story concerning the ring of Gyges.
And it is this. Plato the philosopher in Republics (and there is a treatise of
his so called) introduces a certain myth. And this says that Gyges was a
certain shepherd about Lydia. This one shepherding the sheep on a mountain,
encountered a certain cave, and going into it, found a bronze horse, and found
in the bronze horse a dead human and a ring. Of which ring the head was
turning, and it used to be turned. Which head the divine Gregory calls a
bezel. Gyges then, he says, took the ring, and went out. And whenever the ring
was in the arrangement, he was seen by all, and whenever he turned the bezel
of the ring, he used to become invisible to all. Plato then introduces this
myth, that, he says, the just man, even if he should take the ring of Gyges so
as not to be seen by anyone, not even thus ought he to do injustice. For the
beautiful must be practiced because of the good itself, and not because of
certain others. =4.56= The fifty-sixth is the story concerning the Lindians
cursing Bouthoinas. And it is this. Lindos an old city of Rhodes. In this at
the priest sacrificing and feasting on the ox those sacrificing used to curse
and revile and insult with unholy insults. =4.57= The fifty-seventh is the
story about killing strangers among the Taurians. And it is this. Already we
said that in Scythia there is a nation or city called Taurians. Here Artemis
is honored. Iphigeneia the daughter of Agamemnon then having been brought here
by Artemis, they used to sacrifice the arriving strangers toward Iphigeneia
not becoming evident after the return of the strangers. =4.58= The
fifty-eighth is the story the Laconians being carded beside the altars. And it
is this having been said also already before, and now too it is said. The
Lacedaemonians teaching their own children to be enduring used to use the
scourgings, teaching them to be patient. And they used to card these with the
blows beside the altars, and to the one not making light but bearing it nobly
they used to give a prize. =4.59= The fifty-ninth is the story of the
Phrygians being cut.


And this has been said by us, yet now too let us say. Among the Phrygians Rhea
the mother of the gods was honored. Festival-making for her, they used to cut
their own thighs and shoulders. And others used to pipe so that through the
pleasure of the pipes the pain of the cutting might be stolen away. =4.60= The
sixtieth is the story the bastards having been thrown off into Cynosarges. And
it is this. Cynosarges was a place in Athens in which the bastards were
judged, if he has truly become a son of this one. And they used to call among
Athenians also the freedmen bastards. And these too bastards as toward those
free from birth. And these too were judged and examined, if they were freed at
all. And the place was called Cynosarges from this: as a sacrifice was
happening nearby in a sanctuary, a dog going in snatched meats of the victim,
and led them in this place. And there a certain temple was founded and was
called Cynosarges, as it were kynosar kes, from the fleshes and the dog. And
later as the K went out, the G went in. =4.61= The sixty-first is the story
concerning Xanthus and Chalcis. Homer: Whom the gods call Xanthus, and men
Scamander. And again: The gods call Chalcis, and men Cymindis. The divine
teacher then mocks them. =4.62= The sixty-second is the story about Palamedes.
And it is this. It is said that Palamedes, being Euboean by race (and Euboea
an island opposite Attica), found numbering and playing tables and using many
other methods. And this Palamedes is one of those who campaigned together upon
Ilium. And he died in Troy by a plot of Odysseus. For he had Odysseus as an
enemy for a cause of this sort: as Agamemnon was urging Odysseus toward the
expedition about Troy, and Odysseus not wanting to go away and pretending
madness and taking an ass and an ox, and forsooth ploughing in a pretence of
madness, Palamedes refuted that he is not mad. And he refuted him having set
Telemachus his son before the plough. Then Odysseus arriving about the child,
raised the plough so as not to strike the child, and it was known that he is
sober. This wrath then Odysseus nursing in Troy, makes a forged letter from
Priam as toward Palamedes about betrayal of the Greeks, and puts it under in
the tent of Palamedes. Later as an accusation came to be against Palamedes as
a traitor, the letter was found; and he died by Agamemnon and all the Greeks.
=4.63= The sixty-third is the story concerning the jackdaw and its feathers.
And it is this a myth of this sort. There was a judgment, he says, about
beauty of birds, and Zeus was umpire of the judgment. And the day had been
defined in which the birds must be assembled beside Zeus. And on the day
before the defined day the birds going to the rivers used to wash the feathers
in the streams so that the natural beauty might shine for each. And the
jackdaw (and this bird is very small in body, and ugly in form) putting around
itself of the feathers fallen beside the waters, came itself too beside Zeus,
as about to take the prize of beauty. And a wind blowing thus and scattering
the alien of the feathers, it was found naked of the beauty; and having the
ugliness lying around it from nature, thus it enjoyed the shame. =4.64= The
sixty-fourth is the story about the old woman whose shoulder was shaken. And
it is this. A certain woman called as some say Sibyl, and as others Phemonoe,
and as others Philyra, was shaken by a certain youth. And the woman was
carried more insultingly toward the youth and insulted him. And the insult
said was a metrical verse; and the harmony of the speech of the old woman
pleasing those standing by, they took the meter of the verse. And thus the
things about verses were crafted. =4.65= The sixty-fifth is the story
concerning the Cyclopes. And it is this. In Sicily the island about its
mountainous parts the Cyclopes are said to have come to be, practicing a
pastoral life, but living more violently. And these are said to be man-eaters
and to have eaten up the companions of Odysseus. And three of them are said
the most outstanding, Brontes,


Steropes, Arges. And these themselves are said also most outstanding smiths in
art, who through their own art constructed the thunder and the lightning for
Zeus. And they were called Cyclopes, as Hesiod, because they had one circular
eye in the face, and as Telephatus, because they dwelt a certain circular
island. =4.66= The sixty-sixth is the story about the purple robe. And it is
this. In Tyre beside the shore a shepherd dog going finding a shellfish ate
it. Then the blood of the shellfish dyed the mouth of the dog. And the
shepherd thinking the dog had been struck, taking wool, wiped off the blood of
the mouth. And the dog is found having nothing, and the wool having taken up
the dye of the purple. Then knowing that the shellfish has such a dyeing
nature, he made this very thing public. And thus they used to collect in the
sea the shellfish, and they constructed the purples. =4.67= The sixty-seventh
is the story concerning agriculture and shipbuilding. And it is this as it is
mythologized. Demeter had a daughter Persephone. This one Pluto snatched. And
Demeter going around and seeking the daughter, came into Attica, and lodges
with a certain Triptolemus in a village Eleusis so called. She learns then
from Triptolemus that Pluto snatched Persephone. And for this benefaction she
gives to Triptolemus the seeds, I mean wheat and barley, commanding not to
begrudge, but to go around and scatter the seeds to all humans. so that they
may learn to sow and to farm and to eat the cultivated fruits. For of old they
used to eat the acorns from the oaks; hence from the oak (phegos) eating
(phagein) has been said. And it is said that Triptolemus, taking a chariot of
winged dragons, taking along also Celeus, thus used to wander being ambitious
with the seeds. And the divine Gregory says that shipbuilding too was found
among Athenians. And I think he speaks about the art of sea-fighting. For
Athenians are sea-fighters to the height. For Phoenicians are said first to
have built ships, and Semiramis triremes. =4.68= The sixty-eighth is the story
concerning Celeus and Icarius. And about Celeus we said in the story before
this, that with Triptolemus he used to wander giving the seeds. And about
Icarius it is this. It is said that wine was found from Dionysus, wherefore
also they call Dionysus overseer of the vine. This Dionysus then coming in
Athens encountering a certain Icarius, gave him a vine-shoot to plant. And he
planted, and farmed wine, and both he himself drank, and he gave also to
shepherds to drink. And the shepherds being drunk because of drinking first
from the unexpected thinking they had been poisoned by Icarius, kill him. And
Erigone, his daughter, through the dog of Icarius knowing that he has died,
used to lament and was distressed. And the gods pitying her because of the
suffering, transferred her into heaven. And now she is, he says, among the
stars Erigone. And the saying, A shameful mystery these things made for you,
he says not about Icarius, but about the mythology concerning Demeter. And
this one is said not only to have given them the seeds, but also taught a
certain ordinance of mysteries, which the Greeks are initiated in. For as
among the God-taught Christians there is the illumination, so among those the
such. And such a feast of the Greeks is called both Mysteries and Eleusinia
and Demetria. =4.69= The sixty-ninth is the story concerning worshipping and
being initiated. And it is this. It is said that Orpheus, being Thracian by
race and having theologized the mysteries of the Greeks, taught how both and
... one must honor gods. This then honoring gods he called threskeuein, as the
finding being Thracian. And others etymologized that threskeuein has been
derived from as it were theoderkeuein, that is to see god. For derkein is to
see. And to be initiated has been said either from mysteries and secrets being
performed, or through closing the sen ses and becoming beyond all bodily
appearance thus to receive the divine illuminations. =4.70= The seventieth is
the story about


sacrificing and astronomizing and geometrizing. And it is not unclear. And it
is said that sacrificing to gods Chaldaeans discovered, or Cyprians; the story
then is disputed. And Chaldaeans a Persian nation. And astronomy Babylonians
are said first to have found through Zoroaster, and second Egyptians received
it. And geometry Egyptians found from the boundlessness of the land and the
division of the countries having been taught beforehand, and then thus writing
and using. And magic, he says, Medes found, then Persians, and first Medes.
And magic differs from goeteia and goeteia from pharmakeia in these, that
magic is an invocation of well-doing daemons toward establishment of some
good, as the oracles of Apollonius of Tyana have come to be through a good.
And goeteia is an invocation of evil-doing daemons rolling about the tombs
toward establishment of some evil. And goeteia was called from the wailings
and the laments happening about the tombs. And pharmakeia when through a
certain death-bearing preparation someone should give to someone either toward
a love-charm or through the mouth. =4.71= The seventy-first is the story about
dream-divination. And it is this. Telmessos is a city of Lycia. And this is a
very old city. And it was also in the times of Croesus. These are said first
to have found the dreams, to distinguish also the portents. For they used to
say, if something unexpected has happened, that this signifies, and wholly, as
they said, it used to happen to come out. =4.72= The seventy-second is the
story about the art of bird-omens. Phrygians then are said to have found all
the art of bird-omens. And of the art of bird-omens, one part is
bird-watching, another house-watching, another on-the-road, another
hand-watching, another palmic. Bird-watching then is when as this or that bird
is flying, either in front or behind, or right or left, we say that this
signifies. And Telegonus is said first to have discovered it. House-watching
is when he will explain the things happening in the house, and say that this
signifies. since a weasel appeared in the roof, or a snake, or a mouse, or oil
was emptied, or honey, or wine, or ash, or something else, that this
signifies. And these Xenocrates wrote up. On-the-road is when he will explain
the things meeting on the road, that, If someone meeting you is carrying this,
this will happen to you, if so and so, this. Which Pollis wrote up.
Hand-watching is when through the displacement of the hands and through the
wrinkles we say that this awaits him, or that he marries, or begets children,
or something such; which Helenus wrote up. And palmic is that known through
the quivering of the body, such as, the right eye quivered, this signifies, or
the shoulder, or the thigh; which Posidonius and many others wrote up. =4.73=
The seventy-third is the story about the uprising of the Scythians against the
masters. And it is this. A certain part of the nomad Scythians went out from
Scythia wandering upon piracy. And these left the household slaves with the
women to make cheese of the milk. And the Scythians used to blind these
household slaves, and thus used to provide them to make cheese of the milk.
Time then coming in, and the Scythians who went out not returning, the women
go to the slaves who had been blinded and have intercourse with them, and
conceiving they bear. And children come to be instead of the Scythians who
went out. And they were slaves according to the seed. The Scythians then who
went out of old returning, found these born from the slaves having come to
youth and opposing them. And a war coming to be between the slave-sown and the
returned Scythians, the returned are very much defeated. To these then
defeated a certain one advised not to war against them with weapons, but
through a whip. For to war against some through weapons, he says, the war is
as from equals toward equals. And to go against them through whips is as from
masters toward slaves. The slave-sown then seeing the Scythians with the whips
yielded as to their own masters. And thus the war was dissolved. =4.74= The
seventy-fourth is the story concerning the Thessalian horse and the
Lacedaemonian woman. And it is this. Every city had according to something
exceptional


a peculiarity, such as the country of the Thessalians had the horses, that of
the Athenians the mines of silver, India the gold-bearing sand, and similarly
also Lacedaemon hunting dogs (hence also the Laconian dogs) and brave and
unflinching women. And Sicily too had a spring of water, Arethusa so called,
of which they mythologize that the river Alpheus fell in love. And the water
of Arethusa is unmixed and pure and sweet. The divine Gregory then says that
just as certain countries and cities and citizens had something according to
exception as a peculiarity, so the doctrine of the Christians all these,
orphan-nurturing houses, guest-houses, hospitals, poor-nurturing houses and
all such goods. =4.75= The seventy-fifth is the story about the city of Plato
in words. Plato the philosopher wrote up a composition which he titled
Republic. In this composition he says of what sort a city must come to be and
composed of how many men and administered with what characters and laws. And
this city for him stood together in word only, but in deed neither stood
together nor was administered. In that composition then he says this little
saying, that, Let our citizens be supposed uttering these things, so that we
may see their thought being moved. =4.76= The seventy-sixth is the story about
the Theogony of Hesiod. Hesiod is one of the poets being treated, of whom
three poems are treated, the so-called Theogony and the so-called Works and
Days and the so-called Shield. In the Theogony then Hesiod begins to number
the generations of the gods, that from Chaos and from Aether so and so was
generated, from Heaven and Earth Cronus, from Cronus Zeus, Poseidon, Pluto.
Then he brings down series of races and names of newer gods, and that these
gods warred with these, and these were defeated, and these tore apart this one
or these. For the Titans are said to have torn apart Dionysus. And these names
which he says thus, such as Cottus, Briareos, Hesiod says certain humans are
generated having from a hundred hands, who with the gods warred against the
Giants. For the Giants are mythologized to have risen against gods, until Zeus
thunderbolted them. And similarly about the dragon-footed Hesiod says that
dragon-footed humans were generated. And he now calls thunderbolt-bearing
gods, either Zeus himself, as sending a thunderbolt upon the Giants, or
Steropes and Brontes, those who smithied for Zeus the thunder and the
lightning and the thunderbolts. And he says islands and missiles and tombs
were sent upon the Giants by the gods. These things then he mythologizes. And
the bitter offspring of these he says that from the blood of the Giants these
death-bearing animals have come to be, such as those he himself numbers, the
Hydra (and this is the nine-headed snake, which Heracles destroyed), the
Chimera (about which we having gone before have said, that in front it was a
lion, and behind a dragon, and in the middle a goat, and that Bellerophon
destroyed this beast), and similarly also about Cerberus we have already said
that it was a dog about the gate of Hades having three heads, and those going
down about Hades it used to fawn on, and those going up it used to eat up,
which Heracles murdered. And the about the Gorgons was of this sort, that
there were three women having faces so fearful that those looking at them used
to die. And it is said that Perseus destroyed the one of these with the
spear-sickle. =4.77= The seventy-seventh is the story that says, Let Orpheus
come forward with the kithara. And it is this. Orpheus became a musician,
Thracian by race, who is said to have sung so pleasantly that the oaks and the
irrational of the animals used to follow his song, and the stones, and the
rivers. And poems of this one are carried which Greeks have as theologies. And
in these poems through mythical symbols he says the orders and series of the
gods, and of whom what works and of what sort the completions of whom, and who
creators of whom. Of this Orpheus then certain verses the divine Gregory sets
beside, which have been said about Zeus and Demeter. the


first things he says about Zeus, and the later about Demeter. And the sense of
the verses about Zeus is this that, O Zeus glorious and greatest of all the
gods, being surrounded by all the dung, as much as of sheep, as much as of
horses and mules. And melee dung is that of sheep; for mela are the sheep. And
through this verse forsooth Orpheus signified that Zeus sends out as far as
the beast-like matter his own life-generating powers. And of the verse about
Demeter the sense is this, that, Coming on the goddess drew up her own thighs
(and he speaks about the clothes), so that, he says, she may deem those loving
her worthy of intercourse. And through these Orpheus hints that the divine
power becomes ready for those desiring it to give itself and to initiate them.
The divine Gregory then, as this too being shameful and the one about Zeus
unclean, pulls down and laughs at them. =4.78= The seventy-eighth is the story
that says, Upon all Phanes and also Ericapaeus. And it is this. In the Orphic
poems these two names were introduced with also many others, of whom Phanes he
introduces having a private part behind about the rump. And they say he is
overseer of the life-generating power. And similarly also they say Ericapaeus
is overseer of another power. And about The one swallowing all gods he does
not speak about Ericapaeus but about Cronus. For this one is said to swallow
again the sons he begot, and to vomit those he already swallowed. For he is
said to swallow a stone instead of Zeus and as the stone went down to vomit
all. =4.79= The seventy-ninth is the story that says about Homer. And the
story about this one is much-babbled. For in his poems up and down he
mythologizes both Cronus and Zeus, both Hera and Aphrodite, and Athena upon
all these, andsimply as many as we know from the poetry. And he called Homer a
comedy-maker as certain jeers having been said against gods by him, that Hera
having dressed splendidly and having been beautified went away beside Zeus,
and deceived him, and slept with him, and if anything more ridiculous than
this? And a tragedy-maker, because having mythologized certain mournful things
about the gods such as that he hurled Hephaestus down in Lemnos, and that
Aphrodite was wounded by Diomedes. And these forsooth are worthy of tears. For
tears follow tragedy, and laughter comedy. =4.80= The eightieth is the story
about Oceanus and Tethys. And the poets introduce this myth too, that Oceanus
is father of all the gods, and Tethys mother (and Oceanus and Tethys are
androgynous), and that of old they stood apart from one another, and through
the shame of Hera there comes to be again a joining of them and friendship.
And the myth, he says, hints that Oceanus is the wet nature, and Tethys the
dry, that is the earth; and that of old as the wet nature was not consorting
with the earth, nothing was generated alive, and all things used to perish.
Then Hera, as being overseer of the yoking, reconciles the two elements, and
thereafter there comes to be generation of life. =4.81= The eighty-first is
the story of cloud-gathering Zeus toward Hera. Zeus used rather to favor the
Trojans, and used to make the Greeks be defeated because of the supplication
of Thetis for the sake of Achilles, so that the Greeks being defeated might
seek Achilles. And Hera used to care for the Greeks; and caring, she plans to
deceive Zeus toward mixing and sleep, making him revel, so that, as Zeus is
sleeping, the Trojans becoming unhelped, may be defeated by the Greeks. Having
planned these things she takes all the beautifyings from the cestus of
Aphrodite. And the cestus is mythologized to be a certain casket, in which all
things toward beauty and good form and graces are stored. Then having adorned
herself she goes up beside Zeus, and moves him toward pleasure and lewdness.
And the earth too, he says, sprouted herbs, such as crocus, lotus and
hyacinth. =4.82= The eighty-second is the story how Hera hanging from the
aether and clouds and being dragged down with iron anvils and bound with
golden fetters. And this is the story. Hera, as we often know, used to be
jealous of


Heracles. This same one then after returning from Troy, when with Telamon he
sacked it, she disturbed and stormed in the sea, commanding the winds to
disturb the vessel of Heracles, and exhorting Sleep to go to Zeus and put him
to sleep, so that being awake he might not help Heracles being stormed. And
Zeus learning after the sleep the contrivance of Hera, punishes her thus:
binding two anvils to her about the feet, and about the hands indissoluble
fetters, he hung her from heaven, punishing her. =4.83= The eighty-third is
the story, how there was fear for the gods lest somehow, as heaven was
trumpeting, the seat of the earth be broken up and Hades be made public. And
this too is in Homer, in the upsilon rhapsody. And it is this. As Achilles was
distinguishing himself by force, fear took Zeus lest somehow contrary to what
was fated Achilles sack Ilium. fearing this he permits the gods whoever wishes
to go away to whomever he wishes and to help, so that some may help the
Trojans, and others the Greeks. These having been permitted, Zeus thundered
from heaven, and Poseidon shakes the sea. And Hades, as both the sea and
heaven had been shaken, feared lest somehow as the earth was shaken a
breaking-up of the underworld come to be, and the things according to him be
made public. And the verses are these: And fearing he leapt from the throne
and cried out, lest over him Poseidon the earth-shaker should break up the
earth. =4.84= The eighty-fourth is the story the nodding-together of the brows
and the moving-together of the hairs. And it is this. Homer introduces Zeus
after the supplication of Thetis the one about Achilles having promised,
having moved him the brows upon the promise, and having shaken the hairs upon
the downward-nodding of the head, and having moved together the whole heaven
as his hairs were moved. And he called the hairs ambrosial, such as no mortal
can have. =4.85= The eighty-fifth is the story how Ares is wounded and how he
is shut in a bronze jar. The having been wounded the poet says that he was
wounded by Diomedes; these things he says in the fifth rhapsody. And he was
bound by Otus and Ephialtes. And these were sons of Aloeus, tyrants by nature.
And they came to be about Thessaly. These practiced a revolt against the gods.
Ares then first went against them, then being defeated by them, was bound in a
bronze jar. And a bronze jar is either a city so called, or it is a form of a
bond difficult and hard to loose. And those about Otus and Ephialtes being
nine years old are said to be nine fathoms according to length, and according
to breadth nine cubits. =4.86= The eighty-sixth is the story about the lover
of Aphrodite. And it is this. Aphrodite was wife of Hephaestus. Ares fell in
love with this one, and committed adultery with her. And Hephaestus learning
this, and being a smith in art, sets traps for Ares coming upon the adultery
of Aphrodite. And he falls into the traps and is held, and is caught
committing adultery with Aphrodite. And Hephaestus did not loose them until he
called the gods and made them public. And then the gods coming and seeing,
laughed a great laughter upon them. And then Ares is loosed, having given
certain small ransoms. And he calls Hephaestus Amphigyes as lame. =4.87= The
eighty-seventh is the story about the wars. And it is this. Athena and Hera
caring for the Greeks used to war toward Aphrodite and Ares caring for the
Trojans. And you might say wars also those from the Giants toward the gods and
those from the Titans toward Dionysus. =4.88= The eighty-eighth is the story
about Cronus and the cutting of Heaven. And it is this. Heaven becomes father
of Cronus. Cronus then not wanting another child to come to be for him, taking
a sickle cuts the private parts of the father, and throws the private parts
into the sea. And foam coming to be Aphrodite was generated from the foam;
hence also she was named Aphrodite as from the foam. =4.89= The eighty-ninth
is the story of the uprising of Zeus toward Cronus. And it is this. Zeus
learning that


Cronus is such a child-eater, rises against him, and tears the kingship from

those he swallowed before. =4.90= The ninetieth is the story about the
Profit-bringing god. And it is this. The Greeks call Hermes a Profit-bringing
god, as productive of profit. Wherefore also Aristophanes the comic in the

as Empolaios, that is, overseer of profit. =4.91= The ninety-first is the
story about the one becoming all things because of the women. And he speaks
about Zeus. For Zeus becoming an eagle snatched Ganymede, having fallen in

that Zeus snatched Ganymede so that he might make him a cupbearer whenever he
lunches for the gods. And this Zeus becoming gold consorted with Danae, and as
a bull snatched Europa, and becoming a swan prostituted Leda. And about the
fifty daughters of Thestius, already we have said that Heracles in one night
consorted with the fifty daughters of Thestius. And the same Heracles is
called also the Three-evening one, as already we have said, as having been
generated in three days. =4.92= The ninety-second is the story about cutting
short the spirit of Ares. And this is clear that Ares is said overseer of the
wars and breathing wrath, since we need in the wars that the spirited be moved
in us. =4.93= The ninety-third is the story about the drunkenness of Dionysus.
And it is this. It is said that Dionysus is overseer of the vine and of the
wine and of drunkenness itself, and that they make even him being made drunk,
Hera having introduced to him the being made drunk so that he may become out
of mind. For Hera used to be angry at him as a son of Zeus. For she used to be
jealous of those generated from Zeus. =4.94= The ninety-fourth is the story
about stranger-hating Artemis. And it is clear. For we spoke about Iphigeneia
and the Taurian humans. For Artemis was honored among the Taurians in Scythia,
and having Iphigeneia, the daughter of Agamemnon, as a priestess sacrificing
the strangers to her she used to rejoice in the killing of strangers. =4.95=
The ninety-fifth is the story concerning Loxias the oracle-speaker. And it is
this. Apollo giving oracles, used to speak the oracles to those being
prophesied for neither clearly nor expressly, but unclearly and crookedly;
wherefore he was called Loxias, as prophesying the opposites of the things
that would come out. And from this many were deceived, of whom with all
Croesus the Lydian. For the oracle given to him was this: Croesus having
crossed the Halys will destroy a great rule. And it was not clear which rule
he will destroy. Croesus then thinking that he speaks the rule of the enemies,
crossed the river Halys, and destroyed his own rule. =4.96= The ninety-sixth
is the story about the immoderation of laughter. And it is this. The one about
Hephaestus; when with the traps he bound Ares because of the adultery of
Aphrodite and called the gods, they poured down forsooth much laughter against
him. And he calls a limping god Hephaestus himself; for this god was lame
among them. And Hephaestus used to delight in the shins of Ares as Ares
according to the shins fell into the traps of Hephaestus. =4.97= The
ninety-seventh is the story about Zeus running Upon a rich feast. And this too
is in Homer, that Zeus went away to Ethiopia upon a lunch, which the
Ethiopians having made, called both him and the other gods with him. And the
lunch of the Ethiopians and this feast is this. It was a sacrifice happening
for Zeus. And the verses are these: For Zeus to Oceanus among the blameless
Ethiopians yesterday went according to a feast, and the gods all together

Lock of Berenice, and about the Swan, and the Bull, and the Lion, and the
Serpent-holder. And these are they. Ariadne was daughter of Minos, the king of
the Cretans. This one fell in love with Theseus, the king of Athens, who came
upon destruction of the Minotaur. From Theseus then Dionysus took her, and
brought her up in Naxos, and mixed with her. And toward her honor he
under-drew a crown in heaven through stars. And the one of the Lock of
Berenice is this. Berenice was a certain wife of Ptolemy in Alexandria, the
one called Euergetes. As then her husband Ptolemy was in wars, she vowed that,
if he should return unwounded, having cut off her own lock, she will dedicate
a dedication in the sanctuary; and Berenice dedicated. There was then a
certain Comon an astronomer in her times, and toward flattery of her he says
that the gods dedicated this lock among stars. And now there is a certain
grape-cluster-like position of stars in heaven, which they call Lock of
Berenice. And the about the Swan is this. Zeus having fallen in love as some
say with Nemesis, and others, with Leda, wanting to consort with her and not
to be seen by Hera was likened to a swan, and consorted with the beloved. Now
then this swan was fixed in heaven. And there is, he says, the Swan
under-drawn through stars. And the about the Bull is this. A certain bull was
given forth by Poseidon about Greece. This one used to harm the country and
used to injure many. Upon this Theseus coming destroyed it. And the gods
wanting to show the deed of Theseus great and also to tend Poseidon, fixed the
bull in heaven; and now the Bull is under-drawn through stars in heaven. And
the story about the Lion is this. A lion was a wild animal in Nemea (and this
is a country of the Peloponnese). This lion then used to harm those in the
country. Theseus then ruling Mycenae and being wrathful at Heracles, sends
Heracles upon destruction of the lion. And Heracles coming destroyed it. And
the gods wanting to make the contest of Heracles glorious drew through stars
in heaven the lion. And now the Lion is among stars. And the about the
Serpent-holder is this. This Serpent-holder is said to be Asclepius, overseer
of medicine. And the snake is a symbol of the unaging; for the snake is said
to strip off old age and to be renewed. Since then Asclepius through medicine
renews the human bodies, they make him with the dragon. The gods then wanting
to consecrate him instead of a statue, consecrated him through stars in
heaven. 5.2 The second is the story about Salmoneus thundering from a hide.
And it is this. Salmoneus was son of Aeolus, and king of Thessalians. This one
becoming impious toward gods, attaching to chariots hides dry and hard, and
striking certain cauldrons with the hides, used to accomplish sounds; and
carrying in hands burning torches he used to boast as Zeus to thunder and to
lighten, until being thunderbolted by Zeus he was destroyed. And he left a
daughter under age named Tyro. This one being reared by her own uncle on the
father's side Cepheus, fell in love with the local river Enipeus. And Poseidon
being likened to this river, consorted instead of Enipeus with Tyro. And she
conceived Pelias and Neleus, and had these as embryos. And she was married by
Cretheus; and later Tyro bore, in seeming from Cretheus, and in truth from
Poseidon. And later also from Cretheus himself Tyro had children. 5.3 The
third is the story concerning Zopyrus the one in Babylon. And it is this.
Cyrus the king of the Persians besieging Babylon for much time, and it being
hard to sack and he being distressed for much time, a certain Zopyrus brave in
soul, as the deed showed, and not without glory beside


Cyrus, contrives such a thing. Having cut off his own nose and ears and having
carded himself with whips, he forsooth deserts to the Babylonians, as having
suffered terrible things from Cyrus. Then going away he pretends to be
well-minded to the Babylonians, and suggests to them certain things against
Cyrus. And simply he generates trust in the Babylonians having been general
for the Babylonians and having destroyed many of Cyrus. And having composed a
signal with Cyrus a defined day in which he must attack Babylon, in this he
persuades the Babylonians to take the keys of the gates. Then taking he opened
and led in the Persians. And thus Babylon was subdued. These things Herodotus
in the Third of the Histories mentions. 5.4 The fourth is the story about
Cyrus of Parysatis. And it is this. Darius the small, the son of Xerxes,
begets from Parysatis two sons, Cyrus and Artaxerxes. And as Darius died, the
two disputed about the kingship. And time used to lead Artaxerxes as older
upon being king; and the mother used to put forward Cyrus as younger and being
loved. And Artaxerxes prevails through the time, and he himself is king. Cyrus
then not wanting to yield to Artaxerxes asks his own brother to give him the
satrapy of Asia as it were a prefecture. And taking it he went down about
Ionia. And sitting there coming again to thought of the kingship, and secretly
persuading certain men of the Lacedaemonians and other Greeks as ten thousand,
he persuades them to follow with him. And these following he went up as far as
Persia. And an engagement coming to be between those of Artaxerxes and those
of Cyrus, those of Cyrus conquer even these being much fewest toward the army
of Artaxerxes. For the Greeks knew well how to array themselves, and being few
they used to conquer the many by art. Cyrus then being lifted by the victory
toward a little charges from his own phalanx upon the phalanx of Artaxerxes as
forsooth about to slaughter Artaxerxes himself, and is destroyed being alone.
And thus he ends not having been king. These things Xenophon in the Hellenica
mentions. 5.5 The fifth is the story about the tyranny of the Samians. And it
is this. Histiaeus was Samian by race, and having become a friend of Darius
the king of the Persians, was taken by him in Persia. And living there, he
used to desire to see his own fatherland. And not being released, he writes to
his own cousin, Aristagoras, to practice a revolt. And he persuades also
certain of the Ionians to revolt. And he writes thus: taking a trusted
household slave and having shaved the head of this one, he engraves on the
head what he wished to make clear. Then blackening the engraving he used to
let the hairs grow up. And after he had grown hair he sends him off, saying to
the household slave to be shaved again there so that Aristagoras may read the
letters. Aristagoras then reading and learning, made the revolt of almost all
the Ionians. Then the king learns in Per sia, and becomes a counselor to
Histiaeus as a Greek what one must do. And he is advised by Histiaeus to send
him into Asia, as being sufficient to dissolve the things about the revolt.

whole construction. These things Herodotus says in the Sixth. 5.6 The sixth is
the story about the hundred-handed. And this has been said by us in the

and Briareos, having a hundred hands. And these with the gods warred toward
the Giants. 5.7 The seventh is the story concerning the ebbs. And it is this.
Ebb, as already has been said by us, is opposite to the surf. And these are
sufferings of a narrow sea being enclosed either by islands or by lands. And
ebb is as it were a drinking-up and sucking back, the water being withdrawn
into certain recesses of the underlying earth, and after a little the water
again going out. The water then being withdrawn within into the hollows of the
earth is called ebb, and the flowing out again this is called

then that this one judges those in Hades, and those worthy of punishment he

of the Magi. And it is this. The Magi through sacrifices used to speak the
foreknowledges, through hepatoscopy. For sacrificing and cutting up they used
to observe certain signs in the cuttings and in the livers and in the others
through which they used to say the things that would happen. And the Magi a
race of Medes, from whom the hepatoscopists went out. =39.16= The sixteenth is
the story about the astronomy of the Chaldaeans. The Chaldaeans, of whom first
Zoroaster and after him Ostanes, stood over the heavenly motion and said that
by such a motion of the heavenlies the things about those being born happen.
From which Chaldaeans Greeks learned astrology, and they began to refer those
being generated under the motion of the stars. =39.17= The seventeenth is the
story about the Thracian orgies. It is said that first of all humans Thracians
began to worship and to tend god and to perform rites and to put together
mysteries. From which Thracians threskeuein the piety about the divine was
called. And first of all Thracians Orpheus is said to have introduced the
rites and the manner of the rites and to which god which rites. And this
Orpheus is the one said through the lyre to charm even the lifeless things and
to tame the wild through the strikings of the lyre. =39.18= The eighteenth is
the story about the punishments of Mithras. Others thought Mithras otherwise;
for some the Sun, and others the overseer of fire, and others a certain
particular power. And certain rites happen for this Mithras and especially
among Chaldaeans. And those being initiated to Mithras used to be initiated
according to a certain step of punishments. For first they used to take the
lighter punishments, and


then thus the more drastic, such as first they used to starve them the ones
being initiated upon fifty days, if it happened, then, if he bore enduringly,
they used to make him be scraped upon two days, then again to make into snow
twenty days, and thus by little increasing the punishments upon the greater,
if the one being initiated used to appear enduring, then thereafter they used
to initiate him the most complete. =39.19= The nineteenth is the story about
the tearings of Osiris. Osiris among Egyptians was thought a god. And some say
him to be the same as Dionysus, and others another. This Osiris then they say
to have been torn by Typhon, and a great mourning to have come to be for the
Egyptians so as upon all the time to make memory of the tearing of Osiris. And
just as they say Dionysus to have been torn apart by the Titans, so also
Osiris by Typhon. And this Typhon is a daemon. =39.20= The twentieth is the
story about Isis. Isis was thought the same as Io the one snatched by Zeus.
For Zeus, he says, snatched Io, and fearing Hera, his own wife, changed Io
into a cow, and sometimes into a white, and sometimes into a black, and
sometimes into one becoming violet. And thus he used to wander with her. Zeus
then came in Egypt with Io wandering. And Egyptians honor Io that is Isis.
Wherefore upon her statue upon the head they carve horns of a cow, signifying
the change of the girl upon a cow. =39.21= The twenty-first is the story about
the Mendesian he-goats. In Egyptian the he-goat is called mendes. And some of
the Egyptians used to reverence the he-goat, as being dedicated to the
generative power. For the he-goat is said to be a mounting animal. The
he-goats then those dwelling according to the Mendesian horn used not to eat,
from reverence of the generative god. And they used greatly to honor also the
goat-herds of the he-goats. And there was also a sanctuary of Mendes among
Egyptians, in which sanctuary the statue was goat-legged, having the private
part upright. =39.22= The twenty-second is the story about the manger of Apis.
Over a certain time according to a period there used to be generated for the
Egyptians an ox conceived from a flash of moon. And the ox used to be called
among them Apis. And he had certain signs about the tail and about the tongue,
from which signs it used to be recognized that he is Apis. This ox then being
generated, the Egyptians used to make a feast as of a god having sojourned to
them. And they used to set before the ox in a manger a much public feast,
feasting the god Apis. =39.23= The twenty-third is the story about the Nile.
In the rising they honor him as a god, and they praise as a cause of
fruitfulness, thinking the water itself the increasing to be a god. =39.24=
The twenty-fourth is the story that the Egyptians used to reverence monsters
and creeping things. And they used to reverence the ibises, the crocodiles,
the snakes, the cats and certain races of fishes, from an unreasoning honor

Cecrops or Heracles or the others thought heroes, or also otherwise somehow
from men having become good. The divine Gregory then says that the forefathers
of the most divine Basil were greater and more well-born than those thought
among those outside well-born. And the sense of saint Gregory wants this, and
one must say who these are such as Pelops and Cecrops and Alcmaeon and
Heracles. For the descendants of these are said thereafter from Pelops
Pelopidae, and those from Cecrops Cecropidae, and those from Alcmaeon
Alcmaeonidae, and those from Heracles Heraleidae, and those from Aeacus
Aeacidae. Let us say then first who is Pelops. Pelops became son of Tantalus,
king of Phrygia. And this Tantalus having had a war with Ilus the founder


of Ilium upon Troy, and fearing the defeat, entrusted to Pelops, his own
child, taking money to depart upon the mainland, saying these things that If I
conquer, you return again into Phrygia, and if I am defeated, remain into
Europe. Taking then Pelops money, he came into Greece in a country called
Apia, which Apia country had as king Oenomaus, having a daughter Hippodameia
by name. Then Pelops having contended here a horse contest with Oenomaus, and
having conquered, took as wife the daughter of Oenomaus Hippodameia, and held
the country. And instead of Apia he called it Peloponnese, which is, the
island of Pelops. All then those from Pelops are called Pelopidae, such as
Atreus, Thyestes, Agamemnon, and Menelaus, and last Orestes. And about Pelops
so many, and about Cecrops already it will be said. Cecrops became king of
Athens, who greatly adorned Attica. And this one is the one called
double-natured. And he used to be called double-natured because he had
practiced two voices, one that of Greece, and another that of Egypt. For it is
said that the Athenians are colonists of Egyptians, from a city Sais. Leader
then of the colony this Cecrops became. And coming into Greece he learned the
Greek voice, having also the Egyptian. And the account saying Athenians to be
autochthons is a myth, taking the fiction from those who dwelt into Attica
never having transferred, or rather having stood up. For the country being
thin-soiled, was not very enviable so that those being might be driven out and
others migrate. And about Cecrops enough, and one must speak also about
Alcmaeon. This Alcmaeon was Athenian by race, very distinguished, wanting
always to guard the freedom for the Athenians. The city then being tyrannized
by Peisistratus, this one went out not bearing to see Athens being enslaved.
Going out then he contrives and had a goal how he will restore the freedom to
Athenians. Going away then in Delphi, into the Apollonian sanctuary, he hires
from Delphians the sanctuary upon wage-bearing. And having hired, and having
thereafter under himself all men and all women the servants of the god, he
persuades the priestess the Pythia so that, if ever a Lacedaemonian should
come to seek an oracle, to say nothing else to him or that The god says to you
to free Athens. This happening continually and very many times, Lacedaemonians
were stirred, and coming they threw out from Athens the tyrants. Great glory
then Alcmaeon had from then, having freed Athens by wisdom. But so many about

to be a son of Zeus, just most of all. Wherefore once as a drought came to be
in Greece, those presiding over the cities came toward him calling upon him to
pray to his own father so that rain may come to be. And he standing and having
prayed, loosed through the prayer the droughts. And water flowed down watering
Greece. Especially then also from this the piety of Aeacus was shown. This one
then begets two sons, Peleus and Telamon, of whom Peleus begets Achilles, and
Telamon Ajax. Who used to be called Aeacidae, bringing from this the honor
from the grandfather. It remains then for us Heracles. This Heracles is said
to have become a son of Zeus. For Zeus having been likened to Amphitryon
consorted with Alcmene. And Heracles was generated, who also accomplished
those great labors. Many then have come to be from Heracles, of whom first and
glorious Hyllus. From whom also the kings of the Lacedaemonians. All then the
kings of the Lacedaemonians used to be called Heracleidae. 43.2 The second is
