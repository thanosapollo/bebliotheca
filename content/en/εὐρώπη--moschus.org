#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Europa edited by J. M. Edmonds
#+END_QUOTE

Once Cypris sent a sweet dream upon Europa,
when the third watch of night is set, and dawn is near,
when sleep, sweeter than honey, settling on the eyelids,
that unstrings the limbs, binds the eyes with a soft fetter,
when the host of true dreams also ranges at pasture;
then, slumbering in the roofed halls,
Phoenix's daughter, still a maiden, Europa,
dreamed that two continents were fighting over her,
the nearer land and the land across; they had the form of women.
Of them one had a stranger's look, and the other seemed
a native, and clung more closely to her own girl,
and said she had borne her and that she herself had reared her.
But the other, forcing her with strong hands,
drew her not against her will, since she said it was her destined
prize from aegis-bearing Zeus that Europa should be.
She leapt from her spread bed in fear,
heart pounding; for she saw the dream as if it were waking.
She sat and kept long silence, and both women
she still held in her opened eyes.
At last she lifted her frightened maiden voice:
/Who of the heavenly ones sent me such visions?/

/What dreams over my spread bed in the chambers/
/startled me as I slumbered so sweetly,/
/and who was the stranger I saw as I slept?/
/How desire for her seized my heart, and how she herself/
/received me gladly and looked on me as her own child./
/But may the blessed ones turn the dream to good for me./
So speaking she sprang up, and sought her dear companions,
girls of her own years, dear to her heart, of noble fathers,
with whom she always played, when she would dress for the dance,
or when she would brighten her skin in the streams of the torrents,
or whenever she would pluck fragrant lilies from the meadow.
And they appeared to her at once; each had in her hands
a flower-basket; and they went toward the meadows
by the shore, where they always gathered in a company,
delighting in the growth of roses and the sound of the wave.
Europa herself carried a golden basket,
a marvel to behold, a great wonder, a great labor of Hephaestus,
which he gave as a gift to Libya when she went to the
Earth-shaker's bed; and she gave it to beautiful Telephassa,
who was of her blood; and to unwed Europa
her mother Telephassa bestowed the famous gift.
In it many gleaming works of art had been wrought.
On it was Inachian Io wrought of gold,
still a heifer, and she had not a woman's form.
Wandering on her feet she went over the salt paths,
like one swimming; and the sea was wrought dark blue.
And two men stood high on the brow of the shore

crowded together, and they gazed at the sea-going cow.
And on it was Zeus, son of Cronus, gently laying his hands
on the Inachian heifer, and beside the seven-mouthed Nile
he was changing her back from a well-horned cow into a woman.
The stream of Nile was of silver, and the heifer
of bronze, and Zeus himself was wrought of gold.
And around, under the rim of the rounded basket,
Hermes was fashioned; and near him was stretched out
Argus, equipped with unsleeping eyes.
And from his blood, red with slaughter, there rose
a bird glorying in the many-colored bloom of its wings,
spreading its tail as if it were a swift-sailing ship;
the tail covered the rims of the golden basket.
Such was the basket of beautiful Europa.
Now when they came to the flowery meadows,
one after another they delighted their hearts in different flowers.
Of them one took the fragrant narcissus, another the hyacinth,
another the violet, another thyme; and many leaves
of spring-fed meadows bloomed upon the ground.
And others again were plucking in rivalry the fragrant tresses
of yellow crocus, but the princess in the midst,
gathering in her hands the splendor of the fiery rose,
stood out as the foam-born among the Graces.
Yet she was not to warm her heart long upon the flowers,
nor keep her maiden girdle unstained.
For indeed the son of Cronus, as soon as he saw her, was stirred

in his heart, subdued by the unexpected darts
of Cypris, who alone can tame even Zeus.
For, both avoiding the wrath of jealous Hera
and wishing to deceive the tender mind of the maiden,
he hid his godhead and changed his body and became a bull,
not such as is fed in the stalls, nor such as
cuts the furrow dragging the well-bent plough,
nor such as grazes with the flock, nor such as
one that, yoked, draws a heavy-laden wagon.
The rest of his body was tawny-skinned,
but a silvery circle gleamed in the middle of his forehead,
and his eyes shone grey-blue and flashed desire.
Horns rose equal opposite each other from his head
like the circles of the horned moon cut in half.
He came into the meadow and appearing did not frighten
the maidens, and in all of them desire arose to come near
and touch the lovely bull, and his ambrosial scent
from far off even surpassed the sweet breath of the meadow.
He stood before the feet of blameless Europa,
and licked her neck, and enchanted the girl.
And she stroked him all about and with her hands gently
wiped much foam from his mouth, and kissed the bull.
But he lowed gently; you would have said you heard
the sweet sound of a Mygdonian flute sounding.
He crouched before her feet, and looked at Europa
turning his neck, and showed her his broad back.
And she spoke among the deep-tressed maidens:

/Come, dear companions and age-mates, that upon this/
/bull we may sit and take our pleasure; for he will receive us all,/
/having spread his back beneath, so kindly/
/and gentle to look on and mild, and he is not at all like/
/other bulls; a mind like a man's/
/runs in him, a fitting mind, and he lacks only speech./
So speaking she sat smiling upon his back,
and the others were about to. Straightway the bull leapt up,
seizing her whom he wished; and swiftly he reached the sea.
And she, turning back, kept calling her dear companions,
stretching out her hands, but they could not catch her.
And mounting the beaches he ran forward like a dolphin,
walking with unwetted hooves upon the broad waves.
And then the sea grew calm as he came,
and sea-beasts sported around before the feet of Zeus,
and a dolphin, glad, tumbled over the swell from the deep;
and Nereids rose up from under the sea, and they all
went in a line seated on the backs of sea-beasts.
And the Earth-shaker himself, heavy-thundering under the sea,
guiding the wave, led the sea-path
for his own brother; and about him gathered
Tritons, deep-voiced pipers of the sea,
sounding a wedding song on their long conches.
And she, sitting upon the bull-back of Zeus,
with one hand held the bull's long horn, and with the other
drew up the purple fold of her robe, so that
the boundless water of the grey sea drawn after might not wet her.

And Europa's deep robe bellied at her shoulders,
like the sail of a ship, and it lifted the girl.
And when she was far from her father's land,
and neither a wave-beaten shore nor a steep mountain appeared,
but air above and the boundless sea below,
looking around her she lifted so great a voice:
/Where do you carry me, god-bull? Who have you become? And how do you/
/pass hard paths with rolling feet, and do not fear/
/the sea? For the sea is a course for/
/swift ships, but bulls tremble at the sea-path./
/What sweet drink is yours? What food will there be from the sea?/
/Surely you are some god; for you do things like the gods./
/Neither do sea-dolphins go in line on land, nor bulls/
/at all upon the sea, but you over land and over sea/
/dart unafraid, and your hooves are oars./
/Soon perhaps even lifted high above the grey air/
/you will fly like the swift birds./
/Woe is me, I am greatly ill-fated, who leaving/
/my father's house and following this bull/
/pursue a strange seafaring and wander alone./
/But you, Earth-shaker, lord of the grey sea,/
/may you meet me gracious, you whom I hope I see/
/guiding this way as a forerunner of mine./
/For not without a god do I pass these wet paths./
So she spoke; and the well-horned bull addressed her thus:
/Take heart, maiden, do not fear the sea-swell./
/I myself am Zeus, even if from near I seem to be/
/a bull; since I can appear as whatever I wish./

/And desire for you sent me to measure so great a sea/
/appearing as a bull; and Crete will receive you already,/
/which reared me also, where your nuptials/
/will be; and from me you will bear famous sons,/
/who will be sceptred kings among those on earth./
So he spoke; and what he spoke was fulfilled. Crete appeared,
and Zeus again took up his own form,
and loosed her girdle, and the Hours made ready her bed.
And she who was before a girl at once became the bride of Zeus,
and she bore children to the son of Cronus and at once became a mother.
