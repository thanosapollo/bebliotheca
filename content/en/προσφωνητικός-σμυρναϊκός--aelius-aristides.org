I would have wished, most admirable sir, above all that the city should have
appeared such as you left it; and if not, that I myself should have been
present to make the speeches, so that in either way I should have gained, both
by being with you

* 270
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

and by enjoying what is coming to pass in the city now rising for us. But
since it has so fallen out, I thought it not well to be shut out from the
whole, but that some trace at least of our voice should become known both to
you and to those of the Greeks who are present, both citizens and strangers.
For in general it has almost become a custom with me to hold festival in
absence, since the god leads this way. And the present speech is altogether
timely for me. For since, when the unexpected report arrived, I was the first
to mention the city to the emperors who are best in all things, after spending
not more than one night, it is reasonable that I should also regard what
belongs to praise as belonging to myself. Now the ancient things you remember,
having heard them at the first office which you held jointly with your father:
a certain birth of Zeus, and dances of the Curetes, and Tantalus and Pelops
founding the first city that arose on Sipylus, and Pelops' crossings from here
into the land formerly called Apia, but from him named the Peloponnese; from
whom a third descendant, becoming common king of Greece, overthrew the empire
of the barbarians. And of second praises: Theseus as founder, and Smyrna as
the name of this city, and Attic race, and later the entrance of the Ionians
as into their own, and a third founding and settlement ** Alexander, whose two
fairest and greatest memorials are this city and the one neighboring the Nile.
Such things you heard, and there was a trireme shown at the Dionysia and
hymned in the Kataploi, a token of an ancient victory which the Smyrnaeans won
while celebrating the Bacchic rites over the Chians, who were fenced with arms
and ships. But what the eye related was no longer myth, nor the words of
storytellers and poets; even Homer himself, if present, would have thought it
safer to be silent than to speak of them. You saw the marketplace, hearth of
the gods, and harbors, in one part encircling the city,

:END:

and in another held in the middle by the city; and beauties of temples and
precincts, adorned equally by nature and by art, and all the ornament of the
harbors, how they hung upon one another; and the greatest token of the city,
the agreement through all things and the music that made the whole body one,
as if, according to the myth told of the Theban wall, it had been fitted from
the beginning to lyre and cithara.

So be it. The ancient things, then, and those not ancient but appearing, so to
speak, yesterday and the day before, such you heard, and such you saw. But
nothing among men, they say, is unmoved; land receives sea, and sea becomes
mainland; and some cities have sunk beneath the earth, others have been
destroyed by streams from heaven and of every kind. For changes belong to the
mortal race, and many are the wonders of time. And indeed it befell our city
too, so greatly victorious, in a way to slip; and there it was clearly
revealed how great was the ornament that belonged to Asia herself, I mean not
that as far as the springs of the Maeander, nor as much as the allotment of
your governors bounds, but that which from the beginning the Greeks called
Asia, and named it continent distinctively among the three. But what I wished
to say: at the same time as the fall occurred, everything seemed stripped
bare, and everyone sought as it were the eye of a body. I mean that the blow
happened to the city according to the common fortune and nature of all men,
but what followed already belonged to the good fortune that had been allotted
to the city from the beginning. For when it was the moment, toward ** it
stretched out a hand, having such protectors of the whole, who of all men were
least likely to overlook her lying fallen, and of all most eager not only to
raise her up but even to lift her to excess. "All things now are being
fulfilled," says the child of Meles. And as for the nobility of the people
(let not everything for us be about the buildings), who would not have admired
and

marvelled? For it was neither struck out of its wits at the disaster, nor did
it seek consolation, nor wait for anything fairer than what came from the
emperors in deed and word together. So that the season of mourning had become
for it a kind of festival.

And as for what followed upon these things, we know it by hearing, but you,
seeing it, could yourself relate it even to others. For the city seems to have
fared not far from the tale told of its founder. You know that the poets say
Pelops, cut limb from limb and boiled in a cauldron, was put together again
from the beginning out of the cauldron, and that when the shoulder was missing
he had an ivory one in place of the old; and they say the deed was Demeter's.
So too for the city, after that cauldron which burned everything for it, the
second recomposition of the city-body has come to a wondrous abundance of
beauty, as if Poseidon had on purpose shaken it with his trident, that he
might see it fairer than before; such a kindness as he is said to have done
around Thessaly, making the cleft of Tempe for the Peneius. For formerly it
was not even possible to come to the thought of excess, but the city seemed a
pattern of beauty; but now we have been proven to have judged wrongly. For
then it had conquered the other cities, but now it has, so to speak, almost
conquered itself; and it seems to me to have suffered something like the
metropolis. I shall mention an image not mythical, but one that must be
believed. For that city, burned by the Persian and ruined in every way,
advanced everywhere; and for this city the disaster is a gain: it is being
raised by such a hand, more august and higher and more weather-tight in every
part. We invoke Adrasteia. And though all these ornaments that are seen are
fair, it has taken on another greater still. For its founders are those who
hold all the cities under themselves, whom no one has yet surpassed either in
humanity or in displays of works; and I think it was they alone of whom it was
possible to invent something better than

the Smyrna of that time.

Such, then, has been for us fortune's palinode concerning the city. And
Ionia's crown has been well saved, and Asia has recovered her pride. The
city's fortunes have become a pattern of the unexpected, that for those
running before a fair wind nothing is to be sworn impossible, and that when
the ship has been wrecked it is not beyond hope that something better will
come to pass. As for adorning the Meles, I leave that for now to others; and
yet it is itself, when seen, the greatest adornment to itself, for which
summer and winter have the same account, and it has never grown insolent from
rains nor yielded to droughts, but like some other of the unmoved things it
keeps one form and one color for all time. And the Meles is no wanderer, nor
such as to go away, but it is like some lover of the city who does not dare to
be gone too far, having, I think, an unquenchable love of her and an
unquenchable watch. So that starting from here it also ends here, stretching
itself along some limb of the city. These things, then, like those
nymph-possessed, I seem by some power of the Nymphs themselves to have sung an
additional strain about the Meles, not having so proposed. But perhaps it was
necessary that this too, like some other portion of the speech, be saved for
the children. And one might put this also among good tokens: that you have
come to us on a circuit of the cities, and that the circuit so falls out that
you renew your ancestral office exactly by your sojourns about Smyrna. For the
city's season is abundant; and may the things of good fortune prevail
everywhere.
