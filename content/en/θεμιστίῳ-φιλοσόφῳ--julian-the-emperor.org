* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I pray you earnestly to confirm the hopes, even as you write; but I fear I may
miss the mark, since the promise which you make on my behalf, both to all the
others and still more to yourself, is too great. And when I used long ago to
think that the contest was with Alexander and Marcus, and if any other has
been outstanding in virtue, a certain shudder came upon me and a wonderful
fear, lest I should seem to fall wholly short of the one in courage, and of
the other not even for a little attain to perfect virtue. Looking to these
things I was persuaded to praise leisure, and I myself remembered with
pleasure the Attic ways of life, and I thought it right to sing along with you
my friends, just as those who carry heavy loads lighten their hardship for
themselves in songs. But you now through the recent letter have made the fear
greater for me, and have shown the contest altogether harder, saying that I
have been stationed by God in that portion in which formerly Heracles and
Dionysus were, philosophizing and ruling at once, and cleansing almost all
land and sea of the prevailing wickedness. And you bid me, having shaken off
every thought of leisure and ease, to consider how we shall contend worthily
of the task; then upon these you mention the lawgivers, Solon, Pittacus,
Lycurgus, and you say that men now justly ought to expect from us things
greater than all of these.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

When I met with these words I was almost struck out of my wits. For I supposed
it was in no way lawful for you to flatter or to lie, and I was conscious in
myself that as regards nature nothing outstanding has belonged to me either
from the beginning or now, but that I have only been a lover of philosophy;
for I am silent about the fortunes in between, which have so far kept this
love of mine incomplete. I did not know, then, what one ought to conclude
about such words, until God brought it to mind that perhaps you wish to exhort
by the praises and to show the magnitude of the contests to which it is
altogether necessary that the man living in public life be exposed the whole
time.

But this is more the part of one turning a man away than of one urging him
toward the life. For just as if someone sailing the strait by you, and not
even this enduring easily or comfortably, should hear from someone professing
the mantic art that he must measure out the Aegean and the Ionian and touch
the outer sea, and the prophet should say, /Now indeed/ you see /walls and
harbors, but having come there you will see neither lookout nor rock, but you
will be glad even to catch sight of a ship from afar and to hail those sailing
in her, and having touched land late at last you will pray often to God, at
least at the very end of life, to meet with a haven and to hand over the ship
safe and to present those sailing unharmed by evils to their own, and to give
the body to mother earth, and whether this will be will perhaps be unclear to
you until that last day;/ do you think that one who has heard these words
would choose to dwell in a city near the sea, and not rather, having said
farewell to wealth and the goods that come from commerce, having despised many
acquaintances, foreign friendship, the inquiry of nations and cities, would
declare wise the son of Neocles, who bids one live unnoticed? And you too,
having perceived this, seem to forestall us with the abuse against Epicurus
and to take away such an opinion in advance. For you say somewhere that to
praise a leisure free of affairs and conversations in walks belongs to that
man. And I have long been strongly persuaded that these things did not seem
well to Epicurus; but whether it is right to urge every man whatsoever toward
public life, even the one less gifted by nature and the one not yet fully
able, one must perhaps be in the greatest doubt. For they say that Socrates
also led many who were not very well endowed away from the platform, and that
Glaucon of whom Xenophon speaks; and that he tried to hold back the son of
Cleinias, but was not able to prevail over the young man's impulse.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But shall we compel even those who are unwilling and who understand
themselves, bidding them take heart over such great deeds, of which not virtue
alone nor right choice is master, but far more fortune, prevailing everywhere
and forcing affairs to incline wherever she may wish? And Chrysippus seems in
other things to be wise and justly so reputed, but having ignored fortune and
chance and certain other such causes that fall upon practical affairs from
outside, he says things not very consistent with what time clearly teaches us
through countless examples. For where shall we call Cato fortunate and
blessed? And where Dion the Sicilian happy? To whom perhaps it mattered
nothing to die, but it mattered greatly not to leave incomplete the actions
upon which they set out from the beginning, and they would have chosen to
suffer all things for this. And having failed in those things, if they bore
fortune becomingly, as indeed it is said, they had no small consolation from
virtue, but they would not be called happy, having missed the noblest actions,
except perhaps through the Stoic insistence; against which one must say that
to be praised and to be counted blessed are not the same, and that if the
living creature by nature reaches after happiness, the end that is blessed
according to that is better than the one that is praiseworthy according to
virtue.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

And least of all does the security of happiness like to trust to fortune. And
those living in public life it is not possible without this to breathe, as the
saying is, if we truly consider whether they have also made even a general in
speech, just as those who compose the Ideas, whether also falsely, to be
established somewhere in the bodiless and intelligible things above all that
is of chance, or that man of Diogenes, /Cityless, homeless, deprived of
fatherland,/ having nothing in which he may fare well from her and, on the
contrary, in what he may fail; but this man whom custom is wont to call, and
Homer first, /To whom peoples have been entrusted and so many things are a
care,/ how could one, having led him outside of fortune, keep the position?
And again, he who subjects himself to her, of how much preparation will he
think he has need, and of how great prudence, so as to bear becomingly the
swings to either side, like a helmsman of the wind?

It is not wonderful to stand against her when she is only making war, but much
more wonderful to appear worthy of the goods that have come from her. By these
the greatest king was taken, the one who overthrew Asia, appearing harsher
than Darius and Xerxes and more of a braggart, when he had become master of
their empire; by these shafts the Persians were taken and utterly destroyed,
the Macedonians, the people of the Athenians, the Syracusans, the magistrates
of the Lacedaemonians, generals of the Romans, and after them countless
emperors. It would be a great length to enumerate all who have been destroyed
through wealth and victories and luxury; and as many as, overwhelmed by
misfortunes, have been seen slaves instead of free, humble instead of noble,
and very mean instead of those who were formerly solemn to all, why must I
now, as if transcribing from a tablet, catalog them? Would that the life of
men lacked examples of this kind. But it neither is nor ever would be in want
of such examples, so long as the race of men remains.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

And that I am not the only one who has thought fortune to prevail most in
practical affairs, I would now tell you the words of Plato from the wonderful
Laws, you knowing them and having taught them to me; but making a proof as it
were of my not being idle, I have copied out for you the speech, which runs
somehow thus. /God governs all things, and with God fortune and opportunity
steer all human affairs. But it is milder to grant that a third must follow
these, art./ Then describing of what sort the craftsman and creator of noble
actions and the divine king must be: /Kronos then, knowing, as we have gone
through, he says, that no human nature anywhere is sufficient, administering
human things as autocrat of all, not to be filled with insolence and
injustice, thinking these things then he set at that time kings and rulers
over our cities, not men, but beings of a more divine and better race,
daemons, as we now do for our flocks and as many herds as are tame; we do not
make oxen rulers of oxen for them, nor goats of goats, but we ourselves are
masters of them, a better race than they. The same then also God, being a
lover of men, set over us a better race, that of the daemons, which through
much ease for them and through much for us, caring for us, providing peace and
reverence and indeed abundance of justice, made the races of men without
faction and happy. And this account now also, using truth, says that of as
many cities as a god does not rule, but some mortal, there is for them no
relief from evils or toils; but he thinks we must imitate by every device the
life said to have been in the time of Kronos, and as much immortality as is in
us, obeying this, administer both privately and publicly the dwellings and the
cities, naming the distribution of mind law. But if one man or some oligarchy
or democracy, having a soul reaching after pleasures and desires and needing
to be filled with these, shall rule some city or private man, having trampled
the laws, there is no device of salvation./

I have purposely copied out this whole speech for you, lest you suppose me to
steal and to deal falsely, bringing forward ancient myths, perhaps resembling,
but not altogether truly composed. But what does the true account of them say?
You hear that, even if someone is a man by nature, he must be divine by choice
and a daemon, having simply cast out all that is mortal and beastlike of the
soul, except as much as must remain for the preservation of the body. If
anyone, thinking these things, fears being dragged into so great a life, does
he then seem to you to admire the Epicurean inaction and the gardens and the
suburb of Athens and the myrtles and the little room of Socrates? But there is
no place where I have been seen preferring these things to toils.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

I would most gladly have gone through my own toils for you, and the terrors
hung over me from friends and kinsmen when I began the education among you, if
you yourself did not know them very well. And the things done in Ionia toward
the one related by race and still more close to me by friendship, earlier, on
behalf of a foreign man who had become altogether slightly known to me, I mean
the sophist, have escaped you in nothing. And did I not undertake journeys for
the sake of friends? And indeed you know how I helped Carterius, coming to our
companion Araxius uninvited, to plead for him. And on behalf of the property
of the admirable Arete and of what she had suffered from her neighbors, did I
not come to Phrygia the second time in not even two whole months, my body
already being altogether weak through the illness that followed upon the
former hardship? And finally, before the arrival that came to us into Greece,
when I, running risks concerning the last things, as the many would say,
remained with the camp, recall now of what sort the letters I wrote to you
were, never full of lamentations, never having anything small or humble or too
ignoble. And going away to Greece again, when all thought me to be in exile,
did I not, as at the greatest festival, praising fortune, say that the
exchange was most pleasant to me, and that, as the saying is, /gold for
bronze, a hundred oxen's worth for nine/ had been exchanged? So I rejoiced,
having obtained Greece instead of my own hearth, possessing there no field, no
garden, no little room.

But perhaps I seem not to bear misfortunes ignobly, but toward the gifts from
fortune to be some ignoble and small man, I who love Athens more than the pomp
now around us, praising that leisure, I suppose, and blaming this life because
of the multitude of actions.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

But perhaps one ought to judge better about us, looking not to inaction and
action, but rather to Know thyself and to /Let each man practice whatever
craft he knows./

To me at least the ruling as king seems greater than according to a man, and a
king needs a more daemonic nature, as indeed Plato also used to say; and now I
will copy out a speech of Aristotle tending to the same, not bringing an owl
to the Athenians, but showing that I do not altogether neglect his words. And
the man says in the political writings: /But if someone should set it down as
best for cities to be ruled by a king, how will it be about the children? Must
the race also rule as kings? But if they become of whatever sort they chance,
it is harmful. But he will not hand it over, being master, to the children?
But this is no longer easy to believe; for it is hard and of a greater virtue
than according to human nature./ Then going through in order concerning the
so-called king according to law, that he is a servant and guardian of the
laws, and not even calling this man a king, nor thinking such a one a form of
constitution, he adds: /And concerning the so-called absolute kingship, and
this is that according to which the king rules all things according to his own
will, it seems to some that it is not even according to nature that one man be
master of all the citizens; for to those similar by nature the same justice
must necessarily belong./ Then after a little he says: /He therefore who bids
the mind rule seems to bid God and the laws rule; but he who bids a man adds
also beasts; for desire is such a thing, and spirit which warps even the best
men; wherefore mind without appetite is law./ You see, the philosopher here
seems clearly to distrust and to have condemned human nature. For he says this
in so many words, saying that no human nature is adequate to so great a
superiority of fortune; for he does not suppose it easy for one who is a man
to prefer the common advantage of the citizens to his children, and he says it
is not just to rule many who are similar, and putting the colophon as an end
upon the former words he says that mind without appetite is law, to which
alone one ought to entrust the constitutions, and to no one of men. For the
mind in them, even if they are good, is interwoven with spirit and desire, the
harshest beasts.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

These things seem to me to agree exceedingly with those of Plato, first that
the ruler must be better than those ruled, differing not in practice only but
also in nature; which is not easy to find among men; ... and third that by
every device according to power one must attend to laws, not those laid down
from the moment nor as they now seem to have been established by men who have
not lived altogether according to mind, but whoever, having more purified the
mind and the soul, looking not to the present injustices nor to the fortunes
standing by, lays down the laws, but having learned the nature of the
constitution and having seen what justice is by nature and of what sort
injustice is by nature, then transferring as many things as possible from
there to here and laying down laws common to the citizens, looking neither to
friendship nor to enmity nor to neighbor and kinsman; and it is better if, not
even for the men of his own time, but for those later or for foreigners,
having written, he should send out laws, having nothing and hoping to have no
private dealing with them. Since I hear that the wise Solon also, having taken
counsel with his friends about the cancelling of debts, furnished them a
starting-point of wealth, but himself a cause of shame, and this though he had
freed the people by the measure. So it is not easy to escape such banes, even
if one furnish his own mind passionless toward the constitution.

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Fearing these things I often reasonably praise the former life, and obeying
you especially I think these things, not only because you said the zeal was
set before me toward those men only, Solon and Lycurgus and Pittacus, but also
because you say I must pass from the covered philosophy to the open-air. Just
as if, to one exercising moderately at home with difficulty and barely for the
sake of his own health, you proclaimed: /Now you have come to Olympia and have
passed from the wrestling-school in the room to the stadium of Zeus, where you
will have as spectators the Greeks from everywhere and first at least your own
citizens, on whose behalf you must contend, and some also of the barbarians,
whom one must strike with amazement, having shown the fatherland more
formidable to them, at least as far as now comes to you,/ you would have
thrown him down at once and made him tremble before the contest; so consider
me also now to have been disposed by such words. And concerning these things,
whether I have now judged rightly, or am in part mistaken of what is fitting,
or even miss the whole, you will teach very soon.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

And concerning the things about which perplexity has come to me toward your
letter, O dear head and worthy of all honor from me at least, I wish to make
clear; for I desire somehow to learn more clearly about them. You said that
you praise the life in action beside the philosopher, and you called the wise
Aristotle as witness, setting happiness in acting well, and considering the
difference of the political life and of the life in contemplation, being in
some doubt about them, and preferring contemplation in other places, but here
praising the architects of noble actions. And these you yourself say are the
kings, but Aristotle has said it nowhere according to the wording added by
you, and from what you have copied one would rather understand the other. For
the /And we say they act most of all in the proper sense, even of the external
actions, who are architects with their thoughts/ must be thought to have been
said of the lawgivers and the political philosophers and simply all who act
with mind and speech, and not of the self-workers and laborers of political
actions; for whom it is not enough only to consider and understand and tell
others what must be done, but it belongs to them to handle and do each of the
things which the laws declare and which the occasions often compel besides,
unless indeed we call the architect, as Homer is accustomed to call Heracles
in the poetry /versed in great deeds,/ the most self-working of all.

But if we take this as true, or we even say that only in doing the common
things those who are masters and rule many are happy, what then shall we say
about Socrates? Pythagoras and Democritus and Anaxagoras of Clazomenae you
will perhaps say are happy through contemplation in another sense; but
Socrates, having declined contemplation and having loved the practical life,
was not even master of his own wife nor of his son; how then did it belong to
him to rule two or three citizens? Was he then not practical, since he was
master of no one? I for my part say that the son of Sophroniscus accomplished
greater things than Alexander, ascribing to him the wisdom of Plato, the
generalship of Xenophon, the courage of Antisthenes, the Eretrian philosophy,
the Megarian, Cebes, Simmias, Phaedo, countless others; and I do not yet
mention the colonies that have come to us from there, the Lyceum, the Stoa,
the Academies. Who then was saved through the victory of Alexander? What city
was better inhabited? What private man has become better than himself? For you
might find many richer, but no one wiser or more temperate than himself,
unless even more of a braggart and overweening. But as many as are now saved
from philosophy are saved through Socrates. And this not I alone, but
Aristotle earlier seems to have thought and said, that it belonged to him to
think no less of himself upon the theological writing than the one who took
down the power of the Persians. And this he seems to me to have thought
rightly; for to conquer belongs especially to courage and to fortune, and let
it be set down, if you wish, also to this ready prudence, but to take up true
opinions about God is not the work of perfect virtue only, but one might
reasonably be at a loss whether one must call such a man a man or a god. For
if what is said is right, that each thing is by nature known by those akin to
it, he who has come to know the divine essence would reasonably be thought
some divine one.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

But since we seem again, having started upon the contemplative life, to
compare the practical with this, though you also from the beginning declined
the comparison, I will mention those very men of whom you made mention,
Areius, Nicolaus, Thrasyllus, and Musonius. For not only was none of these
master of his own city, but Areius, as they say, even declined Egypt given to
him to govern, and Thrasyllus, having associated with Tiberius, a bitter and
by nature harsh tyrant, if he had not defended himself through the writings
left by him, showing who he was, would have incurred an indelible shame to the
end, so little did the public career benefit him; and Nicolaus has become a
self-worker of no great actions, but is known rather through the words about
them, and Musonius has become known from the things he suffered courageously
and, by Zeus, bore with self-control the cruelty of the tyrants, perhaps being
no less happy than those who governed the great kingdoms. And Areius, who
declined the governorship of Egypt, willingly deprived himself of the
strongest end if he thought this most sovereign. And are you yourself inactive
among us, neither commanding as general nor speaking to the people nor ruling
a nation or a city? But a man having mind would not say so. For it is possible
for you, having produced many philosophers, or if not, three or four, to
benefit the life of men more than many kings together. For the philosopher
stands over no small portion, nor, as you said, is he master only of counsel
about the common things, nor does the action turn back again for him into
speech, but confirming the words by deed and appearing such as he wishes the
others to be, he would be more persuasive and more effective toward action
than those who by command urge toward noble actions.

But one must go back to the beginning and conclude the letter, being perhaps
greater than what is needed. And the main point in it is that neither fleeing
toil nor hunting pleasure nor loving inaction and ease do I dislike the life
in the constitution; but, as I said from the beginning, being conscious in
myself of neither so great an education nor a superiority of nature, and
besides fearing lest I slander philosophy, which I loved and did not attain,
not otherwise well reputed among the men now, I both wrote those things long
ago and now have answered your censures as far as I am able.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

And may God grant the best fortune and a prudence worthy of the fortune, since
I now think I must be helped, the more part from the Better, and from you who
philosophize by every device, having been set in front of you and running the
first risks. But if some greater good than our preparation and than the
opinion I have of myself God should furnish to men through us, one must not be
angry at my words. For I, being conscious in myself of no good thing except
this only, that I do not even think I have the greatest things, having
nothing, as you yourself see, reasonably cry out and testify not to demand
great things from us, but to entrust the whole to God; for thus I would be
unaccountable for the shortcomings, and, all things having become favorable, I
would be well-disposed and moderate, not inscribing myself upon others' works,
but having referred all things to God, as indeed is just, I myself shall know
the gratitude and I urge you also to know it.
