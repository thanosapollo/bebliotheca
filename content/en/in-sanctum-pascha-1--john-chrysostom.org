* On Holy Pascha

A discourse of our father among the saints John Chrysostom, archbishop of
Constantinople, on the holy Pascha.
Even the mere sight of the feast conquers every weaving of encomia, and every
praise falls short of its worth. For however great a contest the speech may
take up, and however wholly it may stir itself toward encomia, it will be able
to say nothing according to worth. And just as one inexperienced of the sea,
when he looks upon a vast and gaping deep, is struck dead with the greatest
fear before the start, and at the mere sight is undone, and his face is
changed, to set foot and greet the back of the sea: so we too, beholding the
deep of the Godhead, do not dare even to touch the encomia, lest from
ignorance we utter things small and cheap. For it is better to honor in
silence than to insult by speaking. Silence, recognizing its own ignorance, is
granted pardon; but the rash undertaking, belching forth nothing worthy of the
matter at hand, is both unpardonable and has pilloried itself.
Yet since God, through the multitude of his compassions and his mercy toward
us, does not seek according to worth but accepts each one's purpose, let us
speak as much as we can. For the feast is a proof of the divine love of
mankind, and it cries aloud the intelligible goodness of Father and Son and
Holy Spirit; through which the Only-begotten saved the world that was
perishing and called back the man who had been banished; through which he
freed Adam who had been enslaved; through which he healed Eve's right hand,
stretched out wrongly against the tree; through which he opened the gates of
Hades, crushing the bars of transgression, trampling the unchanging cruelty of
demons, and striking the gatekeepers with terror. For when they saw the King
arriving at the prison of Hades they cowered mightily. Hades himself was
shaken with trembling, and he pressed the departed and forced them with haste.
All the myriads of those shut in were stirred, eager to look upon a newer
spectacle, and falling down they begged with one accord, setting forth the
devil's cruelty and his savagery, and with one voice they fell as ambassadors.
Hence the King saved the whole captivity and as Master sets free his own
creatures. Death himself was put to shame, unable to hold the one free among
the dead. For he swallowed the many, and being unable to digest the Savior he
was destroyed. He swallowed him as a man, but he vomited him not as a man, for
with the one he vomited all together. For he saw a body put on and leaped in;
but he took the trial of the power and, shuddering, leaped back. Through the
Jews he shut him in a tomb and thought he had conquered him, and he was
exalted and proclaimed; but when he saw the tomb opened and the divine
resurrection of the body that, as he thought, had died, and the unconquerable
power of the one who had been put to death, his strength was loosed, and the
wretch, fearing his own end, cried: Woe is me, it seems the one who undoes me
and sacks my kingdom has come.
Many came to me, and seeing none did I fear; many I put to death, and none
withstood me. Now I the mighty do not stand; I the lofty am brought low; I the
strong am loosed; I who boasted am dashed to the ground; I am held invisibly;
I cannot bear the power of the one present. I who was seen as fearful am
easily despised; I who was thought mighty am a laughingstock among all. Woe is
me, what shall I do? How was I deceived. He was born as a man, he was carried
in arms as a man, he was put to the breasts as a man, he hungered as a man, he
thirsted as a man, he was scourged as a man, he was spat upon as a man, he was
crucified as a man, he was mocked as a man, he was given vinegar to drink as a
man, he died as a man, he was buried as a man, he rose as God, not as a man.
How was he found to be God self-raised? He suffered many things from many, and
he repaid none of them; he was dishonored by slaves, and he harmed none of
them. The Jews cried out and said: "Is this not the carpenter's son?" and
again: "He has a demon and is mad; why do you listen to him?" and again: "You
are a Samaritan and have a demon"; and he repaid no one evil for evil. These
things deceived my folly; these things mocked me, the wretched one; through
these I was hooked; through these I was baited. He consented to endure all
things through the body; if ever he showed the greatness of the Godhead,
flying with swift feet I turned back invisibly. He often veiled himself with
sufferings, and I could not bear his uncontainable power; hence often, seeing
the proofs of the Godhead, I cried and said through the wicked demons that
follow me: "I beg you, do not torment me," and again: "What have we to do with
you, Son of God? Have you come before the time to torment us?"
But when I heard him crying: "My soul is very sorrowful, even unto death,"
and: "Now is my soul troubled," when I saw him in agony and crying to his own
Father: "Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me," I leaped
again, reckoning him to be a common man. But as it seems, he spoke thus on my
account, that he might bait me and, having deceived me, might redeem the man
who had been deceived by me. But I the wretch did not know. I ought, having
heard him say: "Do not fear those who kill the body but cannot kill the soul,"
to have searched out the truth from the very deeds. For how was he who advises
a man not to fear death himself constrained by fear of death? But having put
to death I was put to death; having crucified I was pilloried; having forged
nails I was pierced through; having plaited thorns I was wounded; having
brought gall I was bitterly put to death; having clothed him with a cloak I
was entangled.
Since then the day has been filled with such gladness, "this is the day which
the Lord has made; let us rejoice and be glad in it." This is the day which
the Lord has made; for he himself made the day and through his own
resurrection made it bright. For the Maker of the day also consented through
the flesh to the number of days and to be subject to months and hours and
years, he whom the blessed John the Evangelist proclaimed, saying: "In the
beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."
John, how did you lift up your mind, how did you raise your thought to a peak,
how did you pass beyond nature, how did you tread heaven, how did you pass the
ranks of angels and archangels and the very powers above and their choirs, how
did you draw near the holy throne itself, and how did you even run past that
and behold the one who is in the beginning, truly the Son of God, the Word?
Who taught you, John, this utterance?
How did you dare to speak the unspeakable? How did you name the Son Word? No
one else uttered these things. And surely John will say that it is not I who
speak, but the Holy Spirit who speaks in me. The Spirit prepared him not to
say, "In the beginning the Son was begotten," since those who think badly are
busybodies and they pry into the words and treat the phrases. And they were
going, having heard that "In the beginning the Son was begotten," to compose
the manner of the begetting, and how and when, and to suppose the begetting to
be passible. For this reason he also drives out the doubt of their soul and
scatters the passible suspicion, saying: "In the beginning was the Word." And
just as the word comes forth from the mind without passion, and neither
diminishes the one who begot it, and itself comes forth perfect, so also the
Son of God has been begotten perfect from the whole, neither diminishing the
Father, and himself having the perfection of the Father; to him be the glory
unto the ages of ages. Amen.
