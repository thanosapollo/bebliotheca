* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:
Hamilcar, son of Hannibal, surnamed Barca, a Carthaginian, in the first Punic
war, but in the last times, quite a young man began to be set over the army in
Sicily. When before his arrival the affairs of the Carthaginians were managed
badly both by sea and by land, he himself, when he was present, never yielded to
the enemy nor gave a place of harming, and often on the contrary, an occasion
having been given, he provoked and always departed superior. By which deed, when
the Poeni had lost almost all things in Sicily, he so defended Eryx that the war
did not seem to have been waged in that place. Meanwhile the Carthaginians,
overcome in a fleet at the Aegates islands by Gaius Lutatius, consul of the
Romans, decided to make an end of the war and permitted that thing to the
judgment of Hamilcar. He, although he burned with a desire of warring, yet
thought one must serve peace, because he understood that the fatherland,
exhausted by expenses, could not longer bear the calamities of war, but so that
he immediately agitated in mind, if the affairs were a little restored, to renew
the war and to pursue the Romans with arms, until either they had conquered by
virtue or, conquered, they had given hands. With this counsel he conciliated
peace, in which he was of such ferocity, when Catulus denied he would compose
the war unless he with his own, who held Eryx, having left arms, departed from
Sicily, that with the fatherland yielding he himself said he would rather perish
than return home with so great a shame: for it was not of his virtue to hand
over to adversaries the arms received from the fatherland against enemies.
Catulus yielded to this stubbornness.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
But he, as he came to Carthage, knew the republic holding itself much otherwise
than he had hoped. For by the long duration of an external evil so great an
internal war burned that Carthage was never in an equal danger except when it
was destroyed. First the mercenary soldiers, whom they had used against the
Romans, defected, whose number was of twenty thousand. They alienated all
Africa, they attacked Carthage itself. By which evils the Poeni were so
terrified that they even sought aids from the Romans; and they obtained them.
But at the last, when they had almost already come to desperation, they made
Hamilcar commander. He not only removed the enemies from the walls of Carthage,
when more than a hundred thousand of armed men had been made, but also compelled
them to that, that shut in by narrowness of places more perished by hunger than
by iron. All the alienated towns, among them Utica and Hippo, the strongest of
all Africa, he restored to the fatherland. Nor was he content with that, but he
also propagated the bounds of the empire, he gave back so much leisure to all
Africa that no war seemed to have been in it for many years.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
These things having been accomplished according to his mind, with a confident
spirit and hostile to the Romans, that he might more easily find a cause of
warring, he effected that he be sent as commander with an army into Spain, and
there he led with him his son Hannibal of nine years. There was besides with him
an illustrious, beautiful young man, Hasdrubal, whom some said was loved more
shamefully than was fitting by Hamilcar (for slanderers could not be wanting to
so great a man). By which it was done that Hasdrubal was forbidden by a prefect
of manners to be with him. To this man he gave his own daughter in marriage,
because by their manners a father-in-law could not be forbidden a son-in-law.
We have made mention of this for this reason, because Hamilcar having been
killed that man was set over the army and did great things and as a first man by
largess overturned the old manners of the Carthaginians and after the death of
the same Hannibal received command from the army.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
But Hamilcar, after he crossed the sea and came into Spain, did great things
with a following fortune: he subjected the greatest and most warlike nations, he
enriched all Africa with horses, arms, men, money. This man, when he was
meditating to bring war into Italy, in the ninth year after he had come into
Spain, was killed fighting in battle against the Vettones. His perpetual hatred
toward the Romans seems most to have stirred the second Punic war. For Hannibal,
his son, was so led by the assiduous adjurations of his father that he preferred
to perish than not to try the Romans.
