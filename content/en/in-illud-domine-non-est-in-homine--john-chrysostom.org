* On that saying, O Lord, the way of man is not in himself

On the saying of the prophet Jeremiah, "O Lord, the way of man is not his, nor
shall a man walk and direct his going."

=56.153=

αʹ. Of this sensible and public road some parts are level and smooth, and some
uphill and rougher; so also of the divine Scriptures some things are at once
clear to all, and some need more working-out and toil. But when we travel a
level and smooth road, we do not need much exactness; but when it is sloping
and narrow and stretching up to the very ridge, and hemmed in by precipices on
either side, we need a sober and wakeful soul, since the difficulty of the
ground does not allow us to be slack.

For if a man looks aside even a little, the foot alone having slipped, the
whole body is hurled down the cliff; and if he stoops down into ravines,
seized with dizziness he is carried down. So too in the divine Scriptures,
some thoughts are easy and simple, and one can travel them without toil; but
the rougher and steeper it is not equally easy to cross. Therefore all must be
sober and watchful when we pass through such places, so that the danger may
not be for us a matter of the last things. For this reason we too sometimes
train you in the easier things, and sometimes lead you to the harder, that we
may both soothe your toil and drive out slackness.

For just as those who enjoy ease continually become more relaxed, so those who
are always dragged toward rougher things despair of the toil =56.154=. The
form of teaching, then, must be mixed, and now this, now that must be taken in
hand, so that our mind may neither be slackened beyond measure, nor, strained
beyond what is due, be torn apart again, giving up before the toil.

For this reason, having lately discoursed to you about Paul and Peter, and the
dispute that arose between them at Antioch, and having shown you that their
seeming battle was more useful than all peace, and having led you onto that
sloping and rough road, when we saw you wearied, on the day after that we
guided you toward another easier subject, recounting the praises of the
blessed Eustathius, and after him going through the praises of the noble
martyr Romanus, when indeed our theater became more brilliant, and the
applause greater, and the clamor larger. For just as one who has grown weary,
then entering a meadow, is gladdened and expands, seeing nothing rough nor
toilsome, but rest and delight and much refreshment of soul; so you too were
disposed then toward me; for from the toils and difficulties of that hearing,
as if entering a meadow of the martyrs' praises, you were filled with that
delight with much freedom from fear.

For there were no grips and counter-grips then, nor wrestlings and escapes,
but with no one opposing, the discourse contended by it =56.155= self, being
loosed and free. For this reason it was also more brilliant and more festal,
and enjoyed more praises.

For the mind of the hearers, when it follows what is said with ease, enjoying
much rest, is more eagerly stirred toward praise of the speaker. Since then we
have sufficiently restored you in these days, having moved nothing toilsome
and rough toward your love, come then, let us lead you again today to the
former exercise, guiding you toward the more laborious parts of


the Scriptures, which need much understanding, not that we may crush you with
the labor, but that we may train you to be able to pass even these places with
safety. Just as then at the beginning there seemed to be some battle and
contention of the apostles, but when we had climbed those crags, we saw above
at last the fruit of the Spirit, love, joy, peace, and the toil was neither
vain nor superfluous for us, but ended in gladness; so also today I expect, by
your prayers, if with endurance and much patience we finish the road set
before us, and are able to climb to the very summit, we shall find all things
there level and smooth and even. What then is the matter now set before us?
That which was read today from the prophet: O Lord, the way is not in the man,
nor shall a man walk and direct his going.

This is the question; and you, as you then furnished the zeal, now also strain
yourselves; for this question is not less than that, but needs even more care.
Why? Because the seeming difference of Paul and Peter, which was not a
difference, was not known to many, whence it was not likely that much harm
would come from ignorance; but this saying is carried about everywhere, in
houses, in marketplaces, in country places, in cities, on land and sea and
islands; and wherever you go, you will hear many saying these things: It is
written, The way of man is not in the man. And they have not practiced this
saying only, but they string together with it others of the same kind, that It
is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth; and after that again,
Except the Lord build the house, they labor in vain that build it. And they do
this, putting forward the divine Scriptures as screens for their own
slackness, and trying through these words to undermine our salvation and our
hope. For they wish to establish nothing else by these things, but that we are
masters of nothing. All our affairs are gone; vain is the promise of the
kingdom, vain the threat of Gehenna, vain laws and penalties and punishments
and counsels.

βʹ. For what would one advise a man who is master of nothing? What would one
promise to one stripped of all authority? Neither is the man who has succeeded
worthy of praise, nor is the one who has failed liable to punishment and
penalty, if the things to be done are not in our power. And if men were
persuaded of this, no one thereafter will either hold to virtue or flee vice.
For if now, when every day we sound in your ears about Gehenna, dis =56.156=
coursing about the kingdom, and setting forth the unbearable punishments and
the prizes that surpass human understanding, counseling, exhorting, moving
every word, scarcely some endure the sweats for virtue, scarcely they stand
off from the pleasure in sins; if you also cut away this sacred anchor, will
not the whole ship be sunk, and will not all be gone, becoming wholly
submerged, and will there not be many wrecks each day? For nothing, nothing
has the devil been so eager for, as to persuade the human soul of this, that
it is neither liable to punishment in its sins, nor worthy of praises and
crowns in its successes, so that he may both loosen the hands of the earnest,
and quench their eagerness, and stretch the carelessness of those who have
fallen back and increase their slackness.

Therefore one must attend with exactness to what is said. There is a precipice
and a pit on either side, if we do not read the saying soberly. For what shall
we say? that the prophet lied? But that is dangerous; for a prophet does not
lie; for he utters the words of God. But the prophet did not lie? Then the
things to be done are not in our power? But both


the things to be done are in our power, and the prophet did not lie; and we
shall establish both of these, if you attend. For this reason I showed the
precipice on either side, so that being sober we may finish the road set
before us. Let us not examine this only, The way of man is not in the man; but
also the whole sequence, and concerning whom it was said, and by whom, and to
whom, and why, and when, and how. For it is not enough to say that it is
written in the Scriptures, nor, simply tearing away words and rending the
members of the body of the inspired Scriptures, taking them bare and stripped
of their own connection, to abuse them at will and without fear. For so many
corrupted doctrines have been brought into our life, the devil persuading the
more slack to report what lies in the Scriptures in a twisted way, either by
adding or by taking away, to darken the truth.

It is not enough, then, to say that it is written in Scripture, but one must
also read the whole sequence; since if we are going to cut apart their
continuity and kinship with one another, many wicked doctrines will thus be
born. For it is written in Scripture that There is no God, and that He has
turned away his face, that he may not look to the end, and that God will not
require it. What then? tell me: is there no God? Does he not look upon the
affairs on earth? And who would endure to say this, or to hear it? And yet it
is written in Scripture; but learn how it is written. The fool said in his
heart, There is no God. The judgment and the vote are not of Scripture, but of
the fool's mind; Scripture did not speak its own things, but reported
another's. And again: How long shall the ungodly provoke God? For he said in
his heart, He will not require it. He has turned away his face, that he may
not look to the end. =56.157= And here again it sets out the judgment and vote
of an ungodly and corrupted man.

So also it is the custom with physicians, when speaking with the healthy, to
tell the errors of those who rave and are out of their minds, that they may
make these more secure. Since then piety is health of soul, and the last
disease and weakness is not to know God, it speaks the words of the ungodly,
not that we may hear them simply, but that we may be on our guard. It says
what the fool said, that you may become prudent, and not receive the saying;
it says what the ungodly said, that you may flee impiety. And one must not
only not cut them out of the sequence, but also bring them forward sound, and
add nothing. Many, then, also carry about certain other things from the
Scriptures, reporting them in a twisted way. For it is written, he says, If
you burn, marry; but nowhere is it written thus; but consider how it was said:
But I say to the unmarried and to the widows, it is good for them if they
remain as I also am; but if they do not contain themselves, let them marry;
for it is better to marry than to burn. Is it not then the same, he says, If
you burn, marry? First of all, even if "If you burn, marry" were the
same, they ought not, leaving the sound report of Scripture, to twist it,
and bring forward the thoughts of Scripture in their own words; but now we
shall find even a great interval. For if you simply say, If you burn,
marry, you have given authority to all who have chosen virginity, whenever
they are troubled
by desire, to dissolve the covenants with God, and desert to marriage, and
forget the former promise.

γʹ. But if you learn to whom Paul is speaking, that it is not to all simply,
but to those not yet bound in promises, you will be able to take away this
harmful and deadly authority. For I say, he says, to the unmarried and to the
widows, not to those who have promised widowhood, but to those who have not
yet voted either for this or for that, but are in a kind of middle ground of
either judgment. For example: someone has lost a husband, he says; she has not
yet judged within herself, nor confirmed, whether she ought to choose
widowhood, or to bring in a second


bridegroom; to this woman I advise, he says, that it is good so to be; but if
she cannot bear the burden, let her marry; but those who have already taken it
on and enrolled themselves for the endurance of widowhood, and have set
covenants with God, he no longer says are mistresses of returning to a second
marriage. For this reason, writing to Timothy about them, he speaks somehow
thus: But refuse younger widows. For when they have begun to wax wanton
against Christ, they will marry, having condemnation, because they have cast
off their first faith. Do you see how he punishes and chastises them here, and
says they are liable to judgment and a sentence, because they cast off the
covenants with God, and belied the promise? So that from this it is clear that
that saying was not spoken to those who have promised.

For this reason one must not bring it forward simply, but know also the
persons to whom the Scriptures speak. =56.158= Again they carry about another
saying, not twisting its composition, but adding something else not written in
it. For such is the devil's villainy, either by excess, or by subtraction, or
by twisting, or by changing what is written, to introduce deadly doctrines.
And what is this saying? Mine, he says, is the silver, and mine the gold; and
to whomsoever I will, I will give them. Of this, one part has been said, and
the other has not been said, but has been thrown in from somewhere outside.
For Mine is the silver, and mine the gold, the prophet said; but To whomsoever
I will, I give them, is no longer added, but is carried about from the
ignorance of the many. And what harm comes even from this?

Many filthy and sorcerous and unbridled men, not even worthy to see this sun,
nor to live, nor to breathe, enjoy much abundance, overturning everything,
seizing the houses of widows, outraging orphans, lifting themselves up against
the weaker. The devil, then, wishing to persuade men that all wealth is from
above and from the gift of God, so that from this he may smear much blasphemy
upon the Master, taking a saying from Scripture that says, Mine is the silver,
and mine the gold, has added another that is not in Scripture, saying: And to
whomsoever I will, I will give them. But the prophet Haggai does not speak
thus; but when the Jews had returned from the barbarian land, and were about
to raise the temple and bring it to its former beauty, since they were at a
loss, enemies standing round, much poverty being present, no abundance
appearing from anywhere, wishing to lead them to good hopes and to persuade
them to be confident about the end, he says in the person of God: Mine is the
silver, and mine the gold, and the last glory of this house shall be greater
than the first. And what is this to the matter at hand? That one must not
simply bring forward the sayings of the Scriptures, nor cutting them from the
sequence, nor tearing them from their kinship, nor taking the words bare and
stripped of the help of what follows or what went before, simply to slander
and abuse them. For how is it not absurd, that in a court, when men are
judging about worldly affairs, they set forth all the justifications, and
bring forward places and times and causes and persons and countless other
things, but when contests for eternal life are set before us, we bring forward
the things from the Scriptures simply and at random? For no one will read a
royal law simply and at random, but if he does not name the time, and show the
one who enacted it, and furnish it sound and complete, he is punished and pays
the last penalty; and shall we, reading not a law of men, but that brought
down from above from the heavens, use such slackness as to tear away members
and parts? And where are these things worthy of defense and pardon? Perhaps I
have stretched the discourse beyond measure, but not simply, but that I may
lead you away from the wicked custom.


Let us not then grow weary, until we find the end; for =56.159= this we
were made, not that we may eat and drink and be clothed, but that we may flee
vice, and choose virtue, laying hold of the divine philosophy. For that we
were not made that we may eat and drink, but for other things far greater and
better, hear God also stating the cause for which he made man. For forming
him, he speaks somehow thus: Let us make man according to our image and
according to likeness. δʹ. And we become like God not by eating and drinking
and being clothed; for God neither eats, nor drinks, nor is clothed; but by
practicing righteousness, showing loving-kindness, being kind and gentle,
having mercy on our neighbors, pursuing every virtue; since eating and
drinking is common to us with the nature of the irrational creatures, and in
this we are no better than they. But from where is our superiority? From
having been made according to the image of God and likeness. Let us not then
grow weary in moving discourses for virtue, but having brought this prophetic
saying into the midst, let us search it out with exactness, and learn who the
speaker was, and concerning whom, and when, and to whom, and how the affairs
stood, examining simply everything that contributes to the finding.

The speaker, then, is Jeremiah the prophet, not indeed making the supplication
for himself, but for others, for the Jews, the ungrateful, the insensible,
those incurably disposed, who ought to be punished and to undergo the last
penalty, concerning whom God says to him: Do not pray for this people, for I
will not hear you; and some say this is spoken concerning Nebuchadnezzar; for
since the barbarian was about to march against them, and to destroy the city,
and taking captives to go away, wishing to persuade all that it is not by his
own strength and power that he prevails over the city, but by their sins, God
commanding the war and guiding him against his own city, he says: I know, O
Lord, that the way of man is not his, nor shall a man walk and direct his
going. And what he means is this: this way, he says, which the barbarian now
walks, marching against us, is not from himself, nor did that man accomplish
this war and the victory, but if you had not delivered us into his hands, he
would not have prevailed, nor have overcome. For this reason, he says, I beg
and entreat, since this has seemed good, that the punishment come with
measure. Chasten us, yet in judgment, and not in wrath. But since some
contradict this, and say it was not spoken concerning the barbarian, but
concerning the common nature of men, it is necessary to fight these also. What
then is there to say to these? that he was making request for men who had
sinned, for whom he had often been forbidden to make request; for this reason
he first prepares the city to lament; for since he kept saying, Do not make
request for them, he first brings down her who needs the loving-kindness, that
from there he may take some starting-point and reasonable pretext for the
supplication to God on their behalf, and he stretches the discourse toward
her, and says: Woe to your fracture; painful is =56.160= your wound. Then she
says: Is this truly my wound? my tent has perished, my skins have been torn
apart, my sons and my sheep have gone out from me, and they are not. My
shepherds have played the fool, and have not sought the Lord. A voice of a
report comes, and of a great shaking from the north, to set the cities of
Judah for destruction and a bed of sparrows.

Then, when she had sung her own disaster as a tragedy, he says, O Lord, the
way of man is not his. What then? since he laments, he says, has he introduced
a deadly doctrine into the world, taking away our authority, and saying that
the things to be done are not in our


power? By no means, but even while lamenting he makes it secure. For having
said that The way of man is not his, he did not keep silent, but added, Nor
shall a man walk and direct his going. And what he means is this: the whole is
not in our power, but some is in our power, and some in God's. For to choose
the best things, and to will, and to be eager, and to undergo every toil,
belongs to our purpose; but to bring them to an end, and not to allow them to
fall through, and to come to the very finish of the successes, belongs to the
grace from above. For God has divided virtue with us, and neither left the
whole to be in our power, lest we be lifted up into madness, nor took the
whole himself, lest we incline into slackness; but having left a little upon
our toils, he himself accomplishes the greater part. For that if the whole
were in our power, this would have lifted many into madness and thrown them
over, let us hear the Pharisee, what he says, into how great boastfulness he
was lifted, how he talked big, and thought himself greater than the whole
world. For this reason he did not make the whole ours, but left something to
be in our power, that he might have a fair pretext for crowning us justly.

And this he made clear through that parable, according to which he says that
finding men about the eleventh hour, he sent them into the vineyard to work.
And yet what did they work in the eleventh hour? But still even the brief turn
of the time was enough for God to give them the wage complete. And that you
may learn that this is truly what the prophet says, and he does not take away
our authority, but here philosophizes about the end of affairs, hear the
addition. For having said, The way is not in man, he immediately adds, Chasten
us, O Lord, yet in judgment, and not in wrath. For if nothing at all were in
our power, he would have said superfluously, Chasten us, yet in judgment. εʹ.
For what would be more unjust than to punish those who are not masters of the
things to be done, and for men to undergo penalty, whose way and life does not
lie in their own authority? So that when he appears entreating God that the
punishment against them may not become more violent, he indicates nothing else
than this, that they are worthy to be punished and to undergo penalty; and
this is nothing else than establishing free will.

For if they were not mas =56.161= ters of the things to be done, he ought not
to ask that they undergo a milder punishment, but that they not be punished at
all; or rather there was not even need of a request; for God does not need one
entreating him, so as not to punish the guiltless. And why do I say God, when
not even a man of sense would? When then the prophet appears entreating for
the Jews, it is quite clear that he entreats as for those who have sinned; and
sin appears then, when we, being masters of not transgressing the law,
transgress it. So that from every side it is clear to us that our successes
lie both in our power and in God's. Such also is, It is not of him that
willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that hath mercy. And for whose
sake do I run? he says; and for whose sake do I will, if the whole does not
lie in me? That through the willing and the running you may draw God's
inclination and goodwill, so that he may work with you and stretch out a hand
and bring you to the end. For if you take this away, and cease running and
willing, neither will God stretch out a hand, but he too will stand off. From
where is this clear? Hear what he says to Jerusalem. How often would I have
gathered your children, and you would not? Behold, your house is left
desolate. Do you see, since they would not, how God also stood off? For this
reason we need both the willing and the running, that we may also draw God.
This then the prophet also says, that to set it aright is not in us, but in
the help of God; but to choose is in us and in our purpose. Then if to set it
aright is in the help of God, he says, even if I do not set it aright, I would
not justly undergo any charge; for when I furnish all the things from myself


and will, and choose, and take hold of the affairs, but he who is master of
the end does not work with me, nor stretch out a hand, I am freed from every
accusation. But these things are not so, they are not; for it is impossible,
when we have willed and chosen and purposed, that God should abandon us.

For if he exhorts and advises those who do not will, that they may will and
purpose, much more does he not abandon those who choose. For look, he says,
upon the ancient generations, and see. Who hoped in the Lord, and was put to
shame? or who continued in his commandments, and he overlooked him? And again
Paul says, And hope does not put to shame, the hope that is toward God. For it
is impossible that the one who hopes in God with his whole mind, and brings in
all the things from himself, should fall from the end. And again, But God is
faithful, who will not allow you to be tempted beyond what you are a =56.162=
ble, but will make with the temptation also the way out, that you may be able
to bear it. For this reason a certain wise man exhorts, saying: My son, if you
come to serve the Lord, prepare your soul for temptation. Make straight your
heart, and endure, and do not hasten in time of bringing-on. Cleave to him,
and do not stand off. And another word exhorts: But he that endureth to the
end, the same shall be saved.

And all these are canons and bounds and unmoved doctrines; and this must be
fixed in our soul, that it is impossible that anyone using eagerness and
caring for his own salvation, and showing all the things from himself, should
ever be abandoned by God. Do you not hear what he says to Peter? Simon, Simon,
how often Satan has asked to sift you as wheat, and I have prayed for you,
that your faith may not fail. For when he sees the burden greater than our
power, he stretches out a hand, and lightens the temptation; but when he sees
us, from our own slackness and carelessness, betraying our own salvation, and
not willing to be saved, he lets go and abandons. For he does not force, nor
compel, and what he did in the teaching, this also happens here. For just as
there he did not drag nor force those who did not wish to hear but leapt
away, but to those who attended he solved the unclear things, and made the
riddles plain; so also in the affairs: those who are without feeling and do
not wish he does not compel, nor force, but those who choose he draws with
much vehemence. For this reason Peter also says: Of a truth I perceive that in
every nation he that feareth God, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with
him.

And the prophet exhorts this very thing, saying: If you are willing, and
listen to me, you shall eat the good of the land; but if you are not willing,
nor listen to me, a sword shall devour you. Knowing these things, then, and
that both the willing and the running are in us, and that through the willing
and the running we draw God to our help, and having drawn him, we shall come
to the end of the affairs; let us rise up, beloved, and show all eagerness in
the salvation of our soul, that having toiled a little time here, throughout
the unaging and immortal age we may enjoy the immortal goods; which may it be
that we all obtain, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ,
with whom to the Father be glory, together with the Holy Spirit, now and
always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
