It was fitting, men of Athens, that those who spoke on behalf of the
Lacedaemonians should need many words, for their case was not, it seems,
simple like that of the Thebans, but it wanted some cleverer orator, as it
appears to me. Now I am, men of Athens, one of those who did not judge the
Thebans to be friends any earlier than you all did; yet I see that for the
Lacedaemonians the claim upon our aid lies in this, that there is no need to
avenge ourselves upon them, while for the Thebans it lies both in this very
thing and in the fact that we must remember the good we have received from
them. So that if one must cease hating, we ceased hating the Thebans long
ago; and if for the Lacedaemonians it is a strong argument that they are no
longer able to do you harm, far stronger for the Thebans is it that they
have long since shown themselves wishing to do you good. The former, then,
are advising you to consider them enemies no longer, while the Thebans I see
were our friends even before they called us. And if laying aside enmity has
such power that you become allies at once, where shall we set the fact that
the others possessed before what these men now strive for? Nor do I reckon
up that you owe gratitude, men of Athens, to the Thebans and the Argives,
for I think the Thebans owe you gratitude far more; but I say that these
things follow from and befit the services you have rendered one another. I
marvel if anyone fears that the Thebans should be our friends and allies
because he thinks them strong, yet judges it expedient that they become
enemies and foes

and that he fears the Lacedaemonians when they are warring upon them, while
he contrives how they shall come against you; and that he bids you take
precautions lest they ever hereafter make war upon you, yet urges that they
be brought into that state at once; and that he bids the Lacedaemonians take
courage as partners against the Thebans, whom he says they outnumber, but
does not allow the Thebans to take courage against these men, as though
alliances were of help chiefly to the weakest, and not to men of the sort
the Thebans are said to be by these very speakers. And when they ask who is
the guarantor that the Thebans will keep within their own bounds, as though
we could not ask in return who is the guarantor that the Lacedaemonians will
keep within their own, if they survive this war.

Yet the future is uncertain, and it is the future they make great use of
against the Thebans; but if we must let go of ready slander and
contentiousness and look at the plain truth, there is far more
trustworthiness in the Thebans than in the Lacedaemonians, and for this I
will offer you as witnesses the very men who dispute it. For if one asked
them: tell me, did the Lacedaemonians act rightly in seizing the Cadmea,
seeing that they now maintain the posture they assumed but lately, the
counterfeit one? They will say, to be sure: not rightly. And why? If one
asked them in turn, they will say: they violated the sworn peace. What else
could one say? Those things, then, which they would not be able to deny on
their own behalf, namely that they have done no wrong: this, I say, is the
testimony they are furnishing. For you know, of course, that in peace and
under truces above all men who wish to act justly are judged; for in wars
character is not in command, but each takes whatever fortune grants and
occasion offers. When, therefore, both were masters of their own judgment,
the Thebans showed themselves thinking they must mind their own affairs,
while the Lacedaemonians were advancing and grasping for more, how must we
not believe the Thebans trustworthy in every way, and transfer this fear to
the Lacedaemonians, lest at some time, finding themselves rich in power,
they do the same to us? For neither should I be persuaded by that other
argument either, that they marched through Boeotia and fell upon the Thebans
then, using their opportunity, but that if they had had the same against
you, they would have held their hand

of their own accord, you who have fought beside the Thebans against them.
Just as, then, if this had truly happened to you, you would rightly have
thought you must guard against them for the future, so, since it is plain it
would have happened if there had been opportunity, you must guard against
them no less. For you share, of course, in the things for which they hold
the Thebans in remembrance, and besides there attaches to them your own
ancient quarrel with them; so that however bitterly they feel toward the
Thebans, you must think what they now conceal out of need is twice as great.

Further, the Thebans, they know, have never tasted empire, nor at the hands
of willing Greeks; but you held it many years, winning it at first by
goodwill and holding the greater part of it afterward by power; and they say
it has always been ancestral to them to lead the Greeks, and they did lead
in both respects in the war against the Persian, and again when they broke
up our empire. These things, then, are not full of every security, nor of
such ease as one might suppose. The Thebans, on the other hand, seem to me
to be themselves our counselors that one must wrest from us nothing of what
is ours, nor set foot on the sea, which belongs rather to all men than to
those others. For the things they have never known themselves to possess
they will not even demand to have; but marching out against the
Lacedaemonians and casting down their power, they will not demand that
anyone resent it on their behalf. If, then, it appears from this that the
Thebans are ill-disposed toward the Lacedaemonians, not toward you, and the
Lacedaemonians toward you far more than toward the Thebans, how are the
Thebans not preferable on both counts? For this very thing, that they hate
your enemies, how is it not very much in your favor, to say nothing of their
having no outstanding grievance against you?

There is, moreover, a certain testimony belonging to the embassy itself. For
now it is calamity and circumstance that have driven the Lacedaemonians
here, and that is no token at all of good faith or of goodwill toward you;
but the Thebans have come as victors and invite you to share in what they
have succeeded in

and what greater token could there be of their good faith toward you? Do
they not show themselves safer and more guileless than all others? And if we
must use an excess of generosity against shameless slander, let us leave the
future alone; for in the whole of time many things might come to pass which
it is impossible to foresee and not possible to trust beforehand; but from
what has already happened, and about which there is no dispute at all,
choosing the Thebans we shall make it plain that we choose justice: first,
in return for the benefits they have rendered you; second, in return for our
having welcomed them before on account of these same things; and third, in
return for the boundless and unjust treatment which the Lacedaemonians have
meted out both to us and to them. But if it will not be possible to offer
strong proof about the future, and those who love the Lacedaemonians to such
a degree that it would have been worth deducting so much even from their
hatred of the Thebans command us to keep silence about the past, from which
justice is judged, what will be left us except to point out our aid by a
nod? For nothing, surely, will be permitted to be said. And who could endure
such unreasonableness?

It is worth examining the cleverness of the Lacedaemonians and the manner
which they always employ in their affairs. These men, having waged two wars
against us, the former Decelean War and this later Corinthian War, how did
they use both? In the former they called in the King as ally, by whose help
they chiefly thought they would overthrow us, and in the latter, through
this same ally they brought their enterprise to its end, sending Antalcidas,
who handed the Greeks over to him as the hire of the peace, and they imposed
this ending upon both. Then shall nothing hinder the Lacedaemonians from
using whatever means they think will give them the advantage, while we, if
fortune has made any of those who belong to us strong, shall abandon them?
And yet the thing, Athenians joining the Thebans against the Lacedaemonians,
is not so great as the Lacedaemonians joining themselves to the Persian and
selling out the Greeks. Or is it a like thing to give up the Ionians and the
Aeolians and the Dorians in order to accomplish something for themselves,
and to betray no man among mankind to their

neighbors and their allies, but to keep what is just? Or do we fear lest we
get some strong ally? And what could be more disgraceful than that one
should hear that the Athenians wish none of their own allies to prosper? And
yet if we shall shun those who have power, what kind shall we pray to have
for you? We once received the Corcyraeans though they were without compact,
judging it profitable, no doubt, to secure them in advance, since they were
strong; and now I know well that if the Thebans had nothing in common with
the city, but were merely strong and seen to be so, everyone would declare
that he set the highest value on attaching these men to us and bringing them
to us and making them ours through ourselves. As it is, then, they come of
their own accord, and they are persuaded that they are joined to the city
and will not fall away, if one receives them. And yet some have before now
thrown out a garrison once installed and have crept under and run in by
force; and all things, so to speak, that are done under compulsion are
easily undone when the occasion serves; but willing goodwill, so long as it
obtains what is just, is most enduring.

I consider, too, this point: if the Lacedaemonians had not made that famous
peace, would we not have continued at war with them to this day, we and the
Thebans, standing together as we stood? That is quite plain. If, then,
without any further offense they would have used us as enemies, how shall
they not justly be used the same way now, when they have committed this very
offense besides, that of making such a peace? And if indeed they have not
only made it, but have also transgressed it, how is aid not all the more to
be brought against them? For that the Thebans' power was not what it now is
does not dissolve the accusation against them; for they did not march
against them in order to make them strong, nor in order that they might
suffer at their hands what they now suffer; for they would have been mad,
had they been aiming at this; but these things, I suppose, came about by
fortune, and the resolve of those men was something else; and the order of
justice, whether the man plotted against be weak or strong, has the same
account. You would learn this best if you considered it so: if they had
marched not against the Thebans but against you, and had

seized the acropolis, what would you have claimed as your due? That these
men grieve with us, or rejoice, or think what? For what you would then have
justly demanded of these men, you ought now to render to them, since they
fell victim to the greed of those men before you did. Now I wish to learn
from those who wail over the Lacedaemonians whether they are eager for their
safety or for their empire. If for their empire, I for my part did not think
we ought to care how the Lacedaemonians may come to rule; but if for their
safety, why are they in haste? For it is possible both for the
Lacedaemonians to be beaten in war and for Sparta to exist, and that is what
is honorable in their eyes; unless they mean that unless they rule the
Greeks and are able to do whatever they wish, they do not even look for
safety, but choose rather to perish. If that is how they stand, they are not
very humble; for I think the Thebans too would very gladly obey you before
they do, if, the war once accomplished, you wish to save their city. For
when they see that this very thing has come to pass for them through you,
that the Lacedaemonians are in their power to treat as they wish, how will
they not choose whatever you command? And what repayment will they say they
owe you for the alliance and the deeds, if they do not repay it through
their vote?

And what could the Lacedaemonians accomplish by being destroyed first? And
how would that come about? If, by Zeus, they all drew up in battle. But most
of all this would never come about; for surely they would not lead out their
children and their wives with them. Then I in turn shall ask: what if the
Thebans were to stumble with all their people, while we stood with the
Lacedaemonians? Shall we not have overstepped the promise of our aid? Shall
we then defend them? And how would that be endured? Or shall we again be
with the other side? And what limit will there be, if again we must ward off
the Lacedaemonians at greater cost? For we shall not, of course, bring a
sieve into the war and make the business stand upon that; but we must choose
our position from the beginning and hold fast to the scale, and not, like
men who sift, turn always to the side that has the more and wall ourselves
off from the weaker.

Since, then, it is common to both sides not to be able to lay the war by
like a deposit

but one must, having chosen a side, neither abandon it nor betray it, let us
consider for whom it is more fitting to provide. Come, then: as for the
freedom of the Plataeans and the Thirty Tyrants, if, when they first took up
their positions against each other for war and had sent embassies, and we
had heard both sides, we had inclined toward the Lacedaemonians and chosen
them, would this not now be the strongest argument for those who speak on
their behalf, that you have already passed judgment upon the Thebans and
must not overlook those whom you then made your allies? But if it was not
against the Thebans that you went out then, but on behalf of the Thebans,
and you kept ever after the same alignment toward them, will not the same
argument hold for us now which would then have held for them? And whereas
you chose these men even before they came to the Cadmea, how will you shift
your position now, when, even if you had been with them, it would have been
fitting to take the side of the wronged in exchange? And yet if the Thebans
were repelling us and refusing to receive us, all would say they were guilty
of outrage and doing terrible things, and would counsel you to change sides.
If, then, they cling to us and long for us and call us, and they need you
fighting at their side no less than victory itself, shall we again set that
at naught? But it is not right.

How, then, shall we make the alliance with them, and on what terms? For that
was the question just now being raised. For my part, I say it outright, not
without wonder, that I think the man who asked that question absurd and
ridiculous; for we ought not, once allies had been made in one way or
another, then to seek whether we must aid, but, having first considered the
alliance, to examine in common counsel on what terms it shall stand. Then,
for those who wish to know already, I have this answer ready: on the same
terms as before. For they call you, surely, just as they did then; how, then,
will they not concede it? But if one must snatch the occasion in advance,
let us consider the same question about the Lacedaemonians; for we shall
find it far more difficult with them. For if they wish to use us as allies
on the terms they resolved on before, they will wish also to rule by land
and by sea. But that the same men should be both suppliants and

protectors, by the gods I at least cannot conceive. But even if the alliance
must be made on equal terms, I should say boldly: choose rather the Thebans,
the men whom these very speakers say are better than the Lacedaemonians. And
I think that to come to equality with the defeated and the unfortunate
leaves behind not even a measure of shame beyond it. For when, men of
Athens, shall we have more power over the Lacedaemonians than now, when they
have come into our need, if we accept them as equals? And if they are
looking to the grandeur of a name, or judging such things by the glory of a
title, the Thebans are the founding fathers of those very men whom they
admire. Let them not, then, refuse them as if they were inferior; for that
all this is but elegance of phrases and nothing like what one might suppose,
I will demonstrate easily, catching them in the act. For if the
Lacedaemonians have met with misfortune and have fallen and nothing at all
is left to them, let them surrender themselves and introduce you into their
city, just as they themselves once entered ours, and so find their
salvation; and by Zeus, not many of us will get there before me, but I shall
rise first and bid the Thebans raise them up, if they are not persuaded by
reason and do not hold their hands from these men. But the Lacedaemonians
will never do this, I suppose. How so? Well, I call the ambassadors; just
watch. So in a moment it will be in your power to make trial of this; but
now give me leave to add a little more.

For while I was speaking there came to my mind that ancient aid, which they
received from us in their extremest peril, having themselves petitioned for
it in the very manner in which they have now come and cast themselves down,
the war standing firm and at its acme against them, and they being in need
of allies, and employing all the other allies present, and not even granting
those men to depart when they asked it. If, then, men of Athens, they do the
same now too and say that they no longer need you, either suspecting as
much, or reckoning it glorious for themselves at any cost, for these men are
insolent and secret haters, we should indeed go away having met with a
charming fate, insulted by them while we looked another way, and having both
of them for enemies hereafter.

But to me this also seems marvelous, if anyone fears that the Thebans alone
will be left as our rivals, yet judges the argument that we shall have to
reckon with both of them as worthy of no fear at all. By Zeus, but with the
trophies standing, it is not worthy to war down the Lacedaemonians. But we
did not spare even those fellow citizens of ours who counseled us, through
whom we raised those trophies, when we later found they had done wrong: we
did not judge that Miltiades, who ended his life in prison, had perished in
a manner worthy of him; and Themistocles, the counselor and general of our
noblest deeds, we made an exile, since we could not seize and kill him
though he was at hand. How, then, is it not a grievous thing that you dealt
thus with these men, through whom, one would say, any of the deeds of that
time succeeded rather than through the Lacedaemonians, and yet let the
Lacedaemonians go free, who pulled down those very achievements when they
found their opportunity?

And indeed you will easily learn that the posture of the war against them is
better than that on their behalf. For in the former there is the appearance
of standing with the weaker, which they say is a noble thing; but in the
latter, in place of this, there is standing with the wronged, which is
greater as touching nobility; and that element of peril which attaches to
the former has here been escaped, because our partners are better men; while
that which, offered by safety, prevails over mere repute, how would any man
judging with sound mind not think the finer? These things, then, I have
demonstrated; but to speak what is both finer and more just, for your glory
and mine, is a necessity laid upon me, and not only to speak, but also to
aid it eagerly with deeds.
