Men who remain of the Rhodians, of the things that have befallen it is neither
easy to forget, nor, remembering them, to bear them moderately. For not only
to you, who once prayed to dwell in Rhodes, but even to those who altogether
belong among the Greeks, what greater disaster than the present could there
be, now that the ornament of the common race has been destroyed? And one would
groan most of all if he considered that formerly this was the greatest thing
to say when speaking of the city, that among others one hears stories and sees
some remnant of a trophy and a monument and a spring, and the guide points
them out in faint tokens, this is the chamber of Semele, this of Harmonia, or
of Leda, or some such thing, but among you alone it was possible to see
exactly, not to hear, what the city was. For as if it had been freshly
finished everything appeared, so that the thing was not like a dirge, but held
astonishment and emulation for all who saw it. As soon as one sailed in, so
many and so great harbors, standing out into the sea with projecting stones,
some receiving those from Ionia, some those from Caria, some those from Egypt
and Cyprus and Phoenicia, as if each had been made for a different city for
reception. And lying upon the harbors the dockyards, as many and of such a
kind as they were when you ruled the sea, and no less throughout the time, nor
forcing one to guess where they once were, but themselves seen, striking
amazement. Of which if anyone viewed the roofs from above, he would have
likened them to some hanging plain. And besides these there were to be seen
triremes two-banked and three-banked and up to seven and to nine

rows, some seaworthy, others laid up as in a storehouse. Of which if anyone
wished to sail, having launched whichever he would, it was possible. And of
others bronze-mouthed rams, some around the harbors, others distributed in the
upper parts of the city together with many and splendid spoils, some from the
Tyrrhenian pirate band, some from the deeds with Alexander, some from wherever
each of them had been brought into the city. And precincts of gods and temples
and statues so many in number, so great in size, so such in beauty, that they
were worthy thank-offerings of the other works, and so that one could not
distinguish which of them one would admire more; of which even one chance
piece held sufficient ambition for another city. And besides these bronze
images compared to all those in the rest of Greece, and paintings of every art
dedicated in various places of the city, and ornaments some unique here,
others the most beautiful; and the acropolis full of plains and groves, and of
the rest of the city nothing exceeding another, but the construction being
sufficient and equal, as would belong not to a city but to a single house. And
streets continuous from beginning to end, least worthy to be called alleys;
and the city stretched out splendidly, splendid everywhere. And the wonder
above all and before all, having no satiety for the eyes, the circuit of the
walls and the height and beauty of the towers mixed in among them, being
instead of beacons for those sailing up, so that it belonged to those putting
in to Rhodes and looking toward her alone that their spirit at once became
greater. And the most beautiful of all, this circuit not detached from the
rest of the city nor making anything empty in the middle, but adjoining the
city, running around it as a crown around a head.

And one could say that Rhodes alone was walled both house by house and in
common. For the walls were like the fence of a courtyard and had so much power
beside the inner city as to bound

it, since in worth all things were equal. And so to speak, one thing of the
ancient was lacking, naval battle; but all the rest of the city was preserved
purely and purely; and you would have said only that it had yielded the
hegemony, being of sound mind, but that it itself was the city that once was,
not a name otherwise, but itself exactly what it was. But now the beauties of
the harbors are gone, the fairest of crowns has fallen, temples are empty of
statues and altars of sacrifices, courses and theatres empty of men; and the
ship-sheds, where on earth or sea they came to be, not even one of those who
did not know them before would guess; and the unspeakable number of both kinds
of images has been mixed up together with the rocks and the remnants of the
destroyed bodies and scattered; ivory and gold, second to the art, are gone;
and the walls that held up the spoils everywhere have perished together with
the ornament; and as many swords and spears as there were of the spoils, these
were brought here from the enemy, but in the evils here they have further
outraged those who fell in their way, so as most of all to take a surplus of
disaster. And the city has been left in the portion of a crag, not level by so
much as the stones rise that stir the memory of the suffering. And this city's
disaster has so surpassed all Greek things we have received by hearing, that
of those I mentioned just now, these very things I suppose, palaces of dynasts
and certain chambers are pointed out, and some parts of the cities still
remain, and you would say they have become smaller from greater; but here not
even enough is left for the guide to dwell. And there you would see a monument
and a trophy, but here not even a tomb has remained unharmed by the outrage,
but both outside and inside the city has fared alike, and it has itself become
instead of a monument for the citizens. And instead of those sailing up and
those at sea seeing the city, those who have landed must seek what each thing
was, looking at an untimely mountain instead of a city; so great is the
difference that has befallen it compared with itself and with the misfortunes
of others. So there is pardon

for you in all you both say and do. But even with things being thus you must
bear it, the ancient saying; thinking it right that what has happened could no
longer be otherwise, not even if you should say everything accusing and
blaming fortune, and destroy yourselves in every way by despondency; but to
bear these things well as far as is possible is no small gain as things are.
And you are not in want of misfortunes, so as to add to these, but rather
subtracting from what has happened and always furnishing yourselves occasions
of the ease that is possible, you would think well and do what is needful. And
you must know that you are still Rhodians, however many of you have been left
at all, and that fortune has taken from you this august and honored name among
Greeks and barbarians, of which you must appear thinking things worthy.

And now if ever, men of Rhodes, is the time to save yourselves from the things
that have surrounded you, and to help the race of the island, and to stand
against fortune splendidly, considering the saying of your fellow-citizen the
helmsman, who said when his ship was storm-tossed and he expected to sink,
that well-known word: But, Poseidon, know that I will sink the ship upright;
as one who would do nothing ignoble nor anything low in the present, but would
fulfill all that was his before perishing badly; since of perishing and of the
ship sinking fortune is always mistress, but of doing what is needful before
this the demand is no longer in another, but each owes this to himself, and
what alone he is master of, he must not abandon while still living. You then
must not throw down the fallen city still more, adding meanness to the
occasion, but since it has already sunk when struck, you who remain, remain in
yourselves. For it is shameful and has the charge from home, that you here
should appear the city more ignoble than one man, and not show this, that even
in misfortune you know yourselves. Thus even contrary to desert you will seem
to have suffered these things. For it is a fine thing I think even in arms

to conquer, and for those fighting at sea to set up trophies has ambition, and
from the zeal in such things it is not worthy that good men stand aside, of

even if she charged upon you very violently at first. For of the victory there
no one is so great as to be master through to the end, but the daimon has the
greatest part; but of the victory here judgment is mistress. Show then that
you have this such as is needful, and do not become in all things inferior to
fortune, but contend against her now, since before it was not possible. For if
we are able to lament worthily, even if we join all the remaining nights and
days of life, let us do this. For it is worthy for Greece to call out, not
three times only but even ten thousand times, Rhodes so faring. But now what
speech is so strong, or what supreme sophist so sufficient, who could worthily
bewail the present things, not leaving out the least part of what he thinks to
say, but all in order? For this is even the most strange of all, that
lamenting what has happened and going through them as unsurpassable we leave
out most of them and think we are speaking when we are not speaking. For who
could either tell worthily these new and strange disasters that have come
beyond every flood of evils, or the very peak of the thing that has surrounded
you? And who would still be of sound mind remembering that wretched noon, in
which the evil first began and came down, when the sea stood waiting for what
was to come, as expecting some other great and incurable storm, and all the
air held silence, as expecting what would be, and birds and all things were
still at

what was coming, and the city then lay subject to such a suffering and upon it
all the earthquake was being prepared; and the sun then shone last upon his
own city, and suddenly all the terrors were present together. The sea withdrew
and all the inside of the harbors was laid bare, houses were thrown up and
tombs were burst open, towers fell upon towers and ship-sheds upon triremes
and temples upon altars and offerings upon statues and men upon men, and
towers upon harbors, and all things upon one another. And while a man was
hanging an anchor for putting out, turning around he no longer had the city to
see, but all things happened together, the harbors on dry land, the city in
dust, courses down through houses from alleys, into alleys from houses, deaths
throughout houses, in temples, in doors, in gates. Outside the tombs threw up
those lying in them, inside those who had died were hidden; of some, as votive
offerings, hands were seen on the tops of walls, of some feet, of some some
other remnant; and not even these could one guess of whom each remained. And
some fleeing their own houses perished in those of others, some remaining in
their own through terror, some running out being caught, some left half-dead,
not able to emerge nor to save themselves, took on famine as an addition of
evils, and gaining only so much as to know that the fatherland was no more,
they perished on top of it. And of some fortune divided the bodies, and the
halves were caught inside the doors, and the half-cut parts lay out outside.
And upon these again other bodies fell, vessels, stones, whatever the
earthquake mixed for each as it carried it. Some expected, some sought their
household, some did not know whether to lament themselves or their own; some
wailed for the city, some were burned, the roofs and the hearths having become
one. Some snatching children were themselves caught, some killed themselves
besides. And the revel was a wild one of that day's midday meal.

Some not long before called bridegrooms lay having sung the hymeneal for no
good, not easy to know whether they themselves were unfortunate, or those they
had married; some coming up from the sea found the ancestral hearth less
trustworthy than the sea; some hastening to put out then last put out from
home, the fatherland having already sunk for them.

And there held together earthquake of sea, crash of cloud, wails, clatter of
falls, earth's blastings. For I think neither the cataracts above Egypt nor
the surf of the outer sea nor fire-bearing thunderbolts nor whatever sounds
most among men could be compared to that then evil and crash, which from all
things one mixed was raised, filling the unexpected and graceless concert, at
which Rhodes had set out; and there had been brought together into the same
place corpses, altars, roofs, dust, blood, furniture, roofs, foundations,
slaves, masters, limbs of bodies, images, slaughters, tombs, dinners. Some
pitching tent with the bodies of their dearest did not know themselves, some
sought the rest of themselves, some feet, some hands, some whatever each had
been maimed of the body, some even buried these with their household, some
held fast by those who had fallen from both sides endured, some were buried
before they died; every form of evils and what one had never yet conceived,
then first he saw. And those remaining could not pray whether that they be
saved or that they die. What heralds, or what poets, will bewail these things
with a voice worthy? Who is sufficient to narrate? For what suffering sharper,
or if you will, longer, what Greek or barbarian city in all time has such a
thing overtaken? When the city, overturned, lay quicker than a ship ever sank,
and days and nights coming upon them revealed some living only so much as to
breathe

wounded, of the rest to most those who had died putrefied, having nothing
accurate of their limbs, but as the fall had taken from or added to each. And
unaccustomed pyres burned equally night and day, the reverse of the former
holy months; and instead of mining of gold and silver mines the floor of the
city provided, and for months to clean out those lying. And formerly, O Zeus,
you judged the homicide cases outside the gates, as not even to vote death
within the wall was pious for you, but now in one day the daimon condemned the
destruction of so many both inside the city and with it, and you who remained
were forced to burn these in it, and the city impassable to murderers he
established as a burial of the perishing for each. And the things formerly
told were these mythologizings, that this island being under the sea and
hidden the gods sent up as a gift to Helios, but now it has come round to the
contrary to narrate, that this city sank under the earth and departed from
men, the sun being above the earth, and the sun saw in Rhodes the sights
unseen to himself. And Homer said that Zeus poured wealth upon the island and
upon Tlepolemus's ancestors of yours, and Pindar taking it up that he rained
gold having set a yellow cloud. But now what gifts the daimon poured, what
cloud he set over the city, how unworthy of those former things, unworthy of
you and of the city's form. And now Carpathos and Casos and other islands can
be seen inhabited, having nothing to say and from whom it was a small thing
before to collect tribute for you, and other poor sandy disputed townships.
But Rhodes, O Zeus, and sun who look upon all except Rhodes, is now nowhere,
but as those who tell myths one must narrate to those who come after, those
receiving from us, that once there was also Rhodes opposite Caria, a city
conspicuous in season and size, and to point out the place

as now that of Ialysos and Cameiros. And the contest of the Halieia you will
hold, and the place has remained safe where you will hold it; but this
namesake of the Nymph of the god has taken on the form of a shore. And of old
you showed to visitors the devices from the women who had cut their hair, and
it was wonderful, but now it remains to present the city itself in such a
spectacle and to show to those arriving theatre and council-house, and
half-broken tablets and crags pointing instead of towers, pitiful remnants of
the once renowned Rhodes. And when the size of other cities is spoken of in
the middle it will be possible neither to take hold nor not to burst remaining
silent. And simply you will know of what kind of city you are deprived, and of
what kind you will not have to show, and for the present time to call to
witness those of the Greeks who knew it before, but when the due fate leads
these away too, what then remains except to be convicted of desolation as they
say, being recognized, O daimones, not from the sea-battles nor from the
harbors and dockyards nor from the ornament throughout the city, but that you
have been most unfortunate of the Greeks; and those remembering Rhodes will
need not to exult but to lament. And by what you have now suffered those
renowned men of six hundred years ago have been wronged together, and even
those who had nothing to do with the city, adorning the city with their own
works, were adorned in turn by the memory.

These things then so terrible and having no consolation, as one might think,
must be borne, most of all and first according to the counsel of necessity
itself and so much the more, as it has passed the place of tears; for what
cannot be lamented worthily, how is it not necessary, having ceased to feel
pain, to let it be and, as the saying goes, shutting the eyes to bear it? And
further, the paltry of misfortunes need no great preparation toward them, but
the greater must be borne greatly too, I do not mean simply greatly,

but with spirit. For as toward the greater of wars more preparation also is
needed, and the lesser one might get through even from a poorer, so also of
the terrors those that have surpassed need a great strength of the sufferers
toward them. One must then stand against them with all one's might and with
all one's spirit prepared contend this contest, if one is not going to be
caught and carried aside, calculating that formerly those who saw the images
and the statues admired the art of those who had made them, but now all will
admire your judgment, if even without these you appear thinking well of
yourselves. And in general as the touchstone shows the gold, so misfortunes
judge the good men; and all these things will now show of what sort you are,
the earthquake, the fire, all that followed upon the one first evil. And yet I
do a paradoxical thing; for needing consolation myself concerning you, I think
it right to hold you. Which itself would justly help me toward persuasion. For
those who have suffered together in their judgments I think would reasonably
be trustworthy counselors also to those of sound mind. For those whom the same
things touch, these reasonably both think about these things and speak with
goodwill, and confirm the possible first by this, that being similarly
disposed they nonetheless think it right to appear bearing it. And I for my
part think that to suffer such great misfortunes is not laid up for the
smallest of men nor for those who have nowhere to fall from, but for those
able to receive this measure of disaster. But do you think, by the gods, of
Seriphos, or of this Syme here, this fall, or of some other of the many
islands in a circle? Rhodes I think was able to be deprived of so many and so
great things. And to you the daimon allotted both extremes; for in virtue and
power and construction of city you surpassed the manifest of the others, and
when it was necessary to take trial of the opposite things too, as it should
not have been, fortune equaled these also to the advantages above; and having
the greatest

you fell from the greatest. And as Hesiod said that if heaven and earth
collided the greatest crash would come to be, as this being the greatest fall
from on high, so of things on earth Rhodes having fallen sounded greatest and
the perception of it reached to most both Greeks and barbarians, and even
those who rule Greeks and barbarians themselves. For nothing else has happened
than that all the sea has been stripped of ornament and is widowed henceforth
of what least it ought. So reasonably you have also most who share the grief.
For the thing is neither obscure because of its greatness nor was there anyone
who will rejoice at you. For you were common hosts and friends of all, and of
many even saviors, good leaders and escorts of those sailing past the island
both ways, sweeter and more splendid than those Homer made in the myth of the
Phaeacians, and renowned even to those at the ends of the earth. So that even
to those who did not know the city, hearing from those who knew, even if they
had the other things of their life to speak of as lived according to pleasure,
this one thing at least seemed not the least lacking, that they had not yet
seen Rhodes. And indeed one might be at a loss for which now it is more worthy
to lament, whether for those who have not seen the city, or for those who have
associated with it. For the one have been deprived of the fairest spectacle,
the others of the greatest of what they saw, and as much as they gained, so
much they have been fined; for they are not ignorant how great a thing has
been destroyed. Wherefore you must most of all be eager to deal with the
present things easily and nobly, because you have many spectators and
witnesses of how you will carry them through, to whom it is well and a Rhodian
work to show the surplus of nobility, that even if the walls fall ten times,
at least the dignity of the city will not fall, so long as one Rhodian is
left, but it will stand and remain safe; so that they may not share grief more
than admire you, nor remember

And I, if I were not going to say things too paradoxical, would have ordered
you to send ambassadors everywhere in Greece, who would console each and bid
them not to pity you, as it is not ancestral for Rhodes and for you being
Rhodians to be lamented rather than emulated; and of these going-about
ambassadors and those asking others for other things this embassy would in my
opinion have been no more dishonorable for the city. For these too I think are
necessary and have need (and who would blame?) to send others elsewhere to
announce, as if imposing common contributions upon all the Greeks as
fellow-citizens; and this seems good to me too. Nevertheless even toward these
very things that form would have contributed. For who would not have wondered?
Who would not have helped? Who would not have thought such a thing to be
rather a favor to himself than to you? But if it is not possible nor allowed
to send around this embassy, you must ambassador these things to yourselves,
so that you may also give the third to those two I just mentioned, and not
fail only where there is most need. Greatest of the Greeks you were struck
greatest; you must bear these things best, so that everywhere the city may
appear to have obtained the greatest and from beginning to end be faring
similarly, and to the two extremes that belong to you one middle may be caught
together and the things now seeming terrible and incurable may come to be on
your behalf. For by how much you appear stronger than them, by so much more
will you be well reputed. And the poets also say about the heroes that those

And to those who think life unlivable if they will not pass it in so great and
such a fatherland as before, I say this, and let no one be harsh toward the

poets say, not even this island, but these things were once bare sea. But I do

there was only the name of the island, and there was not yet a city Rhodes nor

three ancient ones, which Homer also listed. And coming together later from
all the cities they settled this one city not with all fair omens then, whose
great and wonderful name and form and beauty and whatever one wishes saying he
will not miss. This speech then can do two things for me: one, men of Rhodes,
to suggest to your reasonings that you are in those times when you did not yet
have the city, and to bear it more moderately; and another and the whole, not
even now to despair yet of the present things nor to give up, using the
example of the ancestors, that they too taking the judgment that was needful
showed the city existing from not existing and great from none and wonderful
from obscure at first.

And where is it tolerable for those deprived of such a city to live? Not to
use it for bare distress, but to turn it to the restoration of what has
happened, whichever appears possible. And yet the recovery of the city is much
easier for you, if you look rightly, than for them the original settlement.
For they having no starting-point at all from the place, not harbors, not
images, not ornaments, not stones, so to speak, having come into much counsel
and perplexity I think first where in the land they would settle, thus showed
this, and the greatest of all, toward no existing model, but finding it
themselves and setting it forth first as truly a model of a city. But you need
now neither seek anything about the whole, and if any of the ancient remains
it helps; and these are many toward the whole, even if altogether few as
toward all. For you need now neither silt up harbors nor rebuild council-house
nor theatre nor plant groves. And there are also some remaining images on
tablets, and of the bronze works as much as a sample, and the great statue and
this our place here, in which many times we have often contended with you. Of
which even if you despise them and judge the remainder nothing, referring to
the ancient, other cities you well know would have rejoiced if even a part
were among them. And indeed you need not send for a model nor emulate some
foreign one, but from Rhodes make Rhodes new from old. And further they in
wars and tumults, and neither themselves quite able to keep quiet, all the
rest of Greece being carried up and down, sailing and campaigning together
settled the city, as some fort or camp walling it out on campaign. But you in
much peace and deep quiet, which doing well has increased the affairs of all
men, and with many also helping you, not only not hindering, it will be
possible for many of the Greeks

And one must consider also the things of the ancients. It is truly without
feeling to compare your things against anyone's whatsoever; for what I said,
the only thing that lightens disasters, is not in the present, an example; yet
