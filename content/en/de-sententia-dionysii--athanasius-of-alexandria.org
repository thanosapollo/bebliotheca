* On the Opinion of Dionysius

Of the blessed Athanasius concerning Dionysius the bishop of Alexandria, that
he too thought against the Arian heresy as the synod at Nicaea, and in vain
the Ariomaniacs slander him as of one mind with themselves.
1.1 You made known late concerning the discussion that has now taken place
among you toward the Christ-fighters; for even before your affection wrote I
learned of it, being inquisitive and gladly hearing about such things. I
approved your reverence, then, as thinking well concerning our blessed
fathers, and I recognized even now the irrationality of the Ariomaniacs. 1.2
For since their heresy has nothing either reasonable or a saying from the
divine Scripture toward proof, they have always furnished shameless pretexts
and plausible sophisms, but now they have even dared to slander the fathers.
And this is not foreign but even proper to their ill-will; for those who have
attempted to plot against the Lord and against his Christ, what wonder if they
also revile the blessed Dionysius the 1.3 bishop of Alexandria as of one mind
and of one thought with themselves? For whomever these men seem to praise
toward the establishment of their own heresy, even if they name him blessed,
they slander this man not simply but even greatly, like certain robbers and
unseemly in manner, who whenever they are reproached for their own practices
number the temperate with themselves, lying against those men's temperance.
2.1 If then they have confidence in what they think and say, let them bring
forward the heresy naked and show from it if they suppose they have either
something from the Scriptures or even some human pious reasoning toward a
defense; but if they have no such thing, let them be silent. 2.2 For they will
find from nowhere except rather a refutation against themselves, from the
Scriptures, John saying "In the beginning was the Word," whom these men say
was not before he was begotten, and David singing in the person of the Father:
"My heart has belched forth a good word," whom these men say is by conception
and has come to be from things that are not, then John again evangelizing that
"all things came to be through him and without him came to be not even one
thing," and Paul writing: "one Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all
things," and 2.3 in other places: "that in him all things were created." What
boldness will these men have, and will they not rather incur shame, opposing
the words of the saints, when they say that the craftsman of all things is a
work and that this one is a creature, in whom the creatures have come to be
2.4 and consist? Nor is any human pious reasoning left to them toward a
defense. For which of men, whether Greek or barbarian, will attempt to call
him whom he confesses God a creature, or that he was not before he came to be?
Or which of men, having heard from this one whom he believed to be God alone
saying: "This is my beloved Son," and "My heart has belched forth a good
word," will dare even to say that the Word from the heart of God has come to
be from things that are not, or that the Son is a creature and not a proper
offspring of the one who speaks? And again who, hearing this one whom he
believed to be Lord and Savior saying: "I am in the Father and the Father in
me," and "I and the Father are one," will attempt to divide what that one has
united and has kept undivided?
3.1 These things then they themselves also seeing, and not having boldness in
their own, they lie against the pious. But it was necessary that they, being
destitute of all things and seeing themselves in the questionings at a loss
from every side and yawning

rather repent, having turned away from the way of error, and not number with
themselves those whom they do not 3.2 know, lest being refuted even by them
they carry away greater shame. But perhaps they will never wish to stand off
from such knavery; for they have emulated this too of those about Caiaphas,
just as they have also learned from them to deny the Christ. For those men
too, though the Lord had done so many works through which he made himself
known to be the Christ the Son of the living God, and being refuted by him,
thereafter both thinking and saying all things contrary to the Scriptures, and
not being able even for a short while to look toward the refutations against
them, fled to the patriarch saying: "We have Abraham as father," and thinking
in this to be able to cover their 3.3 irrationality. But neither did those men
saying this profit anything, nor will these men, naming Dionysius, be able to
flee the like charge as those. For those too the Lord refuted for the things
in which they were transgressing, saying: "This Abraham did not do," and these
men, being impious and lying, the same truth again will refute, that Dionysius
the bishop neither thought the things of Arius nor was ignorant of the truth.
But both the Jews then and the new ones now have inherited from their father
the devil the 3.4 Christ-fighting madness. A great proof, then, that they do
not even say this truly, but slander the man, is that he was neither condemned
by other bishops for impiety and cast out of the episcopate as these men from
the clergy, nor did he himself go out of the church as championing a heresy,
but he has fallen asleep well in it and his memory until now is with the
fathers and has been written. For if he had thought their things, or had not
made a defense concerning what he wrote, it would not be doubtful that he
himself would certainly have suffered the same things as they.
4.1 And these things were sufficient toward every condemnation of the new Jews
who both deny the Lord and slander the fathers and attempt to deceive all
Christians. But since they suppose they have pretexts of the slander against
the bishop, certain parts of a letter of his, come, let us see these too, that
from these themselves their vain talk may be shown and they may cease, even if
late, from blaspheming the Lord among themselves, and may confess, even with
the soldiers, seeing the creation bearing witness, that 4.2 "truly this is the
Son of God" and he is not of the creatures. They say then that in a letter the
blessed Dionysius has said that the Son of God is a work and originate, and
that he is not proper by nature, but foreign in essence to the Father, as the
husbandman is toward the vine and the shipwright toward the skiff; for as
being a work he was not 4.3 before he came to be. Yes he wrote it; we too
confess that there is such a letter of his. But just as he wrote this, so he
also wrote very many other letters, and they ought to have read these too,
that from all and not from the one only the faith of the man might be shown;
for even if a shipwright has constructed many triremes, his craft is judged
not from one but from 4.4 all. If then he wrote the letter they mention simply
as setting forth a faith, or wrote this only, let them accuse as they wish;
for such a thought is truly an accusation; but if a pretext of time and person
drew him to write such things, and he wrote again also other letters making a
defense concerning the things of which he was suspected, they ought again, not
leaving the causes, to bring envy upon the man rashly, lest they seem to hunt
only little words, and to send past the truth in the other 4.5 letters. For
even a husbandman tends the same trees now in one way now in another because
of the underlying quality of the earth, and one would not blame him for this,
that he cuts this and grafts that, and plants this and pulls up another, but
rather 4.6 having learned

the cause he will admire the variety of his science. Therefore if they have
not read the writings carelessly, let them tell also the subject of the
letter; for thus both their slander and the wickedness of their choice will be
shown. But since they either do not know or are ashamed, it is necessary for
us to speak.
5.1 In Pentapolis of upper Libya at that time some of the bishops thought the
things of Sabellius and prevailed so much by their inventions that the Son of
God was all but no longer preached in the churches. Dionysius having learned
this, for he himself had the care of those churches, sends and advises the
responsible to cease from the evil opinion. But as they did not cease, but
were even more shamelessly impious, he was compelled toward that shamelessness
5.2 to write such a letter and to set beside them the human things of the
Savior from the Gospels, that since those men more daringly denied the Son and
attributed his human things to the Father, thus this man, having shown that it
is not the Father but the Son who became man for us, might persuade the
ignorant that the Father is not Son, and thus thereafter little by little
might lead those men up to the true Godhead of the Son and 5.3 the knowledge
concerning the Father. This is the subject of the letter, and for this cause
he himself also wrote thus, because of those who so shamelessly wished to
transpose the faith of the truth.
6.1 What then is like between the heresy of Arius and the mind of Dionysius?
Or why is Dionysius named as Arius, there being much difference between them?
For the one is a teacher of the catholic church, but the other has become an
inventor of a new heresy. And Arius, setting forth his own evil opinion, wrote
a Thalia in feminized and ridiculous manners according to the Egyptian
Sotades, but Dionysius wrote also other letters, and made a defense 6.2 also
concerning the things of which he was suspected and appeared thinking rightly.
If then he writes things contrary to himself, let them not drag him toward
themselves, for he is not trustworthy in this. But if having written the
letter to Ammonius he was suspected and made a defense healing the first
things, and having made a defense he has not changed, it would be clear that
he wrote even the things suspected according to 6.3 economy. And one must not
receive in a bad way the things written and done according to economy and drag
each toward his own will. For even a physician often applies to the underlying
wounds things that seem to some not to be suitable, as he himself knows,
having no other aim than toward health. And this is the manner of a prudent
teacher, to dispose himself and speak toward the characters of those being
taught, until he transfer them to the way of perfection.
7.1 But if they blame him only because the blessed man wrote thus (for the
Arians' pretexts on his behalf happen to be charges against him), what will
they do hearing also the great and blessed apostles in the Acts, Peter saying:
"Men of Israel, hear these words. Jesus the Nazarene, a man approved from God
unto you by powers and wonders and signs which God did through him in the
midst of you, as you know, this man, delivered up by the determined counsel
and foreknowledge of God, you fastened and took away through the hand of
lawless men," and again: "In the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene, whom you
crucified, whom God raised 7.2 from the dead, in this this man stands before
you healthy"; and Paul interpreting in Antioch of Pisidia, that "God having
removed Saul raised David to be king, to whom also he said having borne
witness: I found David the son of Jesse a man according to my heart, who will
do my will; from the seed of this man God according to promise 7.3 brought to
Israel a savior Jesus," and again at Athens: "The times of ignorance then

God having overlooked now commands men that all everywhere repent, inasmuch as
he has set a day in which he is about to judge the inhabited world in
righteousness, in a man whom he appointed, having furnished faith to all,
having raised him from the dead"; or of Stephen the great martyr, when he
says: "Behold I see the heavens opened and 7.4 the Son of man standing at the
right of God"? For it is time thereafter for them to be bold and to say (for
nothing is undared by them) that the apostles themselves also thought the
things of Arius; for they announce Christ a man from Nazareth and passible.
8.1 Those men then imagining such things, is it that because they used these
words the apostles knew Christ only as man and nothing more? May it not be; it
is not even to take this into mind ever, but they have done this too as "wise
master-builders" and 8.2 "stewards of the mysteries of God" and they have a
reasonable cause. For since the Jews then, having been led astray and having
led Greeks astray, thought that the Christ comes a mere man only from the seed
of David according to likeness of the other children who came to be from
David, and believed neither that he is God nor that the Word became flesh, for
this reason with much understanding the blessed apostles first interpreted the
human things of the Savior to the Jews, that having wholly persuaded them from
the apparent and the signs that came to be that the Christ has come they might
thereafter lead them up also to the faith concerning his Godhead, showing that
the works that came to be are not of a man 8.3 but of God. Indeed Peter who
says Christ a passible man immediately joined: "This one is author of life,"
and in the Gospel he confesses: "You are the Christ, the Son of the living
God," and in the letter he says that he is overseer of souls and Lord 8.4 of
himself and of angels and of powers. And again Paul who says Christ is a man
from the seed of David, this man wrote to the Hebrews: "who being radiance of
the glory and impress of his subsistence," and to the Philippians: "who being
in the form of God did not consider being equal to God a thing to be seized."
And to say author of life and Son of God and radiance and impress and equal to
God and Lord and overseer of souls, what else would it be than that in a body
there was Word of God, through whom all things came to be, and he is undivided
from the Father, as the radiance is toward the light?
9.1 And Dionysius then, having learned this from the apostles, has done it.
For since the heresy of Sabellius crept in, he was compelled to write such a
letter, as I said before, and to throw against them the things said humanly
and cheaply concerning the Savior, that having fenced these men off through
the human things from saying that the Father is Son he might prepare for them
an easier teaching concerning the Godhead of the Son, when in the other
letters he says him from the Scriptures Word, wisdom, power, vapor and
radiance 9.2 of the Father. In the letters of the defense then, saying such
things, he is bold in the faith and in the piety toward Christ. As therefore
the apostles are not to be accused because of the human words concerning the
Lord (for the Lord also became man), but are even more worthy of wonder
because of the economy and the teaching in season, so Dionysius is not an
Arian because of the letter to Euphranor and Ammonius against Sabellius. For
if he also used humble words and examples, yet these too are from the Gospels,
and he has as pretext of these the incarnate presence of the Savior, for which
also 9.3 these and the like have been written. For just as he is Word of God,
so after these things "the Word became flesh," and "in the beginning was the
Word," but the virgin at the completion of the ages conceived in the womb and
the Lord became man. And the one signified from both is one ("for the Word
became flesh"), but the words said concerning the Godhead

of him and of the incarnation have their own and suitable interpretation
toward each 9.4 of the things said. And he who writes the human things of the
Word knows also the things concerning his Godhead, and he who interprets
concerning the Godhead is not ignorant of the proper things of his incarnate
presence, but distinguishing each as a skilled and approved money-changer he
will walk according to the right of piety. When then he says him weeping, he
knows that the Lord having become man shows the weeping as of the human, but
raises Lazarus as God, and he knows him hungering and thirsting bodily, but
filling five thousand from five loaves in a godlike way, and he knows a human
body lying in a tomb, but being raised as a body of God by the Word himself.
10.1 These things Dionysius too teaching wrote in the letter to Euphranor and
Ammonius concerning the things said humanly about the Savior because of
Sabellius (for said humanly about him are "I am the vine, the Father the
husbandman" and "being faithful to him who made him" and "he created" and
"having become so much better than the angels"), but he was not ignorant of "I
am in the Father and the Father in me" and "he who has seen me 10.2 has seen
the Father." For we have known him writing these things in the other letters;
for even there writing these he remembered the human things of the Lord. For
just as "being in the form of God he did not consider being equal to God a
thing to be seized, but emptied himself taking the form of a slave" and "being
rich he became poor for us," so while the words concerning his Godhead are
high and rich there are also the humble and 10.3 poor expressions concerning
his incarnate presence. And that these things have been said humanly
concerning the Savior it is fitting to consider from this: the husbandman is
foreign in essence to the vine, but the branches happen to be consubstantial
and akin and undivided from the vine and these and the vine have one and the
same origin. And it is, as the 10.4 Lord said, "himself the vine, we the
branches." If then the Son is consubstantial with us and has the same origin
as we, let the Son according to this also be alien in essence from the Father,
as also the vine from the husbandman; but if the Son is other than what we
are, and that one is Word of the Father, but we have come to be from earth and
are offspring of Adam, the saying ought not to be referred to the Godhead of
the Word, but thereafter to his human presence, since the Savior also spoke
thus: "I am the vine, 10.5 you the branches, the Father the husbandman." For
we are akin to the Lord according to the body and he for this reason said: "I
will announce your name to my brothers." And just as the branches are
consubstantial with the vine and from it, so we too, having bodies of the same
kind as the body of the Lord, receive from his fullness, and have that as a
root toward the resurrection and the salvation. And the Father has been called
the husbandman; for he himself worked the vine through the Word, which is the
human of the Savior, and he himself through the Word himself prepared for us
the way into the kingdom, and "no one comes to the Lord unless the Father draw
him to him."
11.1 And this being the interpretation of the saying, it is necessary that
concerning the vine so called there be written "being faithful to him who made
him," and "having become so much better than the angels" and "he created." For
when he took what he had to offer for us, and this was the body from Mary the
virgin, then also it has been written concerning him that he has been created
and made and has come to be; for such expressions fit men; and besides he did
not become better than the angels after the body, lest he appear formerly
lesser or 11.2 equal to them. But writing to Jews and comparing the human
ministry of the Lord to that of Moses he said:

"having become so much better than the angels"; "for the law was spoken
through angels"; "because the law was given through Moses, but grace came to
be through Jesus Christ" and the supply of the Spirit. And then the law was
announced from Dan even to Beersheba, but now "their sound has gone out into
all the earth" and the nations worship the Christ and through him know the
Father. Those things then have been written humanly concerning the Savior and
it is not otherwise.
12.1 Is it then, as the Christ-fighters babble, that Dionysius writing the
human things of the Son and speaking thus a work said that he is one of men?
Or saying that the Word is not proper to the essence of the Father did he
think this one consubstantial with us men? And yet he did not write thus in
the other letters, but he appears thinking rightly in them and can through
them cry out against these men saying: I am not of the same 12.2 opinion as
you, O God-fighters, nor did our things furnish a pretext to Arius toward
impiety. But writing to Ammon and Euphranor because of those who Sabellize I
remembered the husbandman and the vine and the other such sayings, that having
shown the human things of the Lord I might persuade those men not to say that
the Father is the one who became man. For as the husbandman is not a vine, so
the one who came to be in the body is not the Father but the Word, and the
Word having come to be in the vine was called vine because of the bodily
kinship toward the branches, which we are. With this mind then I wrote the
letter to Euphranor and Ammonius, but toward your shamelessness I set opposite
the other letters written by me, that the prudent may know the defense in them
and 12.3 my right thinking of the faith in Christ. The Arians then also ought,
if they had a sound mind, to reckon and think such things concerning the
bishop, "for all things are before the face of those who understand and right
to those who find knowledge." But since not having learned the faith of the
catholic church they have fallen into impiety and thereafter, their mind being
maimed, they suppose even the right things to be crooked and say the light is
darkness, and suppose the darkness to be light, it is necessary also to set
beside them the things from the other letters of Dionysius and their cause
toward greater condemnation of the heretics. For having learned from them we
too think and write thus concerning the man.
13.1 And the pretext for which he wrote the other letters is this. Dionysius
the bishop having learned concerning those in Pentapolis and having written in
zeal of piety, as I said before, against the heresy of Sabellius the letter to
Euphranor and Ammonius, some of the brothers from the church, thinking
rightly, but not having asked him, that they might learn from him how he
wrote, went up to Rome and accused him 13.2 before his namesake Dionysius the
bishop of Rome. And that man having heard wrote at once both against those who
hold the opinions of Sabellius and against those who think these things which
Arius also saying was cast out of the church, saying that both the impiety of
Sabellius and that of those who say that the Word of 13.3 God is a creature
and a work and originate are equal and diametrically impiety. And he wrote
also to Dionysius to make clear concerning the things they had said against
him. And he himself wrote back at once and entitled the books Refutation and
Defense. Here consider the abominable workshop of the God-fighters and how
they themselves have stirred the shame against themselves. For Dionysius the
bishop of Rome having written also against those who say the Son of God is a
creature and a work, it is shown that not now first but from of old the heresy
of the Christ-fighting Arians has been anathematized by all. Then Dionysius
the bishop of Alexandria making a defense concerning the letter which he
wrote,

he himself also is shown neither to have thought thus, as they say, nor to
have had the evil opinion of Arius at all.
14.1 And it is sufficient toward perfect condemnation of the Arians and toward
proof of their slander that Dionysius even only made a defense also concerning
the things these men have babbled. For he did not write back as being
contentious, but making a defense concerning the things of which he was
suspected; and he who makes a defense concerning the things of which he was
accused, what else does he do than dissolve every charge suspected against
him, and in this too refute the Ariomaniacs as slanderers? But that these men
may be more ashamed also from what he wrote making a defense, come, let me set
beside you also his 14.2 expressions. For you will know from them that the
Arians are ill-minded, but Dionysius is foreign to their heterodoxy. First
then he entitled his letter Refutation and Defense; and what is this but that
he refutes those who lie, and makes a defense concerning what he wrote,
showing that he himself did not write as Arius supposed, but that having
remembered the things said humanly concerning the Lord he was not ignorant
that this one is 14.3 Word and wisdom undivided from the Father? Then he
blames those who accused him as not speaking complete things but cutting short
his expressions and as speaking not with a good conscience but with a wicked
one as they wish; and he likens these men to those who slander the letters of
the blessed apostle. And such a censure of his wholly releases him from a bad
suspicion. For if he holds those who accused Paul as he holds those who
accused him, he shows nothing else than that thinking the things of Paul he
wrote thus. 14.4 Indeed answering also toward each of the things said by the
accusers he heals all the things brought forward by them, and in those he
overturns Sabellius, and in these he shows his pious faith complete. Those men
then alleging that Dionysius thinks "God was not always Father, the Son was
not always, but God was without the Word, and the Son himself was not before
he was begotten, but there was once when he was not, for he is not 14.5
eternal, but has come to be later," see how he answers. The greater part then
of his words, which either examining he inquires or reasoning he gathers or
questioning he refutes or he blames those who accused, these I pass by because
of the length of the words and set only the things necessary toward the
accusation. Making a defense then toward those things he writes in these
expressions in the first of that entitled Refutation and Defense after other
things thus:
15.1 "For there was not when God was not Father." And he knows this in what
follows: "Christ always is, being Word and wisdom and power, for it is not
that God being barren of these then begot a child, but that the Son has being
not from himself but from the Father." 15.2 And after a little again
concerning the same he says: "And being 'radiance of eternal light' he himself
also is wholly eternal. For the light always being it is clear that the
radiance always is. For by this also that he is light he is understood, by
shining, and light cannot be not 15.3 lighting. For let us come again upon the
examples. If there is a sun, there is a ray, there is day; if none of such
things is, much must the sun also be absent. If then the sun were eternal, the
day also would be unceasing, but now (for it is not) both having begun it
began and ceasing it ceases. But God is eternal light neither having begun nor
ending ever. Therefore eternal also the radiance is set before and is with
him, beginningless and ever-generated appearing before him, which is the
wisdom that says 'I was she in whom he rejoiced; and daily 15.4 I was
gladdened in his face in every season.'" And again he adds after a little
speaking concerning the same: "The Father then being eternal the Son is
eternal, being light from light; for a parent being there is also a child. But
if there were not a child, how and of whom can he 15.5 be a parent? But both
are and they are always." Then again he adds these things:

"God then being light Christ is radiance. And being spirit ('for God,' he
says, 'is spirit'), analogously again Christ has been called vapor; 'for it
is,' he says, 15.6 'vapor of the power of God.'" And from the second again he
says: "And the Son alone always being with the Father and being filled from
the one who is, he himself also is being from the Father."
16.1 If these things have been said ambiguously, they had need of one
interpreting, but since he wrote clearly and often concerning the same things,
let Arius grind his teeth seeing his heresy being overthrown by Dionysius and
hearing what he does not wish, as he says: God was always Father and the Son
is not simply eternal, but the Father being eternal 16.2 the Son also would be
eternal, and he is with him as the radiance with the light. And let those who
have even only supposed that Dionysius thinks the things of Arius lay down
such ill-speaking concerning him. For what communion is there for these, Arius
saying: the Son was not before he was begotten, but there was once when he was
not, and Dionysius teaching: "But God is eternal light neither having begun
nor ending ever; therefore eternal also the radiance is set before 16.3 and is
with him, beginningless and ever-generated appearing before him"? For also
toward the other suspicion of those who say that Dionysius saying Father does
not name the Son and again saying Son does not name the Father, but divides
and distances and parts the Son from the Father, he answers and puts these men
to shame saying in the second book:
17.1 "Each of the names said by me is inseparable and undivided from the
neighbor. I said Father, and before I add the Son, I signified this one too in
the Father; I added Son, even if I had not said the Father beforehand, he
would wholly have been taken beforehand in the Son; I added Holy Spirit, but
at once I also fitted whence and through whom he came. But they do not know
that neither has a father been alienated from a son inasmuch as he is father,
for the name is a preliminary of the conjunction, nor has the Son been settled
away from the Father. For the address Father makes clear the communion, and
the Spirit is in their hands, able to be deprived neither of the one who sends
17.2 nor of the one who bears. How then do I, using these names, suppose that
these have been parted and marked off from one another completely?" And after
a little he adds saying: "Thus we both widen the monad into the triad
undivided, and we sum up the triad again undiminished into the monad."
18.1 Then also as they blame him as saying the Son is one of the originate
things and not 18.1 consubstantial with the Father, he himself again in the
first book refutes such men saying: "Only, I having said that certain things
originate and certain things made are to be understood, I spoke examples of
such things as more useless from a running-over, since I said neither that the
plant is <the same> as the husbandman nor the skiff as the shipwright. 18.2
Then I lingered upon the fitting and more appropriate and went through more
concerning the truer things, having found in addition various proofs, which I
also wrote [to you] through another letter, in which I also refuted the charge
they bring forward, being a lie against me, as not saying that Christ is
consubstantial with God. For if I say I have not found this name nor read it
anywhere in the holy Scriptures, yet my arguments that follow, which they have
kept silent, are not out of tune with this mind. For I also set beside a human
offspring as being clearly of the same kind, having said that parents are
wholly only other than the children, because they themselves would not be the
children, or it is necessary that there be neither 18.3 parents nor children.
And the letter, as I said before, I do not have to produce because of the

19.1 Who hearing these things will not thereafter vote madness against those
who suppose that Dionysius thinks the things of Arius? For behold through
these he openly tramples all his heresy with the reasonings of the truth. For
"he was not before he was begotten" and "there was once when he was not" he
takes away by the account of the radiance and saying that the Father of this
one was never barren, and he takes away their "from things that are not" in
saying that the Word is as a river from a spring and a shoot from a root and a
child from a parent and light from light and life from life; and their fencing
off and dividing the Word from God he slanders, himself saying that the triad
is summed up undivided and undiminished into a monad, and their saying that
the Son is alien from the essence of the Father he tramples outright, saying
that the Son is consubstantial 19.2 with the Father. At which one might even
wonder at the shamelessness of the impious, how though Dionysius, whom they
say is of one mind with themselves, says the Son consubstantial, they go about
buzzing as gnats, as if the synod did not write the 19.3 consubstantial
rightly. For if Dionysius is a friend to them, let them not deny the things of
the like-minded; but if they think it has not been said rightly, how do they
babble that Dionysius who says this thinks their things? Especially that he
does not appear saying these things from a side-work, but having first written
other letters he refutes those who accused him as not saying the Son
consubstantial with the Father as lying, and he strikes aside those who think
he says the Word originate, that he did not think thus as they suppose, but if
he also said the expressions, he says he said them for this, only to show that
it is not the Father but the Son who put on the originate and created and made
body; wherefore the Son is also said to have come to be and to have been made
and to have been created.
20.1 Indeed since he was taken beforehand in writing such expressions, he bids
a long farewell to the Arians, and from the hearers he demands a good
conscience, putting forward the difficulty or perhaps even the ungraspable of
the things sought, that they may judge not the expressions but the mind of the
writer, and especially when the greater part bears witness to this one's
thinking. He himself then also said that "I spoke the examples of such things
as more useless from a running-over, the plant and the husbandman; but I
lingered upon the more appropriate and went through more 20.2 concerning the
truer things." And he who says this shows that it is truer to say the Son
eternal and from the Father than originate. For from this the bodily of the
Lord is signified, but from those the eternity of his Godhead. In these then
he also confirms that those who charged him as not saying him consubstantial
with the Father have been refuted by him, not simply, but with thought and
true proof saying: "even if I did not find this expression in the Scriptures,
yet gathering the mind from the Scriptures themselves, 20.3 I knew that being
Son and Word he would not be foreign to the essence of the Father." For that
he does not think the Son to be a creature nor a work (for

they have also babbled him in this), thus in the second book he says: "But if
some one of the slanderers, since I said God maker and craftsman of all
things, should think I say it also of Christ, let him hear me first having
called him Father, in which the Son also has been written besides. For after
saying Father I have added maker; and neither is a father being a maker, if
the one who begot should be heard as father in the proper sense (for we will
work out the breadth of the address Father in what follows), nor is the Father
a maker, if only the handicraftsman should be called maker. For among Greeks
the wise are called makers also of their own words; and the apostle said
makers of law; and they are established makers also of the in-heart things of
virtue or vice, as God said: 'I waited to make judgment, but he made
lawlessness.'"
21.1 Hearing these things one may truly remember the divine saying that says:
"Wherever the impious turns, he vanishes." For behold the impious turning
variously each day vanish, not having even for this a pretext concerning
Dionysius. For he openly teaches that the Son is not a work nor a creature.
And he blames and heals those who slander him as having said God a maker, that
they did not understand him having said 21.2 God first Father, in which the
Son also has been written besides. And saying this he shows that the Son is
not of the made things, nor that God is maker but Father of his own Word. And
since some without conscience brought against him that he said God maker of
Christ, for this reason making a defense variously he says that not even thus
is the word blameworthy. For he says he has said maker because of the flesh,
which the Word took up, 21.3 it being originate. But if someone should suppose
this to have been said also concerning the Word, even thus they ought to have
heard without contention: "For as I do not think the Word a work, I also say
God not maker but Father of him. And even if from a running-over I say God
maker narrating concerning the Son, yet even thus it is possible to make a
defense. For of their own words the wise of the Greeks say makers, though
being fathers of their own words. And the divine Scripture also declares us
makers of the movements from the heart, saying makers of law and of judgment
and of righteousness." So that from every side he shows that the Son is not a
creature nor a work, and himself foreign to the Arian evil opinion.
22.1 For let not some Arian even in this suppose against him as saying thus:
the Son is with the Father, as the names have been joined to one another, but
the things stand very far from one another, and the Son is not always with the
Father, but from when the Son came to be, from that God has been called Father
and thereafter is with him, as this also comes to be in the case of men. But
let him consider and remember the things said before 22.2 and he will see the
faith of Dionysius right. For saying: "For there was not when God was not
Father," and again: "But God is eternal light neither having begun nor ending
ever; therefore eternal also the radiance is set before and is with him,
beginningless and ever-generated, appearing before him"; no one at all ought
to suppose any such things against him. And the examples of the spring and the
river and the root and the shoot and the spirit and the vapor put to shame the
Christ-fighters who babble the contrary against him. 23.1 But since besides
all his own evils Arius has also heaped to himself this little word as from
dung and adds, that "the Word is not proper to the Father, but other is the
Word in God, and this Lord is foreign and alien from the essence of the
Father, and is called Word only by conception and is not by nature and true
Son of God, but this one too is called Son by adoption as a creature," and
saying such things he boasts among the ignorant as having even in these as of
one mind

Dionysius, see also the faith of Dionysius concerning these things and how he
fights against such 23.2 ill-wills of Arius. For he writes in the first book
thus: "It has been said before then that God is a spring of all good things;
and the Son has been written as a river poured forth by him. For Word is an
outflow of mind and, to speak as in the case of men, is channeled from heart
through mouth, the mind leaping forth through tongue becoming other than the
word in the heart. For the one remained having sent forth and is such as he
was, but the other flew out having been sent forth and is carried everywhere;
and thus each is in each being other than the other, and they are one being
two. For thus also the Father and the Son were said to be one and in one
another." 23.3 And in the fourth book he says thus: "For as our mind belches
forth the word from itself, as the prophet said: 'My heart has belched forth a
good word,' and each is other than the other, having obtained a proper place
even separated from the rest, the one in the heart, the other dwelling and
moving upon the tongue and the mouth; yet they have not stood apart nor are
they deprived of one another once for all nor is the mind wordless nor the
word mindless, but the mind makes the word having appeared in it and the word
shows the mind having come to be in it, and the mind is as it were a word
lying within, and the word a mind 23.4 leaping forth. And the mind is
transferred into the word, and the word cycles the mind into the hearers. And
thus the mind through the word is settled in the souls of those who hear,
entering with the word; and the mind is as it were father of the word being
upon itself, and the word as a son of the mind, impossible before that one but
not from somewhere outside having come to be with that one, but having
sprouted from him. Thus the Father, the greatest and universal mind, has first
the Son as Word, interpreter <and> messenger of himself."
24.1 Arius either never heard these things or having heard the ignorant did
not understand. For he would not, if he had understood, have lied so much
against the bishop, but he would wholly have reviled him too as us because of
the hatred toward the truth. For being a Christ-fighter he will not shrink
from persecuting also those who think the things of Christ, as the Lord
himself has said beforehand: "If they persecuted me, 24.2 they will persecute
you also." Or if the champions of the impiety think Dionysius of one mind with
themselves, let them write these things and confess; let them write the vine
and the husbandman, the skiff and the shipwright, and at once let them confess
also what he said making a defense, the consubstantial and that the Son is
from the essence and the eternal and the mind toward the word and the spring
and the river and the others, that even from the difference they may know how
he said those things according to economy, but these as the account of the
pious faith wishes, and thereafter saying these they may take away what they
thought against these very 24.3 things. For what even near of the evils of
Arius does the faith of Dionysius have? Does not Arius say the Word by
conception, but Dionysius a true and by nature Word of God? And the one
alienates the Word from the Father, but the other teaches that he is proper
and undivided from the essence of the Father, as the word is toward the mind
and a river toward 24.4 the spring. If then someone is able to divide and
alienate the word and the mind or to part the river and the spring and having
walled them off to divide them, or to say the river of another essence toward
the spring and to show the water foreign, or dares to divide the radiance from
the light and to say that the radiance is from another essence and not from
the light, let him be mad with Arius. For such a one will appear no longer
even having a human mind. But if nature knows these things undivided and the
offspring of these proper, let no one thereafter still think the things of
Arius nor slander Dionysius, but rather from these let him admire of him both
the clarity of the word and the rightness of the faith.

and other that concerning which John says: "In the beginning was the Word,"
and that there is another proper wisdom in God, and another concerning which
the apostle says "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God," Dionysius

Word'; but he who sent forth the Word was not Word; 'for the Word was toward

was not wisdom; 'for I was,' he says, 'she in whom he 25.2 rejoiced.' Christ
is truth; 'and blessed,' he says, 'the God of the truth.'" Here he overturns

is the Father of the Word Word, nor is that from the Father a work, but a
proper offspring of his essence, and again the Word who has come forth is not
Father nor again is this Word one from many, but he is only Son of the Father,
true and 25.3 genuine by nature, who both now is in him and eternally and

according to each, through what he wrote, he refuted their ill-mind and

26.1 Therefore from these it is shown that the letter to Euphranor and
Ammonius also was written by him not with a bad mind, but according to
economy; for this his defense also shows. And this is truly a persuasive type
toward overturning the madness of Sabellius, so that he who wishes quickly to
refute such men should not make the beginning from the things that signify the
Godhead of the Word (that the Son is Word and wisdom and power, and that "I
and the Father are one," lest those men, misinterpreting the things well said,
furnish such things as a pretext of their shameless contention, hearing that
"I and the Father are one," and: "He who has seen me has seen the Father"),
but put forward the things said humanly concerning the Savior, as he himself
has done, such 26.2 as are the hungering, the being weary, and that he is a
vine and prayed and suffered. For by as much as these things are said as
humble, by so much it is shown that the Father did not become man. For of
necessity the Lord being called vine there is also a husbandman, and he
praying there is the one who hears, and he asking there is the one who gives.
And such things more easily show the madness of the Sabellians, that other is
the one who prays, other the one who hears, and another 26.3 the vine and
another the husbandman. For as many sayings as are said as alienating the Son
from the Father, these are said concerning him because of the flesh which he
wore for us. For the originate things are by nature foreign to God. For this
reason then, since the flesh being originate, the Word became flesh, as John
says, and though by nature proper and undivided from the Father, nevertheless
because of it even the Father is said far from him. For he endures the proper
things of the flesh to be said concerning himself, that it may be shown that
the body was proper not of another but 26.4 of him. And such being the mind
Sabellius will more quickly be refuted, that it is not the Father who became
flesh, but the Word of this one who also redeems this and brings it to the
Father. And having thus refuted and persuaded he will thereafter also
concerning the Godhead of the Word

teach more readily that he is Word and wisdom, Son and power, radiance,
impress. For here again it is necessary to understand that the Word being
there must also be the Father of the Word, and wisdom being there must also be
the parent of this, and radiance being there must also be the light, and thus
the Son and the Father are one.
27.1 These things Dionysius knowing wrote, and in those he muzzled Sabellius,
and in these he overthrew the Arian heresy. For just as the human things of
the Savior overturn Sabellius, so also toward the Ariomaniacs one must make
the refutations against them not from the human things, but from the things
that signify the Godhead of the Word, lest these men too, misinterpreting the
things said because of the body concerning the Lord, suppose the Word to be
such as we men are, and thereafter remain in their own madness. 27.2 But if
these too are taught concerning the Godhead, they will condemn their own
ill-will, and having learned more easily that the Word became flesh they too
thereafter will distinguish 27.3 the human things and the things that fit the
Godhead. And these things being so and Dionysius the bishop having been shown
pious from what he wrote, what will the Ariomaniacs do thereafter? Of whom
then again, having been refuted from these, will they lie? For it is necessary
that they, having fallen from the foundation of the apostles and having an
unsettled mind, seek 27.4 a support and not finding it thereafter lie against
the fathers. But no one will still believe them, even if they force themselves
to slander. For the heresy has been condemned by all, unless perhaps they will
thereafter speak concerning the devil; for he alone is in agreement with them,
or rather he himself is the one who also suggests the heresy to them. Who then
would still name these men, whose leader is the devil, Christians, and not
rather diabolic? That they may be called not only Christ-fighters but also
diabolic, unless perhaps they change and deny the impiety they invented, and
recognize the truth. For this will benefit them, and it befits us to pray thus
concerning all who have been led astray.
