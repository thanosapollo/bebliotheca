* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

What then must one think concerning those who owe great things and beyond
great things, I do not mean gold or silver, but simply whatever one happens to
have received as a kindness from his neighbor; and then they neither attempt
nor even intend to repay such things, but are idle and careless toward doing
what they can and discharging the debt? Or is it clear that they must be
judged base and wicked? For of none of the other wrongs, I think, do we hate
ingratitude less, and we reproach men when, having been treated well, they are
ungrateful toward their benefactors. And not only is that man ungrateful who,
having been treated well, does harm or speaks ill, but also he who is silent
and conceals, handing the favors over to forgetfulness and making them vanish.
And of that savage and inhuman wickedness the examples are very few and
altogether easy to count; but many conceal the appearance of having been
treated well, I do not know with what intent; yet they say they are avoiding
the reputation of some flattery and ignoble adulation. But I, knowing clearly
that these men say nothing sound, nevertheless let them go, and let it stand
that they escape, as they suppose, a reputation of flattery that is not true,
though they have been shown liable at once to many passions and to diseases
altogether shameful and illiberal. For either, not understanding, they are far
too insensible of things of which one ought in no way to be insensible, or,
understanding, they are forgetful of what they ought to remember for all time;
and if they remember and still hang back for any cause whatsoever, they are
cowards and envious by nature and simply hostile to all men, seeing that they
are not willing to be gentle and kindly even to their benefactors; then, if
they must somewhere revile and bite, they look angry and sharp like the
beasts; and as if fleeing a costly outlay they flee true praise, and I do not
know how they blame the good report for noble deeds, when they might examine
this only, whether they honor the truth and hold it of more account than
seeming to grant a favor in their praises. For one cannot even say this, that
good report is a useless thing, either for those on whose behalf it has been
made or again for the others, as many as have been allotted the same station
in life as they and have fallen short of virtue in their actions. For to the
former it is a pleasant thing to hear and it makes them more eager about the
noble and distinguished deeds; and the latter it has urged, by persuasion and
by force, to emulate those things, seeing that not even some of those who went
before were deprived of that which alone it is noble to give and to receive in
public. For to give money into the open and to look about so that as many as
possible will know what was given belongs to a man without taste; nor would
anyone hold out his hands and receive it in the eyes of all, unless he had
altogether shaken off shame and decency of character. And Arcesilaus even in
giving tried to escape the notice of the receiver; but that man understood
from the act who had done it. But of praises it is a thing to be sought to
find as many hearers as possible, and even a few, I think, are welcome. And
Socrates praised many, and Plato and Aristotle; and Xenophon both Agesilaus
the king and Cyrus the Persian, not that ancient one only, but also the one
with whom he campaigned against the King, and in writing the praises he did
not conceal them.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But to me it seems a wonder if we shall praise willingly the men who are noble
and good, and shall not think a good woman worthy of good report, supposing
that virtue belongs to them no less than to men. For if we think she ought to
be temperate and intelligent and such as to assign to each what is according
to worth, and courageous in dangers, and high-minded and liberal, and that all
such things, so to speak, ought to belong to her, shall we then take away the
encomia upon her deeds, fearing the blame that comes from seeming to flatter?
But Homer was not ashamed when he praised Penelope, nor the wife of Alcinous,
nor any other woman who has been outstandingly good or has even laid claim to
virtue in a small degree. Therefore she too did not miss the good report on
this very point. And besides this, shall we accept being treated well and
obtaining some good, small and greater alike, no less from a woman than from a
man, and then hesitate to pay the thanks for it? But perhaps they will say
that even the needing itself is ridiculous and not worthy of a decent and
noble man, and that even Odysseus the wise was ignoble and cowardly, because
he supplicated the king's daughter as she played on the meadow with the
maidens of her age by the banks of the river. Then perhaps they will not even
keep their hands from Athena, the child of Zeus, whom Homer says, likened to a
fair and noble maiden, both led Odysseus on the road that bears to the palace
and became counselor and teacher to him of what he ought to do and say when he
had gone inside, like some orator with finished art singing an encomium of a
queen, beginning from the family above. And the verses about these things
stand for him in this manner: /The mistress first you will meet in the halls,
Arete is the name given her, and she is from the same parents who bore king
Alcinous./ And taking up from above the beginning of the family from
Poseidon, I think, and telling what they did and suffered, and how her
uncle, when her father had perished young and a bridegroom, both married
her and honored her,
/as no other woman is honored upon the earth,/ and of how many things she
obtains /From her dear children and from Alcinous himself,/ and still, I
think, from the senate and the people, who look upon her as a god as she goes
through the city, he set as the end of the praises a thing to be sought by man
and woman, saying /For she herself is not at all lacking in good
understanding,/ and that she knew well how to judge, for those for whom she
thinks well, and to dissolve with justice the complaints that arise among the
citizens toward one another. If then you supplicate her and happen to find her
well-disposed, he said to him, /Then there is hope for you to see your friends
and to come to your high-roofed house;/ and he was persuaded by the counsel.
Shall we then still need greater images and clearer proofs in order to escape
the suspicion that comes from seeming to flatter? Shall we not already,
imitating that wise and divine poet, praise Eusebia the best, desiring to go
through a praise worthy of her, and content if we should even moderately
attain such fair and many pursuits? And of the goods that belong to her, of
temperance and justice, or gentleness and equity, or of her friendship toward
her husband, or of her high-mindedness about money, or of the honor toward her
household and kin. And it is fitting, I think, following as if in footprints
what has already been said, to make the order of the good report in this way,
rendering the same to her, mentioning, as is reasonable, both country and
fathers, and how she was married and to whom, and all the rest in the same
manner as they.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Concerning her country, then, though I have many august things to say, some I
think I shall pass over because of their antiquity; for they appear to be not
far from the myths; of which sort is also what is said about the Muses, that
they were, I suppose, from Pieria, and did not come from Helicon to Olympus,
summoned to their father. This then, and if there is any other such thing
belonging rather to myth than to argument, must be left aside; but to say a
few things of what is not known to all is perhaps not out of place, nor away
from the present speech. For they say that the descendants of Heracles, the
sons of Temenus, settled the land of the Macedonians, who, holding the Argive
allotment and being at faction, made the colony the end of their strife and
rivalry toward one another; then, having taken Macedonia and leaving behind a
prosperous family, they continued kings from kings, succeeding to the honor as
if to an inheritance. To praise all of them, then, is neither true nor, I
think, easy. But though many good men arose and left behind very fair
memorials of the Greek manner, Philip and this man's son excelled in virtue
all who of old ruled Macedonia and Thrace, and I at least think also all who
ruled Lydians or Medes and Persians and Assyrians, except only the son of
Cambyses, who transferred the kingship from the Medes to the Persians. For the
former first attempted to increase the power of the Macedonians, and having
subdued the greater part of Europe he made the sea his boundary toward the
east and toward the south, and toward the north, I think, the Ister, and
toward the west the Orician people. And this man's son in turn, reared under
the wise man of Stageira, so far excelled all the others in greatness of soul,
and moreover surpassing his own father in generalship and in courage and in
the other virtues, that he did not think it worth living for himself unless he
should rule all men together and all nations. Therefore he went through all
Asia subduing it, and first of men he worshipped the rising sun, but when he
had set out for Europe, so that by taking in what remained he might become
master of all earth and sea, destiny overtook him in Babylon. And the
Macedonians ruled all the cities and nations of which they had obtained
possession under him. Is it then still necessary to make clear by greater
proofs that Macedonia was of old both famous and great? And the strongest part
of this very land is that city which they raised, when the Thessalians, I
think, had fallen, named after the victory against them.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

And concerning these things I no longer need to speak at greater length.

As for nobility, what trouble would we still have, seeking a clearer and more
evident proof? For she is the daughter of a man thought worthy to hold the
office that gives its name to the year, which of old was strong and was named
kingship outright, but changed the name because of those who did not use the
power rightly; and now already, as the power fails, since the affairs of the
constitution have shifted toward monarchy, the honor by itself, stripped of
all the rest, seems to be a counterweight to all strength, laid up for private
men as a prize and a reward of virtue or of loyalty or of some goodwill and
service toward those who rule the whole, or of a splendid action, and for
kings set upon the goods they already have as an image and an ornament; for of
the other names and functions, as many as preserve some paltry and dim image
of that ancient constitution, either they have altogether looked down on them
because of their strength and condemned them, or, accepting them at least,
they enjoy the titles through life; but of this one alone, I think, they
neither looked down on the office from the beginning, and they rejoice even in
obtaining it for a year; and there is no private man nor king who is or has
been who did not think it a thing to be sought to be named consul. And if,
because that man obtained it first and became founder of the glory for the
family, someone supposes him to have less than the others, he is much deceived
and does not understand; for it is altogether better, I think, and more august
to furnish to descendants a beginning of such distinction than to receive it
from ancestors. Since even to become founder of a very great city is better
than to be a citizen, and to receive any good is in every way inferior to
giving. And children seem to receive from fathers, and citizens from cities,
as it were certain starting-points toward glory. But whoever from himself
gives back again to ancestors and fatherland a greater ground of honor, making
that more splendid and more august, and showing the fathers more famous, this
man seems to leave no one a contest in the account of nobility; nor is there
anyone who will say he has been better than that man; for from good men a good
man must be born. And he who from famous men has become more famous, fortune
breathing to the same point as virtue, this man gives no one room to doubt
whether he reasonably lays claim to nobility.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

But Eusebia, concerning whom the speech is, has been the child of a consul,
and is the wife of a king who is brave, temperate, intelligent, just, good and
gentle and high-minded, who, when he had recovered the rule that was ancestral
to him, taking it from the one who had seized it by force, and needed a
marriage for the birth of children who would inherit the honor and the power,
judged her worthy of the partnership, having already become master of almost
the whole inhabited world. And yet how could anyone seek a greater witness
than this? Not only concerning her nobility, but simply concerning all the
goods which, I think, she who was to be joined to so great a king ought to
bring from home as a dowry: right education, well-tuned intelligence, the
prime and bloom of body, and beauty so great that the other maidens are
hidden, just as, I think, about the full moon the bright stars, outshone, hide
their form. For not one of these seems sufficient for partnership with a king,
but all together, as if some god were shaping a fair and temperate bride for a
good king, having come together into the same from far off and not drawn in
from the eyes, led the bridegroom very blessed. For beauty, I think, deprived
of the help from family and of the other goods, has not even the power to
persuade an unbridled private man to light the marriage torch, but both
together have often fitted a marriage, yet when they fall short of the harmony
and grace from character they have not appeared very much to be sought.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Knowing these things clearly, I would reasonably say that the temperate king,
having deliberated many times, chose the marriage, learning, I think, by
inquiry as much as one ought to learn about her by hearing, and judging her
good order from her mother; concerning whom why must we spend time saying the
rest, as if we did not have a separate encomium of her concerning whom the
speech is to go through? But so much perhaps is neither much nor laborious to
say or to hear, that her family is thoroughly Greek, of the Greeks of the
first rank, and her city the metropolis of Macedonia, and her temperance
beyond both Evadne the wife of Capaneus and that Thessalian Laodameia. For
those women, having been robbed of husbands who were fair and young and still
bridegrooms, by the force of jealous daemons or by the threads of the fates,
looked down on living because of their love; but she, when destiny overtook
her wedded husband, sitting by her children worked for herself so great a fame
for temperance that, while to Penelope, though her husband was still living
and wandering, the young men came to woo from Ithaca and Samos and Dulichium,
for her no man fair and tall, or strong and rich, ever endured to come to
speech about these things; but the king judged the daughter worthy to dwell
with him, and he made the marriage splendidly after the trophies, feasting
nations and cities and peoples.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

And if anyone longs to hear those things, how the bride was summoned from
Macedonia with her mother, and what the manner of the procession was, of
chariots and horses and vehicles of every kind worked in gold and silver and
orichalc with the best art, let him know that he desires very childish
hearings; for just as, I think, some cithara-player skilled in the art; let
him be, if you will, this Terpander, or that man of Methymna, whom the story
has it used a divine convoy and met with a dolphin more music-loving than
those sailing with him, and was carried to the Laconian cape; for he charmed,
I think, the luckless sailors by all that he worked from his art, but they
looked down on that art itself and made no account of the music; if then
someone, choosing the strongest of those two men and rendering the adornment
about the body fitting to the art, should then lead him into a theater of men
and women and children of every kind, differing in nature and age and in the
other pursuits, would you not think that the children, and as many of the men
and women as are such, looking to the dress and the cithara, would be terribly
struck by the sight, and that the more ignorant of the men and of the women,
except a very few, the whole crowd, would judge the notes by pleasure and
pain, but that a musical man, who knows thoroughly the laws of the art, would
neither badly endure the melodies mixed for the sake of pleasure, and would be
displeased both if he should corrupt the modes of the music and if he should
not use the harmonies as he ought, nor following the laws of the true and
divine music? But seeing him abide by what has been established and work in
the spectators not a counterfeit pleasure but a pure and unmixed one, he goes
away praising this man and struck with wonder that he has been with the
theater with art, doing no wrong to the Muses. And he thinks the one who
praises the purple robe and the cithara talks nonsense and is foolish; and if
he tells such things at greater length, adorning them with a most pleasant
diction and smoothing out the paltry and ignoble character of the tales, he
thinks him more ridiculous than those who attempt to turn millet-grains on the
lathe, as they say, I think, Myrmecides setting himself against the art of
Pheidias.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Therefore we too will not willingly subject ourselves to these charges,
singing this long catalogue of costly garments and gifts of every kind and
necklaces and crowns from the king, nor how the peoples met her greeting and
rejoicing, nor all that was splendid and to be sought along that road and was
so thought. But when she had gone inside the palace and had been thought
worthy of this title, what was her first deed, and again the second, and upon
it the third, and very many from there? For not even if I should be very
willing to speak and to compose long books about these things do I suppose I
would suffice for the multitude of the deeds which have borne witness to her
more splendidly of prudence and gentleness and temperance and humanity, and of
equity and liberality and the other virtues, than the present speech about her
now attempts to make clear and to teach those who have long known them through
the deeds. Yet since that has appeared difficult, or rather impossible, it is
not altogether right to be silent about all of them, but to try to speak about
them according to our power, and to make as a sign of her prudence and of all
the rest of virtue that she disposed her husband toward herself as it is right
for a fair and noble woman.

So I, thinking many other things of Penelope worthy of praises, wonder at this
among the most, that she so far persuaded her husband to cherish and love her,
looking down, as they say, on marriages with daemons, and holding in no less
dishonor the kinship of the Phaeacians. And yet they all were in love with
him, Calypso and Circe and Nausicaa; and their palaces were very fair, with
certain gardens and parks planted in them with trees very wide-spreading and
shady, and meadows bursting with varied flowers and with soft grass: /And four
fountains in a row flowed with white water;/ and about the house a cultivated
vine was in bloom, in the prime, I think, of the noble grape, heavy with
clusters; and among the Phaeacians there were other such things, except that,
being more costly, as made, I think, with art, they had less share of the
grace of things that grow of themselves and seemed less lovely than those. And
of the luxury in turn and of the wealth and moreover of the peace and quiet
about those islands, whom do you think would not have been overcome, after
enduring so many toils and dangers and still expecting to suffer more terrible
things, some on the sea and some in the house itself, about to contend alone
against a hundred young men well in their prime, which not even at Troy ever
happened to that man? If then someone should ask Odysseus in play somehow
thus: Why ever, O wisest orator or general or whatever one must name you, did
you endure so many toils willingly, when it was possible to be prosperous and
happy, and perhaps even immortal if one must trust the promises of Calypso,
but you, choosing the worse before the better, have added so many toils to
yourself, and were not willing even to remain in Scheria, when it was possible
somewhere there to cease from the wandering and to be rid of the dangers; but
you decided for us to campaign at the house and to accomplish certain contests
and another going abroad, not at all, as is likely, less toilsome than the
former nor lighter. What then do you think he would have to say to this? Would
it not be that, wishing to be with Penelope, he supposed he was bringing her
the contests and the campaigns as graceful tales? This is why he has made his
mother also advise him to remember all the sights he saw and the hearings he
heard, /that you may tell it hereafter to your wife,/ she says. And he,
forgetting nothing, as soon as he arrived and was mastering with justice the
young men reveling at the palace, told her all together, all that he did and
all that he endured, and if he intended to accomplish anything else persuaded
by the oracles; and he made not even one thing a secret toward her, but
thought it right that she become partner of the counsels and consider together
and find out together what must be done. Does this seem to you a small
encomium of Penelope, or has some other wife already, surpassing her virtue,
being the wife of a king brave and high-minded and temperate, created so great
a goodwill for herself in her husband, mixing with the friendship breathed
upon them by the Loves that which is borne from virtue as a divine stream upon
good and noble souls? For these are two jars of friendship, from which she,
drawing in equal measure, has become partner of his counsels and, the king
being by nature gentle and good and reasonable, she calls him more fittingly
toward what he is born for, and turns justice toward pardon. So that no one
could say for whom this empress has been, in justice perhaps or even against
justice, a cause of punishment and chastisement, small or greater.

At Athens, then, they say, when they used the ancestral customs and lived
obeying their own laws, inhabiting a great and populous city, if ever the
votes of the judges came out equal for the defendants against the prosecutors,
the vote of Athena, set upon the one who was about to lose the case, released
both from the charge, the one bringing the accusation from seeming to be a
sycophant, and the other, as is likely, from seeming to be liable to the
wicked deed. This law, being humane and graceful, she makes milder as it is
preserved in the trials which the king judges. For wherever the defendant
comes near to obtaining an equal share in the votes, she persuades, adding her
request and supplication on his behalf, that he be released from the charge
altogether. And he grants such things willingly with a willing spirit, and
does not, as Homer says Zeus, forced by his wife, agrees to whatever he
concedes, give willingly with an unwilling spirit. And perhaps it is not out
of place to concede such things hard and barely against insolent and boastful
men. But not even if some are very fit to suffer harm and to be punished must
these be destroyed at all costs; which this empress also, understanding, never
commanded any evil, neither any other nor chastisement nor vengeance, to be
brought not only upon anyone's kingdom or city, but not even upon one house of
the citizens. And I would add, being very confident that I say nothing false,
that she cannot even be accused in the case of one man or one woman of any
chance misfortune to anyone, but of the goods, as many as she does and has
done and to whom, I would gladly count up for you the most, reporting them
each by each, that this man holds his ancestral lot because of her, and that
man was released from punishment, though he had become liable to the laws,
another escaped a sycophancy, having come near to danger, and countless men
obtained honor and office. And of all men there is no one who will say I am
lying in these things, even if I should not catalogue the men by name. But I
hesitate, lest I seem to some to be casting their misfortunes in their teeth,
and not to be writing a praise of her goods but a catalogue of the misfortunes
of others. Yet to furnish none of so many deeds, nor to bring a proof into the
open, seems somehow empty and leads the praise into disbelief. Therefore,
having begged off those things, as many as it is without envy for me to say
and fair for her to hear, I would already speak the noble things.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

For when, beginning her deeds, she set the goodwill of her husband as a most
far-shining face, according to the wise Pindar, she at once filled all her
family and kin with honor, setting those already known and older upon greater
actions and showing them blessed and to be sought, and she made them friends
to the king and gave them the beginning of the present good fortune. And even
if they seem to anyone, as is indeed true, honored because of themselves, to
her, I think, he will still add the praise; for it is clear that she appears
to assign it not by the partnership of family only, but far more by virtue;
than which I do not know how anyone will speak a greater encomium. Concerning
these, then, such things have come about. But as many as were still unknown
through youth and needed in any way to become known, to these she distributed
lesser honors. And she left off nothing in doing good to them all. And she did
not do so many goods to her kin only, but if she learned that a
guest-friendship had existed for anyone toward her fathers, she did not leave
it unprofitable for those who had acquired it, and she honors these also, I
think, like kin, and to all whom she thought friends of her father she
assigned wonderful prizes of the friendship.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

But I, since I see the speech needing proofs as in a lawcourt, will myself
furnish myself to you as witness of these things for it and as one who
praises; but so that you may never be disturbed, looking askance at my
testimony before you have heard the words, I swear to you that I will say
nothing false nor fabricated; and you would have believed even an unsworn man
that I say all things not for the sake of flattery. For I already have, God
giving and the king, all the goods, she too, I think, joining in the
eagerness, for which someone flattering would let go all words, so that if I
were speaking before these things I would perhaps have had to dread the unjust
suspicion; but now, having come into this fortune and recalling her deeds
toward myself, I will furnish you a sign of my gratitude, and a true witness
of her deeds. For I learn that Darius also, while he was still a spear-bearer
of the monarch of the Persians, met the Samian guest-friend about Egypt as he
was fleeing his own land, and having received a certain purple cloak as a
gift, which he greatly desired, later gave in return the tyranny of the
Samians, when, I think, he had become master of all Asia. If then I too,
having received many things from her when it was still possible to live in
quiet, and the greatest things through her from the noble and high-minded
king, should admit that I fall short of giving back the equal; for she has, I
think, all things, having received them from him who has granted them to us
also; yet by wishing the memory of her deeds to become immortal for her and by
reporting these things to you I shall perhaps appear not more ungrateful than
the Persian, if one must judge looking to the intention, and not by him to
whom fortune granted to repay the benefaction many times over.

What then I say I have been so greatly treated well, and in return for what I
confess myself for all time a debtor of thanks to her, you have been very
eager to hear. And I will not conceal it:

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

For this king, having been gentle to me almost from a child still an infant,
surpassed all ambition, both snatching me from dangers so great that not even
a man well in his prime would escape them, unless he met with some divine and
impossible salvation, and then taking back with justice the house that had
been seized as if in a desert by one of the powerful and showing it rich
again. And I would have other deeds of his toward myself to tell you, worthy
of much thanks, on account of which, furnishing myself well-disposed and
faithful to him for all time, I perceived him recently, I do not know from
what cause, being harsher. But she, when she first heard not the name of any
wrong, but of a vain suspicion otherwise, thought it right to test it
thoroughly and not to admit beforehand nor to receive a false and unjust
slander, and she did not leave off requesting these things until she brought
me into the king's sight and made me obtain speech; and she rejoiced with me
as I was released from every unjust charge, and when I desired to go home
again she furnished a safe convoy, having first persuaded the king to permit
it. And when a daemon, the very one who seemed to have engineered the former
things for me, or some strange chance, cut off this road, she sends me to see
Greece, having asked this from the king on my behalf even when I was already
abroad as the favor, since she had learned that I delight in discourses and
understood that the place is fitting for education. And I then prayed that God
give many good things to her and first, as is likely, to the king, because
they granted me, longing for and loving the true fatherland, to see it; for we
who dwell about Thrace and Ionia are offspring of Greece, and whoever of us is
not too unfeeling longs to greet the fathers and to embrace the land itself.
Which indeed had long been, as is likely, a thing longed for by me, and I
wished this to be mine rather than much gold and silver. For I say that the
company of good men, when weighed against any amount of gold whatsoever, pulls
down the scale and does not permit the temperate judge even to set it at a
small tilt.

And for the sake of education and philosophy the affairs of Greece have now
suffered, I think, something like the Egyptian myths and accounts. For the
Egyptians also say that the Nile among them is, among the rest, savior and
benefactor of the land and keeps off for them the destruction by fire,
whenever the sun through certain long periods, coming together with or
associating with noble stars, fills the air with fire and sets all things
ablaze. For it has not the power, they say, to make vanish or to consume the
springs of the Nile. Therefore philosophy has not altogether gone from the
Greeks either, nor has it left Athens nor Sparta nor Corinth; and least of
all, because of these springs, is Argos much-thirsting; for there are many in
the city itself, and many also before the city about that ancient Mases; but
Sicyon has Peirene itself and not Corinth. And of Athens the streams are many
and pure and native, and many flow in from outside and are brought, no less
honored than those within; and they love and cherish them, wishing to be rich
in that of which almost alone the wealth is a thing to be sought.

And we, what then have we suffered? And what speech do we now intend to

let go even a little of those being praised, since you yourself, I think, do

arguments, wishing to show of how many goods she has been the cause to us by

laid upon me, who loved the work very well and was terribly in love with the
thing, but had been left behind, I do not know in what manner, so that it
happened to be a name only and a word deprived of the deed. But she honored
even the name; for another cause I neither find myself nor can learn from
anyone else, for which she has become so eager a helper to me and averter of
evil and savior, having worked with much toil that the goodwill of the noble
king remain for us unimpaired and unharmed, than which I have never been
caught thinking any of human things a greater good, not the gold upon the
earth and under the earth as of equal worth, nor as much silver as there now
is under the rays of the sun, and if ever other should be added, the greatest
mountains, I think, with their very rocks and trees changing into this nature,
nor the greatest rule nor any other of all things; for from that goodwill
these things have come to me, many and as many as no one would have hoped,
though I did not much need many things nor feed myself with such hopes.

But true goodwill cannot be exchanged for gold, nor could anyone buy it from
there; it comes by some divine and stronger fate, when good men join in the
eagerness. Which indeed I had from the king as a child according to God, and
when it had all but gone it was saved again, the empress defending and keeping
off the false and strange suspicions. Which, when she had altogether dissolved
them, using my life as a clear proof, and when the king was calling me again
from Greece I obeyed, did she then leave me there, as no longer needing much
help, since nothing difficult or suspected was in the way? And how would I be
doing holy things if I were silent and concealing things so clear and august?
For when this decision of the King was being ratified over me she was
especially glad and chimed in musically, bidding me take courage and neither,
fearing the greatness of what was given, to refuse the taking, nor, using a
rustic and stubborn frankness, to dishonor cheaply the necessary request of
him who had worked so many goods. And I obeyed, not enduring this at least
very gladly, but otherwise I knew very well that to disobey was hard. For
those who have the power to do whatever they wish with force, surely when they
request they suffice to put to shame and to persuade.

Therefore when, I having been persuaded, there came about also a change of
dress and of attendance and of the accustomed pastimes and of the dwelling
itself and of the manner of life, all full of pomp and dignity from the former
things that were small, as is likely, and paltry, my soul was disturbed by the
unfamiliarity, not at all struck by the greatness of the present goods; for
almost from ignorance I did not even think these things great, but certain
powers very useful to those who use them rightly, but to those who err about
the use harmful and causes of countless misfortunes to many houses and cities.
And I had suffered something like a man very inexperienced in charioteering
and not even willing to take a share of this art, and then forced to manage
the chariot of a fair and noble charioteer, who keeps many pairs, and many, I
think, four-horse teams, and has mounted upon all, and through nobility of
nature and exceeding strength holds, I think, the reins of all firmly, even if
he should stand upon one rail, yet not always remaining upon it, but often
transferring himself from here to there and changing car from car, if ever he
should perceive the horses laboring or even growing insolent, and among these
chariots possessing a four-horse team insolent from ignorance and boldness,
pressed by the continuous hardship and no more forgetting its boldness,
growing wild, and always being provoked by the misfortunes toward insolence

painful things in every way, honoring and granting favors in deeds and in

I came into her sight, seemed to see as it were an image of temperance set up

wondered greatly and was struck, seeming clearly to have heard temperance

from old age alone, experience, through which the elder has something wiser
than the young to say, it has given to those of the young who are not idle.
And there is, I think, in them also a training toward a noble character, if
someone should know how, setting up the best men and speeches and deeds as a
craftsman sets up archetypes, to shape his own mind already toward these and
to make his speeches like them. From which, if he should not be left behind by
a very great amount, and should attain even a little of the likeness, he would

both make among them a pastime not without the Muses and, when on campaign,
wish to carry these too as necessary food; and the measure of the multitude of
what is carried is the occasion.

But perhaps one must not now write the praise of those things, nor of all the
