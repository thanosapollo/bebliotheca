* In Mariam Sclerenam
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Verses of Psellos, an epitaph for the Augusta. Now a cosmic storm, now a gale
common to all, now a ceaseless surge of calamity has been raised; now, O
heaven, groan aloud from on high, and you, O earth, cry out from below at so
dread a passion. Now, you that bear the light, send blood instead of light;
now, both moon and choir of the stars, pour out fountains of tears instead of
rays; and let the air and the sea, surge as it does, put on haze and deep
gloom. For the Augusta, noble in birth and in life, the shining dignity of the
palace, the honored step, the first foundation, the adornment of character,
the brightness of morals, the living statue, the solemnity of minds, the
pleasant flower, the field of delight, the graceful dwelling of the Graces,
she who alone among men had and bore all beauty and showed it to her lord and
delighted him and shone it forth like a tree blossoming from noetic valleys,
alas, alas, was harvested root and all before her season. O tree, what a tree
you have cut down, O Charon! Its root below still moist, its fruit sweetening
the heart, its leaf as though it would never wither with time; and it
flourished not in spring and summer only, but blossomed in every season with a
fruit-bearing nature. O what a light-bearing star has been hidden, O what a
lamp that lit the earth has gone out! For she alone was a bright star set
between earth and heaven, full of the flower of pleasure, radiant in body,
all-luminous of heart, gleaming with virtues more than pearls, shining with
beauties more than gold, making the female as it were male in character,
bearing a comeliness that reached even to the sight. O you who conquered all
things by your own nature and moved all things to lamentation, O you who
charmed all things with your beauty and nature and have filled all things now
with bitter gloom, O you who illumined the earth with your splendors and have
now dimmed all creation, O you who hid in the fair forms of the earth all its
beauties and the bright things of heaven, and are now hidden beneath a
death-bearing stone, O all things full of utter horror, brimming with tears
and a strange mourning, no longer having the light of your sight, no longer
bearing the charm of your lyre's voice. Who will give me a bloody fountain,
who an abyss of tears that never empties, that I may bewail the common gale
and show in bloody tears the inflammation of my heart's passion? O you who
have lived in vain in this life, O you who have gained in vain a shining rank
and seized the summit of fortune's glory, O you who appeared in vain a bright
sight and flashed on all with strange beauties, hidden beneath a hateful stone
beyond all hope. But the Lord, burned with longing for you and mindful of your
greatest virtues, holding in his heart the sharing of one breath, and bearing
in his mind your supreme excellence of soul, and bearing in mind the bitter
punishment which you were enduring wretchedly in a foreign land, and your
comfort, your delight, how you were a servant to him in your ways, and how a
sea of gifts, a supplier of delight and a handmaid of nourishment, remembering
all these good things, hastened to build up an equal return of gratitude,
hastened to conquer you in the multitude of graces, having found so great a
supply of hand and of power, and honored you with all things fair and
precious, with much wealth and all-beautiful splendor; with pearls he hid you,
and with stones more; he raised you to the height of the highest fortune, he
made you a common guardian of his life; then, pondering greater steps, he
raised you again toward greater glory and transferred you to renown, crowning
you with the honor of shining rank, and honors as Augusta her who is Augusta
by race and by character, and Augusta by fortune and by life. You were to him
a sweetening light, his eye, life, breath, his all, glory and power, seen,
speaking and moving. For beauty was present in you, and character and grace,
and an ensouled sweetness blossomed in your nature; and every beholder
smiled unawares, as if filled full of some grace; and moving his hand, and at
the same time his head, he would say: "Blameless is he who longs for your
sight." Delighting in this, the Lord, as he looked upon her, and alone
gleaning the meadow of life, had unceasing joy in his heart. And if the sun's
light was hidden by a cloud, he saw your shining in place of the light-bearer;
and if the darkness did not show the moon, he had you, the moon, as a
deliverer from gloom; a haze held him down from despondency, and beholding
your starry sight he rejoiced. You were truly an Orpheus, but rather more a
Siren, sending forth an all-fair melody; and charming from every side and
enchanting and sending toward joy and delight and loosing the sieges of
afflictions, thus you conquered the Lord again with bright recompenses of
gifts of the soul, and more than all you prevailed over the heart. And all ran
together to you rather than to Orpheus with insatiable longing, and everyone
who saw stood amazed at beholding; and if some modesty did not hide your
sight, some other new story would have been invented, how the Augusta rivets
the beholder with her strange beauty and makes him a stone. But Charon was
uncharmed by your melody, and the end was unyielding and alone. Alas, alas,
what a discourse do I now introduce? How do I move and speak and write? Rocks,
be rent with mournful stroke; mountains, be torn asunder with a lamenting
strain; groan, O earth, and cry out from the depths; and if you cannot groan
by natural reason, send forth some sound from your abysses; beat the trees,
such as have bright tresses, and cut off every blossom as if locks of hair.
For the Augusta breathes faintly, dimly, most weakly, even before the end; and
the musical and god-inspired mouth the last speechlessness of silence has
seized; all the beauty, the sweet pleasantness, the ensouled grace of roses
upon her lips now remains pale and bloodless. That mind has perished before
the end, the compact of her wits has run away, and the eyelids of her shining
eyes have sunk, grown slack, before her death. Laments circle round, and
groans and wails, the beating of cheeks and the tearing of hair by her own
mother, by the beloved kinswoman, by friends and intimates and relatives and
acquaintances; and all these are deaf to her who lies before them. But the
mother, already overcome by her grief and dying together with her daughter in
all things, since she saw the fair nightingale, the musical one, silent with a
lifeless strain, her soul breaking apart and breathing her last, like a bird
consumed for its children, screeching and wailing and lowing and striking her
hair on every side with dust, uttered such words with groans: "O sweet child,
child full of delight, offspring of my most wretched entrails, who has
snatched you now from my bosom? Who cut you down like an untimely ear of
grain, who harvested you like an unripe cluster? Who has quenched my noetic
lamp and darkened the light of my eyes, alas? O sole prop of my aged flesh,
hand, eye and foot and fair staff, soul, breath and life, mind and heart,
where do you depart now, leaving your mother behind? O double wave of
unmasterable calamities, you bore the loss of a dearest husband before, the
cutting of a string, alas, and I was half-dead as to life; but in you I still
lived, and now, O my grief, I am deprived even of you, of breath and heart.
And now I am breathless and lifeless as to life. I, O daughter, had hopes of
being nurtured in old age by you, dearest one, in your bosom and in your dear
arms to let go the last breath of life. But you, alas, are dead to me, you who
deserved to live and who long ago awaited death. Why indeed have I not died
before you, child, old, all-enduring, wholly made of wrinkles? O wretched one
of ill motherhood, pitiable womb, even if at once you seemed most fortunate,
having brought forth a gilded dove. Alas for my birth-pangs, alas for my
all-wretched childbearing. What shall I first bewail and weep, and what shall
I leave, daughter, unwept, unlamented, without tears, of your graces? Your
inborn beauty of heart? Your outward comeliness? Your very wits? Your
sympathy, your tender-hearted character? Your most exact care for me? You have
sinned a sin, O Charon, sending my dearest before me to Hades, a stranger to
me. For if you were permitted to harvest mortals, do the work of a
harvester: reap the white hairs as a white ear of grain of life. Why do you
cut the unripe before their season? But if you harvest at random, as you will,
even if you have cut me up by the root just now, take me and gather me with my
daughter to the tomb, and let the mound be of your man-slaying harvest, mother
and child together, an untimely ear of grain." So the mother, moved with a
mother's feeling, cried aloud, groaning from the depths, sending fountains of
blood-fouled tears and wetting the bed and washing her dearest with her
natural washings at the last. But the kinsman Romanos of her who lay there,
the glory of the senate, the renown of the consistory, a man in all things by
nature and by heart, young in body but old in wits, wept in turn the more on
the other side, roaring from the depths like a lion and putting a great sound
into his groans, and speaking as it were to her who lay there breathing her
last: "O all things to me, Lady, glory and power, common prop of the race,
common also in grace, mainstay of my life, pillar of my life, O courage, hope,
pillar of my house, O all these things and more longed for, the lamp of my
boldness has gone out, you have gone out, O ornament of the inhabited world;
the hand of the poor has at last run away, the common harbor of those tossed
by storms; by the assault of death you have perished. All things of my
delights are gone to ruin, all things once fortunate in my life have undergone
a most wretched reversal. I live in vain, left alone by you. Let someone give
either a drug or a cutting sword, that I may breathe out my last with my
beloved. I long for Hades in place of this life; that darkness is better than
the light-bearer. For it holds, as it were, the moon of this life, even if it
does not outshine the lower regions that remain; with your shadow I shall lie,
some other shadow dearer than all, perhaps a voiceless image. O unhappy
great-grandfathers, grandfathers and fathers, who saw many storms of affairs
and were tossed together like a wave in life. O unhappy all of us, but I the
more, deprived of the light of my eyes and of my heart, and of life, and
rather of the soul that breathes soul into me, living wretchedly, a breathless
corpse breathing. If anyone bore the cutting of the Lord's limb, I bore the
calamity with a measured grief; and if anyone bereft me of all substance, I
would not grieve at all, for all is dust; and if he drove me to a foreign land
from my homeland, or brought against me every other violence, plunging me into
the deep and shutting me in darkness, I would cheerfully have chosen your
death; only, do not cover her, O earth, until you seize me, a willing corpse.
For those whom one womb bore as kin, one tomb shall hide in the common end."
Weaving together these laments and flowing with an unchecked stream of tears,
he pictured the flood-tide of the sea; and holding with his hands the girdle
that bound her round about, as a shade of her flesh, he washed it with tears
like a shower of rain, looking upon it closely, and setting his heart upon it
and moving both, that senseless nature, into lamentation, into unmeasured
streams of tears. These, then, lamented in turn so, and answered with their
groans fitly; but the Lord, the bright light-bearer of the earth, bearing in
his soul the wave of calamity, the all-at-once loss, turning it in his mind,
and his thoughts whirling with the passion, how she was snatched from the
midst of the palace, whom he had within his own heart, how she flared out, how
she departed all at once, whom he held as a nestling in his arms; he was eager
indeed, bearing a manly resolve, to endure the calamity best, alone; but being
conquered by the tyrannical passion he groaned faintly from the midst of his
soul, and seeing her lying face up, breathless, voiceless, moving not at all,
he begins, alas, the most mournful of discourses: "Today the light of my eyes
is quenched, the eye within that sees of my heart; alas, it is blinded and
darkness covers me; bitter arrows of despondency prick me. O what a tragedy
have you seen, O sun! O what a lamp of utmost brightness has gone out! Where
to me are the many ends of my thoughts, the care, the concern of my labors,
for which I endured many cares? For which I underwent squalls of afflictions?
Where now has everything gone? Where now has it hidden? Alas the dreadful
things of the ever-turning fortune, alas the slipping things of the
much-wandering life. Woe is me, what shall I do? How shall I pass my life
lifeless, breathless, utterly despondent? What comfort of my labors shall I
take? With what spells shall I quench the despondency? Earth, do not cover the
body of my beloved; and if you cover her, do not wither the nature; do not
receive this as a common body and dust; guard the face, for it has beauty;
reverence the shining form of all her limbs; bear her as a statue even buried;
do not rend the circles of her eyes, which have bright rays within their
glances; keep shut the musical mouth of the nightingale, which has the strains
of Orpheus; do not loosen the braid of her tresses; you have received a
treasure, not a dead little body; guard it all well for him who gave it. Woe
is me, what do I say? I am led astray by my words; the rush of my passion
bears me to the precipice. There is no light for me, no breath and heart;
everything has run away with my beloved; my crown is dust, my power fine
ashes; one thing is dear to me, one comfort to me: the tomb that hides you. If
I may look on it, I am comforted, perhaps I am soothed. What to me are the
bright things of contrivances? Let all fall to the earth at once; gold as
dust, the carved work of pearls, a rough stone to me now only; one stone to me
is most beautiful, very lovely, the one covering your flesh, O Augusta; the
others are to me dust and ashes and rottenness." Hold back, O mightiest king
of the whole earth; stay the stream of unmeasured tears; cease groaning; stand
collected, and look as one must. Know our nature, that we are nothing but dust
made [differently?] colored; and life and death are as two gates, and between
them this brief life. No one has passed the gate of entrance who has not also
passed the gate of exit. Fair indeed is she who has died, and (who does not
say it?) precisely above all women, but mortal surely, and she has died to
life, as Abraham before, as Moses who saw the divine light of truth in
darkness, as Christ himself, the Lord of breath, as the one who bore him
without birth-pangs. So has grown the nature of things that flow, so has grown
the brightness that is here. What new thing, that the mortal nature has died?
The body has by nature grown dust-pictured, and the earth is mother, and
everything runs down to the earth, from which it first took its substance. Why
do I say these things? Look closely, O king, upon the whole visible creation
and learn how they stand by natural reason, how all things round about live
and die; you see the trees today in bloom, but tomorrow flowing as if dead.
The leaf falls like a hair from the branches; those are of life, these of the
common end. Behold the bright and shining light-bearer, how he lives at first
as an ever-living nature, shining with strange morning torch-processions, then
dies, collapsing at his setting, and is hidden, alas, and the bright eye of
earth and heaven alike is covered. And the moon in turn, living, has often by
nature so many times died and flowed, suffering myriad pangs of eclipses. Each
of the stars so fares. And nothing new nor strange to nature, if along with
the moon, with the plants, with the sun, your own moon and plant and
light-bearer has departed, has flowed out of this life. Blame death not much,
O king of the earth; by nature she is grown a common benefactress; she leads
to the height of the ancient dignity, she leads to the very field of Eden,
from which we were thrown down by inattention. The cutting of impassioned
motions has by nature grown, the stopping of evils an unchecked stream. The
release of the living is not by nature complete, but a small separation of
things joined; for the soul, even when the little body is dissolved, blossoms
rather, living in a better manner. Why do you trouble and turn your heart
about, since you neither hold nor see your beloved? She did not depart like a
haze, nor like a cloud, nor has she wholly run away like a mist, but she is
and lives and sees the Lord and speaks with you noetically and remains with
you. And she cries these things: "Cease your wailings. I have not wholly left
your heart; I was not scattered like smoke into the air, but I am and am
present in a way that can be known. But leaving to mourn the dissolution of
the little body, remember now my cares for you, my many anxieties and endless
labors, which I endured alone with you in a foreign land, standing by you as a
tower, as a firm rock. Remember your handmaid, but also your beloved, and
now render me recompenses the more. Honor my soul with tender-hearted ways;
appease the Lord for me with gifts, bringing in bright toiling mediators, the
strained hands of poor and needy men. For being mortal, and of a flowing
substance, and a soul moving easily toward the base, I long ago soiled my
soul; and nothing so prevails to whiten it as a broad hand of gifts and
graces. Then, my aged, alas, mother, nurture her in old age, and become a
staff to her flesh worn down by time and labors. And, my mind's care above all
things, my kinsman Romanos, a young branch, I place in your hands; I leave him
to you alone; so may you profit from your scepter, O king, so may you profit
from the power and the crown, as you will hold him most protectively. You know
the grandfather and the grandfather's child, this his offspring, nobly born;
guard for him the dignity of his race, preserve for him the hopes that rest on
us; yes, all things to me before and again, O Monomachos, the calling 'the
all-sweet, the longed-for,' and rejoice and take thought for the commands." So
then, since she speaks with you again and is present with your heart forever,
take comfort, O mightiest one, and let your mind be revived. Then cast the
glances of your eyes, and turn your mind from the passion as from a gale to
the shining lamp of the palace, the common rejoicing of the inhabited world,
the bright splendor of the empress, the prop of the power of the scepter, the
mainstay of the crown of the palace, the tower or the wall or even the
foundation, Zoe, the ornament of earth and heaven. And seeing all the grace of
her face and her flashing utmost brightness, comfort your own heart. Partake
of sleep and of food, O scepter-bearing ruler; for you truly have as handmaid
of your food her given by God to us below, the bright splendor of Byzantion,
the solemn flower, the fruit of the purple, Theodora, the very beauty of life.
Let her become all things to your power, handmaid, lady and again servant. And
rejoice, O meadow, ensouled star; and with God, O mightiest one, who keeps you
as king, reign over the inhabited world.
