* On the Prayers of Christ

That not saying the things we know, and not bringing them out to others, makes
one poorer, and quenches the grace; and on the prayers which Christ prayed,
and the authority with which he did all things; and on the correction of the
old law; and that to become incarnate does not diminish his equality with the
Father, but rather establishes it still more.
Homily ten.
=48.783=
1. We have celebrated sufficiently in the days that have gone before, taking
hold of the apostolic contests, and reveling in the narration of the spiritual
achievements; it is time now at last to pay the debt to you; for there is
nothing that hinders. And I know that you have forgotten my debts because of
the multitude of days; yet I will not on this account hide them, but I will
pay them to you with all eagerness. And I do this not only for gratitude, but
also for my own benefit. For in bodily transactions it is gain to the borrower
that the lender forget; but in spiritual =48.784= contracts it is the greatest
gain to the one about to pay the debt, that those who are about to receive
remember the debts continually. For there the loan, when paid, leaves the one
who paid it, and passes over to the one who receives, and it has lessened the
substance of the one and increased that of the other; but in spiritual things
it is not so, but it is possible both to pay it and to have it; and what is
wonderful, that then most of all we have it, when we have paid it to others.
For if I keep it buried in my mind continually, sharing it with no one, my
gain is lessened, the things of my abundance are diminished; but if I bring it
=48.785= out to all, and make many sharers and partners of all the things I
myself know, the things of my spiritual wealth are increased for me. And that
these things are so, and that the one who shares with others increases the
deposit, while the one who hides it lessens the whole work, the witnesses are
those who were entrusted with the talents, the one with the five, and the one
with the two, and the one with the one. For they brought the things entrusted
as double, and were honored for this; but he, since he kept it by himself and
shared it with no one, and was not able to make it double, was for this reason
punished. Hearing these things then, all of us, and fearing the punishment
from there, whatever good we have let us bring out to the brethren, and let us
pay it into the midst for all, but let us not hide it. For when we share with
others, then we ourselves grow richer; when we make many partners of the
trade, then we increase our own abundance. But you think the things of your
glory are lessened, when you are one who knows together with many the things
you yourself know alone. And yet then both the things of glory and the things
of benefit are increased, when you have trampled down envy, when you have
quenched jealousy, when you display much brotherly love; but if you go about
knowing it alone, men will turn away from you and hate you as jealous and a
hater of the brethren, and God will require of you the last penalty as of a
wicked man; and apart from these things, the grace itself, quickly leaving you
desolate, will go away. Since even wheat, if it lies continually in the
storehouses, is consumed, as the moth eats it; but if it is brought out and
cast into the fields, it is multiplied and is renewed again. So also a
spiritual word,


if it is shut up within always, the soul being corrupted and eaten by envy
and sloth and wasting, is quickly quenched; but if, as into a fertile field,
it is sown into the souls of the brethren, the treasure is multiplied both for
those who receive and for the one who possesses it. And just as a spring
continually drawn is rather cleansed and wells up more, but when it is buried
it is choked; so also a spiritual gift and a teaching word, continually drawn
and giving those who wish to draw, wells up more; but when it is buried by
jealousy and envy, it is lessened and is quenched completely. Since then so
great is the gain for us, whatever we have, come, having set it into the
midst, let us pay you the whole debt, first having recalled the whole sequence
of these debts.
2. You know then, and you remember lately, that when we were discoursing
concerning the glory of the Only-begotten, we were numbering many causes of
the condescension in the words; and we were saying that not only because of
the clothing of the flesh, nor only because of the weakness of the hearers,
does Christ often utter lowly things, but in many places also teaching them to
be humble. And those causes we went through sufficiently then, having
remembered both the prayer that came to pass over Lazarus, and the one at the
cross itself, and having shown clearly that he did the one in order to confirm
the economy, and the other in order to correct the weakness of the hearers,
himself needing no help. But that he also did many things teaching them to be
humble, hear this too. He put water into the basin; and as if this were not
enough =48.786=, he even girded himself still with a towel, bringing himself
down to the last cheapness, and he began to wash the feet of the disciples,
and together with the disciples he washed also the feet of the traitor. Who
would not be struck and wonder? He washes the feet of the one who is about to
betray him; and Peter, who was refusing and saying, "Lord, you shall never
wash my feet," he does not pass by, but says to him: "If I do not wash your
feet, you have no part with me"; and he, "Lord, not my feet only, but also my
hands and my head." Did you see the reverence of the disciple through both,
both through the refusal and through the consent? For even if the words were
contrary, yet both were spoken from a boiling mind. Do you see how he was
everywhere hot and aroused? But as I was saying, lest because of the cheapness
of the act you should condemn cheapness of nature, after the washing hear what
he says to them: "Do you know what I have done to you? You call me Teacher and
Lord, and you say well; for so I am. If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have
washed your feet, you also ought to do so to one another. For I have given you
an example, that as I have done to you, you also should do to one another." Do
you see that he did many things for the sake of an example? For just as some
teacher filled with wisdom lisps together with lisping children, and the
lisping is not a proof of the teacher's ignorance, but of his care for the
children; so also Christ did these things not because of cheapness of essence,
but because of condescension. And one must not simply pass these things by;
for if we now examine this very thing by itself, see how great an absurdity
will follow. For if the one who washes seems to be cheaper than the one who is
washed (and the one who washes is Christ, and the disciples are those who are
washed), then Christ will be cheaper than the disciples. But no one, not even
if mad, would say this. Do you see how great an evil it is not to know the
causes for which Christ did all the things he did? Or rather do you see how
great a good it is to examine all things with exactness, and not simply that
he said or did something lowly, but also to add for whose sake and on account
of what? And he did this not here only, but elsewhere also he hinted at this
same thing. Having said


"Who is greater, the one who reclines, or the one who serves?" he added, "Is
it not the one who reclines? But I am in the midst of you as the one who
serves." And this he said, and that he did, showing that in many places he
snatches the lesser things for the teaching of the disciples, persuading them
at the same time to be moderate. And it is clear that he endures all these
things not because of inferiority of nature, but because of their teaching.
For elsewhere also he says: "The rulers of the nations lord it over them, but
it shall not be so among you; but whoever wishes to be first among you, let
him be servant of all. For the Son of man came not to be served, but to
serve." If then he came to serve and to teach humility, do not be troubled,
nor fall down, if you should see him somewhere performing even the things of
servants =48.787= and speaking them. Thus also he performs many of the prayers
from the same mind. For they came to him saying, "Lord, teach us to pray, as
John taught his disciples." What then ought he to do, tell me? Not teach them
to pray? But for this he came, that he might lead them into all philosophy.
But ought he to teach? Then he had to pray. But he ought to have done this by
a word only, one says. But it is not so that teaching through words, as
teaching through deeds, is accustomed to lead those who are being taught. For
this very reason he does not teach them a prayer through words only, but he
himself also continues doing this, and he prays spending the night in the
deserts, training and admonishing us, whenever we are about to converse with
God, to flee tumults and the disturbances in the midst, and to withdraw to
solitude, not of places only, but also of times. And solitude is not a
mountain only, but also a little house freed from shouting.
3. And that you may learn that the prayer is of condescension, we have already
shown this especially through the things that happened concerning Lazarus; yet
it is clear from another place also. For why does he not pray over the greater
wonders, but over the lesser? For if he prayed needing help and not having
self-complete power, he ought to pray over all things and to call the Father;
and if not over all, then at least over the greater. But now he does the
contrary; over the greater matters he does not pray, in order to show that he
was doing this teaching the others, not needing power. When at any rate he
blessed the loaves, having looked up to heaven he prayed, training us not to
taste of a table before we have given thanks to God who has made the fruits;
and having raised many dead he did not pray, but over Lazarus only. And the
cause we have spoken then, that he was correcting the weakness of those
present, which he himself also stated, adding so clearly that "I said it
because of the crowd standing around." And that it was not the prayer, but the
voice, that raised the dead, we showed sufficiently then; and that you may
learn this more clearly, see. For whenever it is necessary to punish, and
whenever to honor, and whenever to forgive sins, and whenever to legislate,
and whenever it is necessary to do something of the things much greater, you
will nowhere find him calling the Father, nor praying, but doing all things
with authority. And each of these I will number; but you consider with
exactness how nowhere he needs prayer. "Come," he says, "you blessed of my
Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you." And again, "Depart from me, you
cursed, into the fire prepared for the devil and his angels." See, he punishes
and honors with all authority, and needs no prayer. Again, when it is
necessary to set right a body that has been dissolved: "Rise, take up your
bed, and walk"; when to deliver from death =48.788=, "Talitha cumi, arise";
when to set free from sins, "Take courage, child, your sins are forgiven";
when to rebuke demons, "I say to you, you evil demon, come out of him"; when
to still the sea, "Be silent, be muzzled"; when to cleanse someone held by
leprosy, "I will;


be cleansed"; when to legislate, "You have heard that it was said to the
ancients, You shall not murder; but I say to you, Whoever says to his brother,
Fool, shall be liable to the gehenna of fire." Did you see him doing all
things with masterly authority, both casting into gehenna, and bringing into
the kingdom, and setting right paralysis, and driving away death, and
forgiving sins, and rebuking demons, and stilling the sea? And yet what is
greater, tell me, to bring into the kingdom, and to cast into gehenna, and to
forgive sins, and to set laws with authority, or to make loaves? Is it not
evident and confessed by all that these are greater than those? Yet over the
greater he does not pray, showing that even over the lesser he was doing this
not through weakness of power, but through teaching of those then present. And
that you may learn how great a thing it is to forgive sins, I bring you the
prophet as a witness; for the prophet shows this of no other, but of God
alone. "For who is a God," he says, "like you, taking away iniquities, passing
over injustices?" And to bring into the kingdom is also much greater than to
dissolve death; yet even that he does with authority. And to legislate also is
not of those who are subjected, but of those who reign; and this the very
nature of the facts cries out; for it belongs to kings only to set laws; and
the Apostle also shows it, speaking thus: "Now concerning virgins I have no
commandment of the Lord, but I give an opinion as one who has obtained mercy
from the Lord to be faithful." For since he was a servant and a minister, he
did not dare to add to the things legislated from the beginning. But Christ is
not so, but with much authority he reads the old laws, and introduces again
those from himself. And if simply to set laws belongs to kingly authority
only, when this one is found not only setting laws, but also correcting the
old, what argument is left for those who wish to be shameless? For from this
it is clear that he is of the same essence as the one who begot him. And that
what I say may become clearer, let us come to the very passage of Scripture.
"Having gone up into the mountain," it says, "he sat, and with all standing
around he began to say: Blessed are the poor in spirit, the meek, the
merciful, the pure in heart." Then after those beatitudes he says: "Do not
think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets; I came not to destroy,
but to fulfill." For who suspected this? Or what of the things said was
contrary to the former, that he should say this? "Blessed," he says, "are the
poor in spirit," that is, the humble. But the Old also said this: "For a
sacrifice to God is a broken spirit; a broken =48.789= and humbled heart God
will not despise." And again: "Blessed are the meek." And this Isaiah again
cries, speaking from the person of God: "Upon whom shall I look, but upon the
meek and quiet and the one who trembles at my words?" "Blessed are the
merciful." And this again is scattered everywhere; "For do not deprive," he
says, "the life of the poor, do not refuse a distressed suppliant"; and
everywhere there is much discourse concerning loving-kindness. "Blessed are
the pure in heart." This David also says: "Create in me a clean heart, O God,
and renew a right spirit in my inward parts." And if one should go through the
remaining beatitudes also, he will find that the agreement is much. Why then,
having said nothing contrary to the former things, did he add: "Do not think
that I came to destroy the law or the prophets"? Not because of the things
that had been said, but because of the things that were about to be said he
sets this correction. For since he was about to enter into an intensification
of commandments, lest they should think the increase to be opposition, nor the
addition a fight, for this reason he said: "Do not think that I came to
destroy the law or the prophets"; that is, I am about to say some things more
perfect than those said before; as that "You have heard, You shall not murder;
but I say, You shall not be angry."


"You have heard that You shall not commit adultery, but I say that he who has
looked at a woman to desire her has already committed adultery"; and as many
such things. Do not then think the perfection to be a destroying; for it is
not a destroying, but a fulfilling; and what he did in the case of bodies,
this he does also in the case of the law. And what did he work in the case of
bodies? Having come and found many members maimed, and all things in want, he
completed them, and brought them back to the fitting good order, making it
clear to all through the very facts that he himself also set the ancient laws,
and created our nature. And that he was eager to show this is clear especially
from the healing of the blind man. For passing by and seeing a certain blind
man, he made clay, and smeared such clay upon the disabled eyes, and said to
him: "Go, wash in Siloam." For why did he who was continually raising the dead
by a bare command, and working many other such wonders, here also add some
work, making clay, and forming the eyes for him? Is it not evident, that when
you hear that God took dust from the earth and formed the man, you may learn
through what is now coming to pass that this is that one who from the
beginning formed the man? since if he did not wish to show this, what he was
working was superfluous. Then, that you may learn that it was not the need of
the clay that cooperated with him toward the recovering of sight of the
disabled man, but that even apart from that matter it was enough by a command
alone to form the eyes, he adds and says: "Go, wash in Siloam." For after
showing us through the manner of the creating who it was who also from the
beginning made the man, then he says to him: "Go, wash in Siloam." Just as
then an excellent statue-maker, wishing to display his own art through the
works, leaves, while forming the statue, the part, in order that in what is
lacking he may give a demonstration concerning the art of the whole; so also
Christ, wishing to show that he himself made the whole man, left this one
incomplete, that having come =48.790= and given back the eyes, through the
part he might plant in us the faith concerning the whole. And see where he did
this; not upon a hand and a foot, but upon eyes, the fairest and most
necessary of our members, and a member than which nothing is more honorable to
us. And he who was able to form the fairest and most necessary, I mean the
eyes, it is evident that he is able also to construct a hand and a foot and
the remaining members. O blessed those eyes, which became a theater to all who
were present, and drew all toward themselves, and sent forth a voice through
their beauty, teaching all who were present the power of Christ. And the thing
that came to pass was paradoxical; for the blind man was teaching those who
see to see. Which also Christ, making clear, was saying: "I came into this
world for judgment, that those who do not see may see, and those who see may
become blind." O blessed maiming; for what he did not receive from nature, he
received from grace, having lost nothing from the delay so great as he gained
from the manner of the creating. For what could be more wonderful than those
eyes, which blameless and holy hands deigned to form? And what happened in the
case of the barren woman, this also came to pass here. For just as she was
harmed in nothing from the delay, but rather became more splendid, receiving
the child not by laws of nature but by laws of grace; so also the blind man
was harmed in nothing from the past maiming, but even gained the greatest
things from this, having been deemed worthy to see first the sun of
righteousness, and then this sensible one. 5. I say these things so that we
may not be distressed, whenever we see either ourselves or certain others
being in misfortunes. For if we bear all that befalls us thankfully and nobly,
altogether toward a good end and one having many good things every


misfortune will meet us. But what I wished to say, that just as he was
constructing the bodies that were in want as completed, so also having taken
the law incomplete he was ordering it and forming it and leading it out toward
the better. And let no one, hearing that the law was incomplete, think that we
are accusing the one who set it. For that one was becoming incomplete not from
its own nature, but from the time thereafter; since according to the season in
which it was introduced it was exceedingly perfect, and fitted to those who
received it; but when nature, having been trained by it toward the better, had
thereafter advanced, this became more incomplete, not from its own nature, but
from the advance of the virtue of those taught by it. And just as bows and
arrows, constructed for a royal child for exercise rather than for battle and
war, when the child has grown and has learned to excel in wars, become
useless; so also it came to pass in the case of our nature; when we were more
incomplete and were learning to be exercised, he gave us fitting weapons,
which we were able to bear with ease; but when we had thereafter grown
according to the account of virtue, from our perfection those things
thereafter became incomplete. For this reason Christ came, putting into our
hands other greater things. And consider with how much understanding he both
reads the old =48.791= laws, and sets beside them the new. "You have heard,"
he says, "that it was said to the ancients: You shall not murder." Say also by
whom it was said; did you say this, or your Father? But he does not say. Why
then did he keep this silent, and did not make the speaker clear, but
introduced the legislation without a person? Because if he had said: "You
shall not murder, but I say to you: You shall not be angry," the thing said
would have seemed heavy because of the folly of the hearers not yet able to
understand that he was legislating not overturning the former things, but
increasing them; and they would have said to him: "What are you saying? Your
Father said: You shall not murder, and you say: You shall not be angry?" Lest
then anyone should think him to be contrary to the Father, or as bringing in
something wiser, something more, than that one, he did not say that "You have
heard from the Father." Again if he had said: "You have heard that I said to
the ancients," it would have seemed unbearable, and this no less than the
former. For if, when he said, "Before Abraham came to be, I am," they
attempted to stone him, if he had added that he himself also gave the law to
Moses, what would they not have done? For this reason, having said nothing
either concerning himself or concerning the Father, but having left it in the
middle, and having said: "You have heard that it was said to the ancients: You
shall not murder"; what he did in the case of bodies, having taught them
through the filling-up of what was lacking also the one who from the beginning
created the man, this he does here also, teaching through the correction of
the law and the addition of what was wanting who it is who also from the
beginning gave the law. For this reason also when discoursing concerning the
creating of the man, he mentioned neither himself nor the Father, but here
also he makes the word without a person and undefined, saying: "He who made
them from the beginning made them male and female"; and by the word he kept
silent the one who made, but by the work he taught, filling up the things
lacking of the bodies. So also here, having said that "You have heard that it
was said to the ancients," he kept silent by whom it was said, but through the
very works he made himself clear. For the one who filled up what was lacking,
this is the one who also from the beginning introduced it. And he reads the
old laws themselves also, in order that by the comparison the hearers may
learn that the things said are not of opposition, and that he has the same
authority as the one who begot him. These things the Jews also understood and
wondered. And that they wondered, hear the evangelist making this clear and
saying: "The crowds were astonished," he says, "at his teaching, because he
taught them as one having authority, and not as their scribes and the
Pharisees." What then, if they suspected this badly?


one says. And yet he did not accuse them, nor rebuke them, but even confirmed
their judgment. For when the leper immediately came and said: "Lord, if you
will, you can cleanse me"; what does he say? "I will; be cleansed." And why
did he not say simply, "Be cleansed"? And yet the leper bore witness to him
that he has authority, saying, "If you will." But lest you should think that
the "If you will" belongs to the leper's judgment, he himself also added,
saying: "I will; be cleansed." Thus on purpose everywhere he was showing his
own authority, and that =48.792= he does all things from authority; since if
this were not so, the thing said would have been superfluous.
ʹ. Having therefore learned through all things his authority, if we see him
elsewhere doing and saying something lowly, both because of the causes which
we numbered lately, and because of wishing to lead the hearers into humility,
let us not on this account slander him into cheapness of essence. For even the
very taking on of flesh he endured from humility, not because of being
inferior to the Father. And from where is this clear? For the enemies of the
truth also carry this about, saying that "If he was equal to the one who begot
him, why did the Father not take on flesh, but the Son put on the form of the
servant? is it not evident that it was because he was inferior?" And yet, if
it was for this reason that he put on our nature, the Spirit, which they say
is less than the Son (for we would not say it), that one ought to have become
incarnate. For if the Father is greater than the Son for this reason, because
the one became incarnate and the other did not become incarnate, the Spirit
also will be greater than him for the same cause; for neither did it take on
flesh. But lest we should declare this from syllogisms, come, let us set it
forth from the Scriptures themselves, showing that he took on flesh through
humility. For Paul, who knew these things exactly, whenever he is about to
exhort us to some of the things that are useful, brings down to us the
examples of virtue from above; as, he often advises concerning love, and
wishing to lead the disciples into loving one another, he brings Christ into
the midst, saying: "Husbands, love your wives, as also Christ loved the
Church." Again, whenever he discourses concerning almsgiving, he does this
same thing; wherefore he also says: "You know the grace of our Lord Jesus
Christ, that though he was rich, for your sake he became poor, that you by his
poverty might become rich." And what he says is of this sort: just as your
Master became poor, having put on flesh, so you become poor in money; for just
as the poverty of glory harmed that one in nothing, so neither will the
poverty of money be able to harm you, but it will work for you much wealth. So
also again when discoursing to the Philippians concerning humility he brings
Christ into the midst, and having said, "In humility considering one another
as surpassing yourselves," he added: "For let this mind be in you which was
also in Christ Jesus, who, being in the form of God, did not count it robbery
to be equal with God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant." And
yet if because of being less according to nature he accepted to wear flesh,
the thing that came to pass is no longer of humility, but Paul brought it in
superfluously when exhorting to humility; for humility is when an equal obeys
an equal. This then he himself also showing says: "Who, being in the form of
God, did not count it robbery to be equal with God, but emptied himself,
taking the form of a servant." What is, "He did not count it robbery to be
equal with God, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant"? The one
who has snatched something of the things that do not belong to him holds this
continually and would not choose to put it aside, fearing and not having
confidence concerning the possession; but =48.793= the one who has a good that
cannot be taken away, even if he hides this, does not fear. As, that I may
make the word clear also upon an example, let there be someone of


the same, the one a household-servant, the other a son; if then the
household-servant should cry out a freedom in no way belonging to him, and
should stand against the master, he does not endure to do anything slavish,
nor when commanded does he obey, fearing lest this very thing should outrage
the freedom, and the command should work him a stumbling-block; for he has
snatched the honor, and has it contrary to worth. But the son will not refuse
to perform every slavish work, knowing that even if he should serve all the
things of the slaves, the things of his freedom have been harmed for him in
nothing, but they remain unmoved, since the natural nobility cannot be taken
away from the slavish works; since he does not have it from robbery, as the
household-servant does, but it was allotted to him from above and from the
first day. This then Paul also making clear says concerning Christ, that since
he was by nature free and a genuine son, it was not as having taken the
equality by robbery that he needed to hide it, but having confidence he took
on the form of a servant. For he knew, he knew clearly, that the condescension
will be able to lessen his glory in nothing; for it was not brought in from
outside, nor given according to robbery, nor alien and not belonging, but
natural and genuine. For this reason he took the form of a servant, knowing
clearly and being persuaded that this will be able to harm him in nothing.
Therefore it did not harm him, but even in the form of the servant he remained
having the same glory. Do you see that even the very taking on of flesh is a
sign that the Son is equal to the one who begot him, and that this equality is
not brought in from outside, nor coming to be and passing away, but unmoved
and firm, and such as it is fitting for a son to have toward a father?
7. Let us then say all these things to those men, and let us be eager, at
least as far as it lies with us, to lead them away from that wicked heresy,
and to bring them back to the truth. And let us ourselves also not think that
faith alone is enough for us toward salvation, but let us also take care of a
way of life, and let us display a most excellent life, in order that from both
sides the things of our benefit may be completed for us. And what I exhorted
lately, this also now I exhort, so as to accomplish it; and let us dissolve
the enmities toward one another, and let no one be an enemy of his neighbor
more than one day, but before the night let him dissolve the anger, lest
having come to be by himself, and gathering with exactness the things that
have come to pass and the things that have been said from the enmity, he
should work a more difficult end, and should make the reconciliation more
hard. For just as the bones of our body, having slipped out of their own seat,
if they are brought back at once, receive their own place not with much pain;
but if they remain a long time outside their own seat, they come back again
with difficulty and return to their own place, and even when brought back they
need many days, so as to be fitted with exactness and to be settled and to

yet speak of the laughter and the shame. For of how great condemnation would
it not be worthy, to need others to bring us together toward our own members?
And this is not the only terrible thing that comes to pass by the delay and
the putting-off, but also that even the sins that are not, thereafter seem to
be sins; and whatever the enemy may utter, we receive all things with
suspicion, both gestures, and looks, and voice, and gait; for even being seen
he kindles the hardened soul, and not being seen he likewise again grieves.
For not only the sight of those who have done injustice, but also the memory
is accustomed to pain us continually, and if we hear another saying something
concerning


him, we speak likewise again, and we pass simply the whole of life in
despondency and pain, working greater evils for ourselves than for those men,
and keeping a continual war upon the soul. Knowing all these things then,
beloved, let us be eager especially to have enmity toward no one; but if some
enmity should also come to be, let us be reconciled on the very day. For if it
takes hold of a second and a third, quickly the third becomes a fourth, and
that a fifth, and this itself will again beget for us many more days of
enmity; for by as much as we put it off, by so much more do we blush. But is
it shame to come forward and to kiss the one who has done injustice? This then
is praise, this a crown, this an encomium, this a gain and a treasure having
countless good things; and the enemy himself will receive you, and all who are
present will praise, and even if men accuse, God will altogether crown. But if
you wait for that one first to come and to ask forgiveness, you will not have
so great a gain; for that one, having anticipated, snatched the prize, and
transferred the whole blessing to himself; but if you yourself run forward,
you have not been lessened, but you have conquered the temper, you have
prevailed over the passion, you have displayed much philosophy having obeyed
God, you have made the life after these things more to be longed for, you have
been freed from affairs and disturbance. And not before God only, but even
according to man it is precarious and dangerous to have many enemies. And why
do I say many? to have one only enemy is dangerous, just as it is safe and
saving to possess all as friends. Not so an income of money, not so weapons
and walls and ditches and countless other devices are accustomed to make us
safe, as genuine friendship. This is a wall, this safety, this abundance, this
luxury, this will both prepare us to pass the present life with good cheer,
and will grant the life to come. Considering all these things then, and
calculating how great the gain from the matter is, let us do and arrange all
things, so as both to reconcile those who are enemies to us, and to hinder
those who are about to become so, and of friends to make those who =48.795=
are more secure. For the beginning and end of all virtue is love; of which may
it be that we, enjoying it genuinely and continually, obtain the kingdom of
the =48.796= heavens, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus
Christ, to whom be the glory and the might unto the ages of ages. Amen.
