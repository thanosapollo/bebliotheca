* To Bishop Optimus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Even apart from this I was glad to see the good children, both for
the steadiness of their character beyond their years and for their
kinship with your reverence, from which one may also expect something
great of them. When I saw them coming to me with a letter of yours, I
doubled my affection for them. And when I read the letter and saw in
it at once the pastoral care of your disposition for the Churches and
your diligence over the reading of the divine Scriptures, I gave
thanks to the Lord, and I prayed blessings on those who brought us
such a letter, and before them on the one who wrote to us. You asked
about that much-talked-of saying, carried up and down among everyone,
what solution the verse has: "Whoever kills Cain will undo seven
things to be avenged." By this you have already =77.368= commended
yourself, as one who carefully keeps Timothy's charge, just as Paul
gave it to him; for it is clear that you give heed to the reading.
Next you have also roused us elders, already grown numb with time,
with the weakness of the body, and with the multitude of afflictions
(for the many troubles stirred up around us have weighed down our
life); nevertheless you have raised us up, and, fervent in spirit, you
bring us who had grown cold, like animals lurking in their dens, back
to a moderate wakefulness and a living energy. Now the saying can be
understood simply as it stands, and it can also admit a more varied
account. The simpler sense, which anyone can readily set forth from
the words at hand, is this: that Cain must pay a sevenfold punishment
for the things in which he sinned. For it is not the part of a just
judge to appoint equal returns for equal deeds; rather, the one who
began the evil must pay what is owed with an addition, both that he
himself may be made better by the punishments and that he may make the
rest more sober by the example. Therefore, since it has been ordained
that Cain should fulfill the penalty of his sins seven times, "he will
undo," it says, this which the divine judgment has decreed upon him,
whoever kills him. This is the sense that falls on us from the first
reading.
But since the mind of the more laborious is by nature to search the
depths, it asks after the justice of it: how, in the "seven times,"
are the things to be avenged also fulfilled? Are the sins seven, or is
the sin one and the punishments upon the one sin seven? Now Scripture
always limits the number of the forgiveness of sins within the seven.
"How often," he says, "shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive
him?" (it is Peter speaking to the Lord); "until seven times?" Then
the Lord's answer: "I do not say to you until seven times, but until
seventy times seven." For the Lord did not pass over to another
number, but, multiplying the seven, he set the limit in it. And
through seven years the Hebrew was released from slavery. And seven
weeks of years made the famous Jubilee for those of old, in which the
land kept sabbath, there were cancellations of debts and release from
slavery, and as it were a new life was established from the beginning
in the number of seven, the old life in a manner receiving its
completion.
These things are types of this age, which, cycling through the seven
days, passes us by; in it the payments of the more moderate sins take
place, according to the humane care of the good Master, so that we may
not be handed over to punishment in the endless age. The "seven
times," then, is because of kinship with this world, as those who love
the world ought especially to be punished from the very things for
whose sake they chose to do evil. And if you should say that the
things to be avenged in Cain's case are seven, you will find seven; if
you take the things defined upon him by the Judge, you will not miss
the meaning this way either. =77.369= Among the deeds Cain dared, the
first sin is envy at the preference of Abel. Second, the deceit with
which he spoke to his brother, saying, "Let us go into the field."
Third, murder. Fourth, an addition of the evil, in that it was also a
brother's murder, which is a greater intensification. Fifth, that
Cain, the first murderer, left a wicked example to the world. Sixth
wrong, that he caused grief to his parents. Seventh, that he lied to
God.
For when asked, "Where is Abel your brother?" he said, "I do not
know." He therefore undid seven things to be avenged in Cain's being
put to death. For since the Lord said, "Cursed is the earth which
opened to receive your brother's blood, and groaning and trembling you
shall be upon the earth," Cain says: "If you cast me out today from
the earth, I shall also be hidden from your face, and I shall be
groaning and trembling upon the earth, and everyone who finds me will
kill me." To this the Lord says: "Not so; everyone who kills Cain will
undo seven things to be avenged." For since Cain thought himself easy
prey for anyone, because he had no security from the earth (for the
earth was cursed from him) and had been stripped of help from God, God
being angry at the murder, so that no assistance was left him either
from earth or from heaven, he says, "Everyone who finds me will kill
me." The Word convicts his error, saying, "Not so," that is, you shall
not be killed. But he not only hides the wound; he also works another
thing besides the murder, the lie, adding, "I do not know; am I my
brother's keeper?" From this point the punishments are enumerated.
"Cursed is the earth from you." One punishment. "Work the earth." This
is the second; for some unspeakable necessity is yoked to him, driving
him to the work of the earth, so that it is not even possible for him,
if he wished, to rest, but he is always driven to toil at the earth
that is hostile to him, which he made cursed by staining it with a
brother's blood. For it is a terrible punishment to live among those
who hate you, to have an enemy as housemate, to have hatred without
pause. "Work the earth," that is, strained by the labors of the field,
you will not let up at any time, neither by night nor by day being
released from the toils, having a necessity that rouses you to the
works more harsh than any master, "and it will not add to give its
strength." And even if the ceaselessness of the labor had some fruit,
the toil itself would be no small torture for one always strained and
weary; but since the labor is also ceaseless and the misery over the
earth is fruitless, there is the fruitlessness of the toils.
"Groaning and trembling you shall be upon the earth." He added two
more to the three: continual groaning, and trembling of the =77.372=
body, the members not having the bracing that comes from strength.
For since he used the power of the body badly, its tension was taken
from him, so that he is shaken and thrown into disorder, unable to
bring bread to his mouth without fear, or to carry drink with the
wicked hand after the unholy deed, or any longer being allowed to
serve even the body's own necessary needs. Another punishment, which
Cain disclosed when he said, "If you now cast me out from the earth, I
shall also be hidden from your face." Separation from God is the
heaviest punishment for the sober-minded. "And it shall be," he says,
"that everyone who finds me will kill me." He infers this from the
sequence of what went before. If you have cast me out from the earth,
if I shall be hidden from your face, it remains that I be killed by
everyone. What then does the Lord say? "Not so." But he set a sign
upon him; this is the seventh punishment. And that the punishment is
not hidden, but has been proclaimed beforehand by a clear sign, that
this man is the author of unholy deeds (and this belongs to those who
reason rightly), is shame, the heaviest of punishments, which we have
also learned concerning the judgment, when these shall rise to eternal
life, and these to shame and eternal reproach.
A related inquiry follows this, what was said by Lamech to his
wives: "I have killed a man, and a young man for a bruise." A wound,
then, is one thing, and a bruise another; a man is one thing, and a
young man another. So if vengeance has been exacted from Cain seven
times, from Lamech seventy times seven he is to undergo four hundred
and ninety punishments. If he is not a righteous man, if indeed God's
judgment upon Cain is just, so that Cain should bear seven
punishments: for the one did not learn from another how to murder, but
this one saw a murderer paying the penalty.
"But I, having before my eyes the one groaning and trembling, and
the greatness of the wrath of God, was not made sober by the example;
hence I am worthy to pay four hundred and ninety punishments." And
some have rushed to such an account as is not out of harmony with
ecclesiastical doctrine: that from Cain until the flood seven
generations had passed. And immediately the punishment was brought
upon the earth, because there had been a great outpouring of sin. But
the sin of Lamech does not need a flood for its healing, but the very
one who takes away the sin of the world.
