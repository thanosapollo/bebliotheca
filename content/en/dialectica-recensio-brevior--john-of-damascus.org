(Another chapter) <Preface Letter>
To the most holy and God-honored Cosmas, most holy bishop of Maiuma,
John the monk.
Knowing the narrowness of my understanding and the poverty of my own
tongue I hesitated, O blessed ones, to attempt things beyond my power
and to dare the impossible, as some arrogant and rash man, dreading the
danger that hangs over those who dare such things. For if Moses, that
divine lawgiver, who withdrew from every human spectacle and left the
storms of life and wiped away every material impression and purified the
optic of the soul and from there became fit for the vision of God, who
was counted worthy to behold in the bush and immaterial fire the
philanthropic condescension of the Word of God toward us and his
supernatural incarnation, a fire that burned and set the thorn ablaze
and changed it toward its own brightness, yet did not consume it nor
destroy it nor make it depart from its own nature, who first was
initiated into the name of the One who is and who truly is above
substance, when the leadership of his own people and kinsmen was
entrusted to him by God, called himself slow of speech and slow of
tongue, as not able to serve the divine will and bring this into the
open and be appointed mediator of God and

of men, how shall I, stained with the filth of every sin, bearing in
myself the much-troubled surge of thoughts, and neither purified in mind
and understanding so as to be a mirror of God and of the divine
impressions, nor having speech able to suffice for what is thought,
utter the divine and ineffable things that surpass the grasp of every
rational nature? Reckoning these things I hesitated at the word, and I
feared the command, for the truth shall be spoken, lest I incur a double
laughter, of ignorance and of folly together: which is the hardest
thing. For the charge of ignorance is pardonable, if it does not come
from sloth. But to possess, together with ignorance, the conceit of
knowledge is hard and blameworthy and unworthy of all pardon and a proof
of a greater, that I may not say of the extreme, ignorance. But since
the fruit of disobedience is death, and he who becomes a humble and
obedient disciple of Christ is raised to height and receives from God
the light-bringing grace and opening his mouth is filled with the Spirit
and is purified in heart and enlightened in understanding and receives a
word in the opening of the mouth, not taking thought what he will speak,
but becoming an instrument of the Spirit speaking in him, persuaded
through you by Christ who is hierarch in you I bow to the command and
open my mouth, taking courage from your prayers, that it will be filled
with Spirit, and I will speak words not as fruit of my own understanding
but as fruit of the Spirit who enlightens the blind, receiving whatever
he will give and uttering these things. And first I will set forth the
finest things from the wise among the Greeks, knowing that if anything
is good, it has been given to men from above by God, since "every good
gift and every perfect endowment is from above, coming down from the
Father of lights."
If anything is opposed to the truth, it is of satanic deceit "a dark
discovery and a fabrication of a demon-possessed mind," as Gregory great
in theology says. Imitating then the manner of the bee I will join
myself to what is proper to the truth and will gain salvation even from
enemies, and I will send away everything that is base and that clings to
knowledge falsely so called. Then, next after these, I will compose the
babblings of the God-hated heresies, that recognizing the falsehood we
may cling the more to the truth. Then, the destroyer of deceit and
driver-out of falsehood, the truth adorned and compassed as with golden
tassels by the words of the God-inspired prophets and God-taught
fishermen and God-bearing shepherds and teachers, I will set forth with
God and by his grace, whose glory flashing from within enlightens with
its gleam those who meet it with the due purification and the putting
away of troubled thoughts. I will say nothing of my own, as I said, but
collecting into one the labors of the approved teachers I will make the
discourse, as far as I can, abridged, yielding in all things to your
command. But be indulgent toward me, God-honored ones, I pray, who have
obeyed your injunctions, and receiving the obedience give in return the
supply of your prayers. 1 Concerning being, substance, and accident.
Being is a common name of all beings. This being, then, is cut into
substance and accident. Substance is a self-subsistent thing needing
nothing else for constitution, that is, what is in itself and does not
have its existence in another; accident is what cannot be in itself but
has its existence in another. For substance is a subject, as it were
matter of things, and accident is what is observed in the substance, as
body and color; (ῃ. ι. 26–29) for the body is not in the color but the
color is in the body.

The body, then, is substance, and the color accident. Likewise also the
soul and prudence; for the soul is not in prudence but prudence is in
the soul. Therefore neither is the body said to be of the color but the
color of the body, nor the soul of prudence but prudence of the soul.
The soul, then, is substance and prudence accident. For when the soul is
destroyed, prudence also is destroyed; for if there is no soul, in what
will prudence be? But when prudence is destroyed, the soul is not in
every case destroyed; for it is possible for a soul to be without
prudence. Likewise also in all beings, what has being in itself and not
in another is substance, and what cannot be by itself but has its
existence in another is accident. (ῃ. ς. 6–8) (ῃ. ς. 12 ς.) 2 Concerning
genus and species, the most generic and the most specific and the
subalterns. Genus is said in three ways. In one way as from the
begetter, as those from Israel are called Israelites. In another way
from the fatherland, as those from Jerusalem are called Jerusalemites
and those from Palestine Palestinians. In a third way genus is said of
what is divided into species, about which the discourse among the
philosophers is, and defining it they say: Genus is what is predicated
of several things differing in species in the what it is. And species
too has two significations. For species is said of the figure and the
form, as the species of the statue. And again species is said of what is
ranked under the genus, that is, what is divided from the genus. About
this the discourse among the philosophers is. And since in discussing
genus we also mentioned species, saying "genus is what is divided into
species," and again in discussing species we mentioned genus, saying
"species is what is divided from the genus," one must know that just as
when speaking of a father we of necessity also mention a son, for this
man is a father who has a son, and when discussing a son we of necessity
also mention a father, for this man is a son who has a father, so also
here it is impossible to treat of genus apart from species or of species
apart from genus; for the genus is in every case divided into species,
and what does not have species divided from it is not a genus. And the
species are divided from the genus, and those that do not have a genus
are not species. And just as the first man, that is Adam, not having had
a father is not called son, but is called father, for he had sons; and
Seth is called both son of the one who begot him, for he had Adam as
father, and father of the one born from him, for he begot Enosh; and
Abel is called son, for he had Adam as father, but is not called father,
for he did not have a son: so also in the case of genus and species. The
first genus, which is not divided from another genus and does not have a
genus before it, is genus only and is not species and is called most
generic genus, which defining they say: Most generic genus is that
which, being a genus, is not a species because it has no genus above it.
Those divided from it, if they have under them other species divided
from them, are both species of those before them, that is of those
above, from which they are cut, and genera of those cut from them, that
is of those below, and are called both genera and subaltern species. The
last and lower species that do not have under them other species are not
called genera but species only, because they do not have under them
other species divided from them; for it is impossible to set a genus, if
it does not have under it species divided from it. The species that is
not a genus is therefore called most specific species, just as the genus
that is not a species is called most generic genus. One must know that
it is necessary that the species receive both the name of their genus
and the definition, and of the genus from genus up to the most generic.
But the species cannot receive one another's definition.

First, then, and most generic genus is substance; for even if substance
and accident are cut from being, yet being is not their genus, because
they receive the name of being, but they do not receive the definition;
for being is defined thus: Being is a thing either self-subsistent and
not needing another for constitution, or what cannot be by itself but
has its existence in another; but substance is a self-subsistent thing
and not needing another for constitution only, (ῃ. ς. 84–89 ετ–ι.
121–125) and accident a thing that cannot be by itself but has its
existence in another only. See, neither did substance receive the whole
definition of being, nor accident, but substance the half and accident
the other half. (ῃ. ς. 93–96) (ῃ. ι. 138 ς) But the species want to
receive both the name and the whole complete definition of the genus. So
that substance is not a species having no genus above it, but it itself
is first and most generic genus. This substance, then, is cut into body
and incorporeal; body and the incorporeal, then, are species of
substance. Again body is cut into animate and inanimate; see again body,
being a species of substance, is genus of the animate and of the
inanimate.
The animate again is divided into sensitive and insensitive. Sensitive,
then, is the animal that has life and sensation, and insensitive the
plant; for it does not have sensation. The plant is called animate
because it has nutritive and augmentative and generative power. Animal
again is cut into rational and irrational, the rational into mortal and
immortal, the mortal into man, horse, ox, and such things, which are no
longer cut into other species but into individuals, that is hypostases;
for man is divided into Peter, Paul, John, and the remaining particular
men, who are not species but hypostases. For the species, as we said, do
not receive one another's definition, as body does not receive the
definition of the incorporeal, man does not receive the definition of
the horse; but Peter and Paul and John receive one definition, that of
man, and likewise the remaining particular men, so that they are not
species of man but individuals, that is hypostases. Again the species
when cut imparts to those below it both the name and the definition. But
Peter when cut into soul and body imparts neither to the soul nor to the
body the name and the definition; for neither the soul alone is Peter
nor the body alone but both together. Further, every division that takes
place from genus into species reaches as far as two or three, rarely
four; for it is impossible for a genus to be divided into five species
and above. But man is divided into all particular men, who are infinite
in number. Therefore some say that the division from species into
individuals is not even called division but enumeration. Whence it is
clear that Peter and Paul and John are not species but individuals, that
is hypostases. Nor is man genus of Peter and Paul and John and the
remaining hypostases, but species. Therefore man is most specific
species; for he is species of what is above and species of those below.
Likewise also the horse and the dog and such things are most specific
species. Those between the most generic genus and the most specific
species are subaltern genera and species, species of those above and
genera of those below. And these are the essential and natural
differences and qualities, which are called divisive and constitutive,
divisive of those above and constitutive of those below; for body and
the incorporeal divide substance, likewise the animate and the
inanimate divide body, likewise the sensitive and the insensitive divide
the animate. These, then, constitute the animal. For I take substance
animate sensitive and I make the animal; for animal is animate sensitive
substance. I take again substance inanimate insensitive and I make the
stone. Again I take substance animate insensitive

and I make a plant. Again the rational and the irrational divide the
animal, the mortal and the immortal divide the rational. I take then the
animal, being a genus, and the rational and the mortal and I constitute
man; for man is mortal rational animal. And I take the animal and the
irrational and the mortal and I constitute the horse, say, or dog and
such things. And they are called essential and natural differences,
because these make species differ from another species and nature and
substance from another substance and nature.
3 Concerning the individual.
The individual is said in four ways. For individual is what is not cut
and not partitioned, as the point and the now and the monad, which are
also called without quantity. Individual is said also of the hard to
cut, that is what is cut with difficulty, as the adamant stone and such
things. Individual is said also of the species which is no longer cut
into other species, that is the most specific species, as man, horse,
and such things. But individual is said chiefly of what is cut, yet does
not preserve after the cutting the first species, as Peter is cut into
soul and body, but neither the soul by itself is a complete man or
complete Peter nor the body. About this individual the discourse among
the philosophers is, which in the case of substance signifies the
hypostasis.
4 Concerning difference.
This is said in three ways, commonly and properly and most properly. For
it is impossible that two things not differ from each other in
something. By some things, then, species differs from species, and by
others a hypostasis from the homoeidetic and consubstantial hypostasis,
and by others a hypostasis from itself. For the species of man differs
from the species of horse according to the rational and the irrational.
And the rational and the irrational are called essential difference.
Likewise also all things by which species differs from species are
called natural and essential and constitutive difference and quality and
natural property, which exists in the whole species without variation,
which is called among the philosophers most proper difference as more
intimate and indicative of the nature. Again man differs from man or
horse from horse or dog from dog, that is an individual from a
homoeidetic individual, in that the one is tall, the other short, the
one old, the other young, the one snub-nosed, the other hook-nosed, the
one prudent, the other foolish. All these are called non-essential
differences and qualities, which is the accident, about which we will
speak in what follows.
5 Concerning accident.
Accident is what comes to be and passes away without the destruction of
the subject. And again, what admits of both being and not being in the
same thing; for it is possible for a man both to be white and not to be
white, and likewise tall and prudent and snub-nosed. This accident is
divided into two, into the commonly so called difference and into the
properly difference. Commonly, then, difference is the separable
accident, as one sits and another stands; it is possible, when the
sitter rises and the stander sits, that their difference be separated
and they take one in place of the other. And a man is also said to
differ from himself according to the separable accident; for he differs
from himself in sitting and standing, in being young and growing old, in
being sick and being healthy, and such things. Properly, difference is
the inseparable accident, as someone is snub-nosed; it is impossible for
snubness to be separated from him, and likewise grey-eyedness and such
things. According to these inseparable accidents, then, individual
differs from individual, that is hypostasis from hypostasis

differs, but he himself never from himself. And these do not contribute
to the definition of the nature; for it is possible for a man to be
snub-nosed and not to be. And when grey-eyedness passes away the man
remains no less a man.
6 Concerning property.
Property is said in four ways. First, what belongs to the species alone,
but not to all, as doing geometry to man; for only man is a geometer,
but not every man is a geometer. Second, what belongs to all of the
species, but not to it alone, as being two-footed; for every man is
two-footed, but not only man is two-footed but also a dove and such
things. Third, what belongs to all and to it alone, but not always, as
growing grey to man; for this fits every man and man alone, but not
always but in old age. Fourth, what comes to be when the first three
concur, that is to all and to it alone and always, which also converts,
as the risible to man and the neighing to the horse and such things; for
only man has the risible and every man and always, even if he does not
always use it. For if anything is man, it is in every case risible, and
if anything is risible, it is in every case man. This is converting.
About this signification the discourse among the philosophers is. Which
also describing we say: Property is what belongs to all of the species
and to it alone and always. And they divide this in three ways: from the
organization, that is formation, as having broad nails and walking
upright to man; from the activity, as to fire being carried to the
highest; from the power, as we say there belongs to fire the heating
power that exceeds the heats of the other bodies. And property is called
non-essential.
7 Concerning the things predicated.
Every predication is either of more or of equals, but never of less. And
of more, when the more universal are predicated of the more particular;
the more universal, then, are those above, and the more particular those
below. Most universal of all is being, therefore it is predicated of
all; for both substance is called being and accident is called being.
But we cannot say that being is substance; for being is not only
substance but also accident. Likewise also the genera are predicated of
the species as more universal, but the species are not predicated of
their genera; for the species are more particular than the genera.
Substance, then, is predicated of animal and animal of man; for animal
is substance and man is animal. But it does not convert; for every man
is animal, but not every animal is man. For the horse also and the dog
are animals, and likewise every animal is substance, but not every
substance is animal; for the stone also and the wood are substance,
which are not animals. Likewise also the species is predicated of the
individuals contained under it, that is hypostases, as more universal.
But the individual, that is the hypostasis, is not predicated of the
species; for the hypostasis is more particular than the species. Peter,
then, is man and Paul is man, but not every man is Peter or Paul; for
there are also other hypostases under the species of man. And the
differences too are predicated of the species in which they exist and of
their individuals. For the differences are more universal than the
species; for the rational is more universal than the species of man. For
every man is rational, but not every rational is man; for the angel too,
being rational, is not man. And this is predication of more; but
predication of equals is when it converts. For the properties are
predicated

of the species of which they are properties, but the species also are
predicated of their properties; for every man is risible, and every
risible is man. For if a monkey is also said to laugh, it does not laugh
with the heart but with the figures; for it is an imitative animal. So
that the predication of genera of species and that of differences of
species and that of species of individuals is called of more, but that
of properties of equals. And these that convert as equals are also
called counter-predicated.
8 Concerning synonymous and homonymous predication.
Synonymous predication is when the subject receives both the name and
the definition of that very name, as animal is predicated of man. And
man receives both the name and the definition of animal; for animal is
animate sensitive substance. And man too admits this definition; for man
is both substance and animate and sensitive. Homonymous predication is
when it receives the name, but the definition not at all, as the image
of a man receives the name of man, but does not receive the definition
of man; for the definition of man is rational mortal animal, receptive
of intellect and science. But the image is not even an animal; for it is
not animate, nor rational, nor receptive of intellect and science. One
must know that whatever is predicated of something as of a subject, that
is synonymously, will also be predicated of what is subject to it, as
animal is predicated of man as of a subject, that is synonymously, and
man of Peter; for Peter is subject to man. Animal, then, is predicated
also of Peter; for Peter too is an animal. Subject is said in two ways,
one toward existence, the other toward predication. And toward
existence, as substance is subject to accidents; for in it they have
being and apart from it they do not subsist. The subject toward
predication is the particular; for the particular is subject to the more
universal toward predication, since the more universal is predicated of
the more particular, as animal is predicated of man. The universal is
said to be of a subject, and the more particular a subject toward
predication. Accident is said to be in a subject, the substance, and
substance a subject toward existence.
9 Concerning predication in the what it is and predication in the of
what sort it is.
Predication in the what it is is one thing and predication in the of
what sort it is another; and that in the what it is, when asked "what is
man" we say "animal"; and in the of what sort it is, when asked "what
sort of animal" we say "rational mortal." So that genus and species are
predicated in the what it is, but difference, both essential and
non-essential, that is accident, and property are predicated in the of
what sort it is. But hypostasis shows neither what it is nor of what
sort it is, but who it is; for asked "who is this" we say "Peter." Then
asked "what is Peter" we say "man." Again asked "what sort of man" we
say "tall, say, or short." One must know that things differing by nature
are called other and other. For we say: Man is other and horse is other,
and other according to nature; for the species of man is different and
that of horse another. Things differing in number, that is the
hypostases, are called another and another. For we say that Peter is
another and Paul another. But we cannot say that Peter is other and Paul
other, since we would be lying; for they are one in nature, they are
not

one in number. One must know that substance is called other, and
likewise the essential differences, but accident other-like, because the
essential differences are observed about the species, that is about the
nature, and constitute it, but accident about the individual; for the
accidents are constitutive of the hypostasis. Man, then, is other and
horse other, but Peter is other-like and another and Paul other-like and
another. Every difference, essential and non-essential, makes
other-like; for the other-like signifies both the other and the
other-like. And nature signifies the what, but hypostasis the someone
and this something. And every difference is of what sort it is.
10 Concerning hypostasis and the enhypostatic and the anhypostatic.
The name of hypostasis signifies two things; for said simply it
signifies substance simply, but hypostasis by itself signifies the
individual and the distinctive person. And the enhypostatic too
signifies two things; for it signifies also what simply is, according to
which signification we call not only substance simply but also accident
enhypostatic. And it also signifies hypostasis by itself, that is the
individual. And the anhypostatic too is said in two ways; for
anhypostatic is said both of what in no way at all is. Anhypostatic is
said again also of accident, because accident does not have its own
existence, but subsists in the substance.
11 Concerning substance and nature and form, and individual and person
and hypostasis.
The outside philosophers, according to the account already spoken, said
there was a difference of substance and nature, calling substance the
simply being, and nature substance made into a species by the essential
differences and having, together with the simply being, also the being
in such a way, whether rational or irrational, whether mortal or
immortal, that is it itself, as we say, the unchangeable and
unalterable principle and cause and power placed by the Creator in each
species toward motion, in angels toward thinking and imparting thoughts
to one another apart from uttered speech, in men toward thinking and
reasoning and distributing to one another through uttered speech the
thoughts in the heart, in the irrational the vital and sensitive and
respiratory motion, in plants the nutritive and augmentative and
generative power, in stones that of being heated and of being cooled and
the change from place to place moved by another, that is the inanimate.
This they called nature, that is the most specific species, as angel,
man, horse, and such things, as more universal and containing the
hypostases and existing similarly and without omission in each of the
hypostases contained under them. So that they called the more particular
hypostasis, and the more universal and containing the hypostases they
called nature, and the simply existence they called substance. But the
holy fathers, setting aside the many wranglings, called the common and
what is said of many, that is the most specific species, substance and
nature and form, as angel, man, horse, dog, and such things; for
substance is so called from being, and nature from having grown, and
being and having grown are the same; and form and species signify the
same as nature. And the particular they called individual and person and
hypostasis, as Peter, Paul. And hypostasis wants to have substance with
accidents and to subsist by itself and to be observed by sense, that is
in act.

It is impossible for two hypostases not to differ from each other by
accidents and to differ from each other in number. One must know that
the characterizing properties, the accidents, are what characterize the
hypostasis.
12 Concerning division.
Division is the first cutting of the thing, as animal is divided into
rational and irrational; (ῃ. ι. 20–27) subdivision is a cutting and
division of the one part of the two sections, as when animal has been
divided into rational and irrational we divide the one part, that is the
rational, into mortal and immortal; superdivision is when, having made a
division of a thing, I make again another division of the same thing, as
man is divided into male and female (see, a division) and again man is
divided into soul and body (see, a superdivision). Division and
superdivision do not always take place, but when not all things are
contained under the first division; for see, both in the male and in the
female soul and body are observed. One must know that the two species
cut from the same genus are called co-divided, as animal is divided into
rational and irrational, which are called co-divided. And there are
eight divisive modes: either as genus into species, as animal is divided
into rational and irrational, or as species into individuals, as man is
divided into Peter and Paul and the remaining particular men, or as
whole into parts; and this in two ways, either homoeomerous or
anhomoeomerous. Homoeomerous, then, are when the parts admit the name
and the definition of the whole and of one another, as when we cut flesh
into many fleshes, and each part of the flesh is called flesh and admits
the definition of flesh. Anhomoeomerous, when the parts admit neither
the name nor the definition either of the whole or of one another, as
when we divide Socrates into head, feet, and hands; for neither the head
nor the feet nor the hands admit the name or the definition of Socrates
or of one another. or as a homonymous utterance into different
significations, and this in two ways: either as whole or as part. As
whole, as the utterance dog; for it is applied both to the land dog and
to the starry and to the marine, which are a whole something and not a
part of an animal; as part, when the name tongue is applied to the tip
of the shoe and to the tip of the pipe and to the tasting part of
animals, which are parts and not wholes. or as substance into accidents,
as when I say: Of men some are white, some black; or as accidents into
substances, as when I say: Of white things some are animate, some
inanimate; or as accident into accidents, as when I say: Of cold things
some are dry, some wet, or as things from one and toward one; from one,
as from medicine a medical book, a medical tool, toward one as a
healthful drug, a healthful food; for they look toward one thing,
health. In this way, then, being is divided into substance and accident.
One must know that in not even one mode of division is the naturally
prior and posterior observed in the sections, nor the more and less,
except in those from one and toward one.
13 Concerning the naturally prior. Naturally prior, then, is what
brings in with it and is not brought in with it and destroys with it
and is not destroyed with it, as animal is naturally prior to man.
For when animal is destroyed and is not, of necessity neither will man
be; for man is an animal.

But when man is destroyed and is not, it is possible for animal to be;
for there will be horse, dog, and such things, which are animals. Again
when man is brought in, animal too is in every case brought in; for man
is an animal. But when animal is brought in, it is not in every case
that man is brought in with it but horse or dog or one of such things;
for they too are animals. Neither, then, is Peter naturally prior to
Paul, nor the rational animal to the irrational animal. For when Peter
is destroyed and is not, Paul will be; and when Paul is brought in,
Peter is not brought in with him, and when Peter is brought in, Paul is
not brought in with him, and neither is Peter more, that is more so, a
man than Paul, nor Paul than Peter. But a drug is found more healthful
than a drug and a book more medical than another book.
14 Concerning definition.
Definition is a concise account indicative of the nature of the subject
thing, that is the account that by a little signifies the nature of the
subject thing, as man is rational mortal animal, receptive of intellect
and science. For many have made discourses about the nature of man at
length, that is extended and great, but they are not concise, therefore
they are not definitions. There are also concise accounts as the
apophthegms, but since they do not show the nature of a thing, they are
not definitions. And the name too often shows the nature of the subject
thing, but it is not a definition; for the name is one word, but the
definition is an account. And an account is composed of two words at the
least. So that the definition wants to be composed of different words
and to be concise and to show the nature of the thing defined by it. The
definition is constituted from genus and constitutive differences, that
is essential, as in the definition of animal; for animal is animate
sensitive substance. See, substance is genus, and the animate and the
sensitive constitutive differences. It is taken also from matter and
form, as a statue is made from bronze, signifying the form of a man; the
bronze, then, is matter, and the form of the man form. And matter is
analogous to genus, and form to difference. It is taken also from
subject and end, as medicine is an art occupied about human bodies,
productive of health. See, the subjects of medicine are human bodies,
and its end health. Description is composed from the non-essential, that
is properties and accidents, as man is risible, walking upright, having
broad nails; for all these are non-essential. Therefore it is also
called description, as sketching and not showing the essential existence
of the subject but the concomitants. The descriptive definition is mixed
from essential and non-essential, as man is rational animal, walking
upright, having broad nails. And definition is so called by metaphor
from the boundary-marks of the land; for just as the boundary-mark
separates what is proper to each, so also the definition separates the
nature of each from the nature of the other. The health of a definition
is neither to fall short nor to superabound in words, and the vice to
fall short and to superabound. A complete definition is one that
converts with the definable, and incomplete one that does not convert.
Neither, then, the one falling short in words converts, nor the one
superabounding. For when it superabounds in words, it falls short in
things; and when it falls short in words, it superabounds in things. As
the complete definition of man "mortal rational animal." See, it
converts; for every mortal rational animal is man, and every man is
mortal rational animal. If then it falls short by one word, it
superabounds in things, as rational animal. See, it fell short in the
word, for I did

not say "mortal," and it superabounded in things, for not only man is
rational animal but also angel, and it does not convert. But if I say
"rational mortal animal, grammatical," again it does not convert; for it
superabounded in the word by saying "grammatical," and fell short in
things. For it did not define every man, but only the grammatical. For
every grammatical mortal rational animal is man, but not every man is
grammatical mortal rational animal; for not every man is grammatical.
15 Concerning homonyms.
Homonyms are things that share in the name, but differ in the definition
or the description. As the name dog is homonymous, and it shows the land
dog and the marine dog; and the land dog has one definition and the
marine another, since also the nature is other and other. And they
describe homonyms thus: Homonyms are things of which the name only is
common, but the account of the substance according to the name is other.
Here he called account the definition and the description; and he said
according to the name, showing that the definition of that very name, in
respect of which they become homonyms, is other. As the land dog and the
marine dog are homonyms according to the name dog; for if someone wishes
to give the definition of the land dog and of the marine, in respect of
which each of them is called dog, he gives one definition to the land
and another to the marine. But it is possible for them also to share
again in the definition and in the name; for both are called animals and
receive the definition of animal, but they are not homonyms according to
the name animal but rather synonyms. And in the case of homonyms one
must seek three things: whether there are homonyms and of how many
significations it is applied and about which signification the question
has been made.
16 Concerning synonym.
Synonyms are whatever share both in the name and in the definition or
the description of the same name, as animal shows both man and horse,
and according to this name, that is of animal, they are synonyms; for
each of them receives both the name and the definition of animal. And
they describe synonyms thus: Synonyms are things of which both the name
is common and the account of the substance according to the name is the
same.
17 Concerning polynyms.
Polynyms are whatever share in the definition but differ in the name,
that is when the same thing is called by many names, as phasganon, aor,
spathe, machaira, xiphos; for all these names admit one definition, that
is two-edged iron, that is iron sharpened on both sides. And they
describe polynyms thus: Very many names said of one proper thing.
18 Concerning others and heteronyms.
Things that differ in both, that is in the name and in the definition,
either have one subject and are called heteronyms, as ascent and
descent, for they have one subject, the ladder, or they do not have one
subject and are called others, as substance and accident; for these have
both another name and another description and do not have one subject.
Of both of these, the heteronyms and the others, this is the
description: Things of which both the name and the definition are other.

19 Concerning paronyms.
There are certain things in the middle of homonyms and synonyms, sharing
and differing both in the name and in the definition, which are called
paronyms, as from grammar the grammarian; for they share in the name,
but differ according to the ending of the name, that is the last
syllable; and again they both share and differ in the definition,
because grammar is knowledge, but the grammarian a substance in which
the knowledge is. Paronyms are whatever have their appellation from
something, differing in the case, that is in the inflection of the name.
One must know that grammar and music are not paronyms, but the musician
was named paronymously from music and the grammarian from grammar and
the just man from justice. One must know that paronyms contain the
things from which they were named paronymously, as the grammarian
grammar and the just man justice, but those from one in no way; for the
medical tool does not contain medicine.
20 Concerning the ten most generic genera.
Of things said, some are said simply and without combination, as
substance, accident, and such things, some with combination, as a horse
runs, Socrates philosophizes. Of things said simply and without
combination, 1 some signify substance, as man, horse; 2 some the
quantum, as two, three, two cubits, three cubits; 3 some the relative,
as father, son; 4 some the qualified, as white, black; 5 some the where,
as in a temple, in a marketplace; 6 some the when, as last year, today;
7 some lying, as standing, sitting; 8 some having, as being clothed,
being shod; 9 some acting, as burning, cutting; 10 some being acted on,
as being burned, being cut.
These ten are called categories as from being said of certain things;
for to declare is to say. One must know that each of these is a most
generic genus. Of these ten categories, which are also most generic
genera, the one is substance, and the nine accidents. And they are
these: substance, quantum, relative, qualified, where, when, lying,
having, acting, being acted on. 21 Concerning the homogeneous and the
homoeidetic, the heterogeneous and the heteroeidetic, and things
differing in number. Homogeneous are whatever are ranked under the same
category. Heterogeneous are whatever are under another and another
category; these are other in genus. Homoeidetic are whatever are ranked
under the same species and share in the account of the substance, as
Peter and Paul. Heteroeidetic are things that differ from one another in
species, that is whatever differ in the account of the substance, as
man, horse. They differ in number, whatever by the combination of
accidents have marked off the property of their own hypostasis and have
had existence by themselves, that is the individuals, as Peter, Paul,
and each of men. Of things wholly heterogeneous the differences too are
other in species, as animal and science; for animal falls under
substance, and science under the qualified. Of animal, then, constitutive
differences are the animate, the sensitive, and divisive the rational,
the irrational, the winged, the aquatic; of science constitutive those
of existing in the animate and rational, and further the hard to change,
and divisive grammar, philosophy. For of whichever category the genus
is, of that also the species and the differences of the species; but of
genera and species under one another nothing prevents the differences
from being the same, even if not all.
Differences here I mean those that constitute them. One must know that
the nine

categories apart from substance each have, even if they are accidents,
constitutive and divisive differences, and each of them is a most
generic genus and has both species and subaltern genera and most
specific species. For in every case, where there is genus, there are
also divisive differences; for these are what divide the genera into
species; and where there are species, there are also constitutive
differences; for these are what constitute the species. The one is taken
in three ways: either in genus, as we say man and horse are one in genus
and the same; for they are under one genus, under animal; or in species,
as we say Socrates and Plato, being under one species, under man, are
one in species and the same; or in number, as we say Socrates is one by
himself, separated from the remaining men.
22 Concerning the in something.
The in something is in eleven ways: 1 as genus in species, as animal in
the definition of man, being genus of man; 2 as species in genus, as man
in the division of animal; 3 in place, as a priest in the temple; 4 in
time, as Noah in the time of the flood; 5 in a vessel, as wine in a
krater; 6 as whole in parts, as Socrates in his own members, head and
hands and feet; and this is not in something but in some things; 7 as
part in whole, as head, hand in Socrates; 8 as form in matter, as the
form of the statue in the bronze; 9 as in the productive cause, as all
things in God; 10 as in the final cause, as the bed in the rest of men;
for a bed comes to be for the rest of men; 11 as in a subject, as white
in a body. One must know that parts are said of a whole, but a whole of
parts in no way, but a whole in parts.
23 Concerning substance.
Substance is a self-subsistent thing needing nothing else for
constitution; and again substance is everything that is self-hypostatic
and does not have its being in another, that is what is not through
another and does not have its existence in another and does not need
another for constitution, but is in itself, in which also accident has
its existence; for color has come to be through the body, that it may
color it, and not the body through the color. And color exists in the
body and not the body in the color; whence also the color is said of the
body and not the body of the color. Often, therefore, when the color is
changed and altered, the substance is not changed, that is the body, but
remains the same. And substance is so called from being. 24 Concerning
nature. Nature is a principle of the motion and rest of each of beings,
as the earth is moved according to sprouting, and is at rest according
to change from place to place; for it is not moved from place to place.
The principle, then, and the cause of its motion and rest, according to
which it has grown to be moved and to be at rest thus essentially, that
is naturally and not according to accident, is called nature from having
grown to be and to exist in such a way. And this is nothing other than
substance; for from the substance it has such a power, that is the
motion and rest. And the substance is the cause of its motion and rest.
And nature is so called from having grown.
25 Concerning form.
Form is substance as it were formed and made into a species by the
essential differences, which signifies the most specific species; as
substance formed and made into a species by the animate and sensitive
body and having completed the animal, and again having taken on the
rational and the mortal completes the species of man. Which

most specific species is called form, as formed substance. The holy
fathers, then, assign the name of substance and nature and form to the
most specific species and say that substance and nature and form are the
same, the most specific species; and they call consubstantial and of the
same nature and homogeneous and of the same form the individuals under
the same most specific species. And they call heteroousion and of
another nature and heteroeidetic and heterogeneous and of another form
the most specific species. For it is not possible for a species not to
be heteroousion and of another nature and of another form than another
species or a nature than another nature or a substance than another
substance. One must know that it is impossible for one composite nature
to come to be from two substances, that is natures; for it is impossible
for the co-divided differences to exist constitutively together in the
same thing at once. But it is possible for one composite hypostasis to
come to be from different natures, as man is composed from soul and
body; for even if one nature of men is spoken of, yet the particular man
is not said to be of one nature. For one composite nature of men is
spoken of, because all the composite hypostases of men fall under one
species. But the particular man is not said to be of one nature, because
each hypostasis of men has been composed from two natures, from soul I
mean and body, and keeps these unconfused in itself; and witness is the
division that comes to be from death.
26 Concerning hypostasis. The name of hypostasis signifies two things;
sometimes the simply existence, according to which signification
substance and hypostasis are the same, whence some of the holy fathers
called them natures, that is the hypostases; sometimes the existence by
itself and of its own constitution, according to which signification it
shows the individual differing in number, that is Peter, Paul, a certain
horse. For one must know that neither formless substance subsists by
itself, nor essential difference, nor species, nor accident, but only
the hypostases, that is the individuals, and in them both the substances
and the essential differences, both the species and the accidents, are
observed. And simple substance is observed in the same way in all
hypostases, both in the inanimate and the animate, both rational and
irrational, both mortal and immortal. But the essential differences are
some in the inanimate and others in the animate, some in the rational
and others in the irrational, likewise some in the mortal and others in
the immortal, and, to speak simply, in the hypostases of each most
specific species the same, joining them to one another by the account of
the substance, and separating them from the heteroeidetic hypostases.
Likewise also the accidents are observed in them, that is the
hypostases, separating each hypostasis from the homoeidetic hypostases.
Therefore the individual has chiefly inherited the name of hypostasis;
for in it the substance subsists in act, having taken on the accidents.
27 Concerning person. Person is what through its own activities and
properties furnishes us a clear and circumscribed manifestation from
those of the same nature as it, as Gabriel speaking with the holy
Theotokos, being one of the angels, being present there alone was
speaking, separated from the consubstantial angels by the presence in
the place and the speaking, and Paul addressing the people on the steps,
being one of men, was distinguished from the remaining men by his
properties and activities. One must know that the holy fathers called
hypostasis and person and individual the same, what subsists by itself
with its own constitution from substance and accidents and differs in
number and shows the someone, as Peter, Paul, this horse. And hypostasis
is so called from subsisting. 28 Concerning the enhypostatic. And the
enhypostatic too sometimes signifies the simply existence, according to
which signification we call not only substance simply enhypostatic but
also accident, which chiefly is not

enhypostatic but hetero-hypostatic, and sometimes it also shows the
hypostasis by itself, that is the individual, which chiefly is not
enhypostatic but is and is called hypostasis. But chiefly enhypostatic
is either what does not subsist by itself but is observed in the
hypostases, as the species, that is the nature of men, is not observed
in its own hypostasis but in Peter and Paul and the remaining hypostases
of men, or what is composed with another different according to
substance into the genesis of some whole and completes one composite
hypostasis, as man is composed from soul and body; neither the soul
alone is called hypostasis nor the body but enhypostatic, and what is
completed from both is the hypostasis of both.
For hypostasis chiefly is and is called what subsists by itself with its
own constitution. Again enhypostatic is said of the nature taken on by
another hypostasis and having had its existence in it. Whence also the
flesh of the Lord, not having subsisted by itself even for a moment of
time, is not hypostasis but rather enhypostatic; for it subsisted in the
hypostasis of God the Word, taken on by it, and this it both had and has
as hypostasis. 29 Concerning the anhypostatic. And the anhypostatic too
is said in two ways; for sometimes it signifies what in no way at all
is, that is the non-existent, and sometimes what does not have being in
itself but has its existence in another, that is accident. 30 Division
of being and of substance. Being is divided into substance and accident,
not as genus into species but as a homonymous utterance or as things
from one and toward one. Substance is most generic genus; this is
divided into body and incorporeal, body into animate and inanimate, the
animate into sensitive and insensitive (animal, zoophyte, and plant),
animal into rational and irrational, the rational into mortal and
immortal, the mortal into man, ox, and such things, man into Peter,
Paul, and the remaining particular men, who are individuals and
hypostases and persons.
31 Concerning what belongs as property of substance.
What substance and nature and form are has been said, and what
hypostasis and individual and person, both enhypostatic and
anhypostatic, and what the difference of substance and accidents is, and
that substance is more sovereign than accidents as the accidents having
their existence in it. And there has been speech also about its division
and how substance differs from the essential differences, that through
them substance, being made into a species, completes such a species and
becomes of such a sort. And there has been speech also of what nature
is and what form and hypostasis and person and individual, how those
outside have opined about these, and how the holy fathers, the disciples
and teachers of the truth and of true philosophy, have rightly divided.
Come then now let us say the properties of substance.
A property of substance is not being in a subject; for it rather is
subject to the accident for being and does not itself have its existence
in another. And this property belongs also to the essential differences;
for what is in a subject neither preserves when present nor destroys
when absent; therefore it is not taken in the definition, and it is in
every case an accident. But the essential differences are not accidents
and when present they preserve and when absent they destroy; therefore
they are also taken in the definition. A property also is being
predicated synonymously, that is imparting both the name and the
definition; and not having something contrary, for to the stone, that is
to the very substance of the stone, nothing is contrary; and not
admitting the more and less; and this is a property also of the
essential differences, for neither is man more substance or animal than
the horse, nor the horse more than man; and being

receptive of contraries in turn and not at the same time but according
to its own change. And I mean contrary non-essential accidents; for the
rational does not admit the irrational, but the body is heated and being
changed is cooled, and the soul sometimes receives virtue, sometimes
vice. 32 Still concerning the homogeneous and the homoeidetic, the
heterogeneous and the heteroeidetic, and the co-hypostatic and things
differing in number.
Homogeneous are whatever are ranked under the same category, as whatever
are under substance, and likewise in the other nine categories; for one
must know that there are ten categories in all, that is the most generic
genera, under which every utterance said simply is referred.
And they are these: 1 substance, as stone; 2 quantum, as two, three; 3
relative, as father, son; 4 qualified, as white, black; 5 where, as in
Damascus, and this is indicative of place; 6 when, yesterday, tomorrow;
and this is indicative of time; 7 having, as wearing a garment; 8 lying,
as standing, sitting; 9 acting, as burning; 10 being acted on, as being
burned. Heterogeneous are whatever are under another and another
category; and homogeneous, as man and horse, for both are under
substance, and heterogeneous as man and science; for man is under
substance, and science under quality. Homoeidetic are whatever are
ranked under the same species and share in the account of the substance,
as Peter, Paul; for both are under one species, that of man.
Heteroeidetic are whatever differ in species, that is in the account of
the substance, as man and horse. And the holy fathers say that
homogeneous and homoeidetic are the same, the consubstantial, that is the
hypostases under the same species. Co-hypostatic are when two natures
are united in one hypostasis and have one composite hypostasis and one
person, as soul and body. Hetero-hypostatic and differing in number are
whatever by the combination of accidents have marked off the property of
their own hypostasis, that is whatever differ from one another by
accidents and have existence in turn and by themselves, as the
individuals Peter and Paul; for this one is another and that one
another.
33 Concerning the quantum and quantity.
Quantity is a heap of monads; for they do not call the monad a quantity
but a principle of quantity. When monad and monad come together, then,
two come to be, so that quantity is not a division but a heap and a
joining of monads; for to divide the two into one and one in turn, this
is division, but to say the one and one two, this is rather a joining.
One must know that quantity is the measure itself and the number that
measures and numbers, and quanta are the things subject to the number
and the measure, that is the things measured and numbered. Of quanta
some are discrete, some continuous. Continuous, then, is when the thing
measured is one, as one wood is found two cubits, three cubits, or a
stone or one of such things, and being one it is measured and is called
continuous. Discrete are things separated from one another, as in the
case of ten stones or ten palms; for these are separated from one
another. These, then, are said to be numbered, unless through smallness
and multitude they are measured by a modius or one of such things, as
wheat or something such.
Continuous things are defined as those whose parts join toward some
common boundary; for the two-cubit wood being one, that is having two
cubits, the end of the one cubit and the beginning of the other cubit
are one, for they are glued and joined and are not divided from one
another; and the discrete, whose parts do not join toward some common

boundary, as in the case of ten stones. For if you number five and five,
they do not have a common boundary joining them; for if you give
something between the five and the five, they become eleven and not ten;
and the name itself shows the continuous and the discrete. Under the
discrete quantum, then, number and speech are brought. And we call
number here the things numbered; for all things numbered are discrete,
as has been shown. And speech too is discrete; for speech, being numbered
by words, does not have a common boundary joining its parts. For if the
speech has ten words and you divide them into five and five, they do not
have a common boundary joining them; for if something is inserted in the
middle, they become eleven and not ten. Likewise also the word, being
numbered by syllables, does not have a common boundary joining the
syllables, as Socrates; between the syllable So and the kra there is no
common boundary joining them. And continuous quanta are five: body,
surface, line, place, time. One must know that the point is without
quantity; for it is not measured nor numbered, since it has no
dimension. But the line has one dimension, for it is length without
breadth, as: this then is brought under the continuous quantum. For
being one it is measured and its parts have a common boundary joining
them, the point between. Surface is the outer part of the body, from
appearing. And it has two dimensions, length and breadth. And this too,
being one, is measured, and its parts have a common boundary joining
them, the line between. One must know that the even and equal surface is
called plane, and the uneven and crooked simply surface. And body has
three dimensions: length, breadth, depth, that is thickness; and being
one it is measured, and its parts have a common boundary joining them,
the surface. And place too is a surface of the air; for your place is a
surface, that is the end of the air containing you, and as surface it is
brought under the continuous quantum. And time too is measured into the
past and into the future, and its parts have a common boundary joining
them, the now; and the now is without quantity. See then there are three
things without quantity: the monad and the point and the now. And
chiefly these seven are called quanta: number, speech, time, place,
line, surface, body. According to accident we also call quanta the
things observed in them, action, motion, color, and such things; as, if
the action and the motion come to be in much time, we say much action
and much motion, and if in little, little. Likewise also, if whiteness
were in a large body, we say much white, and if in a little, little.
Further of the quantum some is definite, some indefinite. Definite, then,
is what can be measured or numbered, and the indefinite what by some
excess exceeds every measure and every number; and it is called great
and much indefinitely, as we say: Great is the compassion of God, great
the mystery of the economy of God the Word. One must know that Aristotle
places the great and small and much and little and the greater and the
smaller and less and more and double and half and such things under the
relatives. We say, then, that it is possible for the same thing
according to one aim and another to be brought under one category and
another. For as signifying number and measure the things aforesaid are
brought under the quantum, but as having a relation toward one another
and as said toward one another under the relatives; for the great is
said great toward the small and the double toward the half, and likewise
the rest. And body, inasmuch as it is physical, is brought under
substance, but inasmuch as it is mathematical, that is measured, under
the quantum. Further of the quantum some is magnitude, some multitude.
Magnitude, then, is measured, and multitude is numbered. How-great
follows magnitude, and the quantum

follows multitude. There are three properties of the quantum, which are
called concomitants: first, not having something contrary by itself; for
the body itself by itself does not have something contrary, but inasmuch
as it is, say, white, it has something contrary, the black. One must
know that to the number two another number is not contrary; for if it
will be, there will be many.
For all the other numbers will be contrary, and nature is found unjust,
having set many against one; for it is not possible for many to be
contrary to one. Second, not admitting the more and less. For neither
are the two palms more two than the two men. And what does not have a
contrary does not admit the more and less. Third, what belongs to the
quantum alone and to every quantum, the equal and unequal. For a line is
equal to a line and unequal.
34 Concerning relatives. Relatives are these, whatever themselves, just
what they are, are said to be of others or in some other way toward
another. And they are said to be of others as a father of a son; for the
father is in every case called father of a son. And toward another as
great toward small and much toward little; for it is not said much of
little but toward little. One must know that when something is observed
by itself, it is not a relative, but when it has a relation toward
another, then it is called a relative.
So that this is the being of relatives and this is their hypostasis, in
being said toward another, that is in having a relation toward another;
for the relation toward one another makes the relatives. Of relatives
some are named in the same name, as a friend is friend of a friend, an
enemy enemy of an enemy, some in another name, as a father is father of
a son, a teacher teacher of a pupil. Again of relatives some according
to excess, as the greater is greater than a lesser, some according to
the judging and the judged, as science is science of the scientifically
knowable; for science judges the scientifically knowable, that is
knowledge judges the knowable, and sense is sense of the sensible and
position position of the positioned and standing standing of the
standable and reclining reclining of the reclined and such things;
some according to power and inability, power as the hot and the thing
being heated, and inability that according to privation of the power, as
we say the sight has inability to see the starless sphere; some
according to the cause and the caused, as a father is father of a son. A
property of relatives is being said toward converting things; for a
friend is friend of a friend and a friend friend of a friend, a teacher
teacher of a pupil and a pupil pupil of a teacher; and being simultaneous
by nature. And the simultaneous by nature is bringing in with it and
being brought in with it and destroying with it and being destroyed with
it; for when there is a father there will in every case also be a son,
and when there is a son there will in every case also be a father, and
when there is no father neither will there be a son, for of whom will he
be son if there is no father? and when there is no son neither will
there be a father; for he who does not have a son will not be a father.
So that the son is destroyed with the father and the father with the
son, but it is not his hypostasis that is destroyed with it but the
relation. For even if the son remains, yet he does not remain a son; for
not having a father how will he be a son? And if we also say son of the
one who has died, either we do not speak chiefly but by catachresis, or
as the father not having perished and gone into not being because of the
immortality of the soul. One must know that each category is a most
generic genus and has both genera and subaltern species and differences
divisive of the genera and constitutive of the species and most specific
species and individuals; but neither are the constitutive differences
called essential, except of substance alone, nor are the individuals
called hypostases, except of substance alone. One must know that
substance itself too as a genus and having a relation toward another is
brought under the relatives; for the genus is genus of species and the
species are species of a genus and they are among the relatives.
Further, relatives said also toward converting things have the relation
either in the self-founded, that is substances, or not in the
self-founded, that is accidents. And if in the self-founded, either
their relation is natural as of father and son, or not natural.

If it is not natural, it is either by chance as slave and master, or
technical as pupil and teacher, or by choice as friend and friend, enemy
and enemy. And if not in the self-founded but in accidents, either
natural as the double and the half, or not natural. Relatives must first
be brought under another category as observed by themselves and then as
having a relation toward another under the relatives; for something must
first be without relation and then a relation be observed in it.
35 Concerning the qualified and quality.
Quality is that according to which certain things are named qualified.
And again quality is that according to which the things sharing in it
are said paronymously; from prudence the one who has prudence is called
prudent and hot the one sharing in heat. One must know that the
qualified is more universal than quality; for the qualified signifies
the quality and what shares in it, that is the heat and the hot, that
which has the heat; for those who have the quality are qualified, as
those who have heat are called hot. The hot are qualified, and the heat
quality. And often the quality itself too is called qualified, and
likewise in the case of the quantum and quantity. Of qualities some
exist in animate {and rational} bodies as sciences and virtues, diseases
and health, and are called habits and dispositions, some both in the
animate and in the inanimate as heat, cooling, form, figure, power and
inability. Of these some are in potency, some in act; and if in potency,
they make power and inability; and if in act, either they have gone
through in depth, as heat through the whole of fire and whiteness
through the whole of milk and of snow, and they make affection and
affective quality, or on the surface and they make figure and form.
There are, then, four species of quality: habit and disposition, power
and inability, affection and affective quality, figure and form. Habit
differs
from disposition, because habit is hard to change and more lasting, as
prudence; for one does not quickly change from prudence into folly.
Likewise also science, when someone knows accurately, the knowledge in
him is hard to change, it is a habit, and courage and temperance and
justice likewise.
Dispositions are the easily moved and quickly changing, as heat,
cooling, disease, health, and such things; for the man is disposed
according to these, but quickly changes, becoming cold from hot and
healthy from disease. And these very things too, as disease and health
and such things, if they are lasting and hard to change, are habits.
Disposition is more universal; for both are called dispositions, for the
man is somehow disposed according to these, but the hard to change
disposition is called habit, and the easily changed disposition only.
Second species of quality is power and inability, which are not in act,
but have a fitness and natural power or unfitness, as we say the child
is a musician in potency, for even if he does not have music in act, yet
he has a fitness to receive music, and the irrational unmusical as
neither having music in act nor being able to receive it; and the hard
has a power of not being quickly divided. Third species is affective
quality and affection, as heat, whiteness, cooling, blackness, and such
things. Affection, then, is easily thrown off as disposition, as if
someone through shame or through fear grows pale or blushes, but
affective quality is hard to move and hard to change. Of affective
qualities some have not come to be from an affection, that is not from
elsewhere, but exist according to substance, as heat to fire and
sweetness to honey; for neither to fire is heat

acquired, nor to honey sweetness, nor, not being such before, did they
later take on the heat and the sweetness, yet they produce the same
affection according to our sense; for fire, being hot, heats us and
honey, being sweet, sweetens us; some have come to be from some
affection, that is temperament, and sometimes produce an affection
according to our sense but not the same affection and the same quality
which they have, as colors are; for white, coming to be from some
affection and temperament, produces an affection about sight, that is
the discernment and spreading and illumination, but it does not make us
white. The affective, then, that is not acquired either exists in the
whole species, as heat in all fire, or not in all, as blackness in the
Ethiopians; for not all men are black. And these are observed not only
about body but also about soul. Fourth species of quality is figure and
form. Figure, then, is applied both to the animate and to the inanimate,
but form to the animate only. And if form or well-formed is said of the
inanimate, it is said not chiefly but by catachresis. Figure, then, is
more universal; for form too is called figure. But the figure of the
inanimate is not called form. And straightness, that is uprightness, and
crookedness, that is twistedness, belong to quality. One must know that
the qualified are for the most part said paronymously from the
qualities, as hot from heat, some also homonymously, as musical science
and a musical woman who has the musical science, and some, even if
rarely, heteronymously, as from virtue the serious; for the virtuous is
called serious. One must know that affection is twofold; for it is said
either in having already been affected, and it is brought under quality,
as the garment already whitened is called white, or in being affected
now, and it is brought under the category of being acted on, as what has
not yet been whitened but is now being whitened. One must know that
qualities are not bodies but incorporeal. For if they were bodies, they
would have been under substance; and all accidents are incorporeal and
by themselves non-existent, unless they are observed in the substance.
One must know that the essential qualities are brought under substance;
for they are parts of it as dividing it and being taken into the
definition of its species, and under whichever category the whole is,
under this also its parts will be brought. The heavy and light are
either observed in bulks as in things weighed and are brought under the
quantum, or in substance as in the elements, that is fire and earth, and
are brought under substance as essential differences, and likewise the
dense and rare, that is loose; for either they are essentially in the
elements and are brought under substance, or adventitiously in garments
and are brought under the category of lying, for they are by position.
There are three properties of quality, that is concomitants: first,
admitting contrariety; for the hot is contrary to the cold and the white
to the black; second, the more and less; for where there is contrariety,
the more and the less also are found. And the more is intensification,
and the less remission. It is possible, then, to say this species more
white than that and less cold. Third concomitant, and the most proper of
the qualified, the like and unlike. One must know that figure does not
have a contrary. One must know that not all privations are said
negatively, but there are also privations said affirmatively, as
blindness and deafness. And again not every species is named
affirmatively but also negatively, as incontinence, being a species, was
named not affirmatively but negatively; for the putting down is called
affirmation, as "he is beautiful," and the denial negation, as "he is
not beautiful"; and when we say "you are lawless," the a signifies
denial as the not.
And if this white is said to be equal to that white, it is not as
quality

that it is said to be equal but as quantity and the being this surface
equal to that surface, as also in the quantum the like and unlike are
observed not as quantum, but as sharing in quality.
36 Concerning acting and being acted on.
One must know that making and being affected, that is the productive
power and the passive, are under quality, but acting and being acted on
is a certain substance acting or being affected in such a way. Acting,
then, is having in itself the cause of the activity, and being acted on
having in itself and in another the cause of being acted on, as the
craftsman and the thing crafted; for the craftsman has in himself the
cause of the crafting, and the thing crafted has in the craftsman the
beginning of the crafting, and in itself the fitness of the affection.
And craftsman here we call the artisan, as a carpenter, and the thing
crafted the matter subject to the artisan, as the timber; for this is
subject to the carpenter. Of acting and being acted on some are said
simply to act, as in the productive arts, as carpentry, bronze-working,
and such things, in which even after the one acting has ceased the
product remains; for when the builder has ceased building, the thing
built remains; some are said to do, in which the thing done does not
remain when the doer has ceased; for when the piper has ceased, the
piping does not remain but perishes; some are said to contemplate, as
doing astronomy, doing geometry, thinking, and such things, and further
the species observed in the inanimate, as in fire, stone, wood, and such
things.
The first, then, are in the rational, and the last in the inanimate and
irrational; for it does not act as animate but as a body approaching a
body. And it has two properties: first, admitting contrariety, for
heating is contrary to cooling; second, the more and less; for it is
possible to heat more and to cool less, and likewise to be heated and to
be cooled. In all the categories, then, acting and being acted on are
observed; in substance begetting and being begotten, in the quantum
numbering and being numbered, in the relatives doubling and being
doubled, in quality whitening and being whitened, in lying seating and
sitting, in having wearing and being worn, in the where containing and
being contained, and in the when being contained and containing in the
present and past and future time. 37 Concerning lying. Lying is what has
position somehow toward another, as the body lying somehow toward such a
position or toward reclining or sitting or standing. And there are three
species of lying: standing, sitting, reclining; for being upright makes
standing, and lying in part and standing in part makes sitting, and lying
wholly makes reclining. And lying shows neither the thing lying nor the
place but the very position of the thing lying toward the place. Of
things lying some lie according to nature as the elements in their own
places, as earth, water, air, fire, and such things, some according to
position and art as a statue, a column, and such things. Further a
superdivision saying that of things lying some are stationary as earth,
some moved as the heavenly bodies.
Further of things lying some lie in potency, that is those that can
change place, some in act, as where they lie.
38 Concerning the where.

The where signifies place; for asked "where is so-and-so" we say "in the
house, in the city"; and these signify place. And the species of the
where category are according to the differences of places: above, below,
right, left, in front, behind.
39 Concerning the when.
The when shows time; for asked "when did this come to be?" we say "last
year, the year before last," which signify time. And the species of the
when category are as many as the differences of time. And these are
three: present, past, future.
40 Concerning having.
Having is substance about substance. And it shows containing or being
contained and not being some part of the thing. And a tunic contains,
and weapons and such things, and a ring is contained and if anything
else such is small. And both the containing and the contained must be
substances; for if the one were substance, the other accident, as
knowing and science, it is no longer brought under having. And
differences of having come to be according to the difference of beings;
for it is either animate or inanimate, and we are said to have an
animate as a child, a horse, and such things, and an inanimate as a
ring, a shoe, and such things. And having is said also according to many
other significations homonymously, about which we will speak later.
41 Concerning opposites.
Every opposite is opposed either as a thing or as an account. And if as
an account to an account, it makes affirmation and negation. Affirmation,
then, is to say that something belongs to something, as he is beautiful,
and negation to say that something does not belong to something, as he
is not beautiful; and both are called assertion. And if they are opposed
as a thing, either they are said toward converting things and make the
relatives, which also introduce one another and destroy one another, or
not toward converting things and they do not have a relation. And these
either do not change into one another, yet both are according to nature
and make the contraries, as heat and cooling, or the one changes into
the other, and the other does not change. And the one of them is
according to nature and the other against nature, and they make the
things opposed according to privation and habit, as sight and blindness;
for habit is sight as from having, and privation of the habit, that is
of seeing, is blindness. Of contraries some are immediate, some mediate.
Immediate, then, are those of which it is necessary that the one, that
is the one, belong to the subject of them or to these of which they are
predicated, as disease and health in an animal body as subject, and it
is necessary in every case that in the body there be either disease or
health; and we call disease every turning aside of nature. Mediate, those
of which it is not necessary that the one belong to the subject or to
these of which they are predicated, as the white and the black; for they
are contraries, and it is not in every case necessary that one of them
belong to the body; for it is not necessary that every body be either
white or black, for there are grey and ruddy bodies; unless the one of
the opposites belongs to something determinately by nature, as heat to
fire and cooling to snow. Of the mediate some have names, as the middle
of white and black is called grey, some do not have names; for the
middle of the just and the unjust does not have a name, but by the
negation of each the in-between is known, as neither just nor unjust.
Four properties follow the contraries: first, that to the good the bad
is of necessity contrary, but to the bad sometimes a good, sometimes
another

bad; for to temperance incontinence is contrary, and to incontinence
sometimes temperance, sometimes stupidity.
Stupidity is the passions not being moved nor being raised. Incontinence,
then, is a falling short of temperance, and stupidity an excess, and
excess is contrary to falling short. second, that it is impossible for
the contraries to be at once in the individuals according to the same,
for it is not possible for Socrates to be healthy and sick at once, or
according to the same member to be heated and cooled at once. third,
that the contraries are in the same subject either in genus or in
species or in number, in genus as in a simple body the white and the
black, in species as in an animal body health and disease, and in number
it is clear; for the same body admits of being receptive of the
contraries according to its own change. fourth, that the contraries are
either under the same genus as the white and the black under color, or
under contrary genera as justice and injustice under the good and the
bad, which are contraries, or they themselves are contrary genera as the
good and the bad being contrary genera.
42 Concerning habit and privation.
Habit is said of the activity of the had and of the having, as of the
weapon and of the armed, that is of the one clothing and of the one
being clothed; second, the adventitious activities that are lasting,
whether natural or psychic; and natural as heat in things being heated,
and psychic as science; third, what it does not yet have, but has a
fitness to receive, which is the first signification of in potency;
fourth, the natural quality, that is natural habit, as the heat of fire
and the sight of the sleeper, which is the second signification of in
potency, for it can burn, but in act it does not burn, and the first
signification of in act; fifth, as the final activity, as the seeing
that is already seeing and the heat that is already heating. And
privation is a throwing off of the habit. The privation of the weapon,
then, that is of the garment, is opposed to the first signification of
habit, to the second the throwing off of the adventitious habits, when
what has been heated is cooled; to the third, what the genus has not
grown to have at all, as we say the infant has a fitness toward music,
but the fig-tree in no way. The fig-tree, then, has been deprived,
because the genus of plants does not have a fitness to receive music.
And what the genus has a fitness for, but some of the species does not
have; for animal has a fitness to see, but the mole, being a species of
animal, does not have a fitness of seeing; to the fourth the throwing
off of the power according to habit; to the fifth the throwing off of
the final activity, which he said above the opposition of the opposites
according to privation and habit, that is of the power whether productive
or passive; and this is characterized by these three, what it has grown
to have and does not have at all, but has been wholly deprived, and when
it has grown to have, and where it has grown to have. As we do not say
the stone is blind, for it has not grown to have a habit of seeing; nor
do we say the newly born dog is blind nor the newly born infant
toothless, because they have not grown to have at that time; nor do we
say the foot is blind, for the animal has not grown to have the habit of
seeing in the foot. When, then, according to these three it has grown to
have and does not have, it is called privation.
43 Concerning prior and posterior.
The prior is divided into four significations. And most chiefly it is
the prior in time. And it is said chiefly of the animate as the older,
and of the

inanimate as the more ancient; and by catachresis they are also taken
for one another. second, the naturally prior, which is brought in with
it, but does not bring in with it, and destroys with it and is not
destroyed with it, as animal is prior to man; for if there is no animal,
neither will there be man, for man is an animal. But if there is no man,
there will be animal, for the horse also and the dog are animals; and if
there is a man there will in every case be an animal, for man is an
animal. But if there is an animal there is not in every case a man but
perhaps a horse, a dog; for these too are animals. third, that according
to order, as we say first the a, second the b, then the syllables, then
the letters. fourth, that according to worth, as we say: first a bishop,
then a presbyter. And some throw out this mode; for it is possible for
the first according to order to be later according to worth. fifth, as
we say the cause and the caused, as Socrates is prior to his own image;
for he himself is cause of his own image. And the father is prior and
greater than the son; for the father is cause of the son, for from the
father the son is begotten. Therefore also the blessed Gregory took
according to this signification what was said by our Lord in the sacred
Gospels: "My Father is greater than I." And others add that according to
conception, as the wall prior to the foundations.
And this is brought under the fourth mode, that according to worth; for
what is prior in conception, this is later in the thing. And in as many
ways as the prior, in so many also the posterior. And the prior and the
posterior and the more and less are not of the homonyms but of those
from one.
44 Concerning the simultaneous.
Simultaneous is said chiefly of those whose genesis is in the same time,
as two certain ones were born in the same moment. This mode is opposed
to the first mode of the significations of the prior. Second
signification of the simultaneous is things coexisting with one another
and not being one the cause or the caused of the other, as the double
and the half; for they coexist at once and destroy one another and bring
one another in. And this mode is opposed to the second and the fifth
mode of the prior; for in the second they do not introduce one another
nor destroy one another, and in the fifth the one is cause, the other
caused. Third signification of the simultaneous is the co-divided
species; and co-divided species are said of those brought down from the
same division, as the rational and the irrational from animal. And this
mode is opposed to the first and the second and nearly to the other
three.
45 Concerning motion.
Motion is the entelechy of what is in potency, inasmuch as it is such,
as the bronze is a statue in potency; for the bronze can receive the
form of the statue. The entelechy, then, of the bronze that is a statue
in potency is the being melted, the being molded, the being scraped,
which are motions. In as many categories, then, as the in potency is
observed, in these motion will be observed; and in as many as the in
potency is not observed, neither will motion be observed. It is
observed, then, in substance, in the quantum, in the qualified, in the
where category. In substance genesis and destruction, in the quantum
increase and diminution, in the qualified alteration, in the where that
which is carried in a circle, which is called revolution, and that which
is moved in a straight line, which is called motion in a straight line.
And of motion in a straight line there are six differences: the above,
the below, the within, the without, the right, the left. Therefore with
the revolution there are seven local motions; for everything that is
changed is either changed by itself

or according to something in it or according to something about it; and
if by itself, it makes genesis and destruction; and if according to
something in it, either according to the quantum, and it makes increase
and diminution, or according to the qualified, and it makes alteration;
and if according to what is about it, it makes change according to
place; for place is neither the thing moved itself nor something in it,
but it follows the things moved and is about the things moved. Genesis
and destruction differ; for genesis is the advance from not being into
being; for what was not before, this comes to be. And destruction is the
reverse, a change from being toward not being. increase and diminution;
for increase is motion toward the greater; and diminution that toward
the less. and in alteration again the opposite affections, heat to
cooling, to the white the black. Destruction, then, is opposed to
genesis, diminution to increase, and to alteration the opposite and
rest. Opposite to heating, cooling and rest; for when what is being
heated has taken a limit and has come to extreme heat, it rests and
ceases from being heated.
Thus also to change according to place both the contrary motion is
opposed and rest, contrary, as the motion above to the rest below; for
if someone throws a clod upward, it will not be moved downward before
some rest comes to be. And to the heavenly motion there is no contrary
motion. And alteration seems to follow the remaining motions; for both
what comes to be and what is destroyed and what increases and what is
diminished and what is moved according to place is in every case
altered. But in the case of physical motions, even if we find alteration
following the remaining motions, yet it is possible to be altered, but
not however to be moved according to another motion, as in the case of a
stone; for this is heated and cooled and neither increases nor comes to
be; and in the remaining likewise. So that even if alteration follows
the remaining motions, yet it is possible for it to be observed by
itself; whence their difference is agreed. And Aristotle calls motion
change. For in the fifth account of the Physics he showed that genesis
and destruction are changes, but are not motions, because motion comes
to be while the thing is preserved. And if we also said that to
alteration and to change according to place there are two contraries,
both the motion and the rest, one must know that it is not impossible
according to one thing and another for two to be contrary to one; for
rest as cessation and privation, and the opposed chiefly as cooling to
heating.
46 Concerning having.
Having is said according to eight modes: either as a habit or
disposition or another quality; for we are said to have science and
virtue. or as a quantum; for the wood is said to have a magnitude of
three cubits. or as substance about substance, which is a most generic
genus. And this either about the whole body as a tunic, or about some
part as a ring on a finger, or as a part in a whole; for we are said to
have a hand; or as in a vessel, as we say the jar has wine, or as a
possession; for we are said to have a house or a field, and we are said
also to have a wife, and the wife to have a husband. And this mode seems
to be foreign to having, since it converts; for it no more signifies the
man having a wife than the wife a husband, for neither does one prevail
over the other, but they have equally and without difference. And if the
possessor also has a possession and the possession a possessor and it
converts, yet not as a man a wife and a wife a husband; for the
possessor is autocratic and most sovereign of the possession, and for
this reason rather the possessor is said to have, and the possession to
be had. And it is clear that having is of the homonyms. Some say the
same differences of having as also of acting

and of being acted on; for as the things acting and being acted on are
either animate or inanimate, so also here either the having and the had
is animate or inanimate. How then will the same differences be of
different genera? But one may say that the having is either about the
whole body or about a part, and again this is either a defense or an
adornment.
47 Concerning assertion, negation, and affirmation.
One must know that affirmation and negation are called assertion.
Affirmation, then, is that which signifies that something belongs to
something, that is what someone is; as "Socrates is wise," "Socrates
walks." And negation that which shows what does not belong to someone,
that is what someone is not, as "so-and-so is not wise," "so-and-so does
not walk." And since to every affirmation a negation is opposed and to
every negation an affirmation, the negation opposed to the affirmation
and the affirmation opposed to the negation is called contradiction; and
it is necessary that the one be false and the one be true. 48 Concerning
term and proposition and syllogism. One must know that the logical
treatise has its aim about demonstration. And demonstration is a
syllogism; and the syllogism is composed from two true propositions and
the conclusion; as wishing to demonstrate that the soul is immortal, I
say that everything ever-moved is immortal; this is a proposition. Then
I say also a second proposition: The soul is ever-moved; then the
conclusion: The soul therefore is immortal. And each proposition is
composed from terms, for each word of the proposition is called a term;
for a term is that into which the proposition is analyzed. As the
proposition says: Everything ever-moved is immortal; the "Every" is
called a term as a part of a proposition and the "ever-moved" likewise
is called a term and the "immortal" is called a term and the "is" is
called a term. One must know that the propositions must be true and the
conclusion follow the propositions. For if one of the propositions is
found false or the conclusion, it is a paralogism and not a syllogism.
Simple utterance, name, verb, dictum, term: these five according to the
subject differ from one another in nothing, and their difference is only
according to the relation; as man as simply significant of something is
called a simple utterance, as a subject it is called a name, as holding
the rank of predicate it is called a verb, as a part of an affirmation
or negation it is called a dictum, as a part of a proposition and
syllogism it is called a term. One must know that in the proposition,
that is in the affirmation and negation, the subject is called a name,
and the predicate is called a verb. As an affirmation is: A man walks,
the "man" is subject and is called a name, the "walks" holds the rank of
predicate and is called a verb; "Socrates is beautiful," the "Socrates"
is subject and is called a name, the "is beautiful" holds the rank of
predicate and is called a verb as a part of an affirmation, even if
among the grammarians the "beautiful" is called a name. And simply,
that which "is" follows is a verb both in the negation and in the
affirmation, whether it is a name or a verb, it is called a verb among
the philosophers as predicated. One must know that these five have
differed from one another in nothing, assertion, proposition, problem,
objection, conclusion. For when I simply assert that the soul is
immortal, it is called assertion. And when it is taken as a part of a
syllogism, then it is a proposition to say: The soul is immortal. And
when someone objects to the proposition saying: Whence is it clear that
the soul is immortal? then such a thing is named objection. And when
again we propose for inquiry: Is the soul immortal? it is called a
problem. And when it is gathered from a proposition, it is called

a conclusion, as: The soul is ever-moved, the ever-moved is immortal,
from these propositions it is gathered: Therefore the soul is immortal;
this is a conclusion. A proposition is an assertoric account of
something from something, which is a negation, as "Socrates does not
laugh," or of something of something, which is an affirmation, as
"Socrates laughs." A term is that into which the proposition is
analyzed. A syllogism is an account in which, certain things having been
laid down, that is agreed, something other than the things laid down
follows of necessity through the things laid down; for through the
propositions laid down the conclusion comes to be, not needing from
outside some other constitution. A problem is a theorem stretching
toward choice and flight, that is toward denial and assent, toward
knowledge and contemplation. A question is an interrogation demanding an
extended, that is a broad, answer; for the interrogative account differs
from the questioning, because to the interrogative a swift answer
follows, that is through few words, and to the questioning a lasting one
and through many. The dialogical character is said of that according to
inquiry and answer. An objection is that which from the beginnings
overturns the account. And a counter-stand is that which receives the
account as true, but shows that it does not harm at all toward what is
proposed. A lemma is what has been taken as agreed toward the
construction of something. A heresy is an opinion of several men
agreeing with one another, but disagreeing with others.
A common notion is that agreed among all, as that there is a sun. And a
thesis is a paradoxical assumption of one of those known in philosophy,
that is a strange notion, as the account of Parmenides, that he said
being is one, and as that of Heraclitus, that all things are moved.
Common is what is observed in many or predicated of many. And common is
said in four ways: either what is divisible into the parts, as the land
being allotted; or what is taken indivisibly in common use, but not at
once, as one slave or one horse of two masters fulfilling the command
now of this one, now of that one; or what is made one's own in
preoccupation, but is referred to the common, as the place in a theater
or in a bath; or what is put forward indivisibly into a common and the
same notion, as the voice of the herald. In this way one must take in
the case of homonyms and synonyms the "of which the name is common." By
itself is what belongs to something primarily and according to substance
and not according to accident, as the rational to man. Universal is what
signifies many, as man, animal, substance. According to accident is what
can belong to something and not belong, as to man being sick and being
healthy. Acting is said of the productive arts, in which what comes to
be remains, as in carpentry and such things; for after the making the
bed remains. Doing is said of those in which the end, that is the
result, does not remain, as in piping and dancing. And contemplating is
said of thinking, doing astronomy, doing geometry, and such things. True
account declares conception twofold. For the one is a thinking-upon and
a considering-upon, unfolding and making clear the rough and
inarticulate contemplation and knowledge of things, so that what seemed
to sense to be simple is shown by the busy work of the mind to be
many-parted and various, as man appearing simple is understood by
conception as double, composed from soul and body, and the other happens
to be a fabrication of understanding according to a combination of sense
and imagination, composing and opining from beings the things that in no
way are. Such is the myth-making of hippocentaurs and sirens and
goat-stags. For taking the parts of wholes and composing from the parts
something else according to much license and ease it fabricated in the
understanding and in the accounts the things in no way observed in
hypostasis and substance, then also being shaped in the materials it
made idols; and this is called

bare conception. Union comes to be according to different modes; for
either it comes to be according to mixing, as in the case of different
flours being mixed and mingled, or according to glueing, as in the case
of bronze and lead, or according to harmony, as in the case of stones
and woods, or according to confusion, as in the case of the fusible and
the metallic, fusible as wax and pitch and such things, metallic as gold
and silver and such things, or according to blending, as in the case of
liquids, wine say and water, wine and honey. And union according to
composition is the interpenetration of the parts into one another
without disappearance, as it is in the case of soul and body, which
union some have called blending or growing-together. One must know that
some of the fathers did not accept the name of blending in the case of
the mystery according to Christ, but union according to composition all
did. And this is the union according to hypostasis, that according to
composition. According to hypostasis, then, is the thing subsisting from
different natures. And again according to hypostasis is what is from two
things, but in one person. And further union according to hypostasis is
the nature running toward another hypostasis.
Blending is a juxtaposition of bodies, an interchange of qualities. And
again blending is a running together of substances different from one
another and an interchange of the qualities about them. Blending is a
coupling of bodies mixing with one another the qualities in them. There
is also union according to juxtaposition; and it resembles that
according to harmony. Again union is called personal, when someone,
putting on the person of another, makes the words in his behalf instead
of him, and relative union as of a friend toward a friend. And Nestorius
also invents other unions, according to dignity I mean and equal honor
and sameness of will and good pleasure and homonymy. One must know that
in the case of union according to hypostasis the intelligible both are
united to those able to receive them as the things corrupted together,
and being united they remain unconfused and undestroyed and unaltered as
the things lying beside; for the intelligible have such a nature.
49 There are six definitions of philosophy.
1 Philosophy is knowledge of beings, inasmuch as they are beings, that
is of their nature. 2 Philosophy is knowledge of both divine and human
things. 3 Philosophy is a meditation of death, both the chosen and the
natural. 4 Philosophy is likeness to God according to what is possible
for man; and we are made like to God according to the just and the holy
and the good. Just, then, is what distributes the equal, that is neither
to do injustice nor to suffer injustice nor to accept a face in
judgment, but to render to each according to his works, and holy what is
above the just, that is the good, to endure being wronged and to forgive
those who do injustice and rather to do them good. 5 Philosophy is art
of arts and science of sciences; for philosophy is a beginning of every
art, for through it every art has been found. 6 Philosophy is friendship
of wisdom; and true wisdom is God. The love toward God, then, this is
true philosophy. And philosophy is divided into theoretical and
practical; the theoretical into theological, mathematical,
physiological; the mathematical into arithmetic, musical, geometric,
astronomic; and the practical into ethical, economic, political.
Theoretical, then, is contemplating both the incorporeal and immaterial,
that is about God, who first and chiefly is incorporeal and immaterial;
then also about angels and demons and souls, which themselves too are
called immaterial as toward the body, even if toward the chiefly
immaterial, that is the divine, they are material.
This, then, is the theological. And it is also to contemplate the nature
of material things, that is of animals and plants and stones and such
things, which is the physiological.

And it is also to contemplate the things in the middle of these, which
sometimes are observed in matter, sometimes apart from matter, and are
middle of the immaterial and the material, which is the mathematical.
For number is immaterial by itself, but is also observed in matter, in
wheat say or wine; for we say ten modii of wheat and ten xestai of wine;
and likewise in the remaining species of the mathematical. And the
practical adorns character and teaches how one must live as a citizen;
and if it trains one man, it is called ethical, and if a whole house, it
is called economic, and if a complete city, it is called political. One
must know that there are four dialectical methods, that is logical: 1
divisive, which divides the genus into species through the middle of the
differences; 2 definitive, which from the genus and the differences,
which the divisive divided, defines the subject; 3 analytic, which
analyzes the more composite into the simpler, that is the body into the
humors, the humors into the fruits, the fruits into the four elements,
the elements into matter and form; 4 demonstrative, which through some
middle shows what is proposed; as it is proposed to me to show that the
soul is immortal; I take some middle, the ever-moved, and I syllogize
thus: The soul is ever-moved; the ever-moved is immortal; the soul
therefore is immortal. One must know that the syllogisms belong to the
demonstrative. One must know that the analytic is threefold; for there
is physical, as that spoken before; and there is also logical analysis,
when we analyze the proposed syllogism into its own figure; and there is
also mathematical, when we take the thing sought as agreed and arrive at
something agreed, from which what is proposed is hunted. As let the
thing sought be: If the soul is immortal. This thing sought I take as
agreed and I say: Since the soul is immortal, there are recompenses of
base and good actions; and if there are recompenses, there is the judged
and the judging; and if there is the judged and the judging, there is a
provider and providence. We have arrived, then, at providence, which is
agreed among all. From here then according to composition I say: Since
there is providence and a judge, there are recompenses; since there are
recompenses, there is the judged; and if there is the judged, the soul
therefore is immortal.
50 Concerning union according to hypostasis.
One must know that union according to hypostasis completes one composite
hypostasis of the things united, preserving the natures that have come
together toward union and their difference and their natural properties
unconfused and unchanged in itself. And this has toward itself no
hypostatic difference; for of it come to be the characterizing
differences of each of those that have come together toward union, by
which they are separated from those of the same species, as it is in the
case of soul and body. For one composite hypostasis of Peter, say, or of
Paul is completed from both, and it preserves in itself the two complete
natures, that of the soul and of the body, and their difference unmixed
and their natural properties unconfused. And it has in itself the
characterizing properties of each, both those of the soul that separate
it from the remaining souls and those of the body that separate it from
the remaining bodies, in no way separating the soul from the body but
uniting and joining, and at once separating the one hypostasis composed
from them from the remaining homoeidetic hypostases. And once the
natures have received the union toward one another according to
hypostasis, they remain undivided completely. For even if the soul is
separated from the body in death, yet the hypostasis of both is one and
the same; for hypostasis is the compacting at the beginning of the
existence of each according to it. The body, then, and the soul remain,
always having one the beginning of their own

existence and hypostasis, even if they are separated from each other.
One must know that it is possible for natures to be united to one
another according to hypostasis as in the case of man, and it is
possible for a nature to be taken on by a hypostasis and to subsist in
it, both of which are observed in the case of Christ: for in him both
the natures, the divine and the human, were united, and in the
pre-existing hypostasis of God the Word his animate flesh subsisted and
had this as hypostasis, but for one composite nature to be completed
from two natures or one hypostasis from two hypostases is wholly
impossible; because it is impossible for the contrary essential
differences to coexist with one another in one nature; for the work of
these is to set the natures in which they exist apart from one another.
And again it is impossible for things that have once subsisted by
themselves to have another beginning of hypostasis; for hypostasis is
existence by itself. One must know that in the case of the holy Trinity
hypostasis is the beginningless mode of the eternal existence of each.
One must know that when a nature becomes composite, the parts must be
contemporaneous and from others another is completed, not preserving but
turning and altering the things from which it was composed, as when the
body is completed from the four elements another has come to be from
others and it is neither complete fire nor one of the others, nor is it
called so, and as from horse and ass a mule; for it is neither horse nor
ass, nor is it called so, but another from others, preserving neither of
the things from which it was composed unconfused and unchanged.
