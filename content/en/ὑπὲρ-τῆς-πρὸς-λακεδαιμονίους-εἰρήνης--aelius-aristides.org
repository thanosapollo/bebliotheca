First, men of Athens, much thanks to the gods, who have brought those who
formerly were arrogant and would not even endure the name of peace, though we
often invited them, to come here on behalf of peace in this posture, and have
restored to the city the power to deliberate about Greek affairs from the
beginning, however you may be persuaded. It is right to use the opportunity,
and to guard against their unreasonableness and contentiousness, and to remain
true to your own character and ways. There is an old saying that it is not
easy for those who prosper to be wise, but that most men grow worse through
fortune. I, however, men of Athens, take it that those who prosper beyond
their desert or beyond their hopes may reasonably suffer this, being unable to
handle affairs greater than themselves; but for those who have the most
successes among the Greeks, and have given all men no fewer tokens of good
counsel than of those successes, there is no remaining argument that they
should suffer the same as the many and the foolish. And our counsel is not
about the future only

:END:

but also about the very things we have succeeded in. For if we choose what we
ought and these things hold firm, we shall inhabit the city henceforth in
safety and with good repute; but if we contend beyond measure, it is surely
clear that we shall have to run risks even for what we already have. So the
more we take pleasure in these things, the more we ought to look ahead that
they may be secure. I am not unaware that almost all men welcome those who
serve their desires and think them not only well-disposed but the only true
men, and are displeased with those who try to bring them to their senses. Yet
if one could be equally master of words and of deeds, so as not only to say
the most pleasant things but also to accomplish them, one might justly be
angry at those unwilling to do so and take them for difficult men; but if it
is one thing to join in praying for the greatest goods, and another to counsel
about what is at hand, I ask this one thing: that you listen with this thought
set under your judgments, if I do not miss the calculations I have about these
matters.

I think, then, that our city is the most fortunate of the Greek cities, and,
by Zeus, if you like, I will add of all cities, yet not superior to human
nature. And what is that nature? The gods did not allot affairs equally to
themselves and to men, but they, being immortal and masters of all things,
have immortal good fortunes as well, and there is no fear that anyone stronger
will take them away; they have such surplus that they not only possess all
goods themselves, but themselves distribute them to others. And they
distribute them not in the way they themselves possess them, but to those to
whom they have given the strongest of human goods they have given to succeed
in most things, not in all. And the greatest proof of this is these men who
now invite you to peace. For though it was possible for them, if they had seen
fit to be moderate, both to have these things themselves from the beginning
and to award them to others, they were lifted up by what they had, and now, if
they can obtain these same things from you, they count it a windfall. So let
us not, then, what we blame in these men,

suffer this ourselves, nor inherit their lack of counsel rather than use our
own calculations, which we had about these things from the beginning. For you
know, I suppose, that then too, to their many embassies and those commands of
theirs, this was our one answer: to settle by justice, and not to stir a
common war for the Greeks. Then, when they would not accept this, you were
forced into the war; but now, how is it reasonable, when these things have
come into your power, not to use them, the very things you would have done
from the beginning if no one had hindered anything, but for the affair to be
reversed on both sides, and those who then fled peace now to have come
inviting you to it, while we who then held to peace should again appear to
choose war? And they might say they have reasonably changed; but that we
judged rightly then, the very things that have befallen them bear witness. So
we could not justly change, or it would be altogether strange if the
Lacedaemonians have become of one vote with us, abandoning what seemed good to
themselves, while we shall be seen judging contrary to ourselves.

I think some god has directed all this. For if we had from the beginning
voluntarily chosen the war, this might perhaps have shown some token of
courage, but no one could have seen our fairness, at least not in the very
things we chose; or if, when we wished to keep the peace, these men had been
persuaded, one might justly have praised us for looking ahead for Greece and
not being ready for aggrandizement, but not all would have known our courage
and power, at least not in the very choosing of peace. But as it is we have
shown both in the finest way. For in that we sent embassies for peace and
tried to keep from war those we were preventing, we gave proof of gentleness
and of forethought for the Greeks that they might suffer no harm; and in that,
forced to fight, we have won, we have added the reputation of courage and of
power together, so that from both it belongs to the city to be admired with
goodwill. Then, I think, this third occasion has come again, needing good
counsel, which if we handle as we ought,

:END:

we shall be thought superior to our rivals in every way. So, when it is
possible to prevail in all things, both in what we have won and in what we
chose, let us not turn these into the unknown. For if we keep quiet, both our
successes in the deeds are confirmed and moderation is added in every way; but
if we give openings to war, which is accustomed to make revolutions, I fear
that in seeking to make our successes more we may throw away the pride we have
in what we already possess. I think it does not belong to those who fare as we
do to be fond of danger, but much more to those in their condition. For the
former, even if they accomplish nothing more, can still be well thought of
from what they have and belong to the better fortune; but for those for whom
quiet means shame and the disaster is made firm, while if they cast another
risk there is hope of being rid of present evils if they fight better, it is
not unreasonable to attempt it, calculating that in failure they fare much as
before, but if they win they can be rid of all their evils. And that the
future is unknown to all men no one is unaware. For where neither tyranny is
strong nor wealth secure, and the changes are countless both in private and in
public, and, by Zeus, not for cities only but for whole nations, what could
one foresee? It belongs to the wise to examine both sides and so set
themselves toward what happens. We, then, if we defeat the Lacedaemonians and
bring the Peloponnese to terms, shall increase our empire, as it seems; but
if, which may it not happen, we should fail, we shall risk what we have.
Therefore, when the good is less than the hardship, it is better to be
reconciled. And to turn to peace when it is possible to prevail utterly has,
as a counterweight to aggrandizement, the reputation of having chosen what is
best in common and of making more of saving Greece than of destroying our
rivals; but not to guard against the uncertainty of what will happen in the
future is beyond all consolation.

Thus choosing peace is right toward both, while having trusted to war has more

harm than benefit, when you look at them side by side. And I wonder if, after
bringing the Lacedaemonians to terms, you will know how to spare them, yet
now, when it is possible to spare both yourselves and them at once, you will
not dare. And what will you say, men of Athens? For when you were sailing
around the Peloponnese, and making landings in the country, and taking this
Pylos here, and doing the rest, if someone asked you for what you were doing
these things, it was possible to say that you were forced, and that though we
wished to keep quiet it did not seem good to the Lacedaemonians, but they
challenged us to arms and war. But now what shall we say, if someone asks us,
seeing us either sailing or going out by land or doing any of the same things
at all? That we have been forced? But those who then were lifted up by those
things now have come offering peace. That you will be diminished in something
if you accept these terms? But no one now bids you let Aegina go, or leave
Potidaea, or receive the Megarians whether you wish it or not; nor is there
any meddlesomeness in what is being done, but the voting-tablet is sovereign,
the empire of the Greeks is secure, and all the places are safe, and harbors
and horses and triremes and the power to deliberate about them. And those who
made those solemn promises and baited the Greeks with the name of freedom now,
if it is allowed them to inhabit their own land as autonomous, confess they
are content; and those who disputed the hegemony with you have rather become
its confirmers. For before they moved arms someone might perhaps have expected
them to attack; but having tried to oppose and then withdrawn, they will have
nothing to say either that you possess the empire unjustly or that they can
prevent it, so that in deed they will cast their vote that you reasonably have
it, and they will hand the Greeks over to you far more than when, after
Pausanias was removed and you were chosen, they themselves conceded.

And yet if the Lacedaemonians had this mind toward you from the beginning,
what of all things would have led you

into this war? That you would have made light of the truce? And who are better
than you at serving the gods? Or that nothing is enough for you, could someone
say? But the Corinthians slandered you with this, and no man alive is
conscious of it in you. Then, things which, had they been yours from the
beginning, you would never have taken up the war, if now it is possible to
have them, will you not stop, but though you claim you fought unwillingly,
will you not turn to peace willingly? For what? Because, by Zeus, we have
prospered as we would have prayed? Then this very thing is also the best. For
nothing of what we had has been lost, and the reputation of having won has
been added, and that no one again may confidently wrong us. So if then we were
content with what we had, how is it not now proper to be content with those
things and with what has been added besides? And these men who overstrain and
bid you pursue to the end, ask them who will be your surety for so great a
matter. For we shall not always fight around Sphacteria, nor will it be open
to the generals to sail against things already prepared, nor to lead the enemy
as if by prior notice, but there are things that will appear to need a
contest. And if they themselves say they will guarantee these things, they
will speak the speech of madmen, not of the sound. And further, if the danger
were to be for them alone, and not for all, this would be something; but when
those who are persuaded will pay the penalty first, what is the gain in taking
later and being fined with them, when it is possible for both us and them to
be safe together? And yet if these men would not even say so, but someone else
could give surety, how is it well for you to trust things which it seems
shameful for these men to promise? And what is lightness even to say in word
only is, I suppose, folly to set down as altogether going to happen in deed.
For then one must choose to run the risk, even if the future is unknown, when
keeping quiet is clearly worse. As when the barbarians were coming against us,
we had to run the risk. Why? Because it was slavery, if we did not

do this. Again, when these men were ordering things which, if accepted, would
not have gone well. But when in quiet there is reputation with safety, and in
making war the risk is not only for what we have but even for the reputation
itself, why must we contend so infatuatedly, as if angry at ourselves no less
than at them? Consider this too, that all men fairly welcome peace as without
danger, while wars the decent at least seem to undertake for good repute. So
about these there might perhaps be a dispute, and each might counsel according
to his own character, some choosing safety, others the better part; but now it
is not possible to separate them, but as if some god were showing you that you
must accept the peace, and that with it you both dwell safely and have your
pride, while for those who choose war it is theirs no less to be in ill repute
than to run risks. Why so? Because we shall not seem to make the
reconciliation from fear for ourselves, but from humanity; and this is no
small thing in the account of virtue. But if we hold to the war we shall bear
the reputation of the three worst things: insatiability, cruelty, and lack of
counsel. For to grasp at more than one should, and to rejoice in the Greeks'
disasters, and not to know how to counsel about affairs, are in it. And yet no
reproaches more contrary to the city's nature could ever arise than these. And
if a stumble is added besides, what could one say?

So do not wait for that day on which you will seem to desire peace by
necessity, but be willing, while you have been made masters both to make war
and to keep quiet, to grant a favor to the Greeks, if not to these men, using
as examples not only the present but also the former. For after defeating the
barbarians and seeking to go out as far as possible, having sailed to Egypt we
lost two hundred and fifty triremes of two fleets. And that it is much easier
to defeat the barbarians than the Lacedaemonians, who does not know? These
things we must calculate, and

not now suffer anything like it, but think it the strangest of all things if,
when we once held Nisaea and Pegae and Achaea, we voluntarily gave these up,
thinking peace more worth than the gains from war, and now, when it is
possible, without giving up any of what we have, to take in addition the
reputation of prudence and of power together, we will not be willing. But we
once thought we must leave the city and give up both temples and tombs and run
risks with our bodies and our goods together, for the common safety of the
Greeks; yet when it is possible, in the very ceasing from risk, to save their
affairs for them, we will not dare; but of athletes we think those counsel
well who stop when they are winning and not under compulsion, yet we ourselves
will not so act, nor will we know that against the barbarians it is perhaps
proper to go even to the last, but that the contests of the Greeks against one
another should be thought like the crown-games, and victory held a sufficient
end, and nothing sought beyond. And if again that other saying must be thought
true, that war among the Greeks against one another is like civil strife, it
is surely best to consider how one may end it as quickly as possible, and not
seek to stretch into the endless that which it was not even proper to begin.
And if formerly some of the Greeks were ill-disposed toward you from unjust
suspicions, now at least all will judge you their saviors, if you free them
from troubles and evils. For they will not attach the favor to those who asked
of necessity, but to those who, being masters to counsel both these things and
those, preferred what is commonly advantageous. I think if there is any
trustworthiness in the Lacedaemonians, our decisions will in every way have
been well made; but if again they should try to make a revolution, there is no
better hold against them. For if whenever they need to be saved they come
fawning, and when they take heart they attack, there is no way they will not
general for us. For all will think them both base and will hate them, while
for us, with good report, whatever they

are able they will be eager to serve. And I wonder if anyone fears they will
break the truce, yet deliberately chooses to make war, and does not calculate
that he is doing the opposite of what he wishes. For to act so that they will
come about securely, it was not proper from the beginning to prevent them from
coming about. I think if anyone holds the Lacedaemonians to be knavish and
extremely careful of their safety, they will at least have been schooled by
the disasters themselves to be milder; for seeing that not even their former
things came out well for them, if not willingly, then unwillingly they will be
moderate. I for my part would rather fight even a second time if they stirred
again than now. For then I think the gods' help again would be added for the
city, but now there is nothing so great for which it was proper to contend
beyond what will be advantageous. I have said what I thought best. And I pray
for one of two things: either that it seem good to you as well, or that
fortune go with what you in turn decide.
