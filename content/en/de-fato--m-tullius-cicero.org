* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

For when I was at my place at Puteoli, and our friend Hirtius, the
consul-elect, was in the same neighborhood, a man most friendly to us and
devoted to these studies in which we have lived from boyhood, we spent much
time together, seeking above all those counsels that belonged to peace and to
the harmony of the citizens. For since after Caesar's death all the causes of
new disturbances seemed to be sought, and we thought they had to be met,
nearly all our talk was spent in these deliberations; and we did that both
often at other times and on a certain day freer than usual and more empty of
interruptions, when he had come to me, first taking up those things that were
daily with us and as it were our standing business, about peace and about
leisure.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

When that was done, "What then?" he said. "Since you have not, I hope,
abandoned oratorical exercises, but have certainly put philosophy before them,
can I hear something?" "You indeed," I said, "may either hear or speak; for I
have not deserted those oratorical studies, as you rightly judge, with which I
even set you on fire, though I had received you already blazing, nor do the
things I now treat diminish that faculty, but rather increase it. For the
orator has a great partnership with this kind of philosophy which we follow;
he borrows subtlety from the Academy and in turn gives it richness of speech
and the ornaments of speaking. Therefore," I said, "since both studies are our
possession, today let the choice be yours, which you prefer to enjoy." Then
Hirtius: "Most welcome," he said, "and like all your ways; for your will has
never refused anything to my eagerness."

"But since your rhetoric is known to me, and we have often heard you in it and
shall hear you, and the Tusculan Disputations show that you have taken up this

what you have written. So begin. Let us sit here."

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

for he writes that nothing was predicted to him. What wonder, then, that a
rock from the cave fell upon his legs? For I think that even if Icadius had
not been in the cave then, that rock would still have fallen. For either
nothing at all is by chance, or this very thing could have happened by
fortune. I ask, then (and this will have a wide reach), if fate had no name at
all, no nature, no force, and most things or all things happened at random, by
chance, would they happen otherwise than they happen now? What, then, is the
point of pressing fate, when without fate the account of all things is
referred to nature or to fortune?

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

But let us dismiss Posidonius, as is fair, with good leave, and return to
Chrysippus's snares. To him let us first answer about the sympathy of things
itself; we will pursue the rest later. How much difference there is among the
natures of places, we see; that some are healthy, others pestilent; that in
some people are phlegmatic and as it were overflowing, in others dried out and
arid; and there are many other things that differ greatly from place to place.
At Athens the air is thin, from which the Attic people are thought even
sharper; at Thebes it is thick, and so the Thebans are stout and strong. Yet
that thin air will not bring it about that anyone hears Zeno or Arcesilas or
Theophrastus, nor the thick that he seeks victory at Nemea rather than at the
Isthmus. Separate them further.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

For what can the nature of a place bring about, that we should walk in
Pompey's portico rather than on the Campus? with you rather than with another?
on the Ides rather than on the Kalends? As, then, the nature of a place has
some bearing on certain things and on certain things none, so let the
condition of the stars have force, if you like, upon certain things; it will
certainly not have force upon all. But, they say, since there are unlikenesses
in the natures of men, so that sweet things delight some, half-bitter things
others, some are lustful, others angry or cruel or proud, others shrink from
such vices: since, then, he says, nature differs so much from nature, what
wonder is it that these unlikenesses were made from different causes?

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

In arguing this he does not see what the matter is about, and in what the
cause consists. For it is not the case that, if some are more inclined to some
things than others because of natural and antecedent causes, therefore there
are also natural and antecedent causes of our wills and desires. For nothing
would be in our power, if the matter stood so. As it is, we admit that whether
we are sharp or dull, strong or weak, is not in us. But he who thinks it
follows from this that not even our sitting or walking is a matter of will
does not see what follows upon what. For though both the talented and the slow
are born so by antecedent causes, and likewise the strong and the weak, it
does not follow that their sitting and walking and doing some business is also
determined and fixed by principal causes.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

We have received Stilpo, the Megarian philosopher, as a man certainly sharp
and approved in those times. His own intimates write that he was both given to
drink and given to women, and they do not write this in blame but rather in
praise; for they say that a vicious nature was so tamed and crushed by him
through teaching that no one ever saw him drunk, no one saw in him a trace of
lust. What? Do we not read how Zopyrus the physiognomist marked Socrates, a
man who professed to know thoroughly men's characters and natures from the
body, the eyes, the face, the brow? He said Socrates was stupid and dull,
because he did not have hollows at the throat; he said those parts were
blocked and stopped up; he added also that he was given to women, at which
Alcibiades is said to have raised a laugh.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

But these vices can be born from natural causes; that they should be rooted
out and utterly taken away, so that the very man who was inclined to them is
called away from such great vices, is not placed in natural causes, but in
will, study, training. All of which is taken away, if the force and nature of
fate is confirmed from the account of divination.

For if there is divination, from what kind of principles of the art does it
set out? (I call principles of the art what are called theoremata in Greek.)
For I do not believe that either the other craftsmen are employed in their
function with no principle grasped, or that those who use divination predict
the future so.

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Let the astrologers' principles, then, be of this kind: /If someone, for
example, is born at the rising of the Dog-star, he will not die at sea./
Watch, Chrysippus, lest you desert your own case, in which you have a great
struggle with Diodorus, a powerful logician. For if that is true which is so
connected: /If someone is born at the rising of the Dog-star, he will not die
at sea/, this too is true: /If Fabius was born at the rising of the Dog-star,
Fabius will not die at sea./ These things, then, fight with one another, that
Fabius was born at the rising of the Dog-star, and that Fabius will die at
sea; and since it is set down as certain in Fabius's case that he was born at
the rising of the Dog-star, these also fight, that Fabius exists, and that he
will die at sea. Therefore this too is a conjunction of incompatibles: /Both
Fabius exists, and Fabius will die at sea/, which, as it is put, cannot even
happen. Therefore this, /Fabius will die at sea/, is of that kind which cannot
happen. Therefore everything that is said falsely of the future cannot happen.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

But this, Chrysippus, you by no means want, and your contest with Diodorus is
above all about this very thing. For he says that only that can happen which
either is true or will be true, and whatever will be, that he says must
happen, and whatever will not be, that he denies can happen. You say that even
things that will not be can happen, as that this gem be broken, even if that
will never come about, and that it was not necessary that Cypselus reign at
Corinth, although that had been issued by Apollo's oracle a thousand years
before. But if you will approve those divine predictions, you will also hold,
of those things that will be said falsely of the future, that they cannot
happen; and if it should be said that Africanus will take Carthage, and if
something is truly said of the future, and it will be so, you would say it is
necessary; which is the whole view of Diodorus, hostile to you.

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

For if this is truly connected: /If you were born at the rising of the
Dog-star, you will not die at sea/, and the first thing in the connection,
/You were born at the rising of the Dog-star/, is necessary (for all true
things in the past are necessary, as Chrysippus holds, dissenting from his
master Cleanthes, because they are immutable and past things cannot turn from
true to false); if, then, what is first in the connection is necessary, what
follows becomes necessary also. Although this does not seem to Chrysippus to
hold in all cases; yet if there is a natural cause why Fabius should not die
at sea, Fabius cannot die at sea.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

At this point Chrysippus, in a sweat, hopes the Chaldeans and the other
diviners are mistaken, and that they will not use connections, so as to
announce their principles thus: /If someone was born at the rising of the
Dog-star, he will not die at sea/, but will rather speak thus: /It is not the
case both that someone was born at the rising of the Dog-star and that he will
die at sea./ What a joking license! Lest he himself fall into Diodorus, he
teaches the Chaldeans how they ought to set out their principles. For I ask,
if the Chaldeans speak so as to put negations of indefinite conjunctions
rather than indefinite connections, why cannot doctors, why cannot geometers,
why cannot the rest do the same? A doctor first of all will not put what he
has seen clearly in his art thus: /If someone's veins move in this way, he has
a fever/, but rather in this manner: /It is not the case both that someone's
veins move in this way and that he does not have a fever./ And likewise a
geometer will not say: /On a sphere the greatest circles bisect each other/,
but rather in this manner: /It is not the case both that there are greatest
circles on a sphere and that they do not bisect each other./

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

What is there that cannot in that way be transferred from a connection to a
negation of conjunctions? And indeed we can bring out the same things in other
ways. I just said: /On a sphere the greatest circles bisect each other/; I can
say: /If there will be greatest circles on a sphere/; I can say: /Because
there will be greatest circles on a sphere/. There are many kinds of stating,
and none more twisted than this, with which Chrysippus hopes the Chaldeans
will be content for the Stoics' sake.

* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

Yet none of them speaks so; for it is a greater thing to learn these twists of
speech thoroughly than the risings and settings of the signs. But let us
return to that contention of Diodorus which they call the question of
possibles, in which it is asked what force that has which can happen.
Diodorus, then, holds that only that can happen which either is true or will
be true. That topic touches this question, that nothing happens which was not
necessary, and that whatever can happen either already is or will be, and that
those things which are future can no more be changed from true to false than
those which have been done; but in things done the immutability is apparent,
in certain future things, because it is not apparent, it seems not even to be
present, as in one who is hard pressed by a deadly disease it is true /He will
die of this disease/, yet if this same thing is truly said of one in whom so
great a force of disease is not apparent, it will nonetheless come about. Thus
it comes about that no change from true to false can happen even in the
future. For /Scipio will die/ has such force that, although it is said of the
future, still it cannot turn into false; for it is said of a man who must die.

* Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

So if it were said: /Scipio will die at night in his bedroom, overcome by
force/, it would be said truly; for that would be said which was going to be;
and that it was going to be ought to be understood from this, that it was
done. Nor was /Scipio will die/ any more true than /He will die in that
manner/, nor was it any more necessary that Scipio die than that he die in
that manner, nor is /Scipio was killed/ any more immutable from true to false
than /Scipio will be killed/; nor, when these things are so, is there reason
why Epicurus should dread fate and seek help from the atoms and lead them
aside from their path and at one time take up two things that cannot be
untied, one, that something happens without a cause, from which it will follow
that something comes about from nothing, which neither he himself nor any
natural philosopher approves, the other, that when two indivisible bodies are
carried through the void, one moves in a straight line, the other swerves.

* Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

For Epicurus, granting that every proposition is either true or false, may
still not fear that it is necessary that all things happen by fate; for it is
not from eternal causes flowing by the necessity of nature that this is true
which is so stated: /Carneades went down to the Academy/, nor yet without
causes, but there is a difference between causes that have gone before by
chance and causes that hold in themselves a natural efficiency. Thus it was
always true /Epicurus will die when he has lived seventy-two years, in the
archonship of Pytharatus/, and yet there were no fated causes why it should
happen so, but, because it fell out so, it was going to fall out by a definite
series of causes, as it did fall out.

* Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

Nor do those who say that the things which will be are immutable, and that a
true future cannot turn into false, confirm the necessity of fate, but they
interpret the force of words. But those who introduce an everlasting series of
causes bind the mind of man, stripped of free will, with the necessity of
fate.

But so much for this; let us look at other things. For Chrysippus concludes in
this way: /If there is motion without a cause, not every proposition, which
the logicians call an axioma, will be either true or false; for what will not
have efficient causes will be neither true nor false; but every proposition is
either true or false; therefore there is no motion without a cause. But if
that is so, everything that happens happens by causes that have gone before;
if that is so, all things happen by fate; it is brought about, therefore, that
whatever happens happens by fate./

* Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

Here first, if I should please to agree with Epicurus and deny that every
proposition is either true or false, I would rather take that blow than
approve that all things happen by fate; for that view has something to debate,
but this one is not to be borne. And so Chrysippus strains every nerve to
persuade us that every axioma is either true or false. For as Epicurus fears
that if he has granted this, it must be granted that whatever happens happens
by fate (for if either the one or the other is true from eternity, it is also
certain, and if certain, also necessary; so he thinks both necessity and fate
are confirmed), so Chrysippus fears that if he has not maintained that
everything which is stated is either true or false, he will not hold that all
things happen by fate and from eternal causes of future things.

* Section 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

But Epicurus thinks that by the swerve of the atom the necessity of fate is
avoided. And so a certain third motion arises besides weight and impact, when
the atom swerves by a least interval (he calls this elachiston); which swerve
he is forced to confess happens without a cause, if not in words, then in the
thing itself. For the atom does not swerve struck by an atom. For how can one
be struck by another, if the individual bodies are carried by weight
perpendicularly in straight lines, as Epicurus holds? For it follows that if
one is never driven off by another, they do not even meet. From which it is
brought about that, even if there is an atom and it swerves, it swerves
without a cause.

* Section 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23
:END:

Epicurus brought in this account for this reason, that he feared that, if the
atom were always carried by a natural and necessary weight, nothing would be
free in us, since the mind would be moved as it was compelled by the motion of
the atoms. Democritus, the author of the atoms, preferred to accept that all
things happen by necessity, rather than tear natural motions from the
individual bodies. Carneades was sharper, who taught that the Epicureans could
defend their case without this fictitious swerve. For when they taught that
there can be a certain voluntary motion of the mind, that was better to defend
than to introduce a swerve, whose cause they could not find; and if that were
defended, they could easily resist Chrysippus. For when they had granted that
there is no motion without a cause, they would not grant that everything that
happens happens by antecedent causes; for there are not external and
antecedent causes of our will.

* Section 24
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h24
:END:

We therefore abuse the common custom of speech when we so say that someone
wills something or is unwilling without a cause; for we so say /without a
cause/ as to mean: without an external and antecedent cause, not without some
cause; as, when we say a vessel is empty, we do not speak as the natural
philosophers do, who hold that nothing is empty, but so that we say, for
example, that the vessel is without water, without wine, without oil; so, when
we say the mind is moved without a cause, we say it is moved without an
antecedent and external cause, not altogether without a cause. Of the atom
itself it can be said, when it is moved through the void by gravity and
weight, that it is moved without a cause, because no cause comes to it from
outside.

* Section 25
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h25
:END:

Again, however, lest all the natural philosophers laugh at us if we say that
anything happens without a cause, a distinction must be made, and it must be
said thus, that this is the nature of the indivisible body itself, that it is
moved by weight and gravity, and that this itself is the cause why it is so
carried. In the same way, for the voluntary motions of minds no external cause
is to be sought; for voluntary motion contains that nature in itself, that it
is in our power and obeys us, and not without a cause; for the cause of that
thing is nature itself.

* Section 26
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h26
:END:

Since that is so, why is it that not every pronouncement is either true or
false, unless we have granted that whatever happens happens by fate? Because,
he says, those things cannot be true futures which do not have causes why they
will be; therefore those things which are true must have causes; thus, when
they have come about, they will have come about by fate. The business is
finished, if either it must be granted to you that all things happen by fate,
or that anything can happen without a cause.

* Section 27
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h27
:END:

Can this proposition not otherwise be true: /Scipio will take Numantia/,
unless from eternity cause linking cause will bring this about? Or could this
have been false, if it had been said six hundred generations before? And if
then this proposition was not true: /Scipio will take Numantia/, not even when
that city has been overthrown is this proposition true: /Scipio took
Numantia/. Can anything, then, have been done which it was not true would be?
For as we call those past things true whose present fact was true at an
earlier time, so we shall call those future things true whose present fact
will be true at a later time.

* Section 28
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h28
:END:

Nor, if every proposition is either true or false, does it follow at once that
there are immutable causes, and those eternal, which prevent anything from
falling out otherwise than it will fall out; there are fortuitous causes which
bring it about that those things are truly said which will be said thus: /Cato
will come into the senate/, not enclosed in the nature of things and in the
world; and yet it is as immutable that he will come, when it is true, as that
he has come, and fate or necessity is not for that reason to be dreaded. For
it will be necessary to confess: /If this proposition, "Hortensius will come
to his Tusculan estate," is not true, it follows that it is false./ They want
neither of these; and that cannot be.

Nor will that lazy argument hinder us, as it is called; for a certain argument
is named by the philosophers the lazy argument, which if we obeyed we would do
nothing at all in life.

* Section 29
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h29
:END:

For they ask thus: /If it is your fate to recover from this disease, whether
you have called a doctor or have not called one, you will recover; likewise,
if it is your fate not to recover from this disease, whether you have called a
doctor or have not called one, you will not recover; and one or the other is
fate; therefore it is of no use to call a doctor./ This kind of questioning
has been rightly named lazy and idle, because by the same reasoning all action
will be taken out of life. One may even change it so as not to attach the name
of fate and still hold the same view, in this way: /If from eternity this was
true: "You will recover from that disease," whether you have called a doctor
or have not called one, you will recover; and likewise, if from eternity this
was false: "You will recover from that disease," whether you have called a
doctor or have not called one, you will not recover/; then the rest. This
argument is blamed by Chrysippus.

* Section 30
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h30
:END:

For some things, he says, in affairs are simple, some coupled; a simple thing
is: /Socrates will die on that day/; for this, whether he has done something
or has not done it, the day of dying is fixed. But if it will be fated thus:
/Oedipus will be born to Laius/, it will not be possible to say: /whether
Laius has been with a woman or has not been/; for the thing is coupled and
jointly fated; for so he calls it, because it is so fated both that Laius will
lie with his wife and that he will beget Oedipus from her, so that if it had
been said: /Milo will wrestle at Olympia/ and someone replied: /Therefore,
whether he has had an opponent or has not had one, he will wrestle/, he would
err; for /he will wrestle/ is coupled, because without an opponent there is no
wrestling. All captious arguments of that kind, then, are refuted in the same
way. /Whether you have called a doctor or have not called one, you will
recover/ is captious; for it is as fated to call a doctor as to recover.
These, as I have said, he calls jointly fated.

* Section 31
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h31
:END:

Carneades did not approve this whole class and thought this argument was
concluded too carelessly. And so he pressed in another way and used no
quibble; his conclusion was this: /If all things happen by antecedent causes,
all things happen bound and woven by a natural fastening; but if that is so,
necessity brings all things about; if that is true, nothing is in our power;
but there is something in our power; but if all things happen by fate, all
things happen by antecedent causes; therefore not whatever happens happens by
fate./

* Section 32
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h32
:END:

This argument cannot be drawn tighter. For if anyone should wish to recast the
same thing and speak thus: /If every future is from eternity true, that it
will certainly turn out as it is going to be, all things must happen bound and
woven by a natural fastening/, he would say nothing. For it makes a great
difference whether a natural cause from eternity makes future things true, or
even without a natural eternity those things that will be can be understood to
be true. And so Carneades used to say that not even Apollo could tell future
things except those whose causes nature so contained that they must happen.

* Section 33
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h33
:END:

For with what in view would the god himself say that Marcellus, the one who
was consul three times, would perish at sea? This was indeed true from
eternity, but it did not have causes bringing it about. So he held that not
even past things, of which no signs like traces existed, were known to Apollo;
still less future things! For only when the efficient causes of each thing
have been known can it at last be known what will be. Therefore Apollo could
not have predicted about Oedipus, if no causes were set in the nature of
things why his father must be killed by him, nor anything of that kind.
Therefore if it is consistent for the Stoics, who say that all things happen
by fate, to approve oracles of this kind and the rest of the things that are
drawn from divination, but those who say that the things which will be are
true from eternity need not say the same, see that their case is not the same
as the Stoics'; for these are pressed more narrowly, the others' reasoning is
loose and free.

* Section 34
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h34
:END:

But if it should be granted that nothing can happen except by an antecedent
cause, what is gained, if that cause is said not to be fastened from eternal
causes? Now a cause is that which brings about that of which it is the cause,
as a wound of death, indigestion of disease, fire of heat. And so a cause
ought not to be understood thus, that whatever precedes each thing is a cause
of it, but that which efficiently precedes each thing; nor that I went down to
the Campus was a cause why I should play ball; nor was Hecuba a cause of

* Section 36

They say, however, that it makes a difference whether something is of such a
kind that without it a thing cannot be brought about, or of such a kind that
when it is present a thing must be brought about. None of those, then, is a
cause, since none by its own force brings about the thing of which it is said
to be the cause; nor is that without which something does not happen a cause,
but that which, when it has been added, necessarily brings about that of which
it is the cause. For when Philoctetes was not yet ulcerated by the serpent's
bite, what cause was contained in the nature of things that he would be left
on the island of Lemnos? Afterwards, however, there was a nearer cause, more
closely joined to the outcome.

* Section 37

The account of the event, then, opens the cause. But from eternity this
proposition was true: /Philoctetes will be left on the island/, and this could
not turn from true to false. For of two contrary things (and by contrary I
mean here those of which one affirms something, the other denies it), of
these, then, it is necessary, against Epicurus's will, that one be true and
the other false, so that /Philoctetes will be wounded/ was true through all
previous ages, /He will not be wounded/ false; unless perhaps we wish to
follow the opinion of the Epicureans, who say that such propositions are
neither true nor false, or, when that shames them, still say that which is
more shameless, that the disjunctions from contraries are true, but that of
the propositions in them neither is true.

What amazing license and wretched ignorance of argument! For if something in
speaking is neither true nor false, certainly it is not true; and what is not

That, then, will be held which is defended by Chrysippus, that every
proposition is either true or false; reason itself will compel both that
certain things are true from eternity, and that they are not bound by eternal

And to me, at least, it seems that, when there had been two views of the old
philosophers, one of those who held that all things happen by fate in such a
way that that fate brought the force of necessity, in which view were
Democritus, Heraclitus, Empedocles, Aristotle, the other of those to whom the

honorary arbitrator, wanted to strike the mean, but he applies himself rather
to those who want the motions of minds freed from necessity; yet while he uses
his own words he slips into such difficulties that he unwillingly confirms the
necessity of fate.

* Section 40

And if you please, let us see of what kind this is in assents, which I treated
in the first discourse. For those ancients to whom all things seemed to happen
by fate said that assents are brought about by force and necessity. Those who
dissented from them freed assents from fate and denied that, fate being
applied to assents, necessity could be removed from them, and they argued
thus: /If all things happen by fate, all things happen by an antecedent cause,
and if impulse, then also those things that follow impulse, therefore also
assents; but if the cause of impulse is not situated in us, not even impulse
itself is in our power; but if that is so, not even those things which are
brought about by impulse are situated in us; therefore neither assents nor
actions are in our power. From which it is brought about that neither praises
are just nor blames nor honors nor punishments/. Since that is vicious, they
think it is reasonably concluded that not whatever happens happens by fate.

* Section 41
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h41
:END:

Chrysippus, however, since he both disapproved necessity and wanted nothing to
happen without preceding causes, distinguishes kinds of causes, so that he
both escapes necessity and retains fate. /For of causes/, he says, /some are
complete and principal, others assisting and proximate. Therefore when we say
that all things happen by fate by antecedent causes, we do not want this to be
understood: by complete and principal causes, but by assisting antecedent and
proximate causes/. And so he meets that argument which I concluded a little
before thus: if all things happen by fate, that indeed follows, that all
things happen by preceding causes, true, but not by principal and complete
causes, but by assisting and proximate. If these themselves are not in our
power, it does not follow that not even impulse is in our power. But this
would follow if we said that all things happen by complete and principal
causes, so that, since those causes were not in our power, not even impulse
would be in our power.

Therefore against those who introduce fate so as to join necessity, that
conclusion will have force; against those who will not say that antecedent
causes are complete or principal, it will have no force. For what it means
that assents are said to happen by preceding causes, he thinks can easily be
explained by him. For although assent cannot happen unless prompted by a
presentation, yet, since that presentation has a proximate cause, not a
principal one, it has this account, as Chrysippus wants, which we spoke of
just now, not that it can happen with no force from outside stirring it (for
assent must be moved by a presentation), but he reverts to his cylinder and to
his spinning-top, which cannot begin to be moved unless pushed. But when that
has happened, he thinks that for the rest, by their own nature, the cylinder
rolls and the top turns.

/As, then,/ he says, /he who pushed the cylinder gave it the beginning of

cylinder, when pushed from outside, for the rest will be moved by its own

that a cause precedes all things that happen, what can be brought forward why
it should not be admitted that all things happen by fate? provided it is
understood what the distinction and unlikeness of causes is./

Now that Chrysippus has explained these things in this way, if those who deny
that assents happen by fate †yet admit that they do not happen without an
antecedent presentation, there is another account; but if they grant that
presentations go before, and yet that assents do not happen by fate, because
that proximate and containing cause does not move the assent, see whether they

proximate and containing cause of assent is placed in the presentation, grant
that that cause is necessary for assenting, so that if all things happen by
fate, all things happen by antecedent and necessary causes; and likewise those
who dissent from him, confessing that assents do not happen without
presentations going before, will say that if all things happened by fate of
such a kind that nothing happened except by a cause going before, it must be
confessed that all things happen by fate; from which it is easy to understand
that, since both, when their own view has been opened and explained, come to
the same end, they differ in words, not in the thing.

Fragments

And so M. Cicero, in the book which he wrote on fate, when he said that that
question was most obscure and most entangled, says that even the philosopher
Chrysippus did not extricate himself in it, in these words: Chrysippus,
sweating and laboring how he may explain both that all things happen by fate
and that there is something in us, is entangled in this way.

And the definition of fate according to Tullius turns its cycles, who says:
fate is a connection of things through eternity holding one another in turn,
which is varied by its own order and law, yet in such a way that the variety
itself has eternity.

Those Homeric verses also support this view, which Cicero turned into Latin:

/
Such are the minds of men as father Jupiter himself
has bathed the crop-bearing lands with light.
/

Cicero says that Hippocrates, the most famous physician, left it written that
certain brothers, when they began to be ill together and their disease grew
worse at the same time and was relieved at the same time, he suspected were
twins. Posidonius the Stoic, much given to astrology, used to assert they were
born under the same constitution of the stars and conceived under the same.
Thus what the physician believed belonged to a most similar temperament of
health, the philosopher-astrologer referred to the force and constitution of
the stars which had been at the time they were conceived and born.

And lest a poet be a cheaper witness, receive with Cicero as voucher in what
honor this fish was with Publius Scipio Africanus, that Numantine. These are
Cicero's words in the dialogue On Fate: For when Scipio was at his place at
Lavernium and Pontius with him, a sturgeon was by chance brought to Scipio,
which is caught rather rarely, but is a fish, they say, among the foremost in
distinction. When Scipio had invited one and another of those who had come to
greet him, and seemed about to invite still more, Pontius said in his ear:
"Scipio, see what you are doing; that sturgeon is a fish for few men."
