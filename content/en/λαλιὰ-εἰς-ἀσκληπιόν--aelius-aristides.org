O Lord Asclepius, often and many times called by us, by night and by day, in
private and in public: how you granted us, who were glad and more than
longing, as from a wide sea and from gloom, to take hold of a calm harbor, and
to salute the common hearth of mankind. Of that hearth no one under the sun, I
think, is uninitiated; yet it may be firmly said that no Greek has yet enjoyed
more of it, to this day. And though I am well used to saying this, I should
hesitate none the more for that. We do not, then, omit these daily addresses
as if we fled from custom; we keep them for this very reason, that we were so
accustomed

:END:

from the beginning. For my part I am careful, of course, of the grace and
honor that come through victims and incense, whether it is done by Hesiod's
counsel or even more eagerly than my means allow. But the honor that belongs
to speech seems to me by far the most fitting. For if a man's profit in life,
and as it were its sum, is the time spent upon speeches, and of speeches those
concerning the gods are the most necessary, and if it appears to us that even
our power in the speeches themselves has come from the god, then neither, I
think, is there a fairer grace for the god than that of speeches, nor could we
put the speeches to any better use. So let us speak, beginning from the
beginning: common things, I know, and already cried abroad, for how could they
not be? Yet so much the more just for us, inasmuch as we should be better if
we add and even abound in our services than if we omit what no one of all men
thinks it right to pass in silence. The powers of Asclepius are great and
many, or rather they are all powers, more than the life of men can contain.
And it was not without cause that the people here founded a temple of Zeus
Asclepius. But if my teacher is clear to me, and he is likely so more than
anything else, and in what manner he taught these things and how, it has been
told in the Sacred Tales: this is he who leads and allots the whole, savior of
all things and guardian of the immortals, or if you wish to speak more in the
tragic style, watcher of the helm, preserving both what always is and what
comes to be. And if we hold him to be the son of Apollo and third from Zeus,
we also in turn join him to the names; for they say that Zeus himself was once
born, and then they show him as father and maker of the things that are.

But let these things, as Plato says, be held and spoken as is dear to the gods
themselves. Let us return to the point from which we turned aside. Since the
god has all the powers, he chose then to benefit mankind through them all,
rendering to each what is due. And the greatest and most common

benefaction he laid down for all, making the race immortal by succession, and
bringing about marriages and the births of children and the means and ways of
nourishment through health. The particular gifts, looking already to the
individual man, he distributed: arts and pursuits and all ways of life, using
health as a common medicine for every labor and every deed. He set
healing-places in the open, and devoted himself to the practice of his art
night and day, for the cheer of those who always need him and of those who
will. Others, then, sing and will sing for all time of other things; I wish to
mention in this way some of those that concern myself. There are those who say
they rose when they were lying down, telling of things that are granted, I
suppose, and that the god has long practiced. We obtained this benefaction not
once, but it is not easy to say how often. Further, there are those to whom he
granted added years by prophecy; of these we are: for this is the least
painful thing to say. And some, both men and women, claim that members of the
body were made for them by the god's providence, when those given by nature
had been destroyed. They recount, each something different, some speaking it
thus by word of mouth, others setting it forth in their votive offerings. For
us, then, not a part of the body, but the whole body itself he composed and
fitted together and gave as a gift, as Prometheus of old is said to have
molded man. Many pains and aches and difficulties, by day and by night, he has
taken away from many; nor could anyone say from how many. As for our own
triple waves in these matters, he himself knows them best, and he himself is
seen to have stopped them. What is most paradoxical in the god's visions is
abundant: one man to drink gypsum, another hemlock, another to strip and bathe
in cold water, though he needed warmth and not, as anyone would think, the
cold at all. Us also, then, he has honored in this way, stopping catarrhs and
chills with rivers and the sea,

healing hopeless confinement to bed by the lengths of roads, adding the
countless purges with a continuous want of food, and when I could not breathe,
commanding me to speak and write, so that if there is any boast even for those
so treated, we too should not be without a share of it. Some tell of their own
endurance and how many and what things they suffered with the god as leader,
while others tell how they found ease in what they needed. For us most things,
I suppose, have been endured in many and all kinds of ways, but some have come
about quite lightly, in pleasure, so that those who live in luxury would have
no standing, if you wished to set them against us. And when I could name the
other cities of Asia and Europe, and the company about them, and people
rejoicing with me as over goods of their own, how shall I not set this beyond
luxury? What would you say of the applause in council-chambers and the
eagerness beyond example, and of being trusted to excel even before saying
anything at all? Is this not a kind of divine grace, and one that holds the
first place of ease? I would say so, if one is to remember the greater powers.
I have already heard some say that when they were sailing and in tumult the
god appeared and stretched out a hand. Others will say they accomplished
certain affairs by following the god's counsels. These things too we can tell
no less than hear, having known them ourselves. And as many of them as it is
possible to recall are in the Sacred Tales as well. But the god is also said
to have predicted boxing tricks to a boxer of our time who was sleeping in the
temple, which he had to use in order to throw one of the very distinguished
opponents. And to us he gave lessons and songs and subjects of speeches, and
besides these the very thoughts and the diction, as those who teach children
their letters. So then, setting this as it were as the sum of the god's
benefactions, I shall close the speech somewhere here. For to me, O Lord
Asclepius, many and of all kinds,

as I said, have come from you and from your love of mankind, but the greatest,
and worthiest of most thanks, and almost, so to speak, the most intimate, are
the speeches. For you reversed Pindar's case. Pan danced his paean, as the
story goes; but I, if it is lawful to say it, am the actor of yours. For you
yourself urged me toward them and became the leader of the practice. And this
was not enough, but what was likely to follow these things as well, of those
too you took care, that my work should be in glory. And there is no city, no
private man, none of those ranked among magistrates, who, having associated
with us even a little, did not greet us, stretching the praise as far as he
was able. It was not my speeches, I think, that did this, but you, the lord.
And the greatest thing in all this is that you made me so intimate even with
the divine emperors, and, apart from association by letters, showed me
speaking among them and being eagerly received as no one ever was, and that
this came about equally from the emperors and equally from the empresses, and
from the whole imperial chorus. Odysseus had it from Athena to display himself
in the house of Alcinous and among the Phaeacians. That too, I suppose, was
great and very timely. And these things had been so accomplished, and the
signal was there recalling me, you showing in deed that you had brought me
forward into the midst for many reasons, that we might appear in the speeches,
and that the most accomplished might hear the greater powers with their own
ears. Of these and of many others we shall not cease, neither among ourselves
in private nor in our conversations with those we meet, to have the thanks we
can, so long as some share of memory and of mind happens to remain to us. And
I would say that this grace too has been brought from you, that you, the best
in all things, are present with us and ratify the speeches.
