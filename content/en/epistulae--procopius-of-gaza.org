To Caesareius and Eubulus
Pythagoras the Samian: they say not even he honored silence all the way
to the end, but a time was fixed for them that brought a change over to
speech, and they did change. Nor during the time they were silent was it
necessary, I suppose, that they know nothing from one another, but
silence held the tongue, while the hand served the mind, and through
letters they spoke again. If you emulate Pythagoras, then let there be
an end of silence; for time will reasonably dissolve it. But if you have
come bringing something more, you are hard men and do not know how to
love in return. And yet they say Alpheus, being a river and so far
distant from Arethusa, even to this day looks from the Peloponnese upon
Sicily, and is led through the midst of the sea to see that he may be
fair and remain sweet for the spring. And she receives her lover worn
out and gives herself, and then you would see a strange aphrodite of
river and spring. But you neither come to us with words, nor answer
those who come. But do grant us an end of accusations; if notbut I do
not want to say anything, lest I go away having caused pain. 2 To
Hieronymus I thought you had long since reached the Nile, and often
taking this into memory I used to say my customary thing: "How fortunate
are the children of the Egyptians, who of old have the Nile as steward
of all that river knows how to bring, and now have taken on your Muse as
well, in no way less honorable than the Nile, and in my judgment perhaps
even greater. For he, flowing over their land, lets them grow rich, but
you, letting your tongue loose upon the souls of the young, steer them
toward the wealth of knowledge, no less than Pythagoras of old when he
sojourned among them, and after him Plato." These things I was
reckoning, and you were not far off without my knowing. But when the man
who delivered the letter came, at first in wonder I thought the thing a
deception and did not trust my eyes. But when I met what was written and
was persuaded that the graces could not belong to another, I was
pleased, how do you think? For I thought the hopes of seeing you again
closer to me, and I rejoiced reasonably if you had made the fatherland
enviable for even a little time. But you seem to me to accuse it in
play. That the air over our heads happens to be as you said, and that
the water leads those who drink it into memory of the sea, and that our
bread is of barley mixed at random, I would not deny; but these things
have been made less tragic by you out of sparing the fatherland. Yet
still, my good man, one ought to thank her for training the mind toward
philosophy, so that you may someday know how to look down on Egyptian
luxury. As for Odysseus the wise, who dwelt on an island small and rough
together, and what else but Ithaca, neither Calypso persuaded him, nor a
change of nature toward the better, not to choose Ithaca instead of
these. Nor, I think, would a Spartan, thinking of the life at home, have
blamed Sparta. But I know from where you were stirred to accuse. You are
fond of money, my excellent friend. Then standing a little apart from
the gain you cry iou iou, and you call back often the Nile and the
wealth from there. This, I suppose, makes you dry, as you yourself said,
and makes your body no more graceful than Chaerephon's. I think you are
also amorously attached to the bride and try, barely, all that longing
can. But may these things be as is dear to Aphrodite and the Loves.
Farewell, and may I address you sooner also as a father of children. 3
To Pythius If someone came up to me who had not done this before and
thought fit to learn whether I had a lyre, and made a speech about
harmony, I would certainly have perceived the thing, and I would have
known at once that this man was a musician. But if the talk was of
weapons, and he spoke of battles and was always recounting the fortunes
of war, there was no need, I suppose, to go to Pytho and hear that this


man was a soldier. But what do I mean by this? You already understand
it entirely, yet it shall be said no less. You seem to me truly to have
the Muses dwelling in your soul; then, possessed by the goddesses, you
have a life given over to them, and nothing is wonderful to you except
books and speeches and all that leads a soul toward the better. From
this you became a guest among us, making a beginning of friendship that
befits you, I think. But I wish you had not missed the hope you had of
us, and that the book you ask for were with us. For so you would always
have had what you longed for, and it would have been the greatest thing
for me to seem to have done you a favor. As it is I swear, not by
Socrates' plane-tree but by the speeches themselves, that I have not yet
obtained the possession, though I have been in love with it for a very
long time already. A little laterbut let it be said with Hermes and the
Musesit will be brought from Alexandria. For those who were charged with
it have written us this from there. If this should come to pass, you
will certainly see me coming to you of my own accord about sharing it,
as if I myself wanted to receive it. 4 To Germanus How much good men
grieve us when they give a taste of themselves and then withdraw from
those who have come to know them, I have now learned in fact and tell
the others. For I knew you even before we came to speech. If you wonder
from where, I had as teachers all who had tried you and marveled. And
when I too mixed with you and learned, I confessed thanks to fortune,
and again I blamed her if, having given a taste of so great a thing
with the fingertip, as they say, she would then take the pleasure away.
And the present did not gladden me so much as the hopes of the future
grieved me. For I reckoned that you would leave us and be gone too soon.
This both has happened and, having happened, grieves, and I am at a loss
at the thing, while you contend even when far away to kindle our love
still more. For by your zeal toward me you even surpassed my hopes and
won. But I wish some god would be gracious to us and bring you back to
us sooner. 5 To John If there were indictments for neglect of friends,
and someone brought me into court charging just this, that I did wrong
not moderately, making nothing of a friend, I would not have thought to
take the winning side in any other way than by producing you as witness,
you who have now somehow appeared as accuser; so contrary to my hopes
have your doings turned out, if a small matter prepared you to hesitate
about me. For I know even non-friends striking often with letters when
they wish to escape notice, and friends keeping silent when, content
with the memory of the absent, they are busy with others. So neither is
writing a proof of goodwill, nor silence a ground of accusation, unless
the mind confirms both. Therefore leaving these things consider those:
whether nothing of yours gives me forgetfulness, whether I carry you
through all speech and wonder as if adorned by the memory, whether when
I have said "John" I have altogether added "the sweet" to the word.
Considering these things you will love a silent man more than another
who speaks, even if the bronze of Dodona sounds on his tongue. But see
that you do not think you have this as a defense and then keep silent;
if you do, we shall speak the opposite. 6 To my brother Zacharias
Wishing to tell you the affairs of this best of men I fear I may seem
to trouble you in vain, making my speech before those who know. For you
know what sort of man he himself is, how, having dedicated his whole
life to God, he is at enmity with all that is human, and clings to the
hope from above, and for this reason is set out for those who wish to do
wrong, and is fit for suffering. And you know this man too for I am
ashamed to call him brother how he thinks it one virtue to fulfill
every vice, having overlooked a brother pressed by hunger for so long a
time, not meaning to furnish from his own what ought to have come from a
right-thinking man, even if the occasion called for this. As it is the
charge is not this, that he omitted what befits the very temperate, but
that, becoming a lover even of what does not belong to him, he has taken
these things away, and he abuses this man often, and has often laid on
blows. And thinking his own mind a law, he has perhaps blamed nature,
because she invented nothing more for wickedness. Hence he did not stop


his wickedness at these things, or rather these were preludes of the
evils to come. For his own mother, already an old woman, when a woman
hoped to gather the fruits of child-rearing, both through her age and
through her family, and enjoying respect even among those with no claim
on her, this woman he often spoke ill of, and often even raised hands
against her who bore him, O Zeus, respecting neither nature nor time nor
pitiable gray hair nor the humane name of mother, but already even
throwing her down by force he cast her to the ground, and leapt on her
with his heel, while she cried out as one would expect and called her
child. And what would a mother not say, after birth-pangs and nurture
and those fair hopes, enduring such evils from her child? Last of all he
drove her from the house, resourceless, stripped, deprived of
necessities, granting perhaps this one favor, that he freed himself from
living with her. And now this man revels in their evils, while an old
woman, holding the first place of nobility among us, has the hopes of
life in her hands. And for this worthy man her suffering has become
another disaster, and though he is at a loss for his own food he
received her, increasing his want, yet he pays a necessary debt to
nature. And now they live together having one consolation, that they
have not suffered more, while the mother, being a woman and having
suffered such things, has one life: to remember her own evils and weep
at the memory and show the breast to Justice, telling what she has
suffered. Taking these things to mind then, and thinking that we add
nothing to the truths but show only the heads of his wickedness because
of the occasion of the letter, measure your zeal against that man's
vice, and become such as God requires of you, and the law of friendship,
and the hope of the wronged. 7 To my brother Philip You have been silent
toward us a long time and I cannot bear the thing. Consider: it was
winter and we did not bear it moderately, swallows appeared and not even
with them did you speak, and now cicadas sing and contrary to our hopes
there is silence. If then this is toward grief, I do not know what I
did that caused pain, yet I do not think you could take a greater
punishment. But if you have shifted from your old zeal into slackness,
you wrong the law of kinship, and you transgress the ordinance of
friendship. And if it is not harsh to say, perhaps someone would say you
have even become your own accuser. For in the things for which you
rebuked us when we were silent, in these you yourself have been caught.
And how would it make sense that the man who persuaded others to talk
should take over the silence of the one persuaded? But it is time at
last to call me a sophist too and to play along with the speech. This is
no matter at all, and whatever you say I shall bear moderately, if only
I hear you speaking. 8 To Diodorus The report of your education filled
our ears with the sort of words we prayed for. For one recounted the
zeal of your advocacy, another marveled at your vigor in the courts,
another your goodwill toward those who approach, except if someone
happened to have done wrong. I know I heard this too, that seeing some
who had great power but marveled at nothing of what is just, you blew
hard against them and poured down an abuse that is praised. And I,
hearing these things, as different men told different parts, became as
if possessed by pleasure, and thinking highly of the words I seemed
myself to be praised, and already I even confessed thanks to those who
reported, as those who hear people praising them are wont to do. While I
was in this state and reckoning, the fair Orion came up and handed me
the letter written to him from you, and he congratulated me on the
friendship, as if he thought it terrible if a man of your sort escaped
my notice. And I, after the report, learning you by trial, and what sort
of man you are toward those in need even when they are not present, you
could not say how much I marveled, and I called him fortunate who had
obtained your zeal. And when he thought fit to get letters from me that
would still more stir your eagerness by the memory of friendship, I
called him simple; for a man well born toward virtue would not


need letters calling him toward it. Yet I did not persuade him. For he
seemed to say something just as well: "The man who is such by himself,
what would he become if he obtained letters from you?" So may you
yourself become such as you were before, and may I again be praised for
the friendship, and may the young man not miss the hope he has of us,
but may those who seem to have great power know that it is reasonable to
be defeated by what is just. 9 To Hieronymus I thought my sister's
marriage was hidden, not even known to all who live by the house, still
less to you, whom I thought still sat by the Nile. But nothing of what
comes to luxury seems to escape you; the moment something is done, the
scent of what is being accomplished strikes you from far off, and
perhaps you even surpass the Homeric Zeus, in love with libation and
with savor curling round the smoke. The Nile has put you in this state,
and the fortunate people beside it, after whom, living at Elusa, you
have a sharper sense, if anywhere on earth smoke is given off. And I was
almost moved to treat your boy badly, because he did not let you write
the letter at length. But bringing gifts from you he changed me by the
sight and somehow dissolved my anger. Before long I shall repay you for
these things; for either a little daughter has been born to you or she
will be. 10 To my brother Philip The season that grieved through silence
has already its end and has ceased, but I, though I wish to think highly
in hope of letters from you, come to myself and take memory of what has
passed; for so far as it depends on you in your silence the whole year
is winter for us. Or rather you even make the real winter more longed
for by us, inasmuch as in that one there is the consolation of referring
the silence to the necessity of the season, but in summer it is left us
to weep that there is no account of lovers with you; for that the
necessity is absent, and you are silent again, presents us with the
opinion of being despised. But O all you Loves, bring this man to a
trial of our things, so that he may someday take perception of how
terrible it was for a lover to be overlooked. 11 To Zacharias Loose our
silence with the winter; for both are burdensome; and if it were
possible to hear you speaking through everything, even winter would
grieve us nothing so much. But let your spring flow upon us with its
whole stream. For this in my judgment conquers the pleasure from spring,
even if in this the light is new, even if in this swallows fill the
ears with song, hymning the season as it seems to me, and the roses
coming up from the calyx offer a mixed and blended sight and lead into
memory of ancient tales, how Adonis was fair and Aphrodite sought him,
and she who bore the Loves was somehow taught to love, and missing her
darling she is struck in the foot and brings the rose. But to us who
love, these things are thought small compared with your voice. 12 To
Zacharias See, a time again demanding of you the inclination toward
those in need, and I again am the one giving the occasion, so that from
both sides I know well you will confess thanks, because you found a
chance to do good and because this happened for you through me. For you
truly rejoice more in doing good than others in suffering it, and you
would even call the one who gave the occasion a benefactor. So I am
proud as one who will rather give you a favor than receive one. "But
what is the matter?" you inquire, I know well, laughing. A certain
Alexander among us makes his trade in timber, and from this has his
living. As is likely for such men, he makes someone a partner in the
business Euthymius is his name so that one may send what seems good, and
the other staying may receive it, and so they make the trade. This man
then dwells in the Rhodians' country among you, O Zeus, and yet he says
he does not suffer just things. The one doing the wrong you would call
in the Roman tongue a concussor; for he legislates what is not
customary and contrives an occasion of newer profit, as if you were not
the rulers of Rhodes. But these things, as is likely, you did not know.
So let him stop when you have learned, so that I may know thanks to you,
and he who has obtained what is just may know it to me, telling how he
was wronged and has ceased. 13 To Alypius and Stephen the grammarians
and to Hierius the Roman Solon When because of laws the city seemed to


seem well, thinking it terrible that the wonder of his wisdom should
stand only as far as Attica, he went among the Egyptians though they
boasted they were wiser than the rest, and he went also to Croesus as
one priding himself on wealth for happiness; for it seemed greater to
Solon to revel in wisdom than that Pactolus flowing with gold ... the
land, as much as that man traversed. And those who saw marveled, and
when he went away they longed for Solon again. And the Athenians, among
whom he laid the foundation of his lessons, perhaps even prayed Athena
to give the lawgiver back to the city again. And if he even contrives
the absence for these reasons, wishing to know how far the Athenians
have come in love for him, I would not, I think, wonder. Yet if the
Athenians wept at the absence of one man, though they were fortunate, as
is likely, in other wise men, what would we become when deprived of the
whole capital? And the city which one would formerly have hesitated
whether it was proper to call Attica or Italy, this now suddenly is
silent and has been stripped of its marks, and it seems to suffer the
same as Cithaeron, whenever having received the god in abundance it is
suddenly deserted when he goes away again, and Dionysus is nowhere.
Hence I shall cry out against Apollo, who called our things to himself
and overlooked them. And I think the Muses share his joy in the good
fortune; and you, I suppose, are even filled with ancient mythologies,
already by Daphne herself reckoning the god's passion, Daphne's
temperance and her humanity, and a plant consoling a lover; and among
you alone of things seen does the sight grant testimony to the speech.
And if dense cypresses are also given over toward the grace of Apollo's
beloved, and water unstinted, and cicadas sing and a road is spread
soft with grass, and trees upon trees and houses hidden in the midst by
their height, and a moderate breeze and a mixed scent and shade shutting
out the sun's pain, it is yours either to report as quickly as possible
or to tell it in letters. Yet I would wish to see you when you have come
to us, so that besides the rest I may also hear you prophesying. For I
think Daphne has given you this too. 14 To Palladius I think the virtue
of those with great power the greatest consolation to the wronged. For
when power has come to the same point with a mind that honors what is
just, what prevents what the laws think right from going to fulfillment?
But what do I mean by the prelude? Isidorus the scholasticus, of old a
friend while he lived, and now as far as memory, what sort of man he was
in life and how from every side he offered himself to be marveled at,
you yourself could tell even to others. But now, having departed from
men, he has left the house deserted of alliance; for she who was of old
a mother is now childless on the threshold of old age and a mother no
longer, and the wife bears the yoke of widowhood. And what need even to
speak of the children? For these are girls of small age and trying the
fortune of orphanhood. To speak concisely, they are set out for all who
wish to do wrong, unless you prevent it, toward whom their hopes look.
For so-and-so is wicked by nature, and having come into license and seen
the desertion he has appeared more wicked; for living next to their
house and taking the envy from neighborhood, he has made one resolve, to
show the dwelling useless to them. Hence he did not endure the builders
being lawfully prevented, and thinking the things agreed between them
through writings nonsense he shows himself more authoritative than the
laws and than what he himself agreed. But taking a judge's mind that
also hates what is base, become a father to the girls and a child to the
mother and again equal to a husband for the wife, and to us such as
prior trial has often shown; and let him know well that it is not
possible to be deserted of alliance, so long as the laws are preserved
and men honor what is just. 15 To Nephalius I was pleased when I
received your letter, but wishing to bring it to work I did not have
need serving my eagerness. The cause I would never call fortune, and
least of all toward you, but altogether the providence of God, steering
our affairs as it wishes. 16 To Gessius the iatrosophist Taking your
letter into my hands I seemed scarcely


to have recovered the Muses themselves, and I was possessed by pleasure
and did not know what to become, like the Delphians whenever [as] Apollo
has come from the Hyperboreans they who were silent before suddenly
become full of the god. Such a one was I, marveling at everything, the
season of the names, their harmony toward one another, the beauty
appearing through all, and the greatest thing, your manners, from which
the letters came forth to us. And may many good things come to you who
have given us back such a feast. But "one swallow does not make a
spring," he says. And what sort of swallow even the previous one
sufficed for the need of spring? And if you added a second and a third,
then many, neither that Croesus nor Pactolus flowing with gold would be
anything among us. And I saw your children with pleasure and was led up
toward you as through an image. Hence I prayed to see still more, and if
one must speak great things, better than their father. But it is easy
for one who prays to say whatever he wishes, and to imitate Hector. And
as for the companion Dorotheus, think that I myself in person bring him
to you, and say nothing else, but fill for him the hopes which your
affairs themselves offered when he told them. And if he hopes to have
something more through me, it would be yours that he not be cheated of
his judgment. Manner and zeal together will also introduce the young man
to you, which if it were possible to set him beside you clearly together
with the sight, perhaps there would have been no need of our letters. As
it is, having first honored him through me, later I think you will
marvel also through himself. I think too that the companion Anastasius
will look on you with gracious eyes, who also claims our family. For
what more need one say to a man long a friend and now no less choosing
to be well-disposed? 17 To my brother Philip Again letters from us and
again you are of those who keep silent. And I fear you may even condemn
some chatter in us. For being altogether silent you think those who talk
in any way at all are chatterers. But if you overlook friends when
absent, it is left to all the absent to be silent. So become that man
again and speak to us; for good men, when they do not do what is
fitting, are set out as an example to those who wish to go wrong. And I
would wish you also to bear the mark of a man who would not, even if
anything happened, overlook what is fitting. And toward the need written
to you show yourselves so zealous as accomplishing this for yourselves.
For I hesitate to say that I even want the eagerness from you to become
greater. For in place of very many favors we pay this one to the man in
need of the service. 18 To my brother Zacharias As it seems, my
excellent friend, you will never stop the jests against us. And do not
stop them, all you gods. For I, hearing them said, both smile at the
letters, and I come to you in thought, and I seem to say something to
you and to hear you speaking. So if you care for the grace in letters,
and you care altogether "shoot thus" and you will seem to grant greater
things than if you allowed me to become Croesus. And call me a sophist
again and say I love ticks, and add a brow and puffery and whatever is
dear to you. And often even by saying "sophist" you have got off, as if
this were the same as calling a man a braggart. But I would not deny my
art; and if it is proper to this to raise one toward bragging, you too
have, I suppose, a large share of the art, so that I fear lest as much
as you share in this ... but I shall be silent out of respect toward
you. Perhaps you wonder if, being a sophist and already looking at
spring, when I ought to parade in speech, I then passed by in silence;
and somewhere you seek in the letters flowers and swallows and a tame
change of the sea, and fair Adonis and that Aphrodite who loves contrary
to expectation, and because you do not hear the rose and the grace upon
it you marvel again. But I would say nothing of that sort and least of
all to you, lest again you laugh and name me tasteless and a sophist. 19
To Epiphanius You were a terrible man then at moving whom you wish
toward speech and silence, and you seem to me to do something similar,
as if someone having a lyre, having learned to use it, should now weave
a song, and now refrain from the strikings.


For if anything besides these should be thought better, he goes at once
toward that, and neither he himself nor the lyre would say anything
musical. And when you are silent toward us, having changed toward newer
things, at once downcast and silent are those who were of old dearest,
and now perhaps friends. But I know from where the thing comes. The laws
forced you to prepare a solemn man with brows drawn up, then taking a
lawgiver's spirit and already thinking you lead Roman affairs by your
vote, you were so lifted against us. But since a word and memory of the
old custom has barely stolen in upon you, I wish this had happened long
ago too yet still, hearing your tongue sounding something sweet with the
swallows I rise in my soul and think the sun strikes me more brightly,
and now it is truly spring for me. But be gracious to us hereafter, and
come no longer into forgetfulness of old loves. 20 To Strategius and
Hilasius Again fish from the vines for you, and fields, as it seems,
furnish the things of the sea, and it is yours to harvest both lands and
sea at once and here take clusters and there again fish. And the sea
seems to imitate your manners; for you furnish eagerly, and she wishes
to serve your love of honor. But the sea among us makes us equal even to
inlanders, except that it offers the waves and furnishes the threat from
itself without fish. 21 To Sosianus If anyone is a lover of money, he
would gladly say nothing but this and hear others saying it. And if you
also brought gold and offered it, you have the first place of honor with
that man. But for whom affairs are in arms, this man taking a fair
shield both thought highly and, carrying the possession this way and
that, is filled more with Ares. And what could one do to gratify a good
man other than offering like men to see? Hence knowing you such both
having become and wishing to be, it did not seem proper to me that
Diodorus the scholasticus be unknown, but I bring and give him to you,
and you to him, thinking I shall gratify both of you if you come into
trial of one another. And each will certainly praise me for knowing how
to procure such things for friends. 22 To John Let the most learned
Diodorus be a dissolving of the silence toward one another for us, both
demanding that you write and giving you what is from us. For this man is
my friend, and already before seeing you he happened to be your lover
too; for the words from us telling about you made the love even before
the sight. For the man is amorous of what is fair and inquires after the
worthy, as if fearing that someone of that sort might escape notice. And
what need have I to tell you about him? For having learned him by trial
you will certainly think the praise too little and will call me a weak
rhetor, as not even able to say what belongs to him. 23 To Diodorus This
is that which has happened even as I expected. For I thought that as
soon as you were there, seeing a platform of magistrates and a chorus of
rhetors and a crowd of refugees following, you would at once be filled
with spirit, and if someone wished only to talk you would scarcely
endure the speaker; and our account will be short, and "What are those
men hidden in darkness to me?" you will say, I suppose, altogether about
us. And if you also addressed us as going on the ground, I would not
even wonder at this. I both expected the same and this has happened, the
spirit of the rhetors among us, who even call the others refugees,
making their own affairs solemn. And now you seem to have chosen us as
friends formerly, not judging us worthy, as is likely, but because for
want of better men you used those at hand, and you altogether repent of
the time you spent unworthily among us. And now I fear as I write that
you may even take it ill at the letter from us. But I would pray that
you do well; and if it is necessary to be despised again, it is
grievous, yet we shall bear it nonetheless, consoled by your fortune. 24
To Zacharias and Philip Let winter carry off the cause of the previous
silence, grieving in the usual ways; but of the silence from here, if
there should even be any, you would reasonably submit the accounts. And
yet how great a thing silence of the beloved is to those who long, how
could one set it before those who have not come to trial of the thing?
But you would know


altogether, being disposed amorously toward friends; for I would not
think so highly as to suppose myself loved in return by you. And yet I
would pray this happen to me rather than to be rich with much gold and
to have reached the peak of any fortune whatever. For so each, not able
to be slack because of longing, would altogether console my love. But if
there is nothing of this sort, and you will still be silent, I shall
accuse having suffered such things, and some amorous judge will decide,
and he will vote what you must suffer or pay. Or rather the suffering
shall be away, lest you grieve me the lover again, but he will vote that
you pay so many letters as to console the silence by the multitude of
the time that went before. 25 To my brother Philip For good men manner
is enough for the constitution of friendship; but since there ought also
