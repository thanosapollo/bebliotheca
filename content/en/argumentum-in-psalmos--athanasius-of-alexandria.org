* Argument on the Psalms

Of Saint Athanasius, Archbishop of Alexandria, Argument on the Psalms. In the
Hebrew book of the Psalms all were recorded without the addition of the
number, and in different ways: some are joined, and some are divided. For
example, then, the first and the second are joined according to the Hebrew;
and again the ninth, joined among us, is divided into two in the Hebrew. And
the whole book of the Psalms is divided into five parts. And it is to be
observed that the order of the Psalms is not composed according to the
sequence of the times of the history; but it has been exchanged among many of
the times, as the book of Kingdoms makes clear. For when much idolatry had
prevailed over the nation of the Jews, they had made forgetfulness of the
ancestral Scriptures, so that not even a book of the law of Moses appeared,
nor indeed was anything of the piety of the fathers preserved among them.
When, then, they were even killing the prophets, who were convicting their
impieties, and on account of these things they were first delivered over to
the captivity of the Assyrians; and after these things one of the prophets,
being zealous concerning their gathering, had collected, along with the rest,
also the book of the Psalms; not finding all of them at once, but according to
different times; and arranging first those found first; wherefore not even all
those of David are found in succession, but in the interval also those of the
sons of Korah, and of Asaph, and of Solomon, and of Moses, and of Ethan the
Israelite, and of Heman, and again of David, are found mixedly arranged in
=27.57= the book; not according to the times in which they were spoken, but
according to those in which they were found. And you would find the same thing
having happened also in the sections of the prophets. And the untitled psalms
after the first and the twelfth, and those that have a title, not making clear
whose they are, the children of the Hebrews say belong to those whose name is
borne in the titles prefixed to the untitled ones. And the first and the
second, both being one, I consider to belong to David, because all those in
the first part of the Psalms in succession are his. From the third, then, as
far as the 40th, all are titled of David; wherefore both the first and the
second seem to me to belong to the same. And the book of the Acts of the
Apostles confirms the statement, in which it is said: Master, you made the
heaven and the earth, and the sea, and all things in them; who of our father
through the Holy Spirit of the mouth of your servant David said: Why did the
nations rage, and the peoples imagine vain things? For clearly through these
he said that the 2nd psalm is of David. And it is likely that the
superscription of David has been left silent upon it, because it has a bare
prophecy concerning the Lord, in which it is said: Against the Lord and
against his Christ. Following which he adds from the very person of Christ
this: The Lord said to me: You are my Son. There are then psalms in all, 150.
And some think that all are of the blessed David; but this is not indicated.
For all are referred to David; but in the titles it is shown whose psalm each
has become. For four rulers of songs were chosen, 288 followers. Psalms
therefore of the four rulers are set beside them, as the titles also teach.
For whenever it says: A psalm to the sons of Korah, Ethan, Asaph, and Heman:
the sons of Korah, Ethan, Asaph, and Heman themselves sing. When it says, A
psalm of Asaph, Asaph himself spoke; when it says, A psalm of Jeduthun,
Jeduthun himself sings; whenever it says, of David, David himself was
speaking; but when it says, to David, it is spoken by others unto David.

They are in all 150, of which 72 are of David, and 9 to David, 12 of Asaph, 12
to the sons of Korah, one of Jeduthun, one of Ethan, one of Heman, one of
Solomon, two of Haggai and Zechariah, untitled 39, and one of Moses; all 150.
It is necessary then to consider the mystery for what reason, though there are
different songs, and not all the psalms being of David, it is referred to
David; and this we shall not pass by. For on this account it was titled,
because he himself became the cause of these things being; for he himself
chose the singers. He himself then, having become the cause, was deemed worthy
of this honor, that all things whatsoever the singers said might be reckoned
to David. =27.60= That David, being a prophet, and having the inward eyes
opened, and knowing that the evil spirits rejoice at the fall of men, and
again are grieved in turn when defeated at the correction of men, certainly
made the prayers against them intelligibly, transferring from the sensible
enemies to them. For if we do not take the aim of the prophets in this manner,
not only shall we have no edification from the things said by them toward the
acquisition of meekness, but we shall also take on a certain hard disposition
contrary to the evangelical teaching from frequently cursing the enemies, and
not loving; David saying a myriad times, Let all my enemies be ashamed and
confounded, and let me not be ashamed. And we would not be persuaded that men
who were prophets and friends of God had spoken these things thus, except in
the aforementioned manner. For thus the industrious one must understand,
wherever he should find a prophet cursing, transferring from the sensible to
the intelligible enemies. For the rest, then, let the ascetic occupy himself
with the understanding of every word.
