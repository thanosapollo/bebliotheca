OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS JOHN, ARCHBISHOP OF CONSTANTINOPLE, CHRYSOSTOM,
A COMMENTARY ON THE EPISTLE TO TITUS. =62.663= HOMILY I. Paul, a servant of
God, and an apostle of Jesus Christ, according to the faith of God's elect and
the knowledge of the truth which is according to godliness, in hope of eternal
life, which God, who cannot lie, promised before times everlasting, but
manifested in his own seasons his word in the preaching with which I was
entrusted according to the commandment of God our Savior, to Titus, my genuine
child according to a common faith: grace and peace from God the Father and the
Lord Jesus Christ our Savior. 1. This man was approved among those who were
with Paul; for if he had not been approved, he would not have entrusted the
whole island to him, he would not have commanded him to set in order the
things that were lacking. For, he says, that you may set in order the things
that are wanting; he would not have committed to him the judgment of so many
bishops, unless he had great confidence in the man. They say he was also
young, because he calls him child; yet this is not yet clear from that. And I
think there is mention of him in the Acts as well. Perhaps he was also a
Corinthian, unless there was someone else of the same name. And he summons
Zenas to himself, and wants Apollos to be sent, but not this man; for even
before the emperor he bore witness to a greater courage and virtue in these.
And there seem to me to have been some times in between, and Paul writes these
things being at liberty; for he says nothing about trials, but continually
turns about the grace of God both up and down, which is sufficient consolation
for those who have believed toward virtue. For to learn of what things they
were worthy, and to what they were transferred, and these things =62.664= by
grace, and of what they have been counted worthy, is no small exhortation. He
also inveighs against the Jews. And if he insults the whole nation, do not
wonder; for he does it also in the case of the Galatians, saying, O foolish
Galatians. This is not of an insolent character, but of a loving one. For if
he did these things for his own sake, one might reasonably blame him; but if
burning and boiling for the preaching, he did not do it insolently. And Christ
too reviled the scribes and Pharisees ten thousand times, but not for his own
sake, but because they were destroying all the others. He makes the epistle
short, reasonably. And this too was a token of the virtue of Titus, that he
did not need many words, but as it were some reminder. And this seems to me to
be earlier than the epistle to Timothy. For that one he wrote toward the end,
being in bonds, but here he is free and released; for, I have determined to
winter at Nicopolis, was a token that he was not yet in bonds; but there he
continually calls himself a prisoner. What then does he say? Paul, a servant
of God, and an apostle of Jesus Christ, according to the faith of God's elect.
Do you see how he sets them down indifferently, sometimes calling himself a
servant of God and an apostle of Christ, sometimes a servant of Christ, Paul a
servant of Jesus Christ? thus he knows no difference between Father and Son.
According to the faith of the elect of God and the knowledge of the truth
which is according to godliness, in hope of eternal life. According to


the faith of God's elect. =62.665= Because you believed, or because you were
entrusted? I think he says that he himself was entrusted with the elect of
God. That is, I did not receive the dignity from achievements, nor from toils
and sweats, but the whole came of the kindness of him who believed. Then, lest
the grace be thought irrational; for it is not entirely his; since why did he
not also entrust it to others? for this reason he added, And the knowledge of
the truth which is according to godliness. From this, he says, I was
entrusted, or rather this too I was entrusted from his grace; for of this too
he himself is the cause. For this reason Christ himself also says, You did not
choose me, but I chose you; and again this same blessed man writing elsewhere
says, I shall know, even as I also was known; and again, If I may apprehend,
that for which I was also apprehended by Christ Jesus. First we were
apprehended, and afterwards we knew; first we were known, and then we
apprehended; first we were called, and then we obeyed. And by saying,
According to faith, he reckons the whole to them, that For their sake I am an
apostle, not as being worthy, but because of the elect; which he also says
elsewhere: For all things are yours, whether Paul, or Apollos. And knowledge,
he says, of the truth which is according to godliness. For there is a truth of
things, but not according to godliness; as to know the things of farming, to
know arts, is to know truly; but this is the truth according to godliness. Or,
According to faith, that they believed just as the rest of the elect, and knew
the truth. From faith then is the knowledge, not from reasonings. In hope of
eternal life. He spoke of the present, that which is in the grace of God; he
speaks also of the future, and sets prizes for us for the things with which he
has benefited us. For since we believed and were delivered from error, he
wishes to crown us. See how the preface is full of the benefits of God; and
this whole epistle throughout is especially of this kind, stirring up that
holy man himself rather toward the toils, and those who are being taught. For
nothing so benefits us as continually to remember the benefits of God, both
the common and the private. For if, bringing up the benefits of a friend, or
hearing a kind word or deed, we are warmed, much more, when we see in how many
dangers we fell, and God delivered us from all, we shall be more eager toward
obedience. And knowledge, he says, of truth. And he said truth here thus in
contrast to the type. For that too was knowledge, and it was godliness, but
not of truth, nor indeed of falsehood, but of types and an image. He said
well, In hope of eternal life, because that was in hope of the present life;
For he who has done them, he says, shall live in them. Do you see how from the
preface already the difference of grace is shown? Those are not elect, but we;
for even if they too were called elect, yet no longer. Which, he says, God who
cannot lie promised before times everlasting. That is, not now from
repentance, but these things were predetermined from above. He sets this down
in many places, as when he says, Separated unto the gospel of God; and again,
Whom he foreknew he also predestined; showing our nobility, that =62.666= he
loved us not now, but from above. And it is no small thing to have been loved
from above and from the beginning. 2. Which, he says, God who cannot lie
promised. If he cannot lie, what he promised will certainly be; if he cannot
lie, one must not doubt, even if it is after death. Which, he says, God who
cannot lie promised before times everlasting. And from saying, Before times
everlasting, he shows the trustworthiness. Not because the Jews have not now
come forward, he says, for this reason these things, but thus it was typed
from above. Hear then what he says: But he manifested in his own seasons. What
then is the delay? For the sake of care and of doing it in due season; A time,
says the prophet, for the Lord to act. For Own is this, the fitting, the due,


the suitable. He manifested, he says, in his own seasons his word in
preaching, with which I was entrusted; that is, the preaching. For this
contained all things, the gospel, both the things here and the things to come,
the life, the godliness, the faith, all together. In preaching; that is,
openly, with boldness; for this is, In preaching. For as the herald proclaims
in the theater with all present, so we too preach, so as to add nothing, but
to say the very things we heard. For the virtue of the herald is in telling to
all what has happened, not in adding something and taking away. If then one
must preach, one must preach with boldness; since it is not even preaching.
For this reason Christ did not say, Speak upon the housetops, but, Preach upon
the housetops, presenting what happened both from the place and from the
manner. With which I was entrusted according to the commandment of God our
Savior. Both, I was entrusted, and, According to commandment, show the
trustworthiness; so that no one should take it ill, nor play the coy, nor be
displeased. If then it is a commandment, I am not master; for I fulfill a
command. For of the things to be done some lie in our power, and some do not;
for what he says commanding, these are not in our power; but what he permits,
these happen to be in our power. As, If anyone says to his brother, Fool, he
shall be liable to the gehenna of fire, this is a command; and, When you bring
your gift to the altar, and remember that your brother has something against
you, leave there your gift before the altar, and go first be reconciled to
your brother, and then come and offer your gift; this too is a command, and
the one who has not done it must of necessity be liable to punishment. But
when he says, If you wish to be perfect, sell your possessions; and again, He
who is able to receive it, let him receive it, it is no longer a command; for
he makes the hearer master of what was said, and gives him the choice of the
things to be done. For these things both to do and not to do are in our power;
but the commands are not in our power, but of necessity either to do them, or
not having done them to be punished. This then he also says when he says, For
necessity is laid upon me; for woe is =62.667= me if I do not preach the
gospel. And I will say it more clearly, so that it may become evident to all.
As, the one entrusted with the rule of the Church, and honored with the
dignity of the episcopate, if he does not declare to the people the things to
be done, is not without account; but the layman has no necessity of this. For
this reason Paul also says that I do this According to the commandment of our
Savior. And see how the epithets fit what I said. For having said above, God
who cannot lie, here he says, According to the commandment of God our Savior.
If then he is Savior, and he himself commanded these things from wishing to
save, the thing is not then of love of rule; for it is faith, and it is a
commandment of God the Savior. To Titus, my genuine child. For there are also
children who are not genuine, as that one of whom he says: If any brother
named is a fornicator, or covetous, or an idolater, or a reviler, or a
drunkard, with such a one not even to eat. See, he is also a child, and he is
not a genuine child; he is a child, because he once received the grace and was
born again; but not genuine, because he is unworthy of the father, because he
deserts to another tyrant. For in the case of natural children the genuine and
not genuine is defined from the woman in labor and the one who sows; but here
it is not so, but from choice. For it is possible for one who has become
genuine not to remain genuine; it is possible for one who is not genuine to
become genuine; for these things are not shut in by necessity of nature, but
by authority of choice; whence it also has continual changes. Onesimus was a
genuine child, but he was also not genuine; for he became useless; but again
he became genuine so as even to be called apostolic bowels. To Titus, my
genuine child according to a common faith. What is, According to a common
faith? Since he said, Child, and took the rank of his father, how he also
lessens and brings down this honor,


hear. For he added, According to a common faith; that is, According to faith I
have nothing more than you; for it is common, and through the same I too was
born and you. From where then does he call him child? Either wishing to show
the affection only, or that he was earlier in the preaching, or that he was
enlightened through him. For this reason he also calls them children and
brothers; since they were born from the same faith, brothers; since through
his hands, children. By saying then According to a common faith, he hinted at
the brotherhood. Grace and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ
our Savior. Since he said, Child, he added, From God the Father, so as to
raise up his mind, and that he might learn whose child he is, and that not
only by saying, Common faith, but also by adding, Our Father, he shows his
equal honor. 3. And see how the things he prays for the disciples and the
many, these he also prays for the teacher; for he himself likewise needs such
prayers, and much more than they, inasmuch as he has more enmities, inasmuch
as he has more necessities of offending against God. For as great as the
dignity is, so much greater also are the dangers for the one who has the
priesthood; for even one success of the episcopate is enough to carry one up
to heaven, and one sin to cast one into =62.668= gehenna itself. For to pass
over all the other things that fall out each day, if it should ever happen
that he, either through friendship or through some other cause, has brought
forward an unworthy man into the episcopate, and has entrusted him with the
rule of a great city, see of how much fire he makes himself liable. For not
only of the souls that perish (for he destroys them being irreverent), but
also of all things done by that man he himself will give the accounts. For the
one who is irreverent in the rank of a private man, much more when he lays
hold of the rule; for it is a thing to be desired that the reverent man remain
such after laying hold of the rule. For then vainglory attacks more
vehemently, and love of money, and arrogance, the rule furnishing the power,
and offenses and insults and revilings and ten thousand other things. If then
someone is irreverent, he will be more irreverent having become such. When
then he sets up such a ruler, he will be accountable for all the sins
committed by that man, and for whole peoples. And if for the one who causes
one soul to stumble, it is better for him that a millstone of an ass be hung
about his neck, and he be drowned in the depth of the sea, the one who causes
so many souls to stumble, whole cities and peoples and ten thousand souls,
men, women, children, citizens, farmers, those in the city itself, those in
the other cities under that city, what will he undergo? For even if you should
name another punishment three times as great, you will say nothing; he will be
liable to so great a punishment and vengeance. So that this man especially
needs the grace of God and the peace; for if he does not govern the people
with this, all is gone and lost, the rudders not being his. For even if he is
experienced in the art of steering, if he does not have these rudders, the
grace and the peace from God, he will sink the ship and those sailing in it.
Whence it comes upon me to wonder at those who desire so great a burden.
Wretched and miserable man, do you not see what you desire? If you are by
yourself and unknown and undistinguished, even if you sin ten thousand times,
you will give account of one soul, and you will undergo accounts of this only;
but when you are brought to this rule, consider of how many heads you are
liable to the punishment. For hear Paul saying: Obey those who lead you and
submit, for they watch for your souls, as those who will give account. But you
desire honor and rule; And what is the pleasure of this honor? for I do not
even see this; for it is not possible to be truly a ruler. How? Because it
lies in the power of those ruled to obey. And if one should examine the matter
accurately, such a one does not come to rule,


but is a slave to ten thousand masters who desire and say contrary things. For
what one praises, another blames; what this man censures, another admires.
Whom then must one hear, whom obey? There is no one. And the bought slave,
even if the master commands contrary things to him, is also displeased; but
you, if you are pained when so many masters command contrary things, you also
pay the penalty for this, opening the mouths of all against you. Is this then,
tell me, honor? is this rule? is this power? =62.669= 4. The one who has the
episcopate said that money must be brought in; if he does not wish, not only
did he not bring it in, but also, that he may not seem to do this on account
of indolence, he accuses the one who commanded; he steals, he says, he seizes,
he swallows the things of the poor, he eats up the things of the beggars. Stop
reviling; how long do you say these things? You do not wish to bring it in? no
one is compelling, no one is forcing; why do you also revile the one who
exhorts and advises? But someone came into need, and he did not stretch out a
hand, not being able, or being occupied elsewhere. There is no pardon, but
again accusations worse than the former. Is this then rule? And he cannot
defend himself; for they are his own bowels. And as the bowel, even if it
swells, even if it causes pain both to the head and to the rest of the body,
we do not dare to avenge ourselves; for we do not take a sword and burst it;
so even if one of those ruled is such, causing us pains and despondency from
these accusations, we do not dare to avenge ourselves; for these things are
far from a father's mind, but we must bear the pain, until that one is well
healed. The bought servant has some work assigned, and if he accomplishes
this, thereafter he becomes his own master; but this man is dragged about
everywhere, and many things even beyond his power are demanded; if he does not
know how to speak, there is much murmuring; but if he does know how to speak,
again accusations, He is vainglorious; if he does not raise the dead, He is
worthy of no account, he says; so-and-so is reverent, but this man is not. If
he enjoys moderate food, again accusations; He ought to have been hanged, he
says; if someone sees him bathing, many accusations; He ought not even to see
the sun at all, he says. And if he does the same things that I do, and bathes
and eats and drinks and is clothed with garments, and cares for houses and
servants, for what reason does he stand over me? But he also has servants, he
says, who minister to him, and he rides on an ass; for what reason then does
he stand over me? But what, tell me, ought he not to have the one who
ministers, but himself to kindle the fire, and carry water, and split wood,
and go into the market? and how much shame does this become? And those holy
men, the apostles, do not wish the one who attends to the word even to attend
to the ministry of widows, but they think the matter unworthy of him; and do
you drag him down even to the service of your own servants? Why do you who
command these things not come forward yourself and fulfill them? tell me, does
he not minister a greater service to you than you the bodily things? why do
you not send your own boy to his service? Christ washed the feet of the
disciples; if you should furnish attendance to the teacher, do you do some
great thing? But you neither wish to furnish it, and you hinder him. What
then? ought he to live from heaven? But God does not wish it so. What then?
did the apostles, he says, have free men =62.670= serving? Do you wish to hear
this too, how the apostles lived? they made journeys abroad, and free men and
noble women laid down both their souls and their heads for their rest. And
hear also this blessed man exhorting and saying: Hold such in honor; and
again, For the work of God he came near to death, having hazarded his soul,
that he might fill up your lack of service toward me. Do you see what he says?
But you do not even put forth a word for your father, much less do you take on
so great a danger. But to bathe, he says, is not allowed. Why, tell me? where
has this


been forbidden? for neither is filth a good thing. Nowhere do we see these
things, either accused or admired. For other things are what he commanded the
bishop to have, to be blameless, sober, orderly, given to hospitality, apt to
teach; these the Apostle requires, these one must seek from the ruler, and
nothing further. You are not more exact than Paul, or rather you are not more
exact than the Spirit. If he is a striker, or even given to wine, if he is
cruel and unmerciful, accuse; these things are unworthy of the bishop; if he
lives in luxury, this too is an accusation; but if he tends the body that he
may minister to you, if he takes care that he may be useful to you, must he be
accused for these things? Do you not know that bodily weakness no less than
weakness of soul harms both us and the Church? Why does Paul correct it
writing to Timothy, Use a little wine for your stomach's sake and your
frequent infirmities? for if we accomplished virtue by the soul alone, there
would be no need to care for the body. And why were we even made at all? But
if its contribution is great, how is it not the last folly to neglect it? For
let there be a man honored with the episcopate and entrusted with the
presidency of the common of the Church, and let him be in other things
virtuous and having all that it befits the consecrated man to have, but let
him be bound continually to the bed by much sickness, what will this man be
able to benefit? what journey abroad to undertake? what visitations to make?
whom to rebuke? whom to admonish? I said these things, that you may know not
to accuse simply, that you may know rather to accept, that even if someone
desires the rule, seeing the snowstorms of accusations, he may quench such a
desire. Truly great is the danger and in need of the grace of God and of the
peace, which you pray to be much for us, and we for you; that both having
accomplished virtue, we may thus obtain the promised good things, in Jesus
Christ, with whom to the Father together with the Holy Spirit be glory, power,
honor, now and always, and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.

HOMILY II. For this cause I left you in Crete, that you might set in order the
things that are wanting, and appoint elders in every city, as I directed you;
if anyone is blameless, the husband of one wife, having faithful children, not
in accusation of riot, or unruly. 1. For the men of old the whole of life was
ac =62.670= tive and full of contest; but for us it is not so, but full of
indolence. For they knew that they were brought into the world for this, that
they might work according to the mind of him who brought them in; but we, as
if brought in for this, that we may eat and drink and live in luxury, thus
make no account of any of the spiritual things. I do not speak of the apostles
only, but =62.671= also of those after them. You see them then running through
all things, and as if they had taken hold of this as some work, and spending
their time wholly in a strange land, inasmuch as they have no city upon the
earth. Hear then what this blessed man says: For this cause I left you in
Crete. As if they had divided the inhabited world as one house, so they
ministered all things, and took care of those everywhere, one taking this
part, another that. For this cause, he says, I left you in Crete, that you
might set in order the things that are wanting. He does not yet exhort him in
a commanding way; That you might set in order, he says. Do you see a soul
clean of all envy, seeking everywhere the profit of those being taught, not
being exact whether it come about through himself or through another? For
where there was danger and much difficulty, he himself


set it right being present; but the things that rather brought honor or had
praise, these he commits to the disciple, I mean the ordinations of the
bishops, and all the other things that needed some setting in order, or, as
one might say, more complete furnishing. What do you say, tell me? he further
sets right your things? and you do not think the matter shameful nor that it
brings you shame? Not at all; for I look only to the common gain; and whether
it come about through me or through another, it makes no difference to me.
Thus the president must be disposed, not seeking his own honor, but the common
advantage. And appoint, he says, elders in every city. He means the bishops
here, as has been said to us elsewhere. As I directed you; if anyone is
blameless. In every city, he says; for he did not wish the whole island to be
entrusted to one, but each to have his own care and concern; for thus the toil
would be light for him, and those ruled would enjoy more care, if the teacher
did not go about for the presidency of many Churches, but had leisure for one
only, and adorned this. If anyone, he says, is blameless, the husband of one
wife, having faithful children, not in accusation of riot, or unruly. For what
reason does he also bring such a one forward? He stops the mouths of the
heretics who slander marriage, showing that the thing is not unholy, but so
honorable that with it one can even ascend the holy throne; and at the same
time also chastising the unchaste, and not allowing this rule to be entrusted
after a second marriage. For he who kept no goodwill toward the one who has
departed, how would this man become a good president? and what accusation
would he not undergo? For you all know, you know that even if it is not
forbidden by the laws to come together in second marriages, still the thing
has many accusations. He wishes then the ruler to furnish no handle to those
ruled. For this reason he sets down, If anyone is blameless, that is, if his
life has become free of accusation, if no one had anything to lay against him
in his life. Hear Christ saying: For if the light, he says, that is in you is
darkness, how great is the darkness? Having faithful children, not in
accusation of riot, or unruly. Let us consider how much provision he makes
even for the children. For he who could not become a teacher of his own
children, how would he become one of others? If those whom he had with himself
from the beginning and =62.672= reared, and over whom he had authority both
from the laws and from nature, he was not able to train, how will he be able
to benefit those outside? For unless there were much sluggishness of a father,
he would not have overlooked those over whom he received authority from the
beginning becoming bad. For it is not, it is not possible, for one reared from
the beginning with much care to become bad, and having enjoyed much diligence;
for sins are not by nature, that they should overcome so much provision. And
if he set the things of the children in the second place, occupying himself
about money, and did not make so much diligence over them, even so he is
unworthy. For if, where nature compelled, he was so without affection, or so
foolish, that he cared more for money than for them, how would this man be
brought up to the throne and so great a rule? For if he was not able, the
accusation of sluggishness is great; but if he did not take pains, the blame
of want of affection is great. He then who neglects his own children, how will
he take care of those of others? And he did not say simply not riotous, but
not even to have such a slander, nor to be of an evil reputation. For the
bishop, he says, must be blameless as God's steward, not self-willed, not soon
angry, not given to wine, not a striker. 2. For the ruler from outside, since
he rules by law and necessity, reasonably does not in many places share the
mind of those ruled; but he who ought to rule the willing, and those who know
him gratitude for the rule, if he so arranges the matter as from his own


mind only to do all things, and shares speech with no one, has made the
superintendence more tyrannical than popular. For he must, he says, be
blameless as God's steward, not self-willed, not soon angry. For how will he
teach others to master this passion, not having taught himself? For the rule
leads out into many things, and makes even the very reasonable man more harsh
and hard to please, setting around him ten thousand necessities of anger; if
he has not practiced this beforehand, he will both be harsher, and will
destroy and lose many of the things of the rule. Not given to wine, not a
striker. He means here the insolent man; for in admonishing or rebuking one
must do all things, not insulting; for what necessity of insult is there, tell
me? One must frighten with the threat of gehenna, terrify, strike home. For
the one insulted becomes bolder and more despises the one insulting. For
nothing so produces contempt as insult, and it shames the one insulting more,
and does not allow him to be reverend as he ought. Their speech must be full
of much reverence, in sins have remembrance of the coming judgment, and be
clean of all insult; but if there should be some who hinder the things that
must be done, one must pursue the matter with all authority. Not a striker, he
says. The teacher is a physician of souls; and the physician does not strike,
but sets right and heals the one who has been struck. Not greedy of filthy
lucre, but given to hospitality, a lover of good, sober, just, holy,
temperate, holding to the faithful word according to the teaching. Did you see
how much intensity of virtue he required? Not greedy of filthy lucre, he says;
that is, displaying much contempt of money; Given to hospitality, a lover of
good, sober, just, holy; putting forth all his own things, he says, to those
in need; temperate. He did not =62.673= say here the faster, but the one who
masters passion, the one of tongue and hand and unbridled eyes; for this is
temperance, to be dragged by no passion. Holding to the faithful word
according to the teaching. Faithful here he says of the true, or of that
delivered through faith, not needing syllogisms nor inquiries. Holding to,
taking care of, making this his work. What then, if he is inexperienced in the
words from outside? For this reason I said, The faithful word according to
teaching. That he may be able both to exhort, and to convict those who
contradict. So that there is no need of pomp of words, but of mind, and
experience of the Scriptures, and power of thoughts. Do you not see Paul
turning the whole inhabited world, and prevailing more even than Plato, and
all the others? But from the signs, he says. Not from the signs only; for if
you go through the Acts of the Apostles, you will find him in many places
prevailing from the teaching even before the signs. That he may be able also
to exhort in the sound teaching; that is, toward the guarding of his own,
toward the overturning of the enemies. And to convict those who contradict.
For if this is not, all is gone. For he who does not know how to fight the
enemies, and to take captive every thought unto the obedience of Christ, and
to cast down reasonings; he who does not know what one must teach concerning
the right teaching, let him be far from a teacher's throne. For the other
things one might find even among those ruled, as the being blameless, the
having children in subjection, the being given to hospitality, the just, the
holy; but what especially characterizes the teacher, this is it, to be able to
catechize the word, of which there is now no provision. For there are many
also unruly, vain talkers, and deceivers, especially those of the
circumcision; whose mouths must be stopped. Do you see how he shows from where
they are such? From not wishing to be ruled, but to rule; for this he hinted.
When then you cannot persuade, do not arrange things for these, but stop their
mouths for the benefit of the others. What then is the gain, when they are not
persuaded, or even if they are unruly? For what reason must one stop their
mouths? That others may be benefited from this. Who overturn whole houses,
teaching things which they ought not,


for the sake of filthy lucre. For if he has undertaken the teaching, but is
not sufficient to fight those men nor to stop their mouths when they are so
shameless, he will become cause of the harm of those who perish, each one. For
if someone exhorts saying, Do not seek to become a judge, if you are not able
to take away injustice, much more here one might say, Do not seek to become a
teacher, if you fall short of the worth of the matter, but even if dragged,
leap away. Do you see everywhere that love of money, filthy lucre, is the
cause of these things? Teaching, he says, things which they ought not, for the
sake of filthy lucre. 3. For there is nothing that these passions do not ruin;
but as some wild winds falling upon a calm sea, lever the whole of it from
below, so that even the sand is mixed with the waves; so these passions
entering into a soul, make all things up and =62.674= down, they blind the
seeing part of the mind, and most of all the madness for glory. For of money
it is easy for the one who wishes to despise; but to overlook the honor from
the many, there is need of much toil, of great philosophy, of some angelic
soul that touches the very peak of heaven. For there is not, there is not
anything so tyrannical a passion and prevailing everywhere, from a greater and
a lesser portion, but everywhere nevertheless. How then might we get the
better of it, even if not by force, yet at least from the smallest part? If we
look up to heaven, if we have God before our eyes, if we take a
thought greater than earthly things. Consider, when you desire glory, that you
have obtained it, and learn the end, and you will find nothing; consider how
great a loss the thing has, of how many and how great goods it deprives; for
you will undergo the toils and the dangers, but you will be deprived of the
fruits and the prizes; consider that the most are bad, and despise their
glory. Reckon up each one, who he is, and you will see the thing full of
laughter; you will see that the thing is shame rather than glory; and after
these things lead the thought up to the theater above. When doing some good
you consider that one must also show it to men, and you seek some spectators
of the deed, and you travail so as to be seen; consider that God sees, and you
quench all that desire; withdraw from the earth, look toward that theater in
the heavens. Men, even if they praise, afterwards blamed, but they envy, but
they take hold; and even if they do not do this, they will in no way benefit
the one praised; but in the case of God it is not so, but he rejoices praising
our successes. Did you speak rightly, and obtain applause? and what is the
gain? For if those who applauded were benefited and changed and became better,
and stood off from their former evils, one must truly rejoice, not at the
praises that came about, but at the good and wonderful change. But if they
continue always praising and making a tumult and applauding, but reap nothing
from the applause, one must rather grieve, that these things become a judgment
for them. But you have a reputation for reverence? If you are truly reverent,
and are conscious of nothing evil in yourself, one must rejoice, not because
you seem, but because you are; but if not being so, you desire the glory of
the many, consider that these will not judge us on that day, but he who knows
the hidden things with accuracy. And if being conscious of sins in yourself,
you are suspected to be clean among all, not only must one not rejoice, but
even grieve and groan bitterly, considering continually the day on which all
things are unveiled, on which the hidden things of darkness are lighted. Do
you enjoy honor? beat this off, knowing that it makes you a debtor. No one
furnishes you honor? and one must rejoice at this; for God will also set this
before you with the other things, that you enjoyed honor. Or do you not see
how, with the other benefits, God also accuses this through the


prophet: I took from your sons for prophets, and from your young men for
sanctification? Even this then you will gain, that you will not be liable to a
greater punishment. For the one not honored in the present life, and despised
and enjoying no account, but =62.675= even insulted and spat upon, even if
nothing else, at least reaps this, not to be liable also for this, of having
been honored by fellow-servants. And through many other things too he gains
from this; he is contracted, he is humbled, nor, if he wishes, will he ever
think great things, if he rather attends to himself. But the one who enjoys
much honor, besides being liable to great debts, is also lifted into madness
and into vainglory, and becomes a slave of men. Then as this mastery
increases, he is compelled to do many things which he does not wish. 4.
Knowing then that this is better for us than that, let us neither seek honors,
and let us beat off those furnished, let us throw them from ourselves, let us
quench this desire. These things have been said by us both to rulers and to
those ruled. For a soul desiring honor and to be glorified will not see the
kingdom of the heavens. This word is not mine, nor do I speak words of my own,
but of the divine Spirit; it will not see, even if it works virtue. For they
have, he says, their reward. He then who does not receive a reward, how will
he see the kingdom of the heavens? I do not hinder desiring glory, but I wish
for the true glory that is from God; Whose praise, he says, is not from men,
but from God. Let us be reverent in secret, not wearing much conceit and a
stage and hypocrisy; let us throw off the skin of the sheep, or rather let us
become sheep; nothing is more worthless than the glory of men. For tell me, if
you see a multitude of small children, I mean those at the breast, do you
desire the glory from them? Be so disposed toward all men on account of glory.
For this reason it is called vainglory. Do you not see the masks which those
on the stage put on? how beautiful, how splendid, how molded to the last
accuracy of good form? can you show me such a face in truth? Not at all. What
then? were you ever in love with them? No; why? because they are empty, and
imitate beauty, but are not beauty. So also glory is empty and imitates glory,
but is not glory. That alone remains which is natural, the inward; but this
which is laid on from outside often hides the ugliness, hides it among men,
and until evening; but when the theater is dissolved and the masks =62.676=
are taken off, each appears that which he is. Let us not then pursue the truth
as on a stage and in hypocrisy. For tell me, what good does being gazed at by
the many have? it is vainglory, and nothing else; since go up into the house
and become alone, and immediately the whole has flowed away. Did you go into
the marketplace? did you turn those present? and what more? Nothing; it was
quenched and went away as smoke dissolved. Do we then love things so without
substance? and of how much irrationality are these things, tell me? of how
much folly? Let us look then to one thing only, how God will praise, if we
consider this we shall never seek the things from men, but even if it comes
about, we shall despise, we shall laugh, we shall spit upon it; as if desiring
gold, but receiving clay, so we shall be disposed. Let not so-and-so praise
you; for he benefited nothing; and if he blames, he harmed nothing. But in the
case of God both these have some gain and loss; but the things from men are
all vain. And in this we are equaled to God, that he does not need the glory
from men; For glory, he says, from men I do not receive. Is this then a small
thing, tell me? When you do not wish to despise glory, say that I become equal
to God having despised it, and immediately you will despise it. For it is not
possible for a man who is a slave of glory not to be a slave of all, and


more slavish than the slaves themselves. For we do not command such things to
our servants as that does to those caught by it; it makes them both speak and
suffer things shameful and full of shame; and especially when it sees them
obeying, it rather intensifies the commands. Let us flee then, let us flee, I
entreat, this slavery. And how, he says, shall we be able? If we philosophize
about the things here, if we see that the present things are a dream and a
shadow, and nothing else, we shall easily get the better of it, and we shall
be caught neither in the small things nor in the great; but if we do not
despise in the small, we shall easily fall also in the greatest. From far then
let us check its springs; and these are folly and the possessing a low soul;
so that if we take a high mind, we shall be able both to overlook the honor
from the many, and to stretch the mind toward heaven, and to obtain the good
things there; which may we all obtain by the grace and lovingkindness of
Christ, with whom to the Father together with the Holy Spirit be glory, power,
honor, now and always, and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.

HOMILY III. One of them, a prophet of their own, said: Cretans are always
liars, evil beasts, idle bellies. This testimony is true. For which cause
rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith, not giving heed to
Jewish myths and commandments of men who turn away from the truth. 1. There
are many things inquired into here, first, who it was who said it; second, why
ever Paul used them; third, that he brought forward a testimony that is not
even correct. Come then, adding also other things, let us thus bring on the
sea =62.676= sonable solution. For even when he was discoursing with the
Athenians, in the midst of the speech he says, To an unknown God; and again,
For we also are his offspring, as certain also of your own poets have said. It
is Epimenides then who said it, being himself also a Cretan; but from what he
was moved, it is necessary to tell the story to you; and it is thus: The
Cretans have a tomb of Zeus inscribed with this: Here lies Zan, whom they call
Zeus. On account of this inscription then the poet, mocking the Cretans as
liars =62.677=, going on again adds, increasing the mockery rather: For even a
tomb, O lord, the Cretans fashioned for you; but you did not die; for you are
forever. If then this testimony is true, see how great the danger is. For if
the poet is true in saying that they lied, having said that Zeus had died, as
the apostle says, the danger is great. Attend, Beloved, with much accuracy;
The poet said that the Cretans lie who said that Zeus had died; the Apostle
confirmed his testimony. Therefore according to the Apostle Zeus is immortal;
For this, he says, testimony is true. What then shall we say? or rather how is
it possible to solve this? The Apostle did not say this, but simply thus he
took the testimony and artlessly toward their lying character. For why did he
not add, For even a tomb, O lord, the Cretans fashioned for you? So that the
Apostle did not say this, but that so-and-so said well, that the Cretans are
liars. And not from this only do we maintain that Zeus is not a god; for it is
possible also from elsewhere, constructing it from many places, and not from
the testimony of the Cretans, to show this clearly. And otherwise he did not
say they lie in this; or rather it is likely they lied in this too; for they
also thought others


gods. For this reason the Apostle called them liars. But the thing inquired
into is, why ever he brings the testimonies from the Greek things. Because we
especially put them to shame when we bring the testimonies and the accusations
from home, when we set over them those admired among them accusing them. For
this reason he has also used it elsewhere saying, To an unknown God. For the
Athenians, since they did not receive all the gods from the beginning, but
according to times, and some others, as the things from the Hyperboreans, as
those of Pan, as the small, as the great mysteries they brought on later,
these conjecturing from these things that it is likely there is also another
god, and that he has been unknown by them, that they may be well devoted also
about that one, set up an altar to this one inscribing, To an unknown God, all
but declaring this, even if there should be some unknown god. He said then,
that Whom you, taking him beforehand, have known, this one I announce to you.
And, For we also are his offspring, has been said by Aratus concerning Zeus;
who since beginning he said, Full are the streets of Zeus, full also the sea,
then added, For we also are his offspring, showing I think that one too that
we have come to be from God. How then did Paul draw the things said about Zeus
to the God of all? He did not draw the things said about Zeus to God, but the
things that belong to God, and were not genuinely nor properly laid upon Zeus,
these he restores to God; since even the name god is of him alone, and is laid
unlawfully upon the idols. But from where ought one to have discoursed with
them? from the pro =62.678= phets? But they would not have believed; since
even to Jews he utters nothing from the Gospels, but from the prophets; for
this reason he says, I became to the Jews as a Jew, to those without law as
without law, to those under law as under law. 2. This God also does; as in the
case of the magi, he does not lead them through an angel, not through a
prophet, not through an apostle, not through an evangelist, but from where?
Through a star; for since they had their art about these things, from there he
drew them. Again in the case of the cows upon the ark, If they go, he says,
this way, the indignation of God is true, as the diviners suggested. Do the
diviners then speak truth? Away with it; but from their own mouths he convicts
them and strikes them with amazement. Again in the case of the ventriloquist;
for since he believed this woman, through this woman he made Saul hear the
things that were going to overtake him. For what reason then did Paul stop the
mouth of the demon who said, These men are servants of the most high God, who
announce to us a way of salvation? and for what reason does Christ also hinder
the demons from speaking? There it was reasonable; for the signs were going
forward; since here too it was not a star, but he was preaching himself; and
the demons were not being worshipped. For it was not an idol that was
speaking, that it might be hindered. And Balaam too he allowed to bless, and
did not hinder. Thus everywhere he condescends. And why do you wonder at this?
He himself allows evil opinions about himself and unworthy of himself to
stand, as that he was a body formerly, that he is visible; toward this then he
says, God is spirit. Again that he rejoices in sacrifices, which was foreign
to him, and he utters words discordant from his confession; and as many such
things. For nowhere does he look to his own dignity, but everywhere to what is
useful for us. For if a father does not look to his own dignity, but lisps
with the little children, and calling food and meats and drinks not by Greek
names, but by some childish and barbarian speech, much more God. And he
reproaches condescendingly through the prophet, saying: If the nations will
change their gods; and everywhere what is in the Scriptures is condescension,
both words and deeds. For which cause, he says, rebuke them sharply, that they
may be sound in the faith. For this reason he says it, since their character
is bold and crafty and unbridled.


They themselves have these ten thousand evils. And when they also lie readily,
and are crafty and gluttonous and idle, there is need of a vehement and
striking word; for such a one would not be led by gentleness. Rebuke them
then. Here he does not mean those outside, but those of his own. Sharply.
Give, he says, the blow deeper. For one must not deal with all in one way, but
differently and variously, and toward the things at hand. Nowhere does he
exhort here. For as he who strikes the reasonable and noble man kills and
destroys; so he who flatters the one who needs vehemence corrupts and does not
=62.679= allow him to rise up. That they may be sound, he says, in the faith.
This then is health, to bring in nothing spurious nor foreign. And if those
who observe foods are not sound, but are sick and weak (For the weak, he says,
in the faith receive, not unto judgments of reasonings); what would one say
about those who fast the same things with them, about those who sabbatize,
about those who go away to places dedicated to those? I mean the one in
Daphne, the so-called cave of Matrona, the place in Cilicia called of Cronos.
And how are these sound? Wherefore there is need of a more vehement blow for
them. Why then does he not do the same in the case of the Romans? Because
their manners were not such, but they happened to be more noble. Not giving
heed, he says, to Jewish myths. The Jewish things are a myth in a double way,
both because it is a falsification, and because the matter is out of season;
for it becomes a myth thereafter. For when it ought not to come about, and
having come about it harms, it is a myth, as it is useless. As then one must
not obey those, so neither these; for it is not to be sound. For if you
believe the faith, why do you bring in other things, as if the faith were not
sufficient to justify? why do you enslave yourself and submit to the law? do
you not have confidence in the matter? This is of one who is sick and
unbelieving; for it belongs to a faithful mind not to doubt; but such a one
doubts. All things, he says, are pure to the pure. Do you see toward what the
thing said is? But to the defiled, he says, and unbelieving nothing is pure.
3. Not then according to their own nature are they pure or impure, but
according to the choice of those who partake. But their mind, he says, and
their conscience are defiled. They confess to know God, but in the works they
deny him, being abominable and disobedient and unto every good work reprobate.
Therefore even the sow is pure; why then was it forbidden as impure? It was
not impure by nature; for all things are pure; since nothing is more impure
than a fish, where it even tastes of human bodies; but it was permitted, and
seemed to be pure. Again nothing is more impure than a bird; for it eats
worms; nothing than a deer; for they say it is even called elaphos for this,
because it eats snakes; but all these were eaten. For what reason then did he
forbid the sow and some other such things? Not as impure, but cutting off the
excess of luxury. But if he had said this, he would not have persuaded; but
now by the fear of impurity he held them. For what, tell me, is more impure
than wine, if one must examine these things? and what than water, tell me,
with which they were especially purified? they did not touch the dead, and
they were consecrated by a dead thing; for what is slaughtered =62.680= is
dead, and by this they were purified. Thus the teaching was of children. And
consider: Does not wine have its constitution from dung? for as the vine draws
the moisture from the earth, so also from the dung laid beside it. And in
general, if we should wish to be exact, all things are impure. But nothing, if
we should wish not to be exact, is impure, but all things are pure; God made
nothing impure; for nothing is impure, unless sin only; for it touches the
soul, and soils this. And this is a human prejudgment. But to the defiled, he
says, and unbelieving nothing is pure, but their mind and their conscience are
defiled. For how among the pure


would you be impure? He who has a weak soul soils all things. And if such a
reasoning is sown seeking the pure and the impure, he will touch nothing; for
not even these are pure, I mean fish, and the others according to their
reasoning (Their mind, he says, and their conscience are defiled), but all
things are defiled. But he did not say thus, but what? He turned the whole
upon them. For nothing is impure, he says, but they themselves, both their
mind and their conscience; nothing is more impure than these. They confess to
know God, but in the works they deny him, being abominable and disobedient,
and unto every good work reprobate. But you speak the things that befit the
sound teaching. This is impurity; they themselves are impure; but do not be
silent on account of these. Even if they do not receive, he says, you do your
own things; even if they are not persuaded, you exhort and advise. Here he
slanders them more. For even the mad think that nothing stands; but this does
not come about from the things seen, but from the eyes that see. Since they
are unstable and dizzy, they think the earth is turning around for them; but
it is not turning, but stands fixed; for it is the madness of their passion,
not the passion of the element. So also here; when a soul is impure, it thinks
all things impure. It is not then observing that belongs to purity, but to
purity belongs daring all things; for he who is pure by nature dares all
things, but those who are soiled, nothing. This one can say also toward
Marcion. Do you see that he sets down as a token of purity to be above all
filth, and to touch nothing, of impurity? So also in the case of God; that he
took on flesh, this belongs to purity; but if fearing he did not take it on,
of filth. He who does not eat the things that seem to be impure, this man is
impure and weak; but he who eats, no longer. Let us not then call such men
pure; these are the impure; but he who dares all things is pure. This
reverence one must display upon the things that soil the soul; for that is
impurity, that is filth; but this is none of these. Since even those who have
a corrupted mouth think the things brought to them are impure, and this is of
the passion. One must then know strongly the nature both of the pure and of
the impure. =62.681= What then is impure? Sin, vice, covetousness, wickedness.
Wash, he says, become clean, take away your wickednesses from your souls.
Create in me a clean heart, O God. Come out from the midst of them, and be
separated, he says, and touch no unclean thing; since those observances too
were symbols of purifications. Of a dead man, he says, do not touch. For such
is sin, dead and stinking. The leper, he says, is unclean. For sin too is
various and of many forms. And that he hints at this is clear from what
follows. For if the leprosy is throughout and over the whole body, he is
clean; but if in part, no longer. Do you see that the various and changed is
the unclean? Again the
one with a flux is unclean in the soul. Reckon the one with a flux, the one
casting away the seeds. He who has not been circumcised again is unclean. Do
you see how these are not allegory, but types? He who has not stripped off, he
says, the vice of the soul. He who works on the sabbath is stoned; that is, he
who is not continually devoted to God, this man perishes. Did you see how many
manners of impurities? She from childbirth, he says, is unclean. Why, tell me?
Did he not himself make seed and birth? for what reason then is the woman
unclean? unless he was hinting at some other thing. And what is this? He
planted reverence in the soul, he led fornication far away. For if she who has
given birth is unclean, much more she who fornicates; if to approach one's own
wife is not very clean, to mix with another's is much more. He from mourning,
he says, is unclean; much more he from murder and war. And one might find many
manners of impurities, if one must collect them all. But we are not now
required these things,


but the whole has been transferred to the soul. For the bodily things are
nearer to us; for this reason he led in from this; but not now; for one ought
not to sit beside the types, nor to attend upon the shadows, but to hold to
the truth, and to lay hold of this. Sin is unclean; let us flee this, let us
abstain from this; If you approach, he says toward =62.682= it, it will
receive you. Nothing is more unclean than covetousness. From where is this
clear? From the things themselves. For what does it not defile? hands, soul,
the house itself, where the things seized are stored. Toward the Jews then
this seems to be nothing. And yet Moses carried the bones of Joseph, and
Samson drank from the jawbone of an ass, and ate honey from a lion, and Elijah
was fed from ravens, and from a widow woman. And what, tell me, if one must be
exact on account of these things, were not the skins of their books more
unholy than all? for they are from dead animals. Not then the fornicator only
is unclean, but also others more than this, and the adulterer is unclean; and
this man and that are unclean, not through the mixing; since according to this
word even he who approaches his own wife is unclean; but through the injustice
and the covetousness, because he defrauded the brother in the most necessary
things. Do you see that vice is unclean? And he who had two wives was not
unclean; and David having many was not unclean; but when he had one
unlawfully, he became unclean. Why? Because he did injustice, because he was
covetous. And the fornicator too is not unclean for this, through the mixing,
but through the manner, because he wrongs the woman, and they wrong one
another making the woman common, and overturning the laws of nature; for she
ought to be of one. For male, he says, and female he made them; and he said,
The two shall be one flesh; not the many, but The two one flesh. And here then
there is injustice, and for this reason the thing is wicked. Anger again when
it exceeds the measures makes the man unclean, not through the wrath, but
through the manner; since this too is added, He who is angry, not simply, but,
without cause. So that everywhere the desiring more is unclean; for the thing
is born from insatiability and the unsatisfied. Let us then be sober, I
entreat, let us become clean with the true purity, that we may be counted
worthy to see God, in Christ Jesus our Lord, with whom to the Father together
with the Holy Spirit be glory, power, honor, now and always, and unto the ages
of the ages. Amen.

HOMILY IV. That aged men be sober, grave, temperate, sound in the faith, in
love, in patience; aged women likewise in demeanor as becomes holiness, not
false accusers, not enslaved to much wine, teachers of good things, that they
may school the young women to be lovers of their husbands, lovers of their
children, temperate, chaste, keepers at home, good, being subject to their own
husbands, that the word of God be not blasphemed. 1. There are things that old
age too has as defects, and not youth; and it has some even of those of youth,
but it has nevertheless also the sluggish, the hesitant, the forgetful,
=62.682= the dull, the quick to anger. For this reason charging about these he
says: That aged men be sober. For there are many things that make those in
such an age not sober; and first that which I said, the being dulled from
every side, and being raised with difficulty, and being moved with difficulty;
wherefore he also adds, Grave, temperate. Here the prudent


he means; for this is called sophrosyne, the salvation of the mind. For there
are, there are even among those who have grown old men who rage, out of their
mind, some from wine, some from grief; for old age makes them small-souled.
Sound in the faith, in love, in patience. And he has said well, In patience;
for this too especially befits the old; Aged women likewise in demeanor as
becomes holiness, that is, from the very =62.683= habit and the dress showing
the orderliness. Not false accusers, not enslaved to much wine. For this
especially is a defect of women and of old age; for by the ages having been
chilled much desire of this also comes about. Whence also about this
especially he makes the exhortation toward them, cutting out drunkenness from
every side, and wishing them to be outside this disease, and to escape the
laughter from it. For both the vapors from below are more easily brought up,
and the membranes receive the harm by their having been aged by time; and from
this drunkenness especially comes about. There is need then of wine for this
age especially, for it is weak; but not of much, as neither for the young
women, not for the same cause, but because the flame of desire is kindled very
much. Teachers of good things. And yet you hinder women from teaching; how
then do you permit it here, saying elsewhere, But I do not permit a woman to
teach? But hear what he added, Nor to have authority over a man. For to men it
has been permitted from above to teach both men and women; but to women he
permits the hortatory word at home, but nowhere does he allow them to sit in
front, nor does he let them stretch out a long speech. For this reason he
added, Nor to have authority over a man. That they may school, he says, the
young women. 2. Do you see how he weaves together and gathers the people? how
he subjects the younger to those who have grown old? For he is not discoursing
here about daughters, but simply from the age. Let each, he says, older woman
school the younger. To be lovers of their husbands. This is the head of the
good things in the house; For a woman, he says, going about with a husband.
For if this is, none of the unpleasant things will happen. For how, the head
having inclined together toward the body, and there being no division there,
will not all the rest be at peace? for if the rulers are in peace, who is the
one dividing and cutting the peace? as again if these are badly disposed,
nothing will be sound in the house. Nothing then is greater than this; and
than money and nobility and power and all the other things this is more
profitable. He did not say simply, to be in peace, but, to love the husbands.
For when there is love, none of the difficult things will have entrance; for
from this the rest of the good things also are born. Lovers of their children,
he says. Well; for she who loves the root, much more also the fruits.
Temperate, chaste, keepers at home, good. All things are born from that; for
they become both good and keepers at home from the love and the affection
about the husband. Being subject to their own husbands, that the word of God
be not blasphemed; as she who despises the husband neglects the house also.
And from loving much temperance also is born, from loving all contentiousness
is taken away; and if he is a Greek, he will soon be per[su]aded; and if a
Christian, he will be better. Do you see the condescension of Paul? He who
does all things so as to make us stand off from the things of life, now makes
much care about the things in the house. For if these are well administered,
the spiritual things also will have room; but otherwise, those too are ruined.
For the keeper at =62.684= home woman will also be temperate, the keeper at
home also a good manager; she will occupy herself neither about luxury, nor
about untimely goings out, nor about any other such thing. That the word of
God, he says, be not blasphemed. Do you see that he cares principally for the
preaching, not for worldly things? For writing also to Timothy he says: That a
quiet and peaceable


life we may lead in all godliness and gravity; and here, That the word of God
and the teaching be not blasphemed. For if it should happen that a believing
woman living with an unbeliever is not virtuous, the blasphemy is accustomed
to pass over upon God; but if she is orderly, the preaching reaps the glory
from her and from the things accomplished by her. Let women hear, as many as
live with wicked or unbelieving husbands; let them hear, and be taught through
their own manners to lead them into godliness. For even if you gain nothing
else, nor draw the husband to the communion of the right doctrines, yet you
have sewn up his mouth, not allowing Christianity to be blasphemed. And this
is not small, but even very great, that the doctrine be admired from our
manner of life. The younger men likewise exhort to be temperate. See how
everywhere he exhorts to guard what is fitting. For of the teaching toward the
women he assigned the more to the women, setting those who have grown old over
the younger; but of the men he gives and assigns the whole to him. For
nothing, nothing so difficult and hard would there be for this age as to get
the better of the unfitting pleasures. For neither love of money, nor desire
of glory, nor anything else so troubles this age as the love of bodies.
Wherefore leaving all the other things, he sets the exhortation for him about
the timely thing. Then he is not indolent even on account of the others, but
what does he say? In all things showing yourself a pattern of good works. Let
then, he says, the older women also teach the younger, and you yourself also
exhort the younger men to be temperate. And let the brightness of your life be
a common school for all and an example of virtue, set forth in the midst for
all, as some archetypal image, having all the good things in itself,
furnishing with much ease to those who wish to imprint some of the good things
in it the patterns. In the teaching uncorruptness, gravity, sound speech, that
cannot be condemned, that he who is of the contrary part may be ashamed,
having nothing evil to say about us. 3. He of the contrary part he means both
the devil and everyone who ministers to that one. For when both life shines,
and speech is agreeing, reasonable, mild, gentle, furnishing no handle to
those contrary, much and unspeakable is the gain. Great then is the need of
the ministry of the word, of a word not of any chance one, but of some
approved and not to be taken hold of, and furnishing no pretext from anywhere
to those who wish it. Servants to be subject to their own masters, to be well
pleasing in all things. Do you see what he said taking it beforehand, That he
who is of the contrary part, he says, may be ashamed, having nothing evil to
say about us. Worthy of condemnation then is he who unyokes wives from
husbands on the pretext of continence, =62.685= and he who deprives masters of
servants on this same pretence. This is not the speech that cannot be
condemned; and it also gives much handle to the unbelievers, and opens the
mouths of all against us. Servants, he says, to be subject to their own
masters, to be well pleasing in all things, not contradicting, not purloining,
but showing all good fidelity, that they may adorn the teaching of God our
Savior in all things. Reasonably then he said elsewhere, As serving God, and
not men. For even if you minister to the master with goodwill, yet the pretext
has its beginning from fear. So that he who ministers to that one with so much
fear will obtain the greatest rewards. For if he does not master hand, nor an
unbridled tongue, from where will the Greek admire the doctrine among us? But
if they should see the servant who philosophizes in Christ, displaying a
continence greater than those who have philosophized among them, and
ministering with much reasonableness and goodwill, in every way he will admire
the power of the


preaching. For not from doctrine do Greeks judge doctrines, but from deeds and
life. Let then both wives and servants be teachers to them through their own
manner of life. For even among them, and everywhere this has been agreed, that
the race of servants is somehow bold, hard to mold, hard to turn, not very fit
toward the teaching of virtue, not through nature, far be it, but through the
rearing and the neglect from the masters. For since everywhere those who
master them care for nothing else but their own service; and if somewhere they
should also take care of the manners, this again they do for their own rest,
so as not to furnish them troubles either by fornicating, or stealing, or
being drunk; reasonably neglected, and having no one who is curious about
them, they are sunk into the very pits of vice. For if, where a father stands
over and a mother and a pedagogue and a nurse and a teacher and companions of
age, and the very reputation of freedom lying about, and many other things,
scarcely would one escape the companies of the wicked; what do you think those
who are empty of all these, and mixed with the foul, and associating with whom
they wish with license, there being no one curious about their friendships?
what do you think such will be? For this reason it is difficult for a servant
to become good. And otherwise they do not even enjoy teaching, neither of the
things from outside nor of those among us; they do not associate with free
men, orderly, who make much care of their own reputation. For all these
reasons it is difficult and wonderful for a useful servant ever to come about.
When then they see that the race so arrogant the power of the preaching,
having put a bridle around it, has made more orderly and more reasonable than
all, even if the masters are much more irrational than all, they will take a
great thought about the doctrines among us. For it is clear that having first
put into their soul also the fear about the resurrection and that of the
judgment and that of all the other things philosophized among us after death,
thus they were able to beat off vice, having seated some counterbalancing fear
of the pleasure from evils into their own =62.686= soul. So that not in vain
nor simply does he make much speech everywhere about these; for the worse they
are, so much the more is the strength of the preaching admired. For we also
admire a physician then, when the one given up and enjoying no help and not
able to master untimely desires, but wallowing in these, he leads to health
and sets right. And see what he requires from them; the things that most of
all rest the master; Not contradicting, not purloining; that is, to display
much goodwill in the things with which they are entrusted, to be especially
good toward the masters, yielding in the commands. 4. Do not then think that I
am now going through these things simply; for hereafter my word is toward the
servants. Do not then, my good man, look to this, that you serve a man, but
that you serve God, that you adorn the preaching; and you will undergo to do
all things, obeying the master and bearing him when he is untimely indignant
and displeased. Consider that you do not give the favor to that one, but you
fulfill a command of God, and you will undergo anything whatsoever easily. And
what I always say, this I will say now too, that when the spiritual things are
accomplished for us, the things of the present life also will follow. For such
a servant, so well-minded, so reasonable, not only will God accept and share
those splendid crowns, but also the master himself who has been treated well,
even if he is a beast, even if he is some stony and inhuman and cruel man,
will praise and admire and prefer him to all the others, and will set him over
the rest, even if he is a Greek. And that, even if the masters are Greeks, he
commands


the servant to show himself such, if you wish, I will also recount a pattern
to you: Joseph was sold to the chief cook, and was of another religion, not
the Egyptian; what then did that man? Since he saw the young man virtuous, he
did not consider the difference of religion, but he himself both loved and was
fond of him and admired, and entrusted to him the superintendence of all the
others, and of the things in the house he knew nothing because of him; but
that one was a second master, or rather even more master than the master
himself, if indeed the one was ignorant of his own things, and this one knew
the things of that one more than the master. And it seems to me even later,
that he believed the woman who made that unlawful slander against him,
honoring the just man from the former reverence and the honor, that he stopped
the anger at the prison. For if he had not very much revered the man, and
admired him from the things previously done for him, he would even immediately
have killed him, and driven the sword through that body. For jealousy, he
says, is full of a man's wrath; he will not exchange enmity for any ransom,
nor will it be dissolved by many gifts. And if the jealousy of every man is
such, much more of that one, being both an Egyptian and a barbarian, and
having been wronged, as he thought, by the one honored. For you all surely
know that the injustices from all do not bite us alike, but more vehemently
than the others and more bitterly the evils that have come about from those
well disposed toward us, and entrusted and who have trusted us, and who have
been treated well in many things by us, rather pain and grieve us. He did not
consider with himself, nor did he say: What is this? having taken him as a
servant I shared with him all the things of the house, I made him free and
something greater than myself, and such =62.687= returns has he given me? He
said none of these things; so did the former reverence hold his soul. And what
wonder, if in a house he enjoyed so much honor, where even in the prison see
him, of how much care he enjoys? And you know how the character of those
entrusted with the prisons has been trained toward cruelty; they reap the
misfortunes of others, and those whom others feed suffering badly, these they
tear, gaining gains worthy of many tears, being more cruel than beasts. For
from the things from which one ought to pity those thrown in, from these they
themselves reap. And let us not reckon this only, but that these themselves
also do not treat all those thrown in alike. For those upon slanders, and
simply abused and bound they might even pity; but those thrown in upon the
most shameful and most terrible and daring things they maltreat with ten
thousand blows. So that not only from the manner was the jailer going to be
cruel, but also from the cause for which he had been thrown in. For whom would
the young man not have stirred against himself, having enjoyed so much honor,
but suspected of trying the mistress and paying his benefactor with such
returns? Considering these things then the jailer, both the honor of the one
thrown in, and the matter for which he had been thrown in, would he not have
treated the man more harshly than any beast? But the hope toward God became
above all these things; so does virtue of soul know even to tame beasts. For
from the same reasonableness from which he took the master, from the same also
the chief jailer; and again Joseph was a ruler, and in prison he ruled, and in
a house. For since he was going to be king, reasonably he first learned to be
ruled, and being ruled he was a ruler and stood over the house. 5. For if Paul
requires this of the one being drawn into the Church, saying thus: For if
someone does not know how to stand over his own house, how will he take care
of the Church of God? also the one who has come to rule must first be best at
standing over a house. He stood over a prison, not as a prison, but as a
house. For he lightened the misfortunes for all,


and stood over those thrown in as over his own members, not only being curious
about their misfortunes and comforting, but even if he saw someone downcast,
he approached and learned the cause, not bearing even simply to see one
downcast, =62.688= unless first he released him from the downcast look; and
yet no one has displayed such
affection even about children. From these things then the beginning of the
good things also came about for him. For the things from us must be present
first, and then the things from God. For that he used such sparing and care,
he saw, he says, the eunuchs thrown in by Pharaoh, the chief cupbearer, and
the chief baker, and said: Why are your faces sad today? And not from this
only, but also from what those men did, one can learn thoroughly the virtue of
the man. For being servants of a king they did not spit upon him, being in
despondency they did not beat him off, but as to a genuine brother and one who
knows how to share pain they unveiled all their own things to him. All these
things have been said by me, that even if in slavery, even if in captivity,
even if in prison, even if under the earth itself the virtuous man comes to
be, nothing will be able to wrestle him down. These things have been spoken by
me toward the servants, that even if they have beasts as masters, as the
Egyptian, even if cruel, as the chief jailer, they will be able to take them,
even if they are Greeks, as those were, even if anything whatsoever, they will
quickly tame them. For nothing is more harmonious than manners, nothing
sweeter, nothing more pleasant than reasonableness and meekness and obedience;
such a one is fit for all. And such neither are ashamed of slavery, nor flee a
poor man, nor a sick and toiling man; for virtue masters all and gets the
better of all. And if in servants the thing has so much strength, how much
more in free men? Let us then practice these things both servants and free,
both women and men; thus we shall be desired both by men and by God, and by
men not only the virtuous, but also the bad, and especially those; for those
are the ones who especially honor and revere. For as those ruled especially
tremble at the reasonable, so also the unbridled at the virtuous, knowing of
what things they have fallen. Since then so great is the fruit of virtue, let
us pursue this, let us go after this. If we hold to this, nothing will be
terrible for us, all things easy, all things facile; even if we go through
fire, even if through water, all things yield to virtue and give way, even
death itself. Let us then emulate it, that we may also obtain the good things
to come, in Christ Jesus our Lord.

HOMILY V. For the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men,
teaching us that, having denied ungodliness and worldly desires, we should
live soberly and justly and godly in the present age; looking for the blessed
hope and appearing of the glory of the great God and our Savior Jesus Christ;
who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all lawlessness, and
purify for himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works. 1. Having
required much virtue from the servants (for it is much to adorn the teaching
of God and our Savior in all things, and to give no handle to the masters, not
even in a chance thing), he adds =62.688= also the just cause through which
the servants ought to be such. And what is this? For there has appeared, he
says, the grace of


God that brings salvation. Those who happen to have God as teacher, how would
they not reasonably be such as I just now went through, having found release
of ten thousand sins? For you know that with the other things this too not as
it chanced puts a soul to shame and contracts it, that being liable for ten
thousand sins, it did not pay the penalty, but obtained pardon and ten
thousand good things. For if someone, tell me, taking a servant who has
offended ten thousand times, should not stretch him with straps, but on those
things should furnish pardon, and for the things to come should require
accounts of him, and should command him to guard himself =62.689= so as not to
fall into the same, and should honor him with great gifts; whom do you think
he would not put to shame who hears of so great a grace? But do not think that
the grace stops at the forgiveness of the former things, but it also makes us
safe for the future; for this too is of grace. Since if he were going always
not to punish those who do badly, the thing is no longer grace, but some
encouragement toward ruin and corruption. For there has appeared, he says, the
grace of God teaching us that, having denied ungodliness and worldly desires,
we should live soberly and justly and godly in the present age, looking for
the blessed hope and appearing of the glory of the great God and our Savior
Jesus Christ. See how with the prizes he also sets down the virtue. And this
too is of grace, to deliver from the things of life, to lead toward heaven. He
shows two appearings here; for there are two, the former of grace, and the
second of recompense and of the just. That having denied, he says, ungodliness
and worldly desires. See the subject of all virtue. He did not say, That we
may flee, but, That having denied. The denial shows much distance, much
hatred, much turning away; with as much disposition, with as much diligence as
they turned away from the idols, with so much also vice itself and the worldly
desires, he says. For these too are idols, worldly desire and covetousness,
and this he names idolatry; and as many things as are useful to us toward the
present life are worldly desires; all things whatsoever that are dissolved
together with the present life are a worldly desire. Let us have nothing then
toward these. Christ came, that we may deny ungodliness. Ungodliness he means
the doctrines, and worldly desires the unholy life. That we should live
soberly and justly and godly, in the present age. 2. Do you see that, what I
always say, temperance is not this only, to abstain from fornication, but also
to be outside the rest of the passions? Therefore also he who loves money is
not temperate. For as that one of bodies, so also this one of money; or rather
this one is more unbridled, inasmuch as he does not even have so much force
pushing him. For that charioteer would especially be called without control,
not the one who does not hold the rough and hard-to-rein horse, but the one
who cannot subject the one that is quietly more reasonable. What then, he
says? is the desire of money less than that of bodies? It is clear to
everyone, I suppose, and this is shown from many places; and first, that the
desire of bodies has come about by necessity; and what has come about by
necessity is clear that it is also accomplished with much toil, since it has
also been sown in the nature. Second, that of money there was not much account
among the ancients, but of women much on account of temperance; and no one
would blame one who has been with a wife according to law until old age, but
all would blame one who is making money. And of the philosophers from outside
many despised money, but women no longer; so this is more tyrannical than
that. But since our word is toward the Church, let us not bring the examples
from those outside, but from the Scriptures. This then even almost in the part
of a command =62.690= this blessed man sets down saying thus, Having


food and coverings, we shall be content with these; but about the women, Do
not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement; and again, Come together
to the same. And often you might see him legislating about lawful intercourse.
And of this desire he lets them enjoy, and to approach a second marriage, and
he makes much care of the matter, and nowhere does he punish for this; but him
who desires money he condemns everywhere. And about money Christ arranged in
many places, so as to flee the ruin from this; but about abstinence from a
wife, not so. For hear what he says about money: If anyone does not renounce
all his possessions. Nowhere did he say, If anyone does not renounce a wife;
for he knew what tyranny he put in. And this blessed man says: Marriage is
honorable, and the bed undefiled. Nowhere does he call the care of money
honorable, but the contrary. But those who wish to be rich fall into
temptation and a snare and many foolish and harmful desires. He did not say,
To be covetous, but, To be rich. And that you may learn also from the common
notions, it is necessary to bring this word too into the midst. For someone
having been entirely deprived of money would no longer be troubled by the
desire; for nothing so makes one desire money as having money. But in the
desire of bodies it is not so, but even many who have been made eunuchs have
not thrown off the fire troubling within; for the desire lies in other organs,
lying within in the nature. For what reason then have these things been said
to us? That the covetous are more unbridled than those who fornicate, inasmuch
as they are troubled by a lesser desire; or rather the thing is not even
desire, but indolence. For here the desire is so natural, that even if someone
does not approach a woman, nature does its own and works; but there nothing
such happens. And godly, he says, we should live in the present age. And what
is the hope? what the prize of the toils? Looking for, he says, the blessed
hope and appearing. For truly nothing is more blessed than that, nothing more
to be emulated; and words cannot present these things; for the good things
then surpass mind. Looking for, he says, the blessed hope and appearing of the
glory of the great God and our Savior. Where are those who say the Son is less
than the Father? Of the great, he says, God and Savior. He who saved the
enemies, what will he not work then, having taken those [not] in good repute?
Of the great, he says, God. The great when he says it upon God, he does not
say great toward something, but absolutely great, after whom there would not
be a great, since it is toward something. And if toward something, it is by
comparison, and not great by nature; but now he is incomparably great. Who
gave himself for us, he says, that he might redeem us from all lawlessness and
purify for himself a peculiar people, that is, chosen, having nothing in
common with the rest. Zealous of good works. Do you see that there is need
also of the things from =62.691= us? Not simply of works, but zealous, that
is, going upon virtue itself with much eagerness, with the fitting vehemence.
To deliver then those weighed down with evils and sick incurably, was of his
lovingkindness; but the things after these, both of us and of him. These
things speak and exhort, and rebuke with all commandment. These things speak
and exhort. 3. Do you see how to Timothy he commands, and says, Rebuke,
reprove, exhort; but here, These things speak and exhort, and rebuke with all
commandment? Since the character of these was harder, for this reason he
commands to rebuke also sharply and with all commandment. For there are of the
sins some which one must also restrain with commands; as, to despise money one
must persuade by exhorting, and to be reasonable, and as many such things; but
the adulterer, the fornicator, the covetous one must with commandment lead on
to the


better. the one who uses omens, the one who uses signs, the one who does other
such things, not even simply with commandment, but with all commandment. Do
you see how he wishes him to command these things both with authority, and
with much power? Let no one despise you, but, Remind them to be subject to
rulers and authorities, to obey, to be ready toward every good work, to
blaspheme no one, to be not fighters. What then? not even those who do badly,
nor to revile those? But to be ready toward every good work, to blaspheme no
one. Let us hear the exhortation: To blaspheme no one, he says. Our mouth must
be clean from reviling. For whether the revilings are true, it is not ours to
say these things, but the judge's to examine; For you, he says, why do you
judge your brother? or if they are not true, see how great the fire; hear the
robber saying to the other robber: For we also are in the same judgment, we
run the same contest. If you reproach others, you yourself will quickly fall
into the same. For this reason this blessed man exhorts saying: He who thinks
he stands, let him look lest he fall. To be not fighters, he says. Reasonable,
showing all meekness toward all men, both Greeks and Jews, both the wicked and
the evil. For there he frightens from the things to come saying, So that he
who thinks he stands, let him look lest he fall; but here not from the things
to come, but from the things past he puts them to shame, doing the same,
through what he adds, For we ourselves also were once foolish; which he does
in writing to the Galatians: When we were infants, we were enslaved under the
elements of the world. Therefore reproach no one, he says; for you too were
such. For we, he says, ourselves also were once foolish, disobedient, going
astray, serving desires and various pleasures, living in vice and envy,
hateful, hating one another. So that toward all such one must be, to be mildly
disposed. For he who was such formerly, and has been delivered, ought not to
reproach toward such, but to pray and have thanks to him who has given both to
himself and to those the deliverance from the former evils. Let no one boast;
for all have sinned. When then you wish to reproach someone, yourself
succeeding, both considering your former =62.692= life and the uncertainty of
the future, check the anger. For even if from first age you have lived
virtuously, still you would have many sins; and if you do not have them, as
you think, consider that this has not come about of your virtue, but of the
grace of God; for if he had not called your ancestors, you too would have been
disobedient. See how he went through all vice. Did not God manage ten thousand
things through prophets, through all? did we not hear? For we ourselves also,
he says, were once going astray; but when the kindness and the lovingkindness
of God our Savior appeared. How? Not from works in righteousness which we did,
but according to his mercy he saved us through a washing of regeneration and
renewal of the Holy Spirit. Wonderful, how we were baptized in vice, so as not
to be able to be cleansed, but to need regeneration! for this is palingenesia.
For as in the case of a house badly disposed, no one sets a prop, nor sews it
to the old buildings, but having taken it down even to the foundations, thus
he raises it from above and renews it; so he also did; he did not repair us,
but constructed us from above; for this is, And renewal of the Holy Spirit;
from above he made us new. How? through the Spirit. And again, showing this
otherwise, he adds: Which he poured out upon us richly through Jesus Christ
our Savior. Thus there is need of much mercy for us. That having been
justified by his grace. Again by grace, not by debt. We might become heirs
according to hope of eternal life. At once it is both an exhortation to
humility, and a hope about the things to come. For if those so given up,


as to be born from above, as to be saved by grace, as to have nothing good, he
saved, much more in the future will he work this. 4. For nothing was worse
than human savagery before the coming of Christ; for as enemies and foes all
were disposed toward one another; fathers slaughtered their own children, and
mothers raged against children; nothing stood, neither natural nor written
law, but all things had been overturned; continual adulteries, murders, and if
anything more harsh than murders, thefts (and someone from outside says that
the thing even seemed to be of virtue; and reasonably, where they even
worshipped such a god), continual oracles commanding so-and-so and so-and-so
to be killed. Shall I say something of those according to that time? Androgeos
some son of Minos having come to Athens and having won in wrestling, paid a
penalty, and was killed. Apollo then, healing evil with evil, commanded twice
seven children to be led away for this. What more cruel than this tyranny? And
this came about, and a man having come to dissolve the madness of the demon,
slaughtered those children, because among them the deceit prevailed; but when
they rose up and defended themselves, no longer. If then the thing was coming
about justly, it ought not to have been hindered; but if unjustly, as indeed
also unjustly, it ought not even at the beginning to have been com =62.693=
manded. They worshipped boxers and wrestlers. Continual and successive wars,
by city, by village, by house. They loved boys; and among them some
philosopher legislated, that it is not permitted to a slave either to love
boys, or to dry-anoint, as if the thing were virtuous and having much honor.
For this reason they also stood at a brothel doing this openly. And if one
should go through all the things among them, he will find clearly that they
also insulted nature itself, and there was no one who hindered; but all their
dramas are full of these things, adultery, wantonness, corruption. Foul
all-night festivals came about, and women were called to the spectacle. O the
foulness! in night, in a theater there was an all-night festival, and a virgin
sat among raging young men and a drunken crowd .. The darkness was the
festival, and the abominable works accomplished by them. For this reason he
says: We ourselves also were once foolish, disobedient, going astray, serving
desires and various pleasures. So-and-so loved the stepmother, he says, and
so-and-so the stepson, and hanged herself. For the loves of children, which it
is their custom to call boy-loves, it is not even possible to say. But what?
do you wish to see mother-marriages? There is this too among them, and the
terrible thing, the matter came about through ignorance, and the god among
them did not hinder, but overlooked nature being insulted, and this being of
the distinguished. And if those whom it is likely, if not through some other
thing, at least through the glory of the many to hold to virtue, these were so
carried headlong against vice; what is it likely those living many and
hiddenly accomplished? what more various than this pleasure? So-and-so loved
so-and-so; having returned she slaughtered the husband through the adulterer.
Perhaps most of you know the story. The son of the murdered man killed the
adulterer, and sacrificed her too; after these things he himself also went
mad, and was driven by the furies; then this madman himself having gone away
slaughtered another, and takes that man's wife. What worse than these
misfortunes? For this I say these things from those outside, that I may
persuade the Greeks, how many evils held the inhabited world. But, if you
wish, let us show also from our own. They sacrificed, he says, their sons and
their daughters to the demons. And again those inhabiting Sodom perished
through no other thing than that they raged against boys. But again in the
prefaces of the coming of Christ did not the daughter of the king dance in the
midst of the banquets among drunken men? did she not ask for murder, and as
the honor of dancing the head of the prophet? Who will speak the mighty deeds
of the Lord? Hateful, he says,


hating one another. For it is entirely necessary, when we let loose all the
pleasures to the soul, that much hatred come about. Why? Because where love is
with virtue, no one defrauds anyone. See also what Paul says. Be not deceived;
neither fornicators, nor idolaters, nor adulterers, nor the soft, nor those
who lie with males, nor the covetous, nor revilers, nor drunkards will inherit
the kingdom of God. And these things some of you were. Do you see how every
form of vice prevailed, and there was some much mist, and the just was
corrupted? For if =62.694= those who enjoyed prophecy, and seeing so many
evils coming about both among the enemies and in themselves, nevertheless did
not endure, but worked ten thousand new things; what would the others? But
some again among them commanded virgins to wrestle naked before the eyes of
men. May many good things come to you, that you cannot even bear to hear the
word; but the thing itself the philosophers were not ashamed of. And some
other philosopher, the chief of them, even leads them out into wars, and
commands them to be common, as being some pimp and procurer. Living in vice
and envy, he says. For if those who philosophize among them legislated such
things, what would we say about those who have not philosophized? if those who
have the beard deep, and have thrown on the cloak say such things, what would
we say about others? The woman did not come about for this, O man, so as to be
set forth in common to all. O you who overturn all things; who mix with males
as with females, and lead the females out as men into war; for this is the
work of the devil, to confuse and overturn all things, and to shake the bounds
set from the beginning and to move what God set for nature. For to the woman
God gave only to keep the house, but to the man to do the public things; but
you bring the head down to the feet, and the feet up to the head. Do you arm
women, and are you not ashamed? And why do I say these things? they introduce
among them some woman even who killed children, and they are not ashamed nor
put to shame throwing such abominable stories into the ears of men. But when
the kindness, he says, and the lovingkindness of God our Savior appeared, not
from works in righteousness which we did, but according to his mercy, he saved
us through a washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit, which he
poured out upon us richly through Jesus Christ our Savior, that having been
justified by his grace, we might become heirs according to hope of eternal
life. What is, According to hope? That is, As we hoped, so we shall enjoy; or
that Already you are also heirs. The word is faithful. For since he discoursed
about things to come and not yet present, he added the trustworthiness. These
things are true, he says, and it is clear from the things that have arrived.
For he who delivered us from so much lawlessness and so many evils, it is
quite clear that he will also share the things to come with us altogether if
we remain in the grace; for from the same care all things come about. 5. Let
us then give thanks to God, and let us reproach nothing to the others, let us
accuse nothing, but let us rather exhort, let us pray for them, let us advise,
let us counsel, even if they insult, even if they kick; for such are those who
are sick. But those who hasten toward their health bear all things, do all
things, even if they accomplish nothing, that they may not accuse themselves
as having been neglectful. Or do you not know that often when a physician has
given up the one who is ill, some other of those belonging says to him: Apply
a treatment again, and omit nothing, that I may not accuse myself, that I may
not blame anything, that I may not have to accuse myself? Do you not see how
much provision those belonging make about those close? how many things they do
for this, both calling physicians, and sitting beside continually? Let us
imitate these too; and yet the =62.695= care is not about equal things. But
now when the body of the child is ill no one would refuse even


to undertake a long journey abroad, so as to dissolve the disease; but when
the soul is badly, no one makes any account of anyone; but we have all fallen
back, we are all indolent, we all make light, overlooking children and wives
and ourselves being caught by this harsh disease. But afterwards we receive
the perception. Consider how shameful and very ridiculous it is afterwards to
say, We did not expect, we did not hope this would be; and not only shameful,
but also dangerous. For if in the present life it belongs to the foolish not
to foresee the things to come, much more in the future, when we also hear now
many advising and saying the things to be done and the things not to be done.
Let us then hold to that hope =62.696=, let us care for our salvation, let us
call upon God in all things, so as to stretch out a hand. Until when indolent?
until when careless? until when do we despise ourselves, and our
fellow-servants? he himself poured out richly upon us the grace of the Spirit.
Let us consider then how much grace he displayed upon us, and let us too
display so much diligence; or rather so much is not possible, yet at least
less. For if even after this grace we are without feeling, the punishment will
be greater for us. For if I had not come, he says, and spoken to them, they
would not have had sin; but now they have no pretext. But may it not be that
these things be said about us, but may it be that we all be counted worthy of
the good things promised to those who have loved him in Christ Jesus our Lord,
and the rest.

HOMILY VI. And concerning these things I want you to affirm constantly, that
those who have believed God may take care to stand forward in good works.
These things are good and profitable to men. But foolish inquiries and
genealogies and strifes and fightings about the law avoid; for they are
unprofitable and vain. A heretical man after one admonition and a second
reject; knowing that such a one is perverted, and sins, being self-condemned.
1. Having spoken about the lovingkindness of God and about his unspeakable
care about us, and what we were when he made us what, he added and says: And
concerning these things I want you to affirm constantly, that those who have
believed God may take care to stand forward in good works; that is, to
discourse these things, and from these to exhort toward almsgiving. For not
only will the things said fit us toward humility, and toward not being lifted
up and reproaching others, but also toward all the rest of virtue. Thus then
also discoursing toward the Corinthians he says: You know that the Lord became
poor being rich, that we by his poverty might become rich. Having considered
the care of God and his exceeding lovingkindness, he exhorts them toward
almsgiving, not simply nor as a side work; but what? That they may take care,
he says, to stand forward in good works; that is, also to help those wronged,
not with money only, but also with patronages, and to defend widows and
orphans, and to set all who suffer badly in safety; for this is, To stand
forward in good works. For these things, he says, are good and profitable to
men. But foolish inquiries and genealogies and strifes and fightings about the
law avoid; for they are unprofitable and vain. What ever do the genealogies
want? for also in the Epistle to Timothy he sets this down saying: Myths


and endless genealogies. Perhaps both here and there hinting at Jews, who
think =62.696= great things upon having Abraham as ancestor, neglected the
things of themselves. For this reason he also calls them foolish and
unprofitable; for it is of folly to have confidence upon unprofitable things.
And strifes, he says, those toward the heretics, that we may not toil in vain,
when there is no gain; for their end is nothing. When someone is perverted,
and has not chosen, even if anything whatsoever comes about, to change his
mind, for what reason do you toil in vain sowing upon rocks, when one ought to
toil this good toil upon your own, discoursing to them the things about
almsgiving and the rest of virtue? how then does he say elsewhere, If perhaps
God may give them repentance, but here, A heretical man after one admonition
and a second reject, knowing that such a one is perverted, and sins, being
self-condemned? There he speaks about the correction of those who have hope,
and about those who simply set themselves against; but when he is clear to all
and manifest, for what reason do you box in vain? why do you beat the air?
What is, Being self-condemned? For he cannot say that no one said, no one
admonished. When then after the exhortation he remains the same, he becomes
self-condemned. When I send Artemas to you or Tychicus, make haste to come to
me to Nicopolis. What do you say? having set him over Crete, again you call
him to yourself? Not leading him away from that leisure, but training him
more. For that he does not call him to himself so as to lead him about and
have him following everywhere, hear what he says: For there I have determined
to winter. And Nicopolis is of Thrace. Zenas the lawyer and Apollos send on
diligently, that nothing may be lacking to them; and they were not yet of
those entrusted, but of those with him; and Apollos was more vehement, being
powerful in the Scriptures, and an eloquent man. If then that one was a
lawyer, he ought not, he says, to be fed by others. But lawyer here he means
one experienced in the Jewish laws; as if he said, Set them in all abundance,
that nothing may be lacking to them. And let our people also learn to stand
forward in good works =62.697= for the necessary uses, that they may not be
unfruitful. All who are with me greet you; greet those who love us in faith.
Either he means those who love him, or the faithful men. Grace be with you
all. Amen. How then do you command him to stop the mouths of those who
contradict, if one must avoid these, when they do all things upon their own
ruin? And he says principally never to do this for their gain; for they would
never gain, once perverted in mind. For if they should ruin others, one must
stand apart and fight, and with much courage avoid; but if you should be set
in necessity, seeing others being corrupted, do not be silent, but stop their
mouths, caring for those about to perish. And in general it is not for the one
who has diligence and a right life to bear a fight. Yet, as I said, do thus;
for from idleness and the superfluous philosophy the thing comes about, to be
occupied about words only. For even to utter superfluous things is much loss,
when one ought either to teach, or to pray, or to give thanks. For we ought
not to spare money, but not words; but especially these more than those, and
not simply to give ourselves over to all. What is, That they may take care to
stand forward in good works? That they may not wait for those in need to come
to them, but they themselves also may be curious about those in need of their
help; for he who takes care, takes care thus; for thus especially and with
much diligence he will work this. For even in the things in which he does
well, not so much to those who suffer, as to those who do well, the thing that
comes about brings revenue and gain; for it gives them boldness toward God.
But there the end of the fight is nothing. The incorrigible then he calls
heretical. For as to overlook those who have hopes of change, is of
sluggishness;


so to treat those sick incurably, is of folly and of the last madness; for we
make those men bolder. And let, he says, our people also learn to stand
forward in good works for the necessary uses, that they may not be unfruitful.
Do you see that he cares for them more than for those who receive? For it was
possible perhaps from many places to send these on, but of our people, he
says, I care. For what profit, tell me? For if others digging up treasures fed
the teachers, there was not yet any gain for these; for they remained
unfruitful. What then, tell me? was not Christ who from five loaves fed five
thousand, and from seven four thousand, able to feed himself and those with
him? for what reason then was he fed by women (For there followed him, he
says, women, who ministered to him)? Teaching us from above that he cares for
those who do this. Was not Paul who even sufficed for others through his own
hands able to take from nowhere? But you see him both taking and asking; and
hear, on account of what: For I seek, he says, not the gift, but the fruit
that abounds to your account. And at the beginning too, when selling all their
own things they laid them at the feet of the apostles, do you see the apostles
caring for them more than for those who receive? For if they cared only
somehow for the poor, they would have made no =62.698= account about Sapphira
and Ananias, when they purloined the money, Paul would not have arranged
saying: Not from grief, or from necessity. What do you say, Paul? do you
hinder the poor? No, he says; for I do not look to their thing, but to that of
the givers. And see also the prophet, when he advised Nebuchadnezzar that best
counsel, not caring only for the poor; for he did not say, Give to the poor
simply, but what? Redeem your sins in alms, and your injustices in compassions
of the poor. Empty the money, he says, not that others may be fed only, but
that you yourself may be delivered from the punishment. And again Christ: Sell
your possessions, and give to the poor, and come follow me. Do you see that
there too this was commanded on account of the following? For since money is a
hindrance, for this reason he commanded to give to the poor, teaching the soul
to be merciful and compassionate, teaching to despise money, teaching to stand
off from covetousness. For he who is taught to give to the one who does not
have, will be taught in time also not to take from those who have. This makes
like to God. And yet virginity has a harder toil than it, and fasting and
lying on the ground; but nothing is so strong and powerful toward quenching
the fire of our sins as almsgiving; this is greater than all, it sets its
lovers beside the king himself; and very reasonably. For virginity and fasting
and lying on the ground stand about the one doing only, and have saved no
other; but almsgiving stretches out to all, and is twined about the members of
Christ; and much greater than the successes that stand about the one are those
that stretch out toward many. 3. For this is mother of love, of the love that
characterizes Christianity, that is greater than all the signs, through which
the disciples of Christ appear; this is a medicine of our sins, a cleanser of
the filth of our soul, a ladder fixed into heaven; this binds together the
body of Christ. Do you wish to learn how great a good this is? In the time of
the apostles all selling their own things brought them to them, which were
also distributed; For they distributed, he says, to each, as anyone had need.
For tell me, even apart from the things to come (for let us not now move about
the kingdom, but let us see in the present) who gain, those who receive, or
those who gave? For those murmured, and skirmished toward


one another; but these had one soul; For there was, he says, of all the heart
and the soul one, and grace was upon all of them, and they lived with much
benefit. Do you see that from this too those gained? Tell me then, of which
would you have wished to be, of those throwing away their own and having
nothing, or of those taking even the things of others? See the fruit of
almsgiving; the partitions and the hinderances were taken away, and
immediately their souls were joined; Of all the heart and the soul was one. So
that even apart from almsgiving the emptying of money has much gain. And these
things have been said by me, that those who have not inherited a lot from
ancestors may not grieve nor be downcast, as having less than those who are ri
=62.699= ch; for they have greater things, if they wish. For they will also
come to almsgiving with more ease, as the widow, and they will have no
occasion of enmity toward the neighbor, and they will be more free than all;
no one will be able to threaten confiscation to such a one, but he is above
suffering badly. And as no one would quickly catch those fleeing naked, but of
those who have put on many things and drag them one might easily take hold; so
also is the rich man, and the poor man. For the one, even if he is caught,
will be able easily to run through; but the other, even if he is not held, is
entangled with his own ropes, with ten thousand cares, despondencies, angers,
provocations. All these things bury the soul; and not these only, but also
many others, which we drag from wealth. For it is more difficult for the rich
man to be moderate than the poor, and to live in cheapness, and to be
delivered from wrath more difficult for this one than for that. Therefore, he
says, he will have a greater reward? Not at all. Why, if indeed he
accomplishes the difficult things? Because he himself invented the
difficulties for himself; for it has not been commanded to be rich, but even
the contrary; and he prepares for himself ten thousand stumbling-blocks and
hinderances. And others not only put away the money, but also melt down the
more of the body, as going a narrow way; but you not this only, but you also
increase the furnace of the passions, and throw other things around. Therefore
go away into the broad; for that is what receives such; but the narrow those
who are pressed, those who are narrowed, those who bear nothing, except these
loads, which it is possible to be carried through it, almsgiving,
lovingkindness, kindness, reasonableness. If you bear these, you will easily
be able to enter; but if madness and inflammation of soul, and the load of
thorns, wealth, you need much broadness. For it is not possible for you even
to go into the crowd, so as not to strike others being borne from above, but
there was need of much interval here. But he who carries off gold and silver,
I mean the successes of virtue, not only does not have those near fleeing, but
even those becoming near and pairing. Yet if wealth itself is a thorn, what is
covetousness? for what reason do you lead this away there? that you may work a
greater flame, putting loads under the fire? for is not the fire of gehenna
enough? Consider how the three children got the better of the furnace; think
that to be gehenna; with affliction they fell into it bound and shackled; but
they found much broadness within; but not those standing around from outside.
4. Some such thing will be now too, if we wish to stand nobly and courageously
toward the trials brought on; if we have the hopes toward God, we ourselves
shall be in safety and broadness, but those who throw us in will perish; For
he who digs a pit, he says, will fall into it. And if they bind the hands, and
if the feet, the affliction will be able to re =62.700= lease. For see the
wonderful thing; those whom men bound, these the fire released. For as if
someone should hand over some to the servants of friends, and these, revering
the friendship


of the master, not only do not ruin them, but also share much honor; so also
the fire, since it recognized that those children are friends of its Master,
both burst their bonds, and released them and let them go, and became a floor
for them, and was trampled by them, reasonably; for they had been thrown in
for the glory of God. As many of us as are in afflictions, let us hold these
examples. But see, he says, those were delivered from the affliction, but we
are not. And reasonably, since those did not go into the furnace thus, as
going to be delivered, but as going to die completely; for hear them saying:
There is a God in heaven, who will deliver us; and if not, let it be known to
you, O king, that we do not serve your gods, and to the golden image which you
have set up we do not bow. But we as bargaining the chastisements of God, also
define a time saying: If until so much he does not have mercy. For this reason
we are not delivered. Since Abraham too did not go off thus as going to
receive the son safe, but as going to sacrifice; and contrary to expectation
he received him safe. And you when you fall into affliction, do not hurry, do
not hasten to be delivered, prepare your mind toward all patience, and you
will be delivered quickly from the affliction; for for this reason God also
brings it on, that he may teach. When then from the prefaces we are taught to
bear, and we are not restless, he lets go thereafter, as the whole having been
accomplished. I wish to say some useful story to you, and having much benefit.
And what is this? When a persecution had once overtaken, and a vehement war
had been kindled against the Church, two some men were seized; and the one was
ready to suffer anything whatsoever, but the other toward having the head cut
off was himself also ready and noble, but toward the other tortures fearing
and trembling. See then the management: When the judge sat, the one who was
ready to suffer anything whatsoever, he commanded to have the head cut off;
but that one having hung him he tore, and not once nor a second time, but
leading him around all the cities. Why ever was this allowed? That he might
recover the neglected part of his mind through the tortures, that he might
cast out all the cowardice, that he might no longer be fearing nor hesitating
nor trembling toward the matter. And Joseph too when he especially hastened to
go out, then especially he remained in; for hear him saying, I was stolen by
theft from the land of the Hebrews; and, Remember me, he says, to the king.
For this reason he remained in, that he might learn that one must not have
confidence, nor set hope upon men, but throw the whole upon God. Knowing these
things then, let us confess thanks to God, and let us do all things toward the
advantage; that we may obtain the good things to come, in Christ Jesus our
Lord, with whom to the Father be the glory, with the Holy Spirit, now and
always, and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
