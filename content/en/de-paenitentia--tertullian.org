* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

This race of men that we ourselves once were, blind, without the Lord's light,
know repentance only by nature: some passion of the mind that comes from
taking offense at a worse opinion. For the rest they are as far from its
reason as they are from the author of reason himself. Reason is a thing of
God, because God is the maker of all things: he has provided, arranged, and
ordered nothing except by reason, and he has willed that nothing be handled or
understood except by reason. Those who do not know God must therefore also be
ignorant of what belongs to him, since no treasure at all is open to
strangers. So they cross the whole course of life without the helm of reason,
and they do not know how to shun the storm that hangs over the world.

How irrationally they conduct themselves in the practice of repentance will be
enough to show from this one point: they apply it even to their good deeds.
They repent of faith, of love, of simplicity, of patience, of mercy, as soon
as any of these has fallen on ingratitude. They curse themselves for having
done well, and they plant in the heart especially that kind of repentance
which is laid on the best works, taking care never to confer a good turn
again. On repentance for evils they sit more lightly. In short, they more
readily go wrong by that same repentance than they do right by it.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But if they lived as men who had a share in God, and so in reason too, they
would first weigh what repentance is worth, and would never use it as a proof
of perverse amendment. They would keep a measure in repenting, because they
would keep a measure in going wrong as well, fearing the Lord, of course. But
where there is no fear, there is likewise no amendment; where there is no
amendment, repentance is necessarily empty, because it lacks the fruit for
which God sowed it, namely man's salvation.

For God, after so many and so great sins of human rashness, begun from Adam
the first of the race, after man was condemned with the world's dowry, after
he was cast out of paradise and subjected to death, when he had ripened again
toward his own mercy, from that time dedicated repentance in himself,
rescinding the sentence of his former wrath, pledging pardon to his own work
and image. So he gathered a people for himself, and nourished them with many
gifts of his goodness, and though he found them so often most ungrateful, he
always exhorted them to repentance, and sent out the mouths of all the
prophets to prophesy. Soon, promising the grace which at the ends of the times
he would pour as light on the whole world through his Spirit, he ordered the
baptism of repentance to go first, so that those whom he would call by grace
to the promise appointed for Abraham's seed he might first prepare by the seal
of repentance.

John does not keep silent, saying, Begin repentance, for now salvation will
draw near to the nations, that is, the Lord, bringing it according to God's
promise. Serving him in advance, John appointed repentance to stand first for
the cleansing of minds, so that whatever old error had stained, whatever
ignorance had defiled in the heart of man, repentance, sweeping and scraping
and casting it outdoors, might prepare a clean house of the breast for the
Holy Spirit about to come, that he might gladly enter there with heavenly
goods. Of these goods there is one title: man's salvation, with the wiping out
of former crimes going first. This is the cause of repentance; this is her
work, tending the business of divine mercy. What profits man serves God.

For the rest, her reason, which we learn once the Lord is known, holds a fixed
form: that violent hands never be laid on good deeds or thoughts. For God does
not ratify the rejection of good things, since they are his own; being their
author he must also be their defender, and likewise their acceptor, and if
their acceptor, their rewarder too. Let men's ingratitude look to itself,
then, if it forces repentance even onto good deeds. Let gratitude look to
itself as well, if the hunt for it is the spur to doing good. Both are earthly
and mortal. How little is the gain if you do good to a grateful man, or the
loss if to an ungrateful? A good deed has God for its debtor, as an evil deed
does too, because every judge is a rewarder of the case. But since God as
judge sits over the exacting and guarding of justice, which is dearest to him,
and since he sanctions the whole sum of his discipline toward it, can it be
doubted that, as in all our acts, so also in the matter of repentance, justice
must be rendered to God? That will be fulfilled if it is applied to sins
alone. Further, nothing deserves to be called sin except an evil deed, and no
one goes wrong by doing good. But if he does not go wrong, why does he invade
repentance, the private ground of those who do? Why does he lay on his own
goodness the office of malice? So it happens that when a thing is used where
it should not be, it is neglected where it should.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

What things, then, justly and duly call for repentance, that is, what should
be counted as sin, the place does demand that I mark; but it may seem idle.
Once the Lord is known, the spirit, looked upon by its own author, rises of
itself to the knowledge of the truth, and, admitted to the Lord's precepts, is
taught from them at once that whatever God forbids is to be counted as sin.
Since it is agreed that God is some great good, nothing but evil would
displease the good, because between things contrary to each other there is no
friendship. Still it will not be a burden to note that some faults are
fleshly, that is bodily, and some spiritual. For since man is made from this
joining of a double substance, he goes wrong from nowhere else than from what
he is made of. But they do not differ from each other because body and spirit
are two (otherwise they are all the more equal, because the two make one),
lest anyone, by the diversity of the materials, distinguish their sins and
think one lighter or heavier than the other. Flesh and spirit are both things
of God, one shaped by his hand, the other finished by his breath. Since they
belong equally to the Lord, whichever of them goes wrong equally offends the
Lord. Will you distinguish the acts of flesh and spirit, which have such
fellowship and partnership in life and in death and in resurrection that they
are raised together then either to life or to judgment, because, of course,
they have either gone wrong together or acted innocently together? I have put
this first so that we may understand that no less necessity of repentance
hangs over either part, if it has gone wrong, than over both. The guilt of
both is common, the judge is common, God of course, and so the remedy of
repentance is common too.

From this they are named spiritual and bodily, because every fault is either
done or thought, so that the bodily is what is in the deed, because a deed,
like a body, can be seen and touched, and the spiritual is what is in the
mind, because spirit is neither seen nor held. By this it is shown that not
only sins of deed but also of will must be avoided and purged by repentance.
And if our human limits judge only the deed, because it is no match for the
hiding places of the will, we should not therefore neglect the will's crimes
even under God. God is equal to all things. Nothing from which anyone goes
wrong at all is remote from his sight, because he neither is ignorant of it
nor fails to appoint it for judgment. He is no dissembler or traitor of his
own insight. What of this, that the will is the origin of the deed? Let those
things look to themselves which are charged to chance or necessity or
ignorance; apart from these, no one now goes wrong except by will. Since then
the will is the origin of the deed, is it not so much the more liable to
punishment as it is first in the fault, and not even then set free when some
difficulty cuts off its carrying out? For it is charged to itself, and cannot
be excused by the ill luck of finishing, having already done what was its own.
How else does the Lord show that he is building an addition onto the law, if
not by forbidding also the sins of the will, when he defines as an adulterer
not only the man who has fallen at close quarters into another's marriage, but
also the one who has defiled himself by lust of the eyes? So what is forbidden
to be carried out, the mind presents to itself at no small risk, and rashly by
will it writes the effect off as done. Since the force of this will is so
great that, saturating its own comfort, it counts as a deed, it will therefore
be punished as a deed. Nothing is emptier than to say: I wished it, yet I did
not do it. On the contrary, you ought to finish it because you will it, or not
will it because you do not finish it. But you yourself pronounce the sentence
by the confession of your conscience. For if you were craving the good, you
would have longed to finish it; so, just as you do not finish the evil, you
ought not to have craved it. Whichever way you set yourself, you are bound by
a crime, because you have either willed the evil or not fulfilled the good.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

For all sins, then, whether committed in flesh or in spirit, in deed or in
will, he who appointed punishment through judgment has also pledged pardon
through repentance, saying to the people, Repent, and I will make you safe.
And again, As I live, says the Lord, I prefer repentance to death. Therefore
repentance is life, since it is set before death. You, sinner, like me (rather
less than I, for I acknowledge my preeminence in faults), seize it, embrace
it, as a shipwrecked man seizes the trust of some plank. This will lift you,
sunk in the waves of sins, and will carry you on to the harbor of divine
clemency. Snatch the chance of unexpected happiness, that you who were once
nothing with God but a drop from a bucket and dust of the threshing-floor and
a potter's vessel may from then on become that tree which is planted by the
waters and lasts in its leaves and in its season bears fruit, which will see
neither fire nor axe. Repent of your errors, now that truth is found; repent
of having loved what God does not love, since we ourselves do not allow even
our own slaves not to hate the things by which we are offended. For the reason
of obedience is set in a likeness of minds.

To enumerate the good of repentance is a spread-out matter, and for that
reason to be entrusted to a great eloquence. We, for our narrow means, press
one thing: that what God commands is good and best. I count it boldness to
argue about the good of a divine precept. We ought not to listen because it is
good, but because God has commanded it. For the showing of obedience the
majesty of divine power comes first; the authority of the one who commands
comes before the profit of the one who serves. Is it good to repent, or not?
Why do you turn it over? God commands it. And he does not only command, he
also exhorts. He invites with a reward, salvation; even swearing, saying As I
live, he wants to be believed. O blessed are we, for whose sake God swears! O
most wretched, if we do not believe even the Lord when he swears! What God so
strongly commends, what he even attests in human fashion under an oath, we
ought to take up and keep with the highest seriousness, so that remaining in
the assertion of divine grace we may likewise persevere in its fruit and gain.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

For this I say: repentance, which by God's grace has been shown and enjoined
on us, and which calls us back into the Lord's favor, once known and taken up,
ought never afterward to be unsealed by a return to sin. Already no pretext of
ignorance speaks for you, that after knowing the Lord and admitting his
precepts, and after performing repentance for your sins, you put yourself back
into sins again. As far as you are parted from ignorance, so far you are glued
to stubbornness. For if you had repented of going wrong because you had begun
to fear the Lord, why did you choose to undo what you did for fear's sake,
unless because you have ceased to fear? Nothing but stubbornness overthrows
fear. Since even those who do not know the Lord are shielded by no exception
against punishment, because God, set in the open and graspable even from the
heavenly goods themselves, may not be unknown, how much more dangerous is it
to despise him once known? He despises him who, having received from him the
understanding of goods and evils, takes up again what he understands he should
flee, and what he has already fled, and so does insult to his own
understanding, that is to God's gift. He rejects the giver when he deserts the
gift; he denies the benefactor when he does not honor the benefit. How can he
please the one whose gift displeases him? So you show yourself not only
stubborn toward the Lord, but ungrateful as well.

Moreover, he does not sin lightly against the Lord who, after he had by
repentance renounced the Lord's rival, the devil, and by that name had
subjected him to the Lord, by his return raises that same one again and makes
himself the devil's exultation, so that the evil one, having recovered his
prey, rejoices once more against the Lord. Does he not (and it is dangerous
even to say it, but it must be brought out for building up) prefer the devil
to the Lord? For he seems to have made a comparison who has known both, and by
his judgment to have pronounced him better to whom he has chosen to belong
again. So he who had set out by repentance of sins to make satisfaction to the
Lord will make satisfaction to the devil by another repentance of repentance,
and will be the more hateful to God the more he is acceptable to God's rival.

But some say it is enough for God if he is honored in heart and mind, though
in act it is done less; so they sin with fear and faith intact, that is, they
violate marriages with chastity intact, they mix poison for a parent with
piety intact. So they too will be thrust into gehenna with pardon intact,
while they sin with fear intact. First a model of perversity: because they
fear, they go wrong. I suppose they would not go wrong if they did not fear.
Therefore let the man who does not want God offended not revere him at all, if
fear of offending is a defense. But such wits are used to sprout from the seed
of hypocrites, whose friendship with the devil is undivided, whose repentance
is never faithful.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Whatever then our smallness has tried to suggest for taking up repentance once
and holding it for good, looks indeed to all who are devoted to the Lord, as
all are seekers of salvation in earning God's favor; but it hangs especially
over these novices, who are only now beginning to water their ears with the
divine words, and who, like whelps of still fresh infancy, not yet with
finished eyes, crawl uncertainly, and who say they renounce their former
things and take on repentance, but neglect to shut it in.

For the very end of desiring interrupts them, so that they still want
something from the former things, as fruits, when they are already beginning
to go sour or bitter, still in some part flatter their own attractiveness.
Besides, every vice of delay and turning back in the matter of repentance is
brought in by presumption of baptism. Sure of the undoubted pardon of sins,
they steal the time between for a while and make themselves a furlough for
going wrong rather than a schooling for not going wrong. How absurd, besides,
not to fulfill repentance, yet to wait from it for pardon of sins! This is not
putting down the price, yet stretching out the hand for the goods. For at this
price the Lord has appointed to assign pardon; by this compensation of
repentance he offers impunity to be bought. If then those who sell first test
the coin they bargain for, lest it be clipped or scraped or counterfeit, shall
we believe that the Lord too will first enter on a proving of repentance
before he grants us so great a wage, namely everlasting life?

But let us put off for a little the truth of repentance. Then, I suppose, it
will be clear we have been amended, when we are absolved. By no means. But
when, with pardon still hanging, punishment is in view; when a man does not
yet deserve to be freed, that we may be able to deserve it; when God
threatens, not when he pardons. For what slave, after he has been changed by
freedom, still charges himself with his thefts and flights? What soldier,
after he has been discharged from his camp, still worries over his marks? The
sinner ought to weep for himself before pardon, because the time of repentance
is the same as that of danger and fear.

Nor do I refuse that the divine benefit, that is the wiping out of sins, is in
every way safe for those who are about to enter the water; but we must labor
that it may be granted to arrive there. For who will lend even one sprinkling
of any water to you, a man of such faithless repentance? It is easy, to be
sure, to come at it by stealth and to lead the overseer of this matter around
by your assertions; but God looks after his treasure, and does not let the
unworthy creep in. What, finally, does he say? Nothing hidden that will not be
revealed. However many darknesses you pile over your deeds, God is light.

Some think as if God must grant even to the unworthy what he has pledged, and
they make his generosity a slavery. But if he grants us the token of death by
necessity, then he does it unwillingly. And who allows that what he has given
unwillingly will remain? Do not many fall away afterward? Is that gift not
taken from many? These, of course, are the ones who creep in, who, having
taken up the faith of repentance, set a house that will fall on sand.

Let no one therefore flatter himself because he is counted among the recruits
of the hearers, as if even now it were allowed him to go wrong. As soon as you
have known the Lord, fear him; as soon as you have looked on him, revere him.
For the rest, what does it profit you to have known him, when you lie upon the
same things as when you were ignorant? And what separates you from a finished
servant of God? Is Christ one for the baptized, another for the hearers? Is
there another hope or wage, another fear of judgment, another necessity of
repentance? That washing is the sealing of faith, and that faith is begun and
commended from the faith of repentance. We are not washed so that we may cease
to go wrong, but because we have ceased, since we are already washed in the
heart. For this is the hearer's first baptism: fear still whole. From then on,
as soon as you have perceived the Lord, a sound faith, a conscience that has
once embraced repentance.

For the rest, if we cease from sinning only from the waters, we put on
innocence by necessity, not by choice. Who then is outstanding in goodness?
The one who is not allowed to be evil, or the one who is displeased to be
evil? The one who is ordered to be free of crime, or the one who delights to
be free of it? So let us not turn our hand from theft unless the hardness of
locks fights us, nor bridle our eyes from lusts of uncleanness unless we are
dragged away by the keepers of bodies, if no one devoted to the Lord will
cease to go wrong unless bound by baptism. But if anyone has felt so, I do not
know whether, once baptized, he will be more sorry that he has ceased to sin
than glad that he has escaped. Therefore hearers ought to long for baptism,
not presume it. For he who longs, honors; he who presumes, is proud. In the
one, modesty appears; in the other, wantonness. The one is in earnest, the
other neglects. The one desires to deserve, but the other promises it to
himself as a debt. The one takes, the other seizes. Whom would you judge
worthier, if not the more amended? Whom more amended, if not the more fearful,
and for that reason the one who has performed true repentance? For he has
still feared to go wrong, lest he not deserve to receive. But that
presumptuous one, when he promised it to himself, being of course secure,
could not fear; so he has not fulfilled repentance either, because he lacked
the instrument of repentance, that is, fear. Presumption is a share of
shamelessness: it puffs up the petitioner, it looks down on the giver. And so
it sometimes deceives. For it promises before the thing is owed, by which the
one who is going to grant is always offended.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Thus far, Christ the Lord, may it be granted your servants to learn or hear of
the discipline of repentance, as far as hearers ought not even to go wrong; or
may they now know nothing further of repentance, and seek nothing of it. It
irks me to weave in mention of a second, or rather now last, hope, lest by
treating of the remaining help of repenting we seem to be pointing out still a
space for going wrong. Far be it that anyone so interpret, as if even now the
way were open to him for sinning because it is open for repenting, and as if
the overflow of heavenly clemency made a lust of human rashness. Let no one be
the worse because God is the better, going wrong as often as he is pardoned.
For the rest, he will have an end of escaping when he has no end of offending.

We have escaped once; let us go no further in putting ourselves into dangerous
things, even if we seem likely to escape again. Most who have been freed from
shipwreck thereafter serve a divorce on both ship and sea, and they honor
God's benefit, namely their own safety, by the memory of the danger. I praise
the fear, I love the modesty: they do not want to be a burden again to divine
mercy; they dread seeming to wear out what they have obtained; with a good
anxiety, certainly, they avoid trying again what they have once learned to
fear. So a limit on rashness is a witness of fear. And the fear of man is the
honor of God.

But that most stubborn enemy never gives his malice a rest. On the contrary,
he rages most then, when he fully feels that a man has been set free; he is
most kindled then, while he is being quenched. He must grieve and groan that,
pardon of sins having been granted, so many works of death in the man have
been thrown down, so many titles of his former condemnation erased. He grieves
that that servant of Christ, the sinner, is going to judge him and his angels.
So he watches, he attacks, he besieges, if he can either strike the eyes with
fleshly lust, or entangle the mind with worldly allurements, or overthrow
faith by fear of earthly power, or twist a man from the sure way by perverse
traditions; he does not fail in scandals or in temptations.

God, therefore, foreseeing these poisons of his, though the door of pardon is
shut and the bar of baptism bolted, has still allowed something to stand open.
He has set in the porch a second repentance, to open to those who knock; but
now only once, because it is already a second time; but never more, because
the last time was in vain. Is not this too enough, once? You have what you no
longer deserved; for you had lost what you had received. If the Lord's
indulgence lends you the means to restore what you had lost, be grateful for a
repeated benefit, still more for an enlarged one. For it is greater to restore
than to give, since it is more pitiable to have lost than never to have
received at all.

But the mind is not at once to be cut down and undermined by despair, if
someone has become a debtor of second repentance. Let it irk him, to be sure,
to sin again, but let it not irk him to repent again; let it irk him to be in
danger again, but not to be set free again. Let no one be ashamed. Repeated
sickness needs repeated medicine. You will have shown yourself grateful to the
Lord if you do not refuse what the Lord offers you. You have offended, but you
can still be reconciled. You have one to whom you may make satisfaction, and
one who is willing.

If you doubt this, unroll what the Spirit says to the churches. He charges the
Ephesians with deserted love, he reproaches the Thyatirans with fornication
and the eating of things sacrificed to idols, he accuses the people of Sardis
of works not full, he blames the Pergamenes for teaching perverse things, he
rebukes the Laodiceans for trusting in riches, and yet he warns them all to
repentance, under threats, to be sure. But he would not threaten the one who
does not repent if he would not pardon the one who does. It would be doubtful,
if he had not shown this pouring out of his clemency elsewhere too. Does he
not say, He who has fallen shall rise, and he who has turned away shall be
converted? He it is, of course, he it is who prefers mercy to sacrifices. The
heavens rejoice, and the angels who are there, at a man's repentance. Come,
you sinner, take heart! You see where they rejoice at your return.

What are those arguments of the Lord's parables to us? That a woman lost a
coin and seeks it and finds it and invites her friends to the joy: is that not
an example of the restored sinner? One little sheep of the shepherd also
strays, but the flock was not dearer than the one: that one is sought out,
that one is longed for above all, and at last it is found and brought back on
the shepherd's own shoulders; for it had toiled much in straying. I will not
keep silent about that most gentle father either, who calls back the prodigal
son, and after his want, when he repents, receives him gladly, sacrifices the
fatted calf, and dresses his joy with a feast. Why not? For he had found the
son he had lost; he felt him dearer whom he had gained. Who is that father for
us to understand? God, of course. No one is so much a father, no one so
dutiful. He therefore will receive you as his son, even if you have squandered
what you received from him, even if you come back naked, because you have come
back, and he will rejoice more at your return than at the other's sobriety;
but if you repent from the heart, if you compare your hunger with the plenty
of your father's hired men, if you leave the swine, that unclean herd, if you
seek the father again even though offended, saying, I have sinned, father, and
I am no longer worthy to be called yours. Confession of sins relieves as much
as concealment piles on. For confession is the counsel of satisfaction;
concealment is the counsel of stubbornness.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

This second and single repentance, then, the narrower its business, the more
laborious its proving, so that not conscience alone is put forward, but it is
also carried out by some act. That act, which is more often expressed and used
by a Greek word, is exomologesis, by which we confess our sin to the Lord, not
indeed as to one who does not know, but in that satisfaction is arranged by
confession, repentance is born from confession, God is softened by repentance.
So exomologesis is a discipline of prostrating and humbling a man, enjoining a
way of life that draws mercy. It also commands, as to dress and food, to lie
in sackcloth and ashes, to darken the body with squalor, to cast down the mind
with griefs, to change by sad treatment those things which have sinned; for
the rest, to know a plain diet and drink, not for the belly's sake of course
but for the soul's, and often indeed to feed prayers with fasts, to groan, to
weep and to bellow day and night to the Lord your God, to fall before the
elders, and to kneel to those dear to God, to charge all the brothers with the
embassies of one's own pleading. All this is exomologesis, that it may commend
repentance, that it may honor the Lord from fear of the danger, that, itself
pronouncing against the sinner, it may discharge the function of God's
indignation and by temporal affliction, I will not say frustrate, but work off
the eternal punishments. When therefore it rolls a man down, it more relieves
him; when it makes him squalid, it makes him the cleaner; when it accuses, it
excuses; when it condemns, it acquits. As far as you have not spared yourself,
so far, believe it, God will spare you.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Most, however, either shun this work, as a publishing of themselves, or put it
off from day to day. I presume they are more mindful of shame than of
salvation; like those who, having contracted a trouble in the more modest
parts of the body, avoid the knowledge of physicians and so perish with their
blushing. It is intolerable, of course, to modesty to make satisfaction to an
offended Lord, to be restored to a ruined salvation. Ah, you are fine in your
shame, spreading your brow for going wrong, but drawing it back for pleading.
I give no place to blushing, when I gain more from the loss of it, when it
itself in a way exhorts the man, saying, Do not regard me; it is better for me
to perish through you. Its danger is burdensome then, if at all, when it
stands among those who will insult in mockery, where one is lifted up from
another's ruin, where they climb over the one who is down; but among brothers
and fellow-servants, where hope, fear, joy, grief, suffering are common,
because the spirit is common from a common Lord and Father, what do you think
these are but yourself? Why do you flee the partners of your fortunes as if
they were applauders? A body cannot go about glad from the trouble of one
member; the whole must suffer with it and labor together toward the remedy. In
one and another the church is, and the church is Christ. Therefore when you
stretch yourself to the brothers' knees, you handle Christ, you entreat
Christ. Equally, when they shed tears over you, Christ suffers, Christ pleads
with the Father. What the Son asks is always easily obtained.

A great gain, clearly, shame promises from the hiding of a sin. Namely, if we
have withdrawn something from human knowledge, shall we likewise hide it from
God? Are men's opinion and God's knowledge so compared? Or is it better to lie
hidden condemned than to be acquitted in the open? It is a wretched thing so
to come to exomologesis! For one comes to wretchedness by an evil; but where
there must be repentance, the wretched ceases, because it has become saving.

What if, besides the shame which they think stronger, they also dread the
body's inconveniences, that they must lodge unwashed, squalid, outside of
gladness, in the roughness of sackcloth and the horror of ashes and the
emptiness of the mouth from fasting? Are we then to plead for our sins in
scarlet and Tyrian purple? Bring me a pin for parting the hair, and powder for
polishing the teeth, and some forked thing of iron or bronze for recutting the
nails, if any false brightness, if any forced redness, is to be pressed onto
lips or cheeks. Besides, hunt out merrier baths, garden or seaside retreats,
add to the expense, seek out the huge stuffing of fattened fowl, strain the
old age of wine, and when someone asks for whom you are spending these things,
say, I have sinned against God, and I am in danger of perishing forever; so
now I hang and waste and torment myself, that I may reconcile to myself God,
whom I injured by going wrong.

But those who go about in the canvass for office are neither ashamed nor irked
to strive, in the inconveniences of soul and body, and not only inconveniences
but all insults, in the cause of their vows. What meannesses of dress do they
not affect? What halls do they not occupy with night and raw salutations,
shrinking at every meeting of any greater person, famous at no feasts,
gathered at no revels, but exiles from the happiness of liberty and gladness,
and all that for the flying joy of a single year. Do we hesitate to bear, in
the danger of eternity, what the petition for axes and rods endures? And shall
we cease to offer the chastening of food and dress to an offended Lord, which
the nations inflict on themselves with no one at all injured? These are they
of whom the scripture records: Woe to them who bind their sins as with a long
rope.

If you draw back from exomologesis, consider gehenna in your heart, which
exomologesis will quench for you, and first imagine the greatness of the
punishment, so that you may not doubt about taking up the remedy. What do we
reckon that treasury of eternal fire, when certain little chimneys of it raise
such strokes of flame that the nearest cities either already are no more, or
expect the same for themselves from day to day? The proudest mountains burst
from the offspring of fire within, and, what proves for us the perpetuity of
judgment, though they burst, though they are devoured, they never come to an
end. Who will not count these present punishments of mountains as patterns of
the judgment that threatens? Who will not agree that such sparks are certain
missiles and practice-darts of some great and unmeasurable hearth?

Therefore, since you know that against gehenna, after those first defenses of
the Lord's baptism, there are still in exomologesis second aids, why do you
desert your salvation? Why do you delay to take up what you know heals you?
Even mute and irrational souls recognize in their time the medicines divinely
assigned them. The stag, pierced by an arrow, knows that to drive the iron and
its irrevocable delays from the wound he must heal himself with dittany. The
swallow, if she has blinded her young, knows how to give them eyes again from
her own celandine. Shall the sinner, knowing that exomologesis was instituted
by the Lord for restoring him, pass by that which restored the Babylonian king
to his kingdoms? For long he had offered repentance to the Lord, working
exomologesis by a seven years' squalor, his nails grown wild after the manner
of an eagle, and the neglect of his hair presenting a lion's horror. Ah, the
hard handling! Him whom men shuddered at, God was receiving. But on the other
side the Egyptian emperor, who, after the people of God had once been
afflicted and long denied to their Lord, rushed into battle in pursuit, after
so many proofs of the plagues, perished when the sea was parted, which was
allowed as a way only to the people, by the rolling back of the waves. For he
had cast away repentance and its service, exomologesis.

Why should I say more of these two planks, as it were, of human salvation,
tending the business of the pen rather than the duty of my conscience? For
since I am a sinner of every mark, and born for nothing but repentance, I
cannot easily be silent about that of which even Adam, the first of the human
stock and of offense against the Lord, restored by exomologesis to his own
paradise, is not silent.
