A possessionless monk is an unhindered athlete, and a light runner, quickly
reaching the prize of the upward calling. A monk of many possessions rejoices
in many revenues, but the possessionless one in crowns of achievements. A
money-loving monk works fiercely, but the possessionless one is at leisure for
prayers and readings. A money-loving monk fills storehouses of gold, but the
possessionless one treasures up in heaven. "Cursed is he who makes an idol and
sets it in secret," so also he who has the passion of love of money; for the
one worships a useless counterfeit, and the other carries as a statue the
fantasy of wealth. CHAPTER 9. On Anger. Anger is a mad passion, and it easily
puts out of their mind those who have knowledge; it makes the soul beastlike,
and makes it turn aside from every encounter. A violent wind will not move a
tower, and wrath does not snatch away a soul without anger. Water is moved by
the force of winds, and a wrathful man is troubled by senseless thoughts. An
irascible monk saw someone, and he sharpened his teeth. An exhalation of mist
thickens the air, and a movement of wrath the mind of an irascible man. A
cloud running under has darkened the sun, and a thought of resentment the
mind. A lion being in a cage moves the bolts continually, and a wrathful man
in a cell thoughts of anger. A calm sea is a delightful sight, but there is
nothing more delightful than a peaceful state; for in a calm sea dolphins
dive, and in a peaceful state God-befitting thoughts swim. A long-suffering
monk is a still spring, furnishing to all a gentle drink, but the mind of an
irascible man is troubled always and will not give water to the thirsty, and
if it should give it, it is muddied and useless; and the eyes of a wrathful
man are troubled and bloodshot, messengers of the troubled heart. But the face
of a long-suffering man is rightly composed, and the eyes gentle, looking
down.

CHAPTER 10. A man's meekness is remembered with God, and a soul without anger
becomes a temple of the Holy Spirit. =79.1156= Christ bows his head in a
long-suffering spirit, and a mind that has been made peaceful becomes a
dwelling of the Holy Trinity. Foxes dwell in a resentful soul, and wild beasts
make their den in a troubled heart. A solemn man flees a shameful lodging, and
God a resentful heart. A stone falling in has troubled water, and an evil word
a man's heart. Remove thoughts of anger from your soul, and let wrath not
lodge in your heart, and you will not be troubled at the time of prayer; for
in the manner that smoke of chaff troubles the eyes, so resentment the mind,
at the time of prayer. The thoughts of a wrathful man are offspring of a
viper, and they devour the heart that bore them. The prayer of a wrathful man
is an abominated incense; and the psalmody of an irascible man a displeasing
sound. The gift of a resentful man is an ant-eaten sacrifice, and it will not
approach the sprinkled altars. A wrathful man sees troubled dreams, and an
irascible man imagines onsets of wild beasts. A long-suffering man sees
visions, encounters of holy angels, and one without resentment exercises
spiritual words, and in the night receives interpretations of mysteries.

CHAPTER 11. On Grief. A grieving monk does not know spiritual pleasure; and
grief is dejection of soul, and it is constituted from thoughts of anger. For
wrath is an appetite for vengeance, and failure of vengeance has begotten
grief; grief is a lion's mouth, and it swallows the grieving man easily. Grief
is a worm of the heart, and it eats up the mother that bore it. A mother is in
pain giving birth to a child; if she bears it, she is released from the pain;
but grief, being born, stirs much pain, and remaining after the birth-pangs,
it pains not a little. A grieving monk does not know spiritual joy, as neither
does one who is strongly fevered know the taste of honey. A grieving monk will
not



move the mind toward contemplation, nor does he send up a pure prayer; for
grief is an obstacle of every good. A bond of the feet is an obstacle of
running, and grief an obstacle of contemplation. A captive from barbarians is
bound with irons, and a captive from passions is bound with grief. Grief has
no strength when the other passions are not present, as neither does a bond,
when those who bind are not present. He who is bound with grief has been
conquered by passions, and he carries about the bond, the proof of the defeat.
For grief is constituted upon failure of fleshly appetite; and appetite is
yoked together with every passion. He who has conquered appetite has conquered
passions, and he who has conquered passions will not be held by grief. A
self-controlled man is not grieved at failure of foods, nor a chaste man
having missed incontinent folly, nor one without anger, having fallen short of
vengeance, nor a humble-minded man, having been deprived of human honor, nor
one free of love of money, having fallen into loss; for they have strongly
turned aside from the appetite of these things; for just as he who has been ar
=79.1157= mored does not receive an arrow, so the passionless man is not
wounded from grief.

CHAPTER 12. A shield is safety for a soldier, and a wall for a city; but
passionlessness is more secure for a monk than both; for often an arrow borne
with a rush has gone through a shield, and a multitude of warriors has razed a
wall, but grief will not prevail over passionlessness. He who masters passions
has mastered grief, but he who is defeated by pleasure will not escape its
bonds. He who grieves continually and pretends passionlessness is like one who
is sick and feigning health; for as the sick man is shown from his color, so
the passionate man is convicted from grief. He who loves the world will be
grieved much, but he who despises the things in it will be gladdened always. A
money-loving man having suffered loss will be grieved bitterly, but he who
despises money will be without grief. A lover of glory will be grieved when
dishonor comes upon him, but the humble-minded man will receive it as a
companion. A furnace has purified unapproved silver, and grief according to
God a heart in sins; continual smelting lessens lead, and worldly grief
diminishes the mind. Gloom dims the working of the eyes, and grief blunts a
contemplative mind; the sun's light does not pass through a depth of water,
and contemplation of light does not illuminate a downcast heart; the rising of
the sun is sweet to all men, but even at this a grieving soul is displeased.
Jaundice takes away the sense of taste, and grief takes away the sense of the
soul. But he who despises the pleasures of the world will not be troubled by
thoughts of grief.

CHAPTER 13. On Acedia. Acedia is slackness of soul, and slackness of soul, not
having what is according to nature, does not stand nobly toward temptations.
For what food is to a body in good condition, this temptation is to a noble
soul. The north wind nourishes offspring, and temptations confirm endurance of
soul. A waterless cloud is driven by wind, and a mind not having patience, by
a spirit of acedia. Spring dew increases the fruit of the field, and a
spiritual word raises the state of the soul. A flood of acedia drives a monk
out of his house, but he who has patience is still always. The acediastic puts
forward visits of the sick, but he fulfills his own purpose. An acediastic
monk is sharp toward ministry, and he accounts his own full persuasion as a
commandment; a slight breeze has bent a weak plant, and a fantasy of
sojourning abroad has drawn an acediastic. The force of winds has not shaken a
tree well planted, and ace =79.1160= dia has not bent a firmly set soul. A
wandering monk is a dry stick of the desert; he has been still a little, and
again he is carried, not wishing it. A plant that is transplanted does not
bear fruit, and a wandering monk will not make fruit of virtue. The sick man
is not



satisfied with one food, and an acediastic monk with one work. One woman will
not suffice a lover of pleasure, and one cell will not suffice an acediastic
monk.

CHAPTER 14. The eye of an acediastic gazes often at the windows, and his mind
imagines those who visit; the door has creaked, and he has started up; he has
heard a voice, and he has peeked through the window, and he does not stand
away from there, until he grows numb sitting. An acediastic reading yawns
much, and is easily carried down toward sleep; he rubs the eyes, and stretches
the hands, and having withdrawn his eyes from the book, he gazes at the wall;
having turned back again he has read a little, and unfolding the ends of the
words he is busy about them; he counts the leaves, and calculates the quires,
he blames the writing and the ornament; and afterward having folded it he has
put the book under the head, and he sleeps a sleep not very deep, for hunger
then wakes his soul, and makes her own cares. An acediastic monk is sluggish
toward prayer, and he will never speak words of prayer; for just as the sick
man does not bear a heavy burden, so also an acediastic will not do the work
of God carefully; for the one has had the strength of the body taken down, and
the other has had the tensions of the soul slackened. Endurance heals acedia,
and doing all things with much assiduity and fear of God. Set a measure for
yourself in every work, and do not stand away before you finish it, and pray
understandingly and with tension, and a spirit of acedia will flee from you.

CHAPTER 15. On Vainglory. Vainglory is an irrational passion, and it is easily
interwoven with every work of virtue. A line drawn upon water is confused, and
labor of virtue in a vainglorious soul. A hand covered in the fold has become
white, and a hidden practice of light shines forth more brightly. Bindweed
twines about a tree, and when it reaches above it dries the root; and
vainglory grows beside the virtues, and does not stand away, until it cuts out
the power. A cluster dragged on the earth rots easily, and virtue is destroyed
when it leans upon vainglory. A vainglorious monk is a hireless worker; he has
undergone the labor, and he has not received the wage. A pierced purse does
not keep what has been thrown in, and vainglory destroys the wages of virtues.
The self-control of a vainglorious man is smoke of a furnace, and both will be
dissolved into air. Wind makes a man's track vanish, and a vainglorious man
almsgiving. A throw of a stone =79.1161= does not reach into heaven, and the
prayer of a man-pleaser will not go up to God.

CHAPTER 16. Vainglory is a sunken rock; if you strike against it, you have
lost the cargo. A prudent man hides a treasure, and a sensible monk labors of
virtue. Vainglory advises to pray in the streets, but he who wars against it
prays in his storehouse. A foolish man publishes his wealth, and he moves many
toward a plot against himself. But you hide your things, for you happen to be
on a road of robbers, until you come into a city of peace, and you will use
your things securely. The virtue of a vainglorious man is a crushed victim,
and it will not be brought up onto the altar of God. Acedia slackens the
tension of the soul, but vainglory nerves a mind that has fallen away from
God; it has made the weak man strong, and the old man more powerful than the
young, only if many witnesses of the things done are present; then fasting is
light, and vigil, and prayer, for the praise of the many wakes the eagerness.
Do not sell the labors for human glories, nor betray the coming glory through
good report. For human glory encamps into dust, and its fame is quenched upon
earth, but the glory of virtue remains unto the age.



CHAPTER 17. On Pride. Pride is a swelling of soul filled with ichor; if it is
ripened, it will burst, and it will make much disgust. A flash of lightning
foretells the sound of thunder, and a presence of vainglory announces pride.
The soul of a proud man goes up into a great height, and from there it casts
him down into a depth. He is sick with pride who has stood himself away from
God, and ascribes the achievements to his own power. And just as one who has
stepped on a spider's web, having fallen through, has been brought down below,
so he who trusts in his own power falls. Much fruit bends down the branches of
a tree, and a multitude of virtue humbles a man's mind. Rotted fruit is
useless to a farmer, and the virtue of a proud man is not useful to God. A
stake bears up a fruit-laden branch, and fear of God a virtuous soul. Just as
the weight of fruit dashes down a branch, so pride casts down a virtuous soul.
Do not give your soul to pride, and you will not see terrible fantasies. For
the soul of a proud man is abandoned by God, and becomes a delight of demons.
By night he imagines a multitude of wild beasts coming upon him, and

CHAPTER 18. Pride has cast an archangel down from heaven, and has made him
fall as lightning upon earth. But humility leads a man up into heaven, and
prepares him to dance with angels. Why are you lifted up, O man, being clay,
and rottenness by nature, and do you raise yourself above the clouds? Look
upon your nature, that you are earth and ashes, and after a little you are
dissolved into dust, now haughty, and after a little a worm. Why do you raise
the neck that after a little is rotting? Great is a man helped by God; he has
been abandoned, and he has recognized the weakness of nature. You have nothing
which you did not receive from God; why then are you proud in what is
another's as if it were yours? Why do you adorn yourself with the grace of God
as with your own possession? Know the one who has given, and do not raise
yourself much; you are a creature of God, do not set aside the Creator; you
are helped by God, do not deny the benefactor. You have gone up into a height
of way of life, but that one has guided; you have achieved virtue, but that
one has worked it. Confess the one who has raised you, that you may remain
firm in the height; know the one of the same race, that he is of the same
substance. Do not through arrogance deny the kinship.

CHAPTER 19. That man is humble, and of due measure; but the same craftsman has
formed both. Do not despise a humble man; for he has stood more securely than
you; he walks upon earth, and does not fall quickly; but the high one, if he
falls, will be shattered. A proud monk is a rootless tree, and he will not
bear an onset of wind. A walled city is an unpuffed mind, and he who dwells in
it will be inviolable. A breeze of wind lifts a straw, and an onset of folly
raises a proud man; a bubble having burst will vanish, and the memory of a
proud man perishes. The word of a humble man is an emollient of the soul, but
that of the proud man is filled with arrogance. The prayer of a humble man
bends God, but the petition of a proud man provokes God. Humility is a crown
of a housetop and keeps the one who has gone up securely. When you have gone
up into the height of the virtues, then you have much need of the safety. For
he who has fallen from ground-level is quickly raised, but he who has fallen
from a high place is in danger unto death. A precious stone is becoming in a
setting of gold, and a man's humility is made bright with many virtues.
