Let the dream then be waking for us. And you, lady Athena, grant both the rest
of fortune and grace, and lay hold of the present speech, and fulfill what was
shown according to its worth, as vividly as it was displayed in the night. And
so that they may also come to pass in deed, clear and sure, this speech now
will be mixed of prayer and hymn for you. All the fairest things, then,
concern Athena and come from Athena, and among them the most noteworthy is the
birth of the goddess: to say it in a word, that she is child of the maker and
king of all, she alone of him alone. For he had none of equal rank from whom
he might make her, but withdrawing into himself he himself from himself both
begets and bears the goddess. So she alone is securely legitimate of the
father, her lineage having come about equally and in agreement with himself.
And still greater than this, that he also sent her up from the fairest of his
own, from the head, so that neither was there anything fairer to rise from
that head of his, nor a better place for Athena to spring forth; but both came
about according to worth. And being born from Zeus both alone and from the
head, she drew after her a fourth wonder no less than these, which they say
happened as an apparition about the cleft of the god's head: for she came up
at once in arms, like the sun rising together with its rays, having been
arrayed from within by the

father. Therefore it is not lawful for her ever to leave the father, but she
is always present and lives with him, as if grown together with him, and she
breathes toward him and is with him, she alone with him alone, remembering her
birth, and paying a fitting wage for the pangs. And she seems to me also to
have been born eldest of the gods, or of some very few indeed of those who
were first at that time. For Zeus would not otherwise have divided each thing,
unless he had seated Athena beside him as assessor and counselor. For indeed
she alone wears the aegis through the ages, and she alone is arrayed in her
father's arms for the Homeric war. And like wonder-workers in a hall, Zeus and
the goddess use the same things at once. And so worthy of reverence is she to
the father, and she has shared in all things, and she alone has taken the
privileges of seniority, that even of the poets whom one might most say have
met with the goddess, Homer, mentioning the aegis itself and the spirit who
tried to wound, says /σμερδαλέην, ἣν οὐδὲ Διὸς δάμνησι κεραυνὸς,/ speaking
surely, since even for Zeus the things from Athena are worth more than his
scepters and thunderbolts. And Pindar in turn says that she sits at the
father's right hand and receives the commands for the gods. For she is greater
than a messenger, she who orders the messengers this and that for others,
receiving first from the father, being as some interpreter to the gods [and
usher, whenever that too is needed]. And since she was born both on the peak
of Olympus and from the peak of Zeus, she holds the peaks of all cities,
having truly taken them by force, and of men as many as are dear to the gods,
it is not Ate who treads their heads, but Athena who holds them up and walks
upon them, keeping the token of her own birth.

Such then is the power of the goddess in heaven and on earth. And of how great
worth she is and has been and will be to gods and men, the children of the
gods who are choristers of Athena might speak better; but we too must try

to speak somehow, using the poets up to a moderate point and giving the speech
so much scope as not to go far aside, or that the whole may come out equal in
measure to the dream. For it is said that when the Giants drew up in Phlegra,
the goddess slew Enceladus and their leaders, and for the other gods the work
became small, since of the other Giants too she slew whatever was strongest,
defending herself especially as they were by nature her particular enemies.
For they were of a race opposite to her. For they had grown from the hollows
of the earth and from the most irrational things, but she from the purest of
what is in the aether. So against their inborn snakes and the rest of what
they dragged from the earth she brought against them the order native to
herself and the fire from herself, until she burned out and destroyed the
race. This deed of hers is sung as accomplished on behalf of the gods and of
the whole divine nature. But the battle of the Titans and of the gods called
Olympian is not, at least to me, more pleasant to tell. But as for the affairs
of men, how many benefits she laid down is easier to consider than to go
through in speech. The greatest of the benefits, then, and the one that runs
through all that comes to be and stretches over all times and places, is that
with Athena leading nothing was ever done amiss among men, nor again will they
ever do anything good without Athena. And if we must also mention the
particular things and not dishonor the myths, let us add olive oil, a drug of
health revealed by her, and clothing, at once an ornament for the body and a
drug of the same health. And indeed she has become a helper to both kinds of
humankind, handing over spinning to women, and assigning to men the use of
arms. And she has cared for both seasons, war and peace, through arms and
laws. And again she dealt in two ways with each of these. First, to leave the
mountain life and the life of each apart and, coming together, to dwell in the
same place

enclosing one common settlement: she is the one who persuaded them, and cities
are gifts of Athena. Whence indeed she is called city-holder by all. And they
set apart the acropolises for her justly, at once a token of her birth, and at
once, as they set apart precincts and places for kings and leaders, so they
set apart the most commanding spots for the goddess who led the whole. This
then I call one common benefaction of the goddess, toward daily life,
constitution and laws; and another, the arts with fire and without fire, which
she divided and divides among individuals. For she alone is leader of all
wisdom. And of the systems for war she both discovered and equipped the two
foremost: for hoplites and horsemen are among Athena's inventions. And there
are double witnesses for each. Of the hoplite kind, the Egyptians first of
those in Asia, and those named after the goddess first of those in Europe, put
forth the shield. For there is also in Egypt a sacred nome of Athena, to which
these things are dedicated. And likewise concerning horsemanship. For it is
said that Erichthonius, the nursling of the goddess, first of men yoked a
chariot of horses; and that for Bellerophon in turn the bit for the
riding-horse came from Athena. And Triptolemus is younger than Erichthonius;
and for him too the seeds would be from Demeter, but the chariot from Athena.
So one must think that all Athena's gifts to men are of such a kind as those
for the wars are, a phalanx of hoplites and an array of horsemen. And I seem
about to add what just now escaped me, that the naval things too were
Athena's. And let it have a double place in the speech. For toward wars, if
one should also divide thus in two, those on land and those at sea, it will
show that the goddess took thought beforehand. And if again those on land are
divided in two, the naval itself, one and the same, stretches to both:
peaceful, the merchant ships

And the same is most loving of men and most powerful. Of her power, what
greater sign need one say than that she conquers everywhere? For Victory is
not mistress of Athena, but Athena is always mistress of Victory. And her love
of men the tales about Orestes witness. Whom, when he had fled from Argos to
Athens, and was on trial at Athens under the Eumenides, when the votes were
equal, she added her own and saves him. And still even now she saves all, if
they become equal. For from that time it was decided to add to the equal votes
the one from Athena that acquits. And since Athena had to be a maiden through
the ages and none of gods or men was to touch her according to the law of
Aphrodite Pandemos, wondrous things are found for her here too from the
father: for she takes the prizes of the one blessed with fair children. For
Leto
wandering through all earth and sea she leads to the place where it was fated
for her to give birth. And when she was giving birth, she serves as midwife
and receives the children, and crowns Apollo according to the paean of the
Greeks. So that Artemis is goddess of childbirth for the other women, but this
goddess has become childbirth-helper to Artemis for the births. And she
herself seems to have been set as teacher of both, of the art and of the
choice. For she is a maiden

alone after Athena. If then Apollo and Artemis are wondrous beauties, one
might ascribe the providence to Athena. And yet if even gods may reasonably
have thanks on their own behalf, how is it not reasonable for us at least on
behalf of the gods, that she introduced more benefactors for us? Hunts then
and hunting-parties are play of Apollo and Artemis, but they are established
as belonging to the goddess, for one reason because it is a part of the
warlike art, or rather an image. And they say these things concern Athena; and
for another reason, that she also devised how the eponyms of hunting would
come to be. And she would belong also to Asclepius in a great part of the
whole.
And the eldest of the Athenians also founded an altar of Athena Health. And if
their vote is right, what greater thing need one find toward the accord of
Asclepius and Athena? And she also shares with Poseidon in his works, both
those of horses and those of the sea, having first found the bit, and having
built the first ship. And indeed the goddess's partnership with Hermes is
clear to everyone who calls Hermes of speech and of the marketplace and of
traffic. And for the one of contests she has also been set in place of a
choregos. For Homer even outright shows her as of the contest: for he judges
the prizes through her. Having discovered then singing to the aulos and to the
lyre and to the cithara, she gives one to one of the Muses, one to Hermes, and
one to Apollo. And she has a place everywhere so that Ares is a child beside
her in his very own affairs, and Apollo set her in front of his own oracles
and ordered that sacrifice be made first to her. And Hephaestus is compelled
by love to practice the craft, but nature falls short. And Graces stand about
her hands. And the Dioscuri dance the pyrrhic under her. And Iacchus and the
Eleusinian women pursue the goddess's choruses, and Poseidon yielded when he
was beaten. And the

excess of her power is that even the plant was shown to be a symbol of
victory, as it was lawful for her not only to conquer, but also to crown those
who conquer. For indeed the poets ascribe all the most impossible things to
her, whenever they wish to show them as practicable and possible: Odysseuses
swimming in the midst of deserted seas, and young men coming from old, and
beautiful from not beautiful, and those destroying the suitors from the
nations with the help of a paltry and laughable auxiliary lad and two
herdsmen, and things more outlandish than these. For they say that Bellerophon
was carried on winged Pegasus, holding to Athena's gift, and accomplished
among other things the mastering of the Chimaera, being out of range of her
fire from herself. And that Perseus was carried on his own wings into the
Gorgons' lands with Athena escorting him, and was neither caught by the sight,
and when he cut off the head and brought it back, he made those who had
ordered these things in insolence and most of the people into stones, but
himself escaped unharmed to the end, having Athena's presence as an antidote
for all things. And the things toward Heracles follow and are still greater
than these. For she appears as the one who enrolled him a god among gods, if
indeed even when he was among men she led him to all the labors. And this:
having brought him living into Hades she led him out living, after he mastered
Cerberus, and he came out so far beyond likelihood that he even
released another of his companions who was lodged there before his time
and brought him out with himself; and this: wherever he had to raise the
bow against the spirits that opposed him, he took courage with Athena
present. So that to me from these
things she seems to have done nothing else than to speak a motion to the gods,
having voted that Heracles be a god; and they received it as if Zeus himself
were introducing it. She appears then to have taken thought for Apollo and
Artemis, how they would come to be,

Athena as his gate-figure.

But to which of all is the goddess not profitable? Or who is a safer partner?
Or what inescapable fire is there when she is not following, but leading? She
who alone of all the gods, and likewise of all the goddesses, is not named
after Victory, but is of the same name. And she alone has been called Worker
and Providence, having taken up the titles of saving the whole ordinance. And
prophets and priests invoke her as purifying and averter of evil and overseer
of the most complete purifications. Reasonably. For if one must put down the
myths and speak into the open the things of the goddess, she is the one who
keeps off our truly common enemies, and settles well each man's household war,
removing the continuous and inborn foes, by whom houses and cities are laid
waste before they hear a trumpet, one might say, and giving each the true and
sovereign victory, far removed from the Cadmean and in truth Olympian. Through
whom folly and wantonness and cowardice and disorder and faction and insolence
and arrogance toward the gods and all such things one might name withdraw; and
prudence and temperance and courage and concord and good order and prosperity
and honor of the gods and from the gods come in instead. And to speak in a
word, Athena's share, the assembly of the gods, is all affairs. Therefore she
is also nearest to Zeus and about everything the same always seems good in
both. And for me it is good to stop somewhere here. For the speech has gone
back to the beginning, or rather has come to the very end. For one who said
from these things that she is almost the power of Zeus would not miss it. So
why should one be petty in narrating her particular deeds, when it is possible
to say that the works of Zeus are common to Zeus and

Athena. But O forerunner of the royal halls, as a chorus sang in Aeschylus,
both of the heavenly and ancestral and of the greatest in our land, which you
showed me by night, grant honors from both the kings, and grant that I be at
