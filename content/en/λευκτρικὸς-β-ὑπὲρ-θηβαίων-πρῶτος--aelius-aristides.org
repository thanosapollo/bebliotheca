I think, men of Athens, that you are not deliberating today for the first time
about the Thebans' alliance, nor on the basis of whatever we may say, but if I
too remember anything, that you have already examined the matter many times
before the present embassy. Since, however, the Lacedaemonians have
nevertheless found men who will speak for them with a fair show, come, let me
too join in saying what I know on this side, making my zeal for these men so
much less than that of the others for those they have chosen, as my zeal for
you is greater. And I wish first to remind you, when Phoebidas was reported to
have seized the Cadmea (and Phoebidas, to me, is the Lacedaemonians), how you
were disposed, men of Athens, and what judgment you formed. Was it the one
some now bid you take, that of sharing with the Lacedaemonians and taking part
in such arrogance and greed, or quite the contrary: you judged those men the
worst of mankind, and you shared the Thebans' joy and their pain, and you
prayed that it might be granted you to take vengeance? Now, then, this
opportunity has arrived, worthy of your just judgment, men of Athens, and
worthy of the Thebans' eagerness, which they had to free their country. If,
then, you are so strangely disposed as to pray for these things, and yet,
seeing them present, to betray and abandon them, it is not the act of
Athenians, but I do not know whose, that you have suffered. For I think no one
of all men is so full of madness and unreason as to do the opposite of what he
prayed for, when it is present to him. But if not even a madman would suffer
this, nor would be pained that something turned out according to his wish, but
would surely be grateful to fortune and to the occasion, what remains but to
stand with those who have succeeded in the very things you wanted, and that
too when they call you and add to you the

pride in what has been done. For they, judging that if you do not help them
nothing of what they have succeeded in is of any further use to them, by the
very requests they make about the future bring over to you the glory of the
victory they have won.

I for my part would most have wished that you had shared in the battle itself;
for you would have had no finer deed as partners, nor one in which you could
more justly take pride. But I think even the present moment has in a way come
opportunely. For I see that the dangers have become less, while the honor for
you is no meaner. That the Thebans should need you from the beginning was
nothing surprising; but that after their success, and that in things greater
than they could have hoped, they are no more freed from needing you: this has
become both great and fine, and to me at least a source of great pleasure. And
I shall not hesitate to say that it even seems better to me to help them after
they have called than if they had not called. For in that case they might
perhaps have said later that they did not need you; but coming in person and
paying court to you no less than men in misfortune, they could never, I
suppose, deny what they will be shown to be convicted of by themselves. Some
say the Thebans are insensible, and perhaps they speak truly in some other
respect; but the fault that is, so to speak, always common to all men, they
have not suffered. Whereas even those credited with great good sense lose
their heads when they succeed beyond hope, these men after so great a victory
have stood far from this. For men who come to you and know that even if they
beat the Lacedaemonians ten times over, it is of no use to them so long as you
are absent: how are they not of sound mind? And indeed the Lacedaemonians will
not have a just complaint against you, if you take hold of the Thebans'
affairs. Let them consider what they themselves once decided about Aegina and
Potidaea; and if they are willing to look at the truth, they will know they
cannot speak otherwise or cast any other vote than that you must not let them
go nor let them off. For they

could not even now show or charge what of all things they had been wronged in
by us at that time. Yet they demanded that we let Aegina go autonomous and
withdraw from Potidaea, much like what Cleombrotus lately enjoined upon the
Thebans. For you know, of course, that nothing is more specious than the
Lacedaemonians when they seek to grasp more. Then they are champions of the
Greeks, then they are terrible guardians of freedom, whenever they wish to
snatch something away. And when we would not endure it nor obey, doing well,
men of Athens, and preserving the city's pride, then already they took along
all they could and made war, thinking little of the oaths and the covenants
they made with us when we took Euboea, and little of the trophies and the
sea-fights, of which who would ever have thought that, so long as they
existed, they would remember them and yet do even a little contrary to their
judgment against our city? They took no thought even of the challenge by which
we challenged them to give and receive justice by argument about our
differences. But having lightly done away with all this and set it at nothing,
I pass over the yearly invasions and the endless destruction of the country
and the contests one by one; but they emptied our rolls, they made most of our
allies revolt, and they brought some over by force, until they stripped the
city bare. And at last they took away the grain-convoy, they seized the ships,
and they razed the walls with our own hands, O Zeus.

When, then, they judged that we should suffer these things in return for the
Aeginetans and for besieging Potidaea, what would they say they themselves
ought to suffer in return for occupying the Cadmea, having entered in time of
peace and under these excellent treaties, O earth and gods and justice, by
which, that I may pass from one thing to another, but I let this go for the
present. But that at any rate, having then judged that those men must be
autonomous, and having done these things because you did not concede them, to
the city's cost, they will have nothing at all to say

themselves now, if, having shown such arrogance toward the Thebans and having
begun such injustice, they shall pay the penalty both to those men and to you:
this, I think, is shown from these things. For it is not even equal to bring
over such-and-such who have revolted, or to bid such-and-such use the laws one
uses oneself, and to occupy and hold the Thebans' acropolis by force contrary
to the treaties, if one must name the greater, their own. For you know, of
course, that neither we nor the Thebans first disturbed this, nor were we
eager about these things, but they, taking the King as a partner, forced us,
and so we conceded. I marvel, then, if, having imposed on others the necessity
of minding their own affairs, they themselves, after meddling so greatly and
in such a way, will ask for help, not undergo punishment. Yes, by Zeus, but
they have already paid a sufficient penalty, and it is the city's custom not
to remember past wrongs, and some such arguments meet us on the score of
justice, to which I should most gladly say this: if it had been the reverse,
and the Thebans had appeared coming against the Lacedaemonians then for the
first time, not those men against these, what would those who bid you help the
Lacedaemonians have said? Not that they have been wronged and that one must
for this reason stand with them? I know with what a great outcry this would
have been said. If, then, [if on the one hand] they had been first wronged,
they would have put this forward and for this one would have had to help; but
since they have been seen doing wrong, shall we help them all the same because
one must not remember past wrongs? And when shall we stand with the Thebans,
or from what, men of Athens, shall we make the examination of which of the two
we must choose, if we shall put none of what they have done into the account?
For we shall not, I suppose, bid them be decided by lot, but to whichever
their deeds, I think, lead us, to those, if we do what we ought, we shall give
help. But as to "one must not remember past wrongs" and "the Lacedaemonians
have already paid the penalty," if the one were true, they would have granted
both. What do I mean? The Lacedaemonians are not yet present, men of Athens.
How so? At the point to which they once brought us? They would suffer much

if they are going to come to that. For one part of that former war [a day and
an occasion], I mean the Sicilian, brought us more evils and troubles than
what the Lacedaemonians now lament. Or against which of those things do you
wish us to examine these? Against the sea-fights, so many of which we lost, or
against the battles on land, or against the burning of the triremes, or
against the all-destroying retreat? But by Zeus and the gods I do not know if
I shall suffice to go through these things without tears.

What, then, did the now-humbled Lacedaemonians do, when so great and so large
a disaster encompassed the city? Did they judge it enough? On the contrary,
they then united all mankind against it: Tissaphernes, Cyrus, the Phoenician
triremes, the King's slaves, all the Greeks, until, going through everything,
they reduced our fleet at Aegospotami. And when such a disaster had befallen
the city, how did they act? Occupying the entrance they kept the ships from
the Piraeus; and then there was famine. Nor was even this enough, but with the
islands held, with all the other possessions held, with slaves having
deserted, with the country being ravaged by those at Decelea, and with the
ground alone remaining to the city, they were so far from being willing to
help if anyone did us wrong, that they did not even think us worthy of a
settlement in such evils, but even the envoys who came to them from us they
first sent away altogether unsuccessful, and at last they held on until they
filled the city with corpses, and this we paid them as an extra of evils. Then
they made a truce on condition of adding some further evil, on condition that
we hand over to them the remaining ships and raze the walls with our own
hands. Perhaps, then, someone will say that this very thing is to remember
past wrongs, to examine these things closely; but it is quite the contrary
that is shown from this, that we do not remember past wrongs. Why? Because
while the Lacedaemonians have not yet suffered what we suffered, nor have they
come to an equal point, one cannot say that we have taken a sufficient
penalty. And if

we do not yet have the adequate one, we escape the appearance of remembering
past wrongs if we proceed against them. For I think that whoever, having
obtained his rights, then clings to the charges in order to work some further
evil, this man has been provoked beyond the occasion, and I call the excess
beyond what is due remembering past wrongs; but whoever has not yet obtained
what is fitting, if he seeks how he shall obtain it, I think no one would
justly resent him, nor can I apply this word to him. For if we shall say that
those who wish to take their rights remember past wrongs, what prevents us
from admiring as noble those who take the lead in doing harm? Or shall we also
think that those who prosecute wrongdoers in the courts remember past wrongs?
To whom even more than according to what they have suffered is given by the
laws for the account of punishment. But it is another thing, I think, for a
man to remember what he has suffered and to remember past wrongs; and both of
these are marked off by measure. And let the man who has reminded you of the
Thebans' faults learn that he does not come into an equal account. First, for
I do not know what I should say first among many things, if someone asked me,
if you join the Thebans, in return for what and for what reason did you
resolve to make war on the Lacedaemonians? I should say neither for private
interests nor for the wrongs they once did our city, but because the Thebans
seemed to us to speak more justly about the war now existing for them, for
this reason, I should say, we chose these men.

From what, then, did I turn aside to the examination of what the
Lacedaemonians did in the Decelean war? From wishing to show you this, that
the Lacedaemonians themselves, for far meaner charges, brought us into far
greater evils than those now upon them. What, then, does the accusation of the
Thebans mean here, whom, even if they did wrong then, one must not now abandon
for the reasons I have stated? And indeed if one must say something also about
those former things at a little more length, I do not for my part exempt the
Thebans, nor would I say they are

altogether without fault; I am not so forgetful nor so shameless. But judging
them against the Lacedaemonians' deeds, I find that they ask to be excused for
the very thing I myself avoid. For when they themselves do not exceed by a
little, how do they not do what I think it strange to say? For you all know,
of course, that it was not the Thebans who had the Lacedaemonians and made war
on us then, but the Lacedaemonians who had the Thebans. What does this mean? I
think that those who make war in order that they themselves may rule and hold
power do this from desire of greed and, by Zeus, from envy of those who
already stand as champions, while those who follow and take part share in an
unjust deed, yet cover the blame with another just claim, that of being
faithful to their leaders. And if I judge this rightly, I find the one set
worthy of anger and the other of praise, judged from the same things; and I
know that if the Lacedaemonians had been willing to mind their own affairs,
neither the Corinthians' anger nor the Megarians' complaint nor the Thebans'
folly would have had any force, nor would it have added to the hatred the
power of ever doing the city harm. But as it is, I think, enmity and envy and
habitual distrust were what moved the Lacedaemonians against us; and all the
rest, being clever as they are, they put forward as pretexts not untimely. And
they showed this not least about the walls immediately after the war against
the barbarian. Or did the Thebans then too bid them send those who would
prevent the walls from being raised? Then do I blame the Phliasians or
such-and-such for the evils of the war, and ascribe the deeds to these, and
not to those who used these men against us in order that they themselves might
rule Greece? Or shall I blame Pharnabazus, or Pissuthnes among the barbarians,
and not the King who is master of the whole? And who ever attributed to slaves
whatever they do when ordered, and not to the masters who lead them to these
things?

Besides, then, that equal blame is not owed to both for those former deeds
which someone says

unjustly went against these, who were once their own allies. So that if one
must not remember past wrongs against either, it is surely worth helping the
Thebans for these reasons. And if one must say the most familiar thing of all,
we must no longer examine the Thebans; for that was needed long ago, before
the alliance. But now the alliance, on whose account one would have examined
these things, has already existed many times before. And what is the outcry
and the heavy vote, which one Theban man cast? And even if it seemed good to
all of them in the highest degree, they ought to have brought it forward then,
when they first came into need of you. For then, I suppose, they were nearer
the charges. And I for my part would gladly ask those who now strain on behalf

the Lacedaemonians marched out of the Peloponnese to chasten, as they said,
the Thebans, because they had not campaigned with them against the Piraeus,

the tyrants, and the Thebans came here calling you and begging not to be
overlooked, and you were masters both to abandon them and not to: what did
those who now stand with the Lacedaemonians think then? For if they thought
one must abandon and overlook the Thebans, they did not persuade you of what

judgment, not only against yours.

I would not myself call them clumsy and shameless, yet someone who did so
would not miss one of the two: clumsy, if they have reversed their post, and
having been silent when one ought to have examined and accused these things,
they now teach, when even if they had spoken then, it would have been fitting
thereafter to keep quiet; and shameless, if having spoken then too and been
defeated, they force the same things again later, past the occasion. For if
you were sufficiently persuaded by the Thebans then in their speeches that
they did not jointly bring about the enslavement, how is it right now to hear
about these things, which you then agreed with them when they were clearing
themselves? But if you did not believe this, yet from later experience of
their goodwill in the time of need you were of such a mind that even about
those things, though you could not be persuaded otherwise, you helped them
because of the subsequent intimacy and trust, for what reason will you now
abandon these same men, when of all things one must more eagerly stand with
them? For first, men of Athens, it is not the same to do good to men who are
already allies and have become friends purely, as before they were set in this
rank. Next, if then the Thebans' services moved you and because of those you
thought you must run risks on their behalf, now those remain and what has come
from you to them has been added. Then it is also utterly terrible that when we
had neither triremes nor walls nor had we recovered at all from the great and
continuous misery, then

we appeared caring for them and not overlooking them, but now, when the city
has returned, thank heaven, to its affairs and has recovered its strength, not
only to shun helping them, but even to take up war against them, having
nothing to blame, and to make more account now of the Lacedaemonians' safety
than of our own then, as if someone, being sick and in a weak state, were
eager to help his friends, when he cannot even walk outside the house, but
having recovered and gained power, then struck and throttled and beat these
same men instead of helping them against others. To put it in a word, not to
receive the Thebans as allies nor to use them as partners in counsel was a
judgment; but having received them and made them such, then to abandon them
will seem to be the limit of madness. For it is not the man who failed to save
one he did not think worthy that has done wrong, but the man who stood forth
as well-disposed and then does harm by a change. And it will be the strangest
of all things if, when they had become enemies and when we had charges to
make, we resolved to admit the Thebans and made them allies, but now that they
have become allies we shall appear remembering the original difficulties and
shall make them enemies again, in the same way as we then made them allies
from enemies. Now of disputed matters that come into court the vote is the
settlement and the end, but of those that come before you, the people, what
else would one say but the decrees? Well then, in these it is written to help
the Thebans. Then those whom we have already made allies, and about whom we
have already decided what one must do, do we examine these from the beginning?
As if even today, having judged to use them as allies, we should then on the
morrow consider again whether we must do this very thing we have resolved, or,
by Zeus, having judged it today at once, we should then again consider about
this very thing whether we must. One can hear from the older men that
Thessalian horsemen once came here under alliance and during the battle went
over to the Lacedaemonians.

Do not, then, think it so great a charge to change sides before joining in the
fight, as it is, having shared many great contests with some, then easily to
set one's arms against them: that I may thus leave aside the not slight
difference between Thessalians and Athenians. For we shall not, at any rate,
say we forget the battle at Haliartus, which we won in common against the
Lacedaemonians, nor the later great one at Corinth, nor the one against
Agesilaus at Coronea; and I pass over the others, for perhaps it is not easy
to list them all. Yes, by Zeus: the King's clever device, by which he kept us
at odds on unequal terms during the great war. And what greater token of
faithlessness could one name than claiming to be friend to neither, yet enemy
to both, and hating those who stood against him no more than those with whom
he was on each occasion? For this reason we might even emulate that man's
perjuries and all the other evils he has been eager for: but let it not be
among shameful things, or rather altogether strange, that being Athenians we
imitate the Persian's wickedness. For you and he must not even deliberate
alike about Greece, but he as an enemy and one who hates, you as rulers and
champions. I think, then, it has no good argument otherwise, nor is it worthy
of this title, to appear worse than the Thebans. For if they, though they have
succeeded, do not leave us, but abide by the existing pledges, while you will
take no thought, abandoning and leaving those men, and that when you are
called, how will what one must not even say not come about? Nor is that other
thing fine either, that you should have been supposed better by the Thebans
than you shall be shown by yourselves. For when those men call you as
remembering what is just and keeping faith, neither to listen nor to give
yourselves to the others: how would this have escaped that shame? And it is
worth examining how you yourselves stand toward those of your allies who
revolt. Do you

stand mildly and moderately? Those who have tried it would not say so. For
some of them will be found to have paid a far greater penalty than open
enemies, and not even one would justly blame or find fault with you for this.
For in general it is not possible to bear being wronged, but to be wronged by
those by whom one least ought raises anger that cannot be appeased. Do not,
then, appear yourselves overstepping what you have defined as just for others,
but consider that for some of those men there is even some pretext through
which they might sometime obtain pardon, such as the tributes being greater,
being driven, some of them privately exacting extra, such things; but in your
case not even one would say any such thing, nor could you yourselves have it.
How, then, must there not be some excess of good faith? For we shall not, I
suppose, say it is noble to work with some only until they do something good,
and then to become enemies, changing oneself as if one had been wronged. For
in general, to abandon friends, whether they are unfortunate or doing well, is
the name of the same shame; next, it seems to me at least so much worse and
more wicked to be faithless to those who succeed, as those who abandon men in
disasters might even plead inability, through which they cannot rescue them,
while those who are ill-disposed to men doing well would have left as their
remaining reputation that of envy and malice and of no civic cause: which
exceeds all reproaches among men. For just as, I think, a steersman who lets
go the ship in the storm and the squall has pardon, but if, when it is
possible to sail with a fair wind, he abandons it, there is accusation and
inescapable blame, so also in such cases the difficulties of the occasions
give those who have stood off a defense, while arrogance in times of success
works a clear and pure blame. Next, nothing seems to me more senseless than to
be willing to share toils and evils with those who need it, but when the time
to gather the fruits comes, then to seek others with whom one will again
suffer hardship. Besides, I do not even see the end. For if we shall always
receive those who are being overcome, but when we have set them where

Why, then, must we still run risks, if we shall not have the prize when we
have acquired it? But by Zeus these things are clear, yet to take up power
again with the Lacedaemonians is otherwise without fear and has no fear
whatsoever. But I, and let no one be startled at the excess, would not
hesitate to say that I far more fear the Lacedaemonians when they are masters
of their own than the Thebans when they are masters of Greece. For these
latter, even if they had the empire, seem to me they would not keep it, being
too small for the affairs, but in a moment of time they would easily become
again what they were; while those, so long as they have any starting-point at
all, seem to me they would not with difficulty grow little by little, since
even of old, setting out from quite small and mean towns, Boeum and Cytinium
and Erineus, and being no more august than anyone, but even coming where they
now live as a thing to be content with, through us they first held two parts
of the Peloponnese, having destroyed Messene, and then they ruled all Greece,
having little by little come into plenty of power. But, O you who fear in
vain, the gratitude they owe us for the help will make them more moderate,
someone might say. But what gratitude is there which the Lacedaemonians ever
know toward any man, they who have suffered the opposite of other men? For
others, even if they have been wronged to the utmost, become milder through
later services, but for the Lacedaemonians, if anything else has been
practiced, this too has been trained: when they have been treated best, then
to go against those who did it. First, that we may begin from our own, these
men, saved by us in the war against the barbarians (for you know, of course,
that they themselves furnished ten ships to the fleet and four hundred
hoplites to the Gates, and these were all they had at first, which even if
they had been at war with the Megarians would have been a joke, while the
whole hung upon us and our triremes, which in fact prevented

the Peloponnese from being taken city by city), yet nevertheless, having
experienced so great and so large a service, and themselves sending envoys,
when Mardonius invited us into alliance with gifts, and uttering words no less
than now, not to betray them, not much time passed after this and they were
preventing us from walling the city, and they asked for help at Ithome, but
when it came they sent it away in dishonor, and at last, having set us into
open war, they worked upon us what they themselves ought to have suffered.
Again in this war, when the King had become useful to them and had joined in
accomplishing the greatest things, how did they remember the gratitude?
Through Cheirisophus and Clearchus they brought Cyrus against him. And this
was not enough, but again going through all Europe with Agesilaus, imitating
Agamemnon's expedition against Troy, they plundered and harried Pharnabazus,
and what evil did they not do to the King's land and his satraps, until they
failed in the power to war with us and the Thebans and him together? And what
again of the Thebans' case? As soon as they had joined them in finishing the
war against us, they were at once in danger about their own. Very well indeed,
by Zeus, and most justly of men, someone might say; for so they were most
going to see clearly their own folly. I say so too. But it was not for this
that they turned against them, that they might gratify us and take vengeance
on our behalf; for that at least is a joke; they would have punished
themselves sooner than these men for those. But they came, I think, against
these too in due course. To put it briefly, they have gone on inheriting the
wars from which each party had delivered them. Do the Lacedaemonians feel
gratitude to their benefactors and pay back more than they receive? What,
then, is the cause, and what is their nature? They seem to me to judge the
