* On that saying, If your enemy hungers

To those who did not come to the assembly, and on the apostolic saying that
says, "If your enemy hungers, feed him," and concerning bearing a grudge.

=51.171= αʹ. Nothing, as it seems, have we profited, having stretched out a
long word to you the other day on behalf of the eagerness concerning the
assemblies; for again the church is desert of children for us. Wherefore I am
compelled again to appear burdensome and heavy, rebuking those present,
accusing those left behind; those, because they have not put away the
listlessness, you, because you have not taken hold of the salvation of the
brothers. I am compelled to appear heavy and burdensome, not on behalf of
myself and my possessions, but on behalf of you and your salvation, than which
nothing is more precious to me. Let him who wishes be burdened, and call me
tiresome and shameless; but I will not cease continually troubling about the
same things; for nothing is better for me than this shamelessness. =51.172=
Perhaps, perhaps, if nothing else, at least having blushed at this, not to be
continually troubled about the same things, you will someday take hold of the
care of your brothers. For what profit is there to me of the praises, when I
do not see you advancing according to virtue? And what harm to me from the
silence of those who hear, when I see your piety increasing? For the praise of
the speaker is not the clapping, but the zeal of the hearers concerning piety;
not the tumult at the time of the hearing, but the eagerness through all time.
The clapping has gone out together from the mouth, and having been poured into
air it has perished; but that those who hear become better brings an unaging
and immortal reward both to the speaker and to those obey =51.173= ing. The
praise of your shout makes the speaker splendid here, but the piety of your
soul gives much boldness at the tribunal of Christ to the one who taught. So
that if anyone of those who speak is in love, let him be in love not of the
clappings, but of the profit of those who hear.

It is not a chance evil to neglect brothers, but last punishment, and
inescapable vengeance; and this the one who buried the talent showed. He was
accused of nothing on behalf of his own life; for he did not become bad
concerning the deposit; for he gave it back whole; but nevertheless he became
bad also concerning the working of the deposit. For he did not double what was
entrusted; wherefore he was also punished. Whence it is clear that even if we
are earnest and well composed, even if we have much desire concerning the
hearing of the divine Scriptures, it does not suffice for our salvation. For
it is necessary to double the deposit, and it becomes double when along with
our own salvation we also take hold of the care of others. Since that man also
said, See, you have your own safe; but this did not suffice for him toward a
defense. For it was necessary, he says, to put down what was put down upon the
bankers. And consider with me how light the commands of the Master are. For
men make those who lend the master's money responsible also for the
collection. You put it down, you, he says, collect; I have no account toward
the one who received. But God is not so, but he commands only to put it down,
and no longer makes us responsible also for the collection. For the speaker is
master of advising, not of persuading. Wherefore I make you responsible only
for the putting down, he says, but no longer for the collection. What is
lighter than this? But


nevertheless the slave called the master who was so gentle and loving of man
hard. For such are the customs of ungrateful and listless household servants;
they always transfer the causes of their own sins onto the masters. For this
reason that man, being twisted and bound, was led out into the outer darkness.
Which, that we may not suffer, let us put down the teaching upon the brothers,
whether they are persuaded or not persuaded.

For if they are persuaded, they will profit both themselves and us; but if
they are not persuaded, they draw upon themselves inescapable punishment, but
they would not be able to harm us even in the least. For we have done our
part, having advised; but if they should not be persuaded, no harm would come
to us from this. For the charge is not not to have persuaded, but not to have
advised; and after the exhortation and the advice, the continual and the
constant, the account will no longer be toward us, but toward them, with God.
I would have wished then to know clearly that you continue exhorting them, and
if those remain throughout in listlessness, I would never have troubled you;
but now I fear lest perhaps from your contempt and neglect they remain
uncorrected. For it is impossible that a man continually enjoying exhortation
and teaching not become better and more earnest. And the proverb which I am
about to speak is popular; yet this very thing confirms it. For a drop of
waters continually falling hol =51.174= lows a rock, it says. And yet what is
softer than water? And what harder than rock? But nevertheless continuance
conquered nature. And if continuance conquers nature, much more would it be
able to prevail over choice. Christianity is not playthings, beloved, nor a
side work. Continually we say these things, and we do nothing more.

βʹ. How do you think I am pained, remembering that in the festivals the crowds
of the assemblies imitate long seas of sea, but now not even the smallest part
of that crowd is gathered here?

Where now are those who troubled us in the feasts? Those I seek, and for those
I lament, considering how great a multitude of those being saved is lost, how
great a loss of brothers I endure, how the things of salvation are reduced to
few, and the greater part of the body of the Church resembles a dead and
unmoving body. And what is that to us, he says? To you then especially, who do
not heal, who do not exhort and advise, to you who do not put on necessity,
and drag with force, and lead away from the much listlessness. For that one
must not be useful to himself only, but also to many, Christ showed, having
called us salt and leaven and light; and these things are useful and
profitable to others. For the lamp does not shine for itself, but for those
sitting in darkness; and you are a lamp, not that you alone may enjoy the
light, but that you may bring back that one who has wandered.

For what profit of a lamp, when it does not shine for the one sitting in the
darkness? And what profit of a Christian, when he gains no one nor brings
anyone back to virtue? Again the salt does not tighten itself only, but also
contracts the rotten parts of bodies, and does not let them perish having
melted away. So then you too, since God has made you spiritual salt, tighten
and contract the rotten members, that is, the listless and vulgar of the
brothers, and having freed them from listlessness, as from some rottenness,
unite them to the remaining body of the Church. For this reason he also called
you leaven; for the leaven too does not leaven itself, but the remaining lump,
the great and unspeakable, the small and short. So then you too; even if you
are few according to number, yet become many and


powerful in faith and in eagerness according to God. As then the leaven is not
weak because of its shortness, but prevails because of the heat lying in it
and the power of the quality; so then you too will be able to bring back many
more to the same eagerness with you, if you wish. But if they put forward the
summer; for I hear them saying these things too, that The stifling is violent
now, the heat unbeara =51.175= ble, we are not able to be crowded and pressed
in the multitude, flowing with sweat from all sides and burdened by the heat
and the crowding; I am ashamed for them, believe me; for the excuses are
womanish, or rather not even for those do the pretexts suffice toward pardon,
whose bodies are softer, and nature weaker. Yet even if it is shameful to
answer toward such a defense, it is nevertheless necessary. For if those put
forward such things and do not blush, much more must we not be ashamed
contradicting these things. What then should I say to those putting these
things forward? I wish to remind them of the three children in the furnace and
in the flame, who seeing the fire running round them from all sides, and
poured around the mouth and the eyes, and the very breath, did not cease
singing that holy and mystic hymn to God with the creation, but more eagerly
than those spending time in a meadow, standing then in the midst of the pyre,
they sent up the praise to the common Master of all; and along with these
three children, I think it right to remind them again of the lions in Babylon,
and of Daniel and the pit; and not of this only, but also of another pit and
prophet, and of the mire choking Jeremiah up to the neck. And having gone up
from the pits, I wish to lead those who put forward the heat into the prison,
and to show Paul there and Silas bound to the wood, full of weals and wounds,
having had the whole body torn by the multitude of the blows, in the midst of
the night hymning God, and accomplishing that holy all-night service.

For how is it not absurd that those holy ones, being in furnace, and fire, and
pit, and beasts, and mire, and prison, and wood, and blows, and guards, and
the unbearable terrors, never blamed any of these, but with much intensity and
boiling eagerness spent time continually in prayers and holy hymns, but we,
having endured none, not small, not great, of the sufferings enumerated,
through heat, and a brief warmth and sweat, neglect their own salvation, and
having left the assemblies wander outside, wasting ourselves on gatherings
that have nothing sound? So great a dew of the divine oracles, and you put
forward heat? The water which I will give him, says Christ, will become in him
a fountain of water leaping up into eternal life; and again, He who believes
in me, as the Scripture said, rivers of living water will flow from his belly.
Having fountains, tell me, and spiritual rivers, do you fear sensible heat?
And in the marketplace, tell me, where there is so much tumult and crowding
and much burning, how do you not put forward stifling and warmth? For you
would not be able to say this, that there it is possible to enjoy a colder
nature of the air, but here all the stifling has been gathered for us, but
quite the contrary, here both from the underlying pavement, and from the other
construction of the house (for it has been raised to an unspeakable height)
the air is lighter and colder; but there much sun everywhere, and much
crowding and smoke and dust, and other things far more than these stretching
the unpleasantness. Whence it is clear that of listless =51.176= ness and of a
soul that has fallen and been deprived of the flame of the Spirit these
irrational pretexts are.


γʹ. These things I say now, stretching them out not so much toward those as
toward you who do not draw them, who do not raise them from listlessness, and
drag them toward this table of salvation. And servants about to fulfill a
common service call their fellow servants; but you, about to meet this
spiritual ministry, overlook the fellow servants being deprived of the gain.
What then, if they should not wish, he says? Make them wish by continual
attendance; for if they see us pressing upon them, they will altogether wish.
But these things are an excuse and a pretext. How many fathers then are here
and do not have their sons standing with them? Was it difficult for you to
draw even the children? Whence it is clear that the rest also remain outside
not only through their own listlessness, but also through your contempt. But
if not before, now at least rise up, and let each enter into the church with
his member, and let father wake and urge son, and son the one who begot, and
men wives, and wives men, and master slave, and brother brother, and friend
friend toward the gathering here; or rather let us call not only friends, but
also enemies to this common treasure of good things. If the enemy sees your
care, he will altogether dissolve the enmity. Say to him: Are you not ashamed
of Jews, nor do you blush, who keep the sabbath with such exactness, and from
the evening itself stand off from all work? And if they see the sun hastening
toward setting on the day of preparation, they even cut contracts, and split
sales; and if someone having bought from them before evening comes in the
evening bringing the price, they do not bear to take it, nor to receive the
silver.

And why do I speak of the price of wares and contracts? Even if it were
possible to take a treasure, they would choose to lose the gain rather than to
trample the law. Then Jews, even keeping the law unseasonably, are so exact,
and hold to a guarding that profits them nothing, but even harms; but you, who
are above the shadow, who have been counted worthy to see the sun of
righteousness, who belong to the citizenship of the heavens, do not display
even the same eagerness as those who sit by evil unseasonably, you who have
been entrusted with the truth, but being called here for a small part of a
day, you do not even bear to spend this toward the hearing of the divine
oracles? And of what pardon would you obtain, tell me? And what reasonable and
just defense will you have to speak? There is not, there is not that one so
neglectful and listless should ever obtain pardon, even if ten thousand times
he puts forward necessities of the affairs of life.

Do you not know that if having come you worship God, and share in the spending
of time here, the affairs in your hands are rather made easy for you? Do you
have cares of life? For these very things then meet here, that having drawn
the goodwill =51.177= of God through the spending of time here, you may thus
go out with safety; that you may have him as ally, that you may become
unconquerable to the demons, being helped by the hand from above. If you enjoy
paternal prayers, if you share in common prayer, if you hear divine oracles,
if you draw the help of God, if having fenced yourself with these weapons you
thus go out, not even the devil himself will be able to look you in the face
any longer, much less wicked men, eager to insult and to slander. But if you
come from the house to the marketplace, having been found naked of these
weapons, you will be easy to take for all who insult. For this reason many
things both in public and in private affairs meet us against our mind,


because we have not been eager first about the spiritual things, and then
about the things of life, but we have reversed the order. For this reason also
the sequence and straightness of affairs has been reversed, and all things are
full of much confusion for us. How do you think I am pained and hurt, when I
consider that when a festival and feast has come, even if there is no one
calling, the whole city runs together; but when festival and feast has gone,
even if we spend the whole day tearing ourselves and calling you, there is no
one who heeds? For often turning these things in mind, I have groaned heavily,
and said to myself: What need is there of exhortation, or of advice, when you
do all things simply by custom, and become nothing more eager from our
teaching? For when in feasts you need nothing of exhortation from us, but when
those have gone you have been profited nothing from our teaching, do you not
show our word superfluous, as far as it has come to you?

δʹ. Perhaps many of those hearing these things are pained. But the listless do
not know it thus; for if they had put away the slighting, as we who each day
are anxious for your things. What do you gain so much from the outside
affairs, as you damage yourself? It is not possible, having taken so much gain
from another assembly or gathering, to go away, as from the spending of time
here; even if you speak of a law-court, even if a council, even if the palaces
themselves. For we do not put into the hands of those entering here the
management of nations and cities, nor the command of camps, but another rule
more august than even the kingdom itself; or rather we do not put it in, but
the grace of the Spirit. What then is the rule more august than the kingdom,
which those entering here receive? They are taught to master the absurd
things, to be king over wicked desire, to rule anger, to dismiss envy, to
enslave vainglory. There is not so august a king, the one sitting upon the
royal throne, and wearing a diadem, as a man who has brought the right
reasoning in himself up onto the throne of the rule of the slavish passions
=51.178= , and has bound his head with the mastery against those, as with some
bright diadem. For what profit of purple, tell me, and golden garments and a
crown set with stones, when the soul is captive of the passions? What gain
from the outer freedom, when what is more sovereign in us is slavishly and
pitiably led in slavery? For just as when a fever sinks toward the depth, and
burns up all the insides, there is no profit from the outer appearance of the
body, if it suffers nothing such; so, when our soul is swept away by the
passions within, there is no profit of the outer rule, nor of the royal seat,
when the mind with much tyranny is overthrown from the throne of the kingdom
by the passions, and bows and trembles at their uprisings. Which, that it may
not happen, prophets and apostles run together from all sides, settling our
passions, and casting out all the beastliness of the unreason in us, and
putting into our hands the rule much more august than the kingdom. For this
reason I was saying that those who deprive themselves of this care receive the
blow about the vital parts, enduring a loss such as from nowhere else; since
they also gain gains coming here, such as they would not be able to gain from
anywhere else; as then the word also showed. You shall not appear empty before
the Lord, the law said; that is, do not enter without sacrifices. And if it is
not necessary to enter into a house of God without sacrifices, much more is it
necessary into assemblies with brothers; for this sacrifice and offering is
better than that, when having taken a soul with yourself you enter. Do you not
see the doves


that have been trained, how going out they hunt others? Let us also do this.
For what excuse will there be, when of the irrational animals being able to
hunt the animal of the same race, we who have been honored with reason and so
much wisdom overlook such a hunt? I exhorted in the former discourse saying:
Let each of you go to the houses of the neighbors, wait for them having come
out, hold them, and bring them back to the common mother; and imitate those
mad for the theater, who having arranged with one another with all eagerness,
thus wait toward dawn for that unlawful spectacle. But nothing more has come
to us from that exhortation. For this reason I say again, and saying I will
not cease, until I persuade. Hearing profits nothing, if practice is not
present with it. It makes the vengeance heavier for us, then, when continually
hearing the same things. we do nothing of the things said.

And that the punishment is heavier, hear Christ saying: If I had not come, and
spoken to them, they would not have sin; but now they have no pretext
concerning the sin; and the Apostle also, For not the hearers of the law will
be justified. And these things he says toward those who hear; but wishing also
to teach the speaker, that not even for that one will there be any more gain
from the teaching, when he does not have a way of life yoked to the teaching,
and a life agreeing with the word, hear how both the Apostle stretches toward
him, and the =51.179= Prophet. For the one says: But to the sinner God said,
Why do you declare my statutes, and take up my covenant through your mouth,
but you have hated instruction? And the Apostle stretching again toward these
very ones, those thinking great things upon the teaching, says somehow thus:
You have persuaded yourself that you are a guide of the blind, a light of
those in darkness, an instructor of the foolish, a teacher of infants; you
then who teach another, do you not teach yourself? Since then neither for me
the speaker would speaking be able to profit, nor for you the hearers would
hearing without being persuaded by the things said, but even condemns more,
let us not display the eagerness as far as the hearing, but let us keep the
things said upon the works. For it is a good thing to spend time continually
in hearing of divine oracles; but this good thing becomes useless, when it
does not have the profit from obedience yoked with it. Therefore that you may
not be gathered here in vain, with all eagerness, which I have often begged of
you, and begging I will not cease, draw the brothers to us, exhort those who
wander, advise not by word only, but also by work.

Greater is this teaching, the one through the manners, the one through the way
of life. And if you say nothing, but go out from assembly, through the form,
and the look, and the voice, and the walk, and all the rest of the composure
showing to the men left behind the gain, which you went out from here having
taken, this suffices for exhortation and advice. For thus we must go out from
here, as from holy inner shrines, as having come down from the heavens
themselves, having become composed, philosophizing, doing and saying all
things with rhythm; and let a wife seeing the husband withdrawing from
assembly, and a father the son, and the child the father, and the slave the
master, and the friend the friend, and the enemy the enemy, let all take
perception of the profit that has come to us here; and they will take it, if
they perceive you having become meeker, more philosophical, more pious.
Consider of what mystagogy you enjoy, you who have been initiated, with whom
you send up that mystic song, with whom you cry the, Thrice-holy. Teach those
outside, that you danced with the seraphim, that you belong to the people
above, that you have been enrolled in the choir of the angels, that you


have conversed with the Master, that you have been with Christ. If we thus
rhythm ourselves, going out we will need no word toward those left behind; but
from our profit they will perceive their own loss, and they will run quickly,
so as to enjoy the same things. For seeing the beauty of your soul shining
through their senses, even if they are slower than all, they will fall into
love of your comeliness. For if beauty of body wings those who see, much more
would good form of soul be able to wake the spectator, and call him toward the
equal zeal. Let us then beautify our inner man, and let us remember outside
the things said here; for there especially is the time of the memory of them;
and just as =51.180= an athlete, whatever he learns upon the wrestling-ground,
these things he displays upon the contests; so then we too, whatever we hear
here, these things we must display upon the outside affairs.

εʹ. Remember then the things said here, so that, when you go out, and the
devil takes hold of you, either through anger, or through vainglory, or
through some other passion, having recalled the teaching here, you may be able
easily to strip off the grips of the wicked one. Do you not see in the pits
the trainers, who after ten thousand contests, having received at last a
release from the wrestlings from age, sitting outside the pits beside the dust
itself, call out to those inside and wrestling, so as to hold a hand, so as to
drag a leg, so as to take behind the back, and saying many other such things,
that if you do this and that, you will easily stretch out the antagonist, they
contribute the greatest things to the disciples? And you, see your trainer,
the blessed Paul, who after ten thousand crowns sitting now outside the pit, I
mean of the present life, calls out to us wrestling, and shouts through the
Epistles, when he sees us held by anger and by bearing a grudge, and choked by
the passion, If your enemy hungers, feed him. And just as the trainer says,
that if you do this and that, you will prevail over the antagonist, so this
one also adds: For doing this, you will heap coals of fire upon his head. But
in the midst of this reading of the law by me, there came upon me the question
that seems to grow from it, and to furnish to many a handle against Paul,
which I proposed today into the midst for you. What then is that which lies
under the thoughts of those not wishing to examine all things with exactness?
Leading away from anger, he says, Paul, and persuading them to be reasonable
and moderate toward those near, made them more beastlike, and raised them
toward wrath. For to say, If your enemy hungers, feed him, if he thirsts, give
him drink, is a good command and full of philosophy, and useful both to the
one doing and to the one suffering; but the rest from there has much
difficulty, and seems not to agree with the mind of the one who has said the
former things. And what is this? The saying, that Doing this, you will heap
coals of fire upon his head. For through these words he wronged both the one
doing and the one suffering; having kindled the head of the one, and having
put coals of fire on. For what so great good would come from being fed and
given drink, as the evil from the heap of the coals? The one then being well
treated, he says, he thus wronged, having thrown him into a greater vengeance,
and the one doing well again he harmed otherwise. For what can this one also
gain from the benefaction from enemies, when he does it in hope of the
vengeance? For the one feeding and giving drink to the enemy for this, that he
may heap coals of fire upon his head, would become not loving of man and kind,
but raw and harsh, through a small benefaction


having thrown unspeakable punishment. For what would become harsher than the
one feeding for this, that he may heap coals of fire upon the =51.181= head of
the one being fed?

This then is the objection; and it is necessary next to bring on the solution,
so that through these very letters of the law that seem to take hold you may
see exactly all the wisdom of the lawgiver. What then is the solution? That
great and noble man perceived this well, that it is a heavy and hard thing, to
be reconciled to an enemy quickly; and it is heavy and hard, not from its own
nature, but from our listlessness. And he commanded not only to be reconciled,
but also to feed, which was much heavier than the former. For if some, only
seeing those who have pained them, become beastlike, how would they choose to
feed them hungering? And why do I say, seeing? If someone mentions them, and
only brings the name into the midst, he renews the blow of the mind for us,
and makes the inflammation greater. All these things then Paul having
perceived, and wishing to make this hard to accomplish and hard thing easy and
ready, and to persuade the one who does not even bear to see the enemy to come
into benefaction of that one, he set the coals of the fire, so that having
been urged by hope of the vengeance, he may run upon the benefaction of the
one who has pained. And just as the fisherman covering the hook from all sides
with the bait, offers it to the fishes, so that having run to the accustomed
food, through that he may be caught and held easily; so then Paul too, wishing
to embark the one wronged upon the benefaction of the one who has wronged,
does not offer the hook of philosophy naked, but having covered it as with
some bait, with the coals of the fire, he calls the one insulted by the hope
of the punishment toward the benefaction of the one who has pained; and having
come he holds him thereafter and does not let him leap off, the nature itself
of the thing nailing him to the enemy; and he all but says to him: You do not
wish through piety to feed the one who has wronged? Feed then through the hope
of the punishment. For he knows that if he touches the benefaction toward him,
it becomes thereafter a beginning and a way of the reconciliation. For no one,
no one would bear to have as enemy the one continually fed by him and given
drink, even if at the beginning he does this in hope of vengeance. For time
going forward loosens also the intensity of the anger. As then the fisherman,
if he offered the snare naked, would not have drawn the fish, but now having
covered it, he secretly sends the hook into the mouth of the approaching
animal; so also Paul, if he had not stretched forward the expectation of the
punishment, would not have persuaded those wronged to touch the benefaction of
those who have pained. Wishing then to persuade them leaping off, and being
displeased, and growing numb even toward the very sight of the enemies, to
benefit them the greatest things, he set the coals of the fire, not so that he
may throw those into inescapable punishment, but so that having persuaded
those wronged in the expectation of the punishment to benefit the enemies, he
may thereafter by time persuade them also to let go all the anger to them.

ςʹ. And the one wronged he thus comforted; but see also the one who has
wronged, how he joins him to the one who has been anger =51.182= ed again.
First by the manner of the benefaction; for there is no one so wretched and
without perception, who being given drink, and being fed, would not wish to
become a slave and friend to the one working these things for him; and second
by the fear of the vengeance. For he seems to stretch toward the one feeding,
saying: For doing this, you heap coals of fire upon his head; but most of all
he lays hold of the one who has pained, so that by the fear of this punishment
he may not remain throughout being an enemy, but


knowing that the being fed and given drink would be able to harm him the
greatest things, if he should remain continually upon the enmity, he may
dissolve the anger. For thus he will be able to quench the coals of the fire.
So that both the punishment and the vengeance lying draws the one wronged into
benefaction of the one who has pained, and frightens and raises the one who
provoked, and pushes toward reconciliation of the one feeding and giving
drink.

With a double bond then he bound both to one another, both that of the
benefaction, and that of the vengeance. For the hard thing is to begin and to
find an entrance for the reconciliation; and this having been opened in
whatever way, all the things after these will be ready and easy. For even if
at the beginning the one pained feeds the enemy in hope of punishment, yet
becoming a friend by the very feeding, he will be able to cast out the desire
of the vengeance. For having become a friend, he would no longer feed the one
reconciled to him with such an expectation. Again the one who provoked, seeing
the one wronged choosing to feed him and give him drink, through this very
thing, and the fear of the punishment laid up for him, casts out all the
enmity, even if he is ten thousand times iron, and harsh, and adamant, being
put to shame by the kindness of the one feeding, and fearing the punishment
laid up for him, if he should remain an enemy after the food. For this reason
then he did not even stand here of the exhortation, but when he emptied the
anger of each, then he also corrects their mind saying, Do not be conquered by
the evil. For if you remain, he says, bearing a grudge and defending yourself,

the anger; so that, if you wish to conquer, be reconciled, and do not go out

bearing of evil, you conquer the evil, having cast out the anger and the

inflamed would not have borne. For this reason, when he had satisfied his
wrath, then he also led him upon the best cause of the reconciliation, and did
not let him remain in the wicked hope of the vengeance. Did you see wisdom of
a lawgiver? And that you may learn that because of the weakness of those not
bearing otherwise to be joined to one another, he introduced this law, hear
how Christ legislating this same thing has not set the same prize; but having
said, that Love your enemies, do well to those who hate you, which is to feed
and give drink, he did not add, that For doing this you will heap coals of
fire upon their heads, but what? That you may become like your Father =51.183=
in the heavens. Reasonably; for he was speaking to Peter, James and John, and
to the remaining choir of the apostles; for this reason he has set this prize.
But if you say that even thus the command is burdensome, you defend more for
Paul again for us, and you deprive yourself of all pardon. Why then? Because
this thing that seems to be burdensome, I show you accomplished in the Old,
when there was not yet a display of so much philosophy. For this reason Paul
too did not introduce the law with his own words, but having used the very
sayings with which the one who introduced it at the beginning set it, so that
he may leave no pardon to those not keeping it. For the, If your enemy
hungers, feed him, if he thirsts, give him drink, is not first a word of Paul,
but of Solomon.

For this reason he has set the words, so that he may persuade the hearer, that
it is of the most shameful things, to think an ancient law, and one
accomplished often by the ancients, now having been given over into so much
philosophy, to be burdensome and heavy. And who of the ancients accomplished
it, he says? Many others also, but especially David with more abundance. He
did not only feed the enemy, nor give him drink only, but also in danger


often snatched him from death, and having become master of his slaughter, he
spared both once, and twice, and often. And Saul so hated him and turned away
after the ten thousand benefactions, after the splendid trophies, and the
salvation upon Goliath, as not even to bear to remember that one's name, but
to call him from the father. For when a feast had come, since having woven
some deceit for him, and having stitched a hard plot, he did not see him
arriving. Where is, he says, the son of Jesse? He called him from the father,
both through the enmity not bearing to recall the name, and thinking from the
low birth of the father to maltreat the distinction of the just man, thinking
wretchedly and miserably; for especially, even if he had to accuse the father,
this harmed David nothing. For each is responsible for the things done by him,
and from these he is able to be praised and accused. But now having no
wickedness to speak, he brought the low birth of the race into the midst,
expecting by this to overshadow his brightness, which itself also was of last
folly. For what charge is it to be from cheap and humble people? But that man
did not know to philosophize these things. Saul then called him son of Jesse;
but David having found him inside in the cave sleeping, did not call him son
of Kish, but by the name of honor; For I will not bring my hand, he says, upon
an anointed of the Lord. Thus he was clean of anger and of all bearing of a
grudge; he calls anointed of the Lord the one who has wronged so many things,
the one thirsting for his blood, the one who after ten thousand benefactions
often attempted to kill him. For he did not look at what that one was worthy
to suffer, but he looked at what it was fitting for him both to do and to say,
which is the greatest definition of philosophy. What is this? as having taken
the enemy in a prison, held by a double bond, or rather a triple, both by the
crowding of the place, and by the =51.184= desert of those who would help, and
by the necessity of sleep, do you not demand justice nor vengeance of him? No,
he says; for I do not see now what this one is just to suffer, but what it was
proper for me to do. He did not look toward the ease of the slaughter, but he
looked toward the exactness of the philosophy fitting for him. And yet what of
the things then was not sufficient to raise him toward the slaughter? The
enemy having been delivered bound to him? For you know this surely, that we
run more upon affairs full of ease, and the hope of accomplishing puts in us a
greater desire of the deed, as also it was then upon that one. But the general
then advising and waking? But the memory of the things past? But nothing moved
him toward the murder; rather the very ease of the slaughter turned him away;
for he considered that God delivered him for this, that he might furnish him a
greater subject and occasion of philosophy. You then perhaps wonder at him,
that he remembered none of the past evils; but I am struck by him through
another thing much greater. What then is this? That not even the fear of the
things to come pushed him toward putting the enemy to death. For he knew
clearly that having escaped his hands, he would again stand against him; but
he chose rather himself to be in danger having released the one who had
wronged, than taking thought for his own safety to put the enemy to death.
What then would become equal to that great and noble soul, who, the law
commanding to dig out an eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth, and to
defend with equal things, not only did not work this, but also displayed much
more philosophy? And yet if he had killed Saul then, even thus an unmixed
encomium of philosophy would have remained for him, not only because he
defended himself, not himself beginning unjust hands, but because also, An eye
for an eye, he conquered with much reasonableness. For he did not raise one
slaughter against one slaughter, but against


many deaths, which that one brought on, having attempted to kill him not once,
not twice, but often, he was about to bring on one death; or rather not these
things only, but also coming thus upon the defense fearing the future, and
this together with the things said raises for him the whole crown of bearing
of evil. For the one being angry over the things that have come to be toward
him and demanding vengeance would not be able to obtain the encomia of bearing
of evil; but the one who has let go all the past things being many and hard,
but fearing over the future and preparing safety for himself, and through this
being compelled to go up upon the defense, no one would deprive of the crowns
of reasonableness.

paradoxical manner of philosophy; and neither the memory of the things past,
nor the fear of the things to come, nor the urging of the general, nor the

What pardon then will we have, remembering past sins, and defending ourselves
against those who have pained, when that blameless one, having suffered so
many things, and expecting more and harder evils to happen to him from the
salvation of the enemy, appears sparing thus, as to choose rather himself to
be in danger, and to live with fear and trembling, than justly to slaughter
the one about to furnish ten thousand troubles for him? The philosophy then is
possible to see from these things, that not only he did not slaughter, there
being so much necessity, but he did not even bring out a blasphemous word
against him, and this when the one being insulted was not about to hear. And
yet we often speak ill even of friends absent, but that one not even of the
enemy and the one who has wronged so many things. The philosophy then is
possible to see from these things; but the love of man, and the other care,
from the things which he did after these. For having cut the fringe of the
garment, and having taken away the flask of the water, having gone far off,
and having stood he shouted, and showed these things to the one saved, not
doing this toward display and ambition, but wishing through the works to
persuade him, that he suspected him as an enemy vainly and in vain, and
through this hastening to draw him toward friendship.

But nevertheless not even having persuaded him thus, and being able to put him
to death, again he chose rather to fall out of the fatherland and to spend
time in a foreign land, and being distressed each day, to furnish the
necessary food for himself, than remaining at home to pain the plotter. What
would become gentler than that soul? Truly he said justly, Remember, Lord,
David, and all his meekness. Let us also imitate this one, and let us neither
speak, nor do ill to the enemies, but let us also benefit according to power;
for we will do well rather to ourselves, than to those. For if you let go, he
says, to your enemies, it will be let go to you. Let go slavish sins, that you
may receive a master's forgiveness of sins; and if he has wronged great
things, by as much as you let go greater, by so much you will receive the
greater forgiveness. For this reason we have been taught to say, Let go to us,
as we let go, that we may learn, that the measure of the letting go first
takes the beginning from us. So that, by as much as the enemy works harder
evils, by so much more he benefits. Let us then hasten and be eager to be
reconciled toward those who have pained, whether they are angry justly, or
unjustly. For if here you are reconciled, you have been freed from the
judgment there; but if, the enmity remaining in the midst, death


having come leads away, having intercepted the hatred, thereafter it is
necessary that the case be introduced there.

Just as then many of men disputing toward one another, =51.186= if outside
they dissolve more friendly toward one another, they also free themselves of
losses and fear and many dangers, the end of the case coming out according to
the mind of both; but if they commit it to the judge against themselves, both
a loss of money and vengeance often, and that the enmity remain unmoved will
be left to them; so then here too, if in the present life we dissolve, we will
free ourselves of all punishment; but if remaining enemies we go away to that
fearful tribunal, we will altogether give the last penalty upon the vote of
that judge, and both will undergo inescapable vengeance, the one angry
unjustly, because of this that unjustly, and the one justly, because of this
that he remembered injuries justly. For even if we have suffered some evil
unjustly, it is necessary to assign forgiveness to those who have wronged. And
consider how he pushes and presses those who have pained unjustly toward the
reconciliation of those wronged. If you offer your gift, he says, before the
altar, and there remember that your brother has something against you, go,
first be reconciled to your brother. He did not say, Gather, offer the
sacrifice, but, Be reconciled, and then offer. Let it lie, he says, so that
the necessity of the offering may compel even unwilling the one justly angry
to come toward the reconciliation. See how he urges again to come toward the
one who provoked, having said, Let go to your debtors, that your Father also
may let go your trespasses. For he has not set a small wage, but one even
exceedingly surpassing the greatness of the achievement.

Thinking then all these things, and reckoning the recompense that comes upon
this, and that it is not of much toil and eagerness to wipe out sins, let us
forgive those who have wronged us. For what others scarcely accomplish through
fasting, and lamentations, and prayers, and sackcloth, and ash, and ten
thousand confession, I mean the wiping out of their own sins, this it is
possible for us to do easily without sackcloth and ash and fasting, if only we
wipe out the anger from the mind, and with sincerity let go to those who have
wronged us. And may the God of peace and love, having banished all wrath and
bitterness and anger from our soul, count us worthy according to the sequence
of the members, having been bound to one another with exactness, with one
accord, in one mouth and one soul, continually to send up to him the
thanksgiving hymns owed, because to him is the glory and the power, unto the
ages of the ages. Amen.
