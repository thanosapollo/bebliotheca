The speech is not on equal terms for the city and for the others. For they
could show nothing so great as they might say, while we could say nothing so
great as there is to show. Yet it is necessary to pay the due rite toward the
law, since these men too command it. Still even in this there is gain for the
city, and for the speaker almost the least of danger. For he, the more
beautiful what is seen appears, the more he will seem to have suffered what is
likely in falling short, so that he will at once be refuted and have pardon
from the refutations. But to the city a pride is added, if, when the things
she has to show are of such quality and such number, she does not rely only on
what is seen, but also on the speeches concerning her ancestral things. For
the city has not lately been fortunate for us, but from our ancestors, and she
stands toward herself in the form of a colony and a metropolis: for she is
third from the ancient one. For just as statues and speeches wrought with
precision are established by a second and a third hand, so too the city,
founded by a third hand, must perfect her beauty, and she has become both most
ancient and most new, the same city, having renewed herself, as they say of
the sacred bird. The oldest city, then, is founded on Sipylus, where they say
are both the beds of the gods and the dances of the Curetes about the mother
of Zeus. And she was so beloved of the gods from the beginning that the poets
say the gods together with the heroes contributed to a feast in her, dining
mixed together. That city, then, the Nymphs receive, and now she is under
water, having gone beneath the lake, as they say. And after this a second was
inhabited under Sipylus beside the spit of the shore, in the middle between
the ancient and the present, the

one that is now ancient in relation to this present city. These were as it
were rehearsals of this settlement in other, poorer moulds. And at a third
step, as the poets call it, the city was moved and came to rest in this one
form. As for the people, the oldest is autochthonous to her. And when she also
had to receive settlers, she received these too from the autochthones of the
other continent, and she was blended, by some divine fortune, with the Greeks
opposite, both giving and receiving founders: to the Peloponnesians giving the
eponymous hero and the pride of the name, and from Athens receiving the
Erechtheids, as the story goes, from the war against the Amazons, and later
indeed the later ones. And they enjoyed both benefits of the metropolis,
luxuries for daily life, and daring in wars greater than it profits many to
rival; so that already to some of the poets such a thing has been called a
Smyrnaean manner. One sample will suffice, of which you will also see the
memorial. For they invite you at the first season of spring at the Dionysia: a
sacred trireme for Dionysus is carried in a circle through the marketplace.
And this is a token that once the Chians sailed against the city, thinking to
take it empty, the men being away on the mountain, and then, seen from their
ships by the men coming down, they not only failed in the attempt but lost
their ships as well, the men then dancing the armed dance for Dionysus and
celebrating the Bacchic rites upon those men's bodies. And this is a relish to
the city's deeds and as it were a token of the others which one must leave
aside; wherefore it is also on the lips of the many. And one could tell of
many other contests of hers as well, and indeed the last ones, those on your
behalf and with you in every emergency, a task for no small leisure to
narrate.

But why must one wear oneself out over these things? For those cities whose
ambition is in myths or narratives, it is reasonable to retreat to these; but
one that at first sight subdues and does not allow one to seek the ancient
things, why should one exalt her from what has passed, and not rather conduct
a tour as if holding her by the hand, making the spectator a witness of the
words? For I think, if some city's image had to appear in heaven, as they say
of Ariadne's crown and other images of rivers and animals, all that were
honored by the gods, this city's would appear and win. So she seems to me to
have been born a pattern of a city, and to need neither her citizen poet as a
matchmaker nor any other art for praise, but herself to procure love of
herself for all men and herself to compel it, persuading eyes, not ears. For
she is stretched out over the sea, sending forth a flower of beauty without
cease, as if not built up little by little, but risen from the earth all at
once, presenting her size not under compulsion nor in haste, but everywhere
ample and of like kind. And her size is a surplus of beauty. Nor would you say
there are many cities scattered little by little, but one that balances many,
and one, then, of one hue and in accord with herself, presenting her parts,
like a human body, in agreement with the whole. And the ornaments both in her
and about her are equally many and splendid and have left none more to be
desired. But she is all as if through statues, like the embroidered tunics.
Walking from west toward east you will come from temple to temple and from
hill to hill through a single street fairer than its name. And for one
standing on the acropolis the sea flows below, and the suburbs lie at anchor
beneath; and the city, mixing herself with both, through three of the sweetest
spectacles pours out the soul, nor is it possible to find

where one may rest the eye: for each thing draws it to itself as in a varied
necklace. And as one descends from the acropolis the eastern parts receive
him, and again the fairest of temples of the goddess who has obtained the city
by lot, and the things after these are a task even for an old man to mention
with due order. For all already as far as the seashore is lit up with
gymnasia, marketplaces, theaters, precincts, harbors, beauties native and
handmade competing. And nothing is idle of spectacle or of use. Baths, at any
rate, so many that you would be at a loss where to bathe, and courses of every
kind, some in the depth of the city, others at the limits, each preventing
another from being the fairest, and fountains and springs both at houses and
more than one to a house, and streets instead of marketplaces, and streets
splitting one another four ways for the reception of the sun, I almost say
what I denied before, making many cities by their outline, each an imitation
of the whole. And breezes of spring and of summer, more melodious than the
nightingales and cicadas among the poets, darting some through some places,
some through others, make the whole city as a grove. What painting or
sculpture would these not be better to imitate? And always, as if adorned for
a yearly procession, she is made fair with both private and public buildings,
a sight more luxurious than a Median tent. And delights never leave her, nor
of all the Muses that visit cities of men does any dwell elsewhere. For much
is native, and much is immigrant: you would say she is the hearth of the
continent for the sake of culture. And of all theaters, both for contests and
for the other displays, the abundance is beyond telling. And besides these,
works of hands of every kind, and a rival yield of land and sea. To speak
concisely, this one city is most measured for either tribe, both those who
have chosen to pass life in ease and those who think it right to philosophize
without deceit. For they have as rivals to their labors the

rests, so that they do not grow weary. And the ornament before the doors,
instead of Apollo Agyieus, is a propylon for the city: Meles the eponymous, a
canal of Nymphs from springs to sea, both to be a flowing bath for them and to
receive Nereids from Nereus at a short distance. And Meles flows alike from
caves and houses and trees, rising even from the middle of the stream and
going forward as far as the sea; and of him the part at the upper springs is a
circle, and you would most liken it to a harbor, and what follows to a
Euripus. And at the outlets he roars least of all, but smoothing the surge he
mixes in without a sound, lifting the sea as it comes on from the winds, and
following it as it goes, showing a back from either water, so that you would
not know where they met. And indeed that he is everywhere full of fish, and
these tame and messmates of those who camp on the banks, and not on the dry
land, but dancing in place to the pipe and in their drunkenness tumbling
toward the drinkers, these things even the boys could explain, and all may see
them. But the fairer offspring of the river, and those that carry to all
cities, Homer, the common nurse and friend of the Greeks, from fathers and
from childhood for each. Wherefore they all seem to me justly to make
first-offerings of their words at the springs of Meles, as that man said of
Spercheius, as of locks of hair, singing the river for Homer's sake. And when
one has crossed Meles a place meets him, a gift of Poseidon, as I think, to
the city, something both like and not like what is told about Thessaly. For
there, having parted the mountains, he made Thessaly level land, sending the
Peneus out of a lake in a stream; but here he brought the sea back from the
mountains, fitting to the city the ornament from the mainland as a counterpart
to that from the sea. Yet he did not leave silt, nor even so much as set the

ground, but for one who digs there are signs of the ancient nature, and for
one who looks at the things surrounding in a circle, the places on which the
sea once rested, and for one driving through the salt-flat among seasons and
springs, and these things at the end. And when one has gone a little forward
the city appears again as if escorting, and here already her beauties become,
through a smaller space, countable and measurable. No one is in such haste
that, seeing the road ahead, he fails to turn: he puts what was before his
eyes on his right, and the city, which was on his left, in front of him. For
the city draws to herself as the magnet-stone draws iron, and subdues through
a willing necessity. And the city has suffered the same toward the outside,
and the outside toward the city. For neither is there satiety for one looking
from the town at the suburbs, nor, if someone should look from without at the
city, is there ever enough of the same, so as to have sufficient. And to this
city alone among cities reputation turns to loss: for no one hearing of her
abroad could reach so far as to grasp it all.

And it seems to me one might even make a decree from them, as they say the man
struck by the viper does not wish to tell another except one who has suffered
it, so too of the city's beauties, having seen, to share only with one who has
seen, or at least is about to at once, but not readily to carry the words out
to others as one would carry sacred things to the profane: just as those
possessed by love do not wish to narrate to mere children. For this much
perhaps even a poet might say and charm the soul: that the harbor is in the
navel of the city, and the open sea in the eye, no less in view to those at
the far ends on the other side than to those who dwell beside it; and the
acropolis holds the city up through the whole, and the sea stretches alongside
as a base, differing from Meles toward the east only so far as to go out into
a bend. But these things one might also adorn in part, while the

grace over all, stretched like Iris and fitting the city like a lyre, in
accord with herself and with the ornaments outside, and the continuous gleam
through all things, as of Homer's bronze, as far as heaven: who could bring
these things within the reach of speech? Neither is Meles so blessed in
children, nor is anyone so fortunate in words. And indeed she alone of cities
needs seeing, not hearing; and she alone needs not seeing so much as
continuous dwelling and converse, if one is going to behold her well and carry
off the wages of praise from the spot itself. And I will still add this much
to the circuit. For I mentioned the recess of the gulf a little earlier, and
concerning the whole a little remains. For it is exactly according to the
city's name and fitting no other. In gentleness and use and beauty and form it
is a gulf, but in power an open sea in view: and though it has the additional
name of one gulf, it unfolds into many gulfs, like folding tablets; for
different headlands shape different ones; and harbors and resting-places are
countless. You would say it resembles the many-spouted bowls, so always
through a short peninsula it has its anchorages, a voyage of not a few days if
one should put in harbor by harbor. And for me it is already time to moor the
speech here: for what need is there to speak of the people? You yourself will
judge and will make it still better, explaining toward what is fairest. Now,
then, I know that both to you and to most of those present I seem to have
spoken in measure; but presently all these things will be proved inferior. And
it is my gain to be defeated in such things.
