* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Let us count as wisdom such things as philosophizing and speaking with art,
taking up poetry and music and geometry, and, by Zeus, as much astronomy as is
not superfluous. Wisdom also is the ordering of an army, and still such things
as all of medicine, and painting, and sculptors, and the forms of statues, and
hollowed stone, and hollowed iron. As for the banausic crafts, let them be
granted an art by which some tool or vessel will be finished correctly, but
let wisdom be reserved for those I named alone. I except the helmsman from the
banausic, since he understands stars and winds and lays hold of what is
unseen. Why I have said this will be shown. As for gymnastics, let us call it
a wisdom no less than any art, so as to be composed into notes for those who
wish to train. For the gymnastics of old produced Milons and Hippostheneses,
Poulydamases and Promachoi and Glaucus son of Demylus, and the athletes still
before these, Peleus of course and Theseus and Heracles himself. That of our
fathers knew lesser men, yet admirable and worth remembering. The one now
established has so changed the condition of athletes that most people even
resent those who love training.
* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

It seems to me I should teach the causes why these things have declined,
contribute to trainers and to those being trained whatever I know, and plead
for nature, which is hearing itself ill spoken of, since the athletes of now
fall far short of those of old. For she still feeds lions no worse, and the
same holds for dogs and horses and bulls; and as for what of hers belongs to
trees, vines are still alike, and the gifts of the fig, and gold and silver
and stones have not altered at all, but as she herself ordained she grows all
things like those of former times. As for athletes, whatever virtues once
belonged to them, nature has not been carried away from those, for she still
brings forth the high-spirited, the well-formed, the quick-witted, and these
are of nature; but training that is not sound, and practice that is not
vigorous, have taken from nature her own strength. How this came about I will
show later. First let us examine the origins of running and boxing and
wrestling and such things, and from what each began and from whom. The Eleans'
evidence will be set beside us everywhere, for such things must be told from
the most exact sources.
* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Of all contest, then, these are the light events: the stade, the dolichos, the
hoplites, the diaulos, the jump, the javelin. The heavier are the pancratium,
wrestling, boxers, the discus. The pentathlon was fitted from both: for
wrestling and throwing the discus are heavy, while javelin, jumping, and
running are light. Before Jason and Peleus the jump was crowned on its own and
the discus on its own, and the javelin was enough for victory in the times
when the Argo sailed. Telamon was strongest at the discus, Lynceus at the
javelin, and the sons of Boreas ran and jumped; Peleus was second in these,
but he beat all in wrestling. So when they competed at Lemnos, they say Jason,
to favor Peleus, joined the five, and Peleus gathered the victory in this way,
and was thought the most warlike of his time both for the excellence he used
in battles and for his practice of the five, which is so warlike that one even
throws the javelin among the prizes.
* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

The origin of the dolichos was this. Running heralds from Arcadia used to go
about Greece as messengers of war, and they were forbidden to ride, but had to
do the running themselves. So always covering in a short part of the day as
many stades as the dolichos made the running heralds long-distance runners and
trained them for war.
* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

The stade was invented thus. When the Eleans had sacrificed as they
customarily do, the offerings lay on the altar, but fire was not yet set to
them. The runners stood a stade from the altar, and before it a priest stood
with a torch as judge, and the winner, having set fire to the offerings, went
away an Olympic victor.
* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

When the Eleans had sacrificed, the theoroi of all the Greeks also had to
sacrifice, and so that their approach might not be idle, the runners ran a
stade from the altar, as if summoning the Greek world, and turned back to the
same place as if announcing that Greece would arrive rejoicing. So much for
the diaulos.
* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

The races of the hoplites are various, and most of all those at Nemea, which
they name both armed and horse-races, and they are dedicated to the seven
around Tydeus. The Olympic hoplite race, as the Eleans say, was instituted for
this reason. The Eleans joined war with the Dymaeans so undeclared that not
even the Olympic games were a truce, and when the Eleans were winning on the
very day of the contests a hoplite from the battle is said to have run into
the stadium bringing good news of the victory. This is plausible, and I hear
the same from the Delphians, when they warred against some of the Phocian
cities, and from the Argives, when they were worn by continuous war against
the Lacedaemonians, and from the Corinthians, when they warred both in the
Peloponnese itself and beyond the bounds of the Isthmus. To me other things
seem true of the hoplite. For I say he was established from a warlike cause,
but that he enters the contests for the sake of the beginning of war, the
shield declaring that the truce has ended and that arms are needed. And if you
listen to the herald not carelessly, you see that at the close of all he
proclaims that the steward of the games is to cease the contest, and that the
trumpet is to signal the things of Enyalius, calling the young men to arms.
This proclamation also orders them to take up the oil and carry it out of the
way, not as men about to anoint themselves, but as men who have ceased from
anointing.
* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

The best hoplite race was thought to be that in Boeotia at Plataea, both for
the length of the course and for the armor being down to the feet and covering
the athlete as if he were fighting, and because it is set upon a splendid
deed, the Median one, and because the Greeks established these things against
barbarians, and moreover because of the law laid on those who will compete, as
Plataea has established it. For one already crowned among them, if he competed
again, had to set sureties for his body, since death was appointed for the
loser.
* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Boxing is a Laconian invention and once came to the Bebrycian barbarians, and
Polydeuces used it best, whence the poets sang of him from these things. The
ancient Lacedaemonians boxed for this reason: the Lacedaemonians had no
helmets, nor did they think fighting under them native, but a shield served as
helmet for the man who carried it with skill. So that they might guard against
blows to the face, and endure when struck, they practiced boxing and trained
their faces to withstand in this way. Later they gave up boxing and the
pancratium alike, thinking it shameful to compete in those things in which, if
one man gives up, Sparta is slandered as not stout-hearted.
* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Ancient boxing was armed in this way. The four fingers were set into a
wrapping, and they overhung the wrapping as much as, if they were closed,
would make a fist, and they were held by a cord which they had thrown as a
stay from the forearm. Now it has changed again, for they tan hides of the
fattest oxen and make a boxing thong that is sharp and projects, and the thumb
does not join the fingers in striking, for the sake of measure in the wounds,
so that the whole hand may not fight. Hence they exclude thongs from swine
from the stadia, thinking the blows from them painful and hard to heal.
* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

That wrestling and the pancratium were invented as useful for war is shown
first by the deed at Marathon, fought out by the Athenians so as to seem near
to wrestling, this being added to the deed, and second by that at Thermopylae,
when the Lacedaemonians, their swords and spears broken, did much with bare
hands. Of all that is in contest the pancratium is preferred by some, though
composed of incomplete wrestling and incomplete boxing; it is preferred among
others, whereas the Eleans at least have judged wrestling strong and grievous
in the poets' sense, not only for the intertwining of holds, which need a
supple and easy body, but also because one competes three times in the
preliminary, since so many falls are required. At any rate, though they think
it terrible to crown pancratium and boxing and wrestling without dust, they do
not drive the wrestler away, since the law says such a victory is granted only
to the rounded and toilsome wrestling, and the reason why the law so commands
is clear to me at least. For though competing at Olympia is terrible, training
seems still harder. The light athletes train thus: the dolichos-runner some
eight or ten stades, the pentathlete some one of the three, the runners the
diaulos or the stade or both. What is terrible in such things? Nothing. For
the manner of the exercises is the same, whether the Eleans train them or
others. The heavier athlete is trained by the Eleans in the season of the year
when the sun most burns the mud in hollow Arcadia, and he endures dust hotter
than Ethiopian sand, and he holds out beginning from midday. And of these,
toilsome as they are, the wrestlers are the most laborious. For the boxer,
when the time of the stadium comes, will be wounded and wound and will close
on the shins, but in training he will show a shadow of the contest; and the
pancratiast will compete in every way that belongs to the pancratium, but he
trains now one and now another; wrestling is the same in the preliminary and
the same in the contest, for each offers a trial of whatever it knows and
whatever it can. It is reasonably called rounded, for even the upright of
wrestling is rounded, whence the Eleans crown the most gymnastic thing even
from training alone.
* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

They say these did not all enter the contests together, but one after another
as gymnastics discovered and perfected each. For of old the Olympics, down to
the thirteenth Olympiad, were of the stade only, and among them Eleans won
three, seven Messenians, a Corinthian, a Dymaean, a Cleonaean, each a
different Olympiad, and no one the same man twice. At the fourteenth the
diaulos began, and the victory in it belonged to Hypenus of Elis. At the next
after that there was a dolichos contest and Acanthus the Spartiate won. Men's
pentathlon and men's wrestling were practiced at the eighteenth Olympiad;
Eurybatus of Lousoi won wrestling, and Lampis the Laconian the five. Some
write Eurybatus also as a Spartiate. The twenty-third Olympiad already called
a man as boxer, and Onomastus of Smyrna was strongest; boxing, he won,
inscribing Smyrna with a fine deed. For of all Ionian cities and Lydians and
those along the Hellespont and Phrygia, and whatever nations of men there are
in Asia, Smyrna surpassed them all together and was first to obtain an Olympic
crown. And this athlete wrote boxing laws, which the Eleans used for the
boxer's wisdom, and the Arcadians were not angry that a man coming from
luxurious Ionia wrote contest laws for them. At the thirty-third Olympiad the
pancratium was instituted, not yet having been set, and Lygdamis of Syracuse
won. So great was this Sicilian that his foot was a cubit long; at any rate
the stadium is said to have been measured by as many of his own feet as cubits
are counted to the stadium.
* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

They say boys' pentathlon also entered there at the thirty-eighth, when
Eutelidas the Lacedaemonian won, and that this form was never again contested
by a boy at Olympia. The winner of the boys' stade at the forty-sixth Olympiad
(for then it was first instituted) was a beautiful boy, Polymestor of Miletus,
who by the rush of his feet outran a hare. Boys' boxing some say began at the
forty-first Olympiad, and Philetas of Sybaris won, others at the sixtieth,
Crius from the island of Ceos. Damaretus at the sixty-fifth is said to have
been first to win the hoplite, being, I think, of Heraea. At the hundred and
forty-fifth Olympiad they enrolled a boy pancratiast, I do not know from what
they were so late in noticing him when he was already in repute among others;
for it began late among the Olympiads, when Egypt was already being crowned,
and that victory too was an Egyptian's: Naucratis at any rate was proclaimed
when the Egyptian Phaedimus won. These things would not, I think, have entered
contests one by one in this way, nor would they ever have been taken seriously
by Eleans and all Greeks, if gymnastics had not been advancing and training
them. For the victories of the athletes themselves are credited to the
trainers no less than to the athletes.
* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

What then should one know about gymnastics? What else than to count it a
wisdom composed of medicine and of the trainer's craft, being more complete
than the one and a part of the other? How much it has of both I will show. The
kinds of holds, as many as there are, the paidotribes will show, proposing
times and rushes and measures and how one may either guard or, when the other
guards, prevail; and the gymnast too will teach these even if the athlete does
not yet know them. But there are places where one must handle wrestling well,
or the pancratium, or escape or knock aside some advantage the opponents have,
none of which would come into the gymnast's mind if he did not also know the
paidotribes' things. In this the arts are equal; but to purge humors and take
away excesses and smooth what has grown hard and fatten something or change it
or warm it belongs to gymnastics in wisdom. For those things the paidotribes
will either not know, or if he knows something he will use it badly on the
boys, torturing the freedom of unmixed blood. Gymnastics is so much more
complete than the aforesaid science; toward medicine it stands thus. Diseases
we name catarrhs and dropsies and consumptions, and whatever sacred diseases
there are, doctors stop by pouring something on or washing off or plastering,
but gymnastics checks such things by regimens and massage. But one who has
broken something or been wounded or whose light in the eyes has been clouded,
or who has slipped some joint, must be taken to a doctor, since gymnastics is
nothing toward such things.
* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

I think I have sufficiently shown how much gymnastics is toward each science,
and I think I see this in it too: no one man could take up all of medicine,
but one understands fractures, another those with fever, another those with
eye disease, another consumptives, all of them soundly, and though it is a
great thing to work out even some small part of it correctly, the medical men
say they know it all. But no one would profess gymnastics all at once, for he
who knows the running events will not know those of wrestlers and
pancratiasts, and he who trains the heavier will touch the rest of the science
unskillfully.
* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

This is the share of wisdom in the art. Its origin is that man was born able
to wrestle and box and run upright. For not one of such things would come to
be if that through which it comes to be did not already exist, and just as the
origin of bronze-working is iron and bronze, and of farming earth and what
comes from earth, and of seafaring that there is a sea, so let us count
gymnastics too as most kindred and congenital to man. And a story is sung that
gymnastics did not yet exist, but Prometheus did, and Prometheus first
trained, and Hermes in turn trained others, and admired him for the invention,
and the first palaestra was Hermes', and the men molded of clay by Prometheus
would then be these who trained in the clay in which they were, whom people
thought Prometheus molded, since gymnastics made their bodies fit and well
composed.
* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

At Pytho and the Isthmus and wherever on earth contests were instituted, the
trainer, wearing a cloak, anoints the athlete and no one will strip him
against his will; but at Olympia he stands naked, as some think, the Eleans
testing the trainer in the season of the year, whether he knows how to endure
and be burned; but as the Eleans say, Pherenice of Rhodes was daughter of
Diagoras the boxer, and Pherenice's character was so strong that to the Eleans
she seemed at first a man. At any rate she went about under a cloak at Olympia
and trained her own son Peisirodus. He too was a boxer, skilled of hand and no
less than his grandfather. When they understood the deceit, they shrank from
killing Pherenice, remembering Diagoras and Diagoras' sons (for Pherenice's
house were all Olympic victors), but a law was written that the trainer be
stripped and that not even he go untested among them.
* Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

The trainer also carries a strigil there for this reason perhaps: at Olympia
the athlete must be dusted in the palaestra and sunned. So that they may not
ruin their condition, the strigil reminds the athlete of oil and says he must
apply it so freely that after anointing he can even scrape it off. Some say a
trainer at Olympia killed the athlete with a sharpened strigil when he would
not hold out for the victory, and I concede the story, for it is better
believed than disbelieved. Let the strigil then be a sword against the
worthless among athletes, and let the trainer at Olympia have something beyond
the Hellanodikes.
* Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

The Lacedaemonians also wanted the trainers to know tactics, counting even the
contests themselves a practice of war, and one need not wonder, since even
dancing, the more idle of things in peace, the Lacedaemonians referred wholly
to war, dancing in a manner by which one will guard against a missile or send
it forth or be lifted from the ground and use the shield handily.
* Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

Whatever trainers contributed to athletes, whether by some encouragement or
rebuke or threat or device, these things are many and more than speech, but
let the more notable be told. Glaucus of Carystus the boxer, then, when he was
not trusted at Olympia against his opponent, Tisias the trainer led to victory
by bidding him strike the one from the plough; this was the drive of the right
hand against the opponent. For Glaucus had that hand so strong that once in
Euboea he straightened a bent ploughshare by striking it with his right as
with a hammer.
* Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

Arrichion the pancratiast, already winning two Olympiads, and fighting a third
Olympiad after those for the crown and already giving up, Eryxias the trainer
set into a love of death by shouting from outside that it is a fine shroud not
to give up at Olympia.
* Section 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

Promachus of Pellene: the trainer understood he was in love, and when the
Olympics were near, "Promachus," he said, "you seem to me to be in love." When
he saw him blush, "I did not ask this to expose you," he said, "but to help
you in the love. For I would even speak to the girl for you." And having
spoken nothing he came to the athlete bringing a speech not true, but worth
most to the lover: "She does not think you unworthy," he said, "to be among
her beloved if you win at Olympia." And Promachus, breathing again from what
he heard, not only won but even beat Poulydamas of Scotoussa after the lions
that Poulydamas had taken beside Ochus the Persian.
* Section 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23
:END:

Of Mandrogenes of Magnesia I myself heard the endurance he had used in his
prime for the pancratium, as he credited it to the trainer. For he said his
father was dead, and the house was upon a mother both strong and beyond a
woman, to whom the trainer wrote a letter of this kind: "If you hear your son
is dead, believe; if defeated, disbelieve." He said that admiring this letter
he showed every stoutness of heart, so that neither the trainer would lie nor
the mother be lied to.
* Section 24
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h24
:END:

Attalus the Egyptian won at Plataea for a second contest when the trainer
strengthened him, there being a law among them that the man defeated after a
victory die publicly and be judged selected before he set sureties for his
body. When no one would guarantee so great a thing the trainer put himself
under the law and strengthened the athlete to a second victory. For those
intending to take hold of a greater deed it is, I think, of good hope not to
be distrusted.
* Section 25
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h25
:END:

Since a crowd of such things is flowing in as we mix new with old, let us
examine the trainer himself, of what sort he should be and how much he should
know when he stands over the athlete. Let the trainer be neither a chatterer
nor untrained in tongue, so that the energy of the art is not dissolved by
chatter, nor what is done without speech appear too rustic, and let him have
surveyed all physiognomy. I command this for this reason. A boy athlete some
Hellanodikes or Amphictyon judges from such things as these: whether he has a
tribe and a fatherland, whether a father and a family, whether he is of free
people and not a bastard, and on all, whether he is young and not beyond a
boy. But whether continent or incontinent, whether a drunkard, whether a
glutton, whether bold or cowardly, they neither know, nor if they knew do the
laws speak to them about such things; but the trainer must know these
thoroughly, being a judge of nature. Let him know all the ethics in the eyes,
by which the sluggish among men are shown, and the intense, and ironists, and
those less enduring, and the incontinent. For black-eyed men have one
character, another those of bright and grey and bloodshot eyes, another of
yellow and speckled, of protruding and hollow, for nature marked seasons by
stars, but characters by eyes. And the proportion of the parts of the body, as
in statue-making, must be examined thus: the ankle must agree with the wrist,
the forearm with the shin, and the upper arm be matched with the thigh and the
buttock with the shoulder, the back be viewed against the belly, and the chest
stand out similarly to what is under the hip, and the head, being the figure
of the whole, stand in due measure to all these.
* Section 26
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h26
:END:

With these things said by me thus, let us not think training follows upon
them, but rather setting the trainee apart and putting nature to the test, how
it is composed and toward what. For where should huntsmen and horsemen have so
much account of dogs and horses as not to use the same for every form and
every quarry, but some dogs for this and some for that, and of horses make
some hunting partners, some racers, some chariot-drivers, and not even these
simply, but as each is fit for a side or a trace of the chariot, yet men be
unjudged, whom one must lead to Olympia or Pytho for proclamations that even
Heracles loved? I command him then to have surveyed the proportion of all I
named, and besides proportion also the characters of the humors.
* Section 27
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h27
:END:

And yet there is something older than this, which even Lycurgus the Spartiate
thought good. Standing by Lacedaemon as athletes of war he says, "let the
girls train and practice running in public, for the sake of good offspring of
course and of bearing better children through the body's being strong. For
when she comes to a husband she will not shrink from carrying water, nor from
grinding, because she has been trained from youth, and if she is yoked also to
a young man who trains with her, she will give back better children, tall and
strong and healthy." And Lacedaemon became so great in war because their
marriage matters were done thus.
* Section 28
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h28
:END:

Since then it is proper to begin from a human's birth, let the trainer go to
the boy athlete, looking first from the parents, whether they were joined
young and well-bred and free of diseases, as many as rest on the sinews and
the seats of the eyes, and go out into ears or viscera, for these diseases
somehow lodge along with nature, and while they are children they have slipped
in unseen, but they break out when the blood takes a change in the turns of
age. The seed of the young, if both also come together well-bred, contributes
strength and vigor to an athlete and unmixed blood and power of bones and pure
humors and even stature, and I would still say they bring bloom as well. Let
them not be unknown if they are not present at the boy's judging, if we are to
test the sowing soundly. For the argument will fall into simple-mindedness if
we put off the athlete already standing at the stadium and longing for the
wild olive and the laurel to the father and the mother, perhaps even dead when
he was an infant. The decisive moment must go forward, at which looking at the
athlete naked we shall not seem even to have been ignorant of how his
ancestors stand for him. The thought is hard and not altogether easy, yet not
far from the art. I therefore hand it over for knowledge.
* Section 29
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h29
:END:

What kind the noble and youthful sowing will send up I have shown. That from
those past their prime must be tested thus. Their skin is thin, the clavicles
cup-like, the veins raised as in those who have toiled, and their hip is
bloodless and the muscular parts weak. When they train there are more tests:
they are sluggish and their blood is raw from coldness, and the sweats lie on
the surface rather than rising from the convex and hollow parts, and these do
not bloom upon the labors unless we draw off the sweats. Some are not even fit
for ease, but need pauses, and they are spent even by labors beyond what they
have done. I disqualify these from all that is in contest (for this does not
confirm a man), and most of all from pancratium and boxing, for those not even
strong in the skin are easy to take with blows and wounds. Yet they must be
trained, or rather flattered by the trainer, since they need this both when
toiling and when training. If the sowing appears past its prime on one of the
two sides, the defects will be similar, but less evident. Diseased conditions
the blood will convict, for it must appear somehow turbid and sunk under the
bile. And such blood, even if it ever becomes breathing under a trainer,
changes again and is clouded, for what is not well born turns out hard.
* Section 30
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h30
:END:

A protruding throat also shows something, and wings of the shoulders, and a
neck that is raised and slipping too much where the clavicles meet. Moreover
those with the ribs closed and those opened beyond measure mark much of the
diseased. For in the former the viscera must be squeezed and they cannot bring
the breath out freely, nor take it in during labors, and they are caught by
continuous spoiling of food; in the latter the viscera are heavy and hanging,
so that the breath from them is also dull and supine in impulse, and food will
be distributed less to these, going more to the belly than to nourishment of
the body. So much about the birth of those who will compete; the one suited to
each of the contests must be examined thus.
* Section 31
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h31
:END:

Let the one who will compete in the five be heavier than the light athletes
and lighter than the heavy, tall, well-built, upright, without excess in the
muscular parts and not cramped. Let him have the legs rather long than
proportionate, and the loins supple and easy, both for the turns of the
javelin or also the discus and for the jump. For he will jump with less pain
and will break nothing of the body if, letting the hip down, he plants it in
the stance. He must also be long of hand and tall in the fingers, for he will
throw the discus much better if through the size of the fingers the rim of the
discus is sent from a more hollow hand, and he will move the javelin more
