To the Emperor Caesar Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Augustus and to the Emperor
Caesar Lucius Aurelius Commodus Augustus, Aelius Aristides sends greeting.

Formerly, greatest of kings, I used to send you declamations and speeches from
the schools and things of that kind; but now fortune has given me another
subject. Smyrna, the ornament of Asia, the pride of your empire, lies wasted
by fire and earthquakes. Stretch out a hand, by the gods, stretch it out, as
far as becomes you. Smyrna, which has enjoyed the greatest good fortune of the
Greece that now is, both from the gods and from you, from the emperors always
and from the Senate in common, has also suffered the greatest of the disasters
on record. Yet even in these things one thing was preserved for her by
fortune, as it were a token of salvation: you have seen the city; you know the
loss. Remember what you uttered on your journey as you looked toward her;
remember, when you had gone inside, how you were affected and how you affected
others. They were holding the Theoxenia, and you were taking your rest as if
among the most civilized of your possessions. What approach of the sight did
not make you gladder? What of all things

did you behold in silence and not with the praise that befits you? Nor did you
forget these things even after you had gone away. All of which now lie in
dust. That harbor is shut; the beauties of the marketplace are gone; the
ornaments of the streets have vanished; the gymnasia have been destroyed
together with the men and boys; of the temples some lie fallen, others have
sunk. The city that was fairest to look upon, and that took its name from
beauty for all mankind, has been shown as the most untimely of spectacles, a
mound of ruins and of corpses. The west winds blow over a desert. All that
remains looks to you, and all the rest of Asia looks with it, praying good
things for you now and always, and for Smyrna mercy from you, if indeed the
ground is still Smyrna. I know that I shall speak a paradoxical word, yet not,
I think, a foolish one. For even if the subject is unwelcome to you, it has
come of your good fortune, which has given you an occasion on which you will
use your nature most finely and most conspicuously, in addition to the many
great things you have already displayed. Let me hear no more of Lysimachus,
nor of Alexander himself, nor of Theseus and myths: become yourselves the
founders of the city; show her new from the beginning; let the whole city,
through and through, be yours. Whether you reckon her a veil of queens or a
diadem of kings or however you please, so save the city. What founders, what
kings will you not eclipse? What bounds of magnanimity will you not surpass,
if you make what was acquired for the city over time and piece by piece the
work of a single founding? And I have not said this as one giving counsel, nor
as teaching those who do not know; I have not been so maddened by the
disaster. But men think it right to pray even so: "Give victory to Ajax," and
"Now again befriend me,

Athena." In the same manner, then, in which we converse with the gods, it is
without offense that we write to you. And indeed we pray to the gods on behalf
of these things, but of you, the most divine rulers, we make request. And it
is a fine thing, I suppose, to make such requests both of gods and of men.

You perhaps wish to hear how I myself escaped. Some days before what happened,
the god moved me and set me in a certain place, and ordered me to remain where
I was; and it was while I was staying there that I learned of what had
occurred. And when I learned it I could not keep still; nothing else remained,
I think, but to call upon the gods and you. For this reason I neither waited
for a public embassy nor thought it right to look to anyone else for what he
would do, but, considering that if the matter spoke to anyone at all it spoke
also to me, I appointed myself a servant, thinking myself sufficient, if for
nothing else, at least to bewail the city's misfortunes. Others, then, who
have had power with emperors have obtained gifts for their fatherlands when
those fatherlands were doing well; but I, if I have anything of my own with
you, ask and beg that this favor be granted to the city: that she not be
thrown out like a broken vessel condemned as useless, but that she live again
through you. There came into my mind also the story of Solon, who, they say,
when the constitution had been overthrown, took shield and spear and sat
before his house, unable, I suppose, to help, but showing how he stood in
mind. And my case is even more. For even if I had no deed to accomplish, I
knew that to call your humanity into action would become a deed of mine, so
that I should not have been eager in vain. And as for what is outside the
gates, it does not much disturb me, since I did not even congratulate the
patrons when they were overdoing it; let whoever wishes make request for those
things: nothing on my part stands in the way. But as for what is within the
circuit, both old and new, memorials of honor toward you and at the same time
of honor from you, and things that give the city distinction before her peers

and before the strangers who arrive, and further for strangers an occasion and
consolation of every fortune, I would gladly come to that day on which I shall
see these things standing. And whenever I reflect by myself that one of the
emperors of old, not of your house (may that never be, nor could it happen
under any of you), but one of those before, while playing at draughts, as the
story goes, let fall a remark that he did not wish the Nasamones to exist, and
then the Nasamones perished, I think it reasonable to hope of you that if you
are in earnest and use your own character, and if you say and show only this
much, that you wish Smyrna to exist, you will quickly show us all what we long
for. And I do not use examples that are foreign, but your own, the care you
took of the cities in Italy, so as to restore them in every respect. Those,
then, you recovered when they had long been exhausted; but Smyrna, which was
just now in bloom and has just now collapsed, restore again to the number of
cities, having formerly adorned her temples and now saving her whole form. And
the city deserves to be saved not only for the sake of her appearance, but
also for the goodwill which she has shown you through all time, having taken
part in the war against Antiochus, and having taken part in that against
Aristonicus, having endured sieges and fought battles of no small account, of
which the memorials were still at her gates even now. And further, when your
army lacked clothing and the general had been killed, they brought the general
into the city and buried him within the present gates, and they distributed
their tunics to the soldiers, man giving to man. Such they were toward you who
rule; and toward their kinsmen, what? To write everything would be no small
task. But when earthquakes and continuous famines once occurred about this
lower

region of Asia, and some were even destroyed by chasms, and a manifold
disaster held the cities, they saved some of the cities with grain and money
and by leaving nothing of zeal wanting: the Chians, the Erythraeans, the
Teians, the Halicarnassians. For the things, then, in which one city then
aided the others, not even any city at all is now sufficient to aid her. The
hope from you remains. And formerly she used to carry off prizes for her
deeds, land and panoplies and spoils, with which you honored her; but now she
has taken from fortune the first place in disasters, whose solution is yours.
And what is most worth saying and wondering at of all is the change of
fortune. For of the temple that has now sunk, so splendid, it is said that she
obtained it in this way: when a contest took place, Asia was preferred over
the other nations, and among the cities in Asia Smyrna so far that the rest of
Asia received only seven votes, and four hundred went to the city alone. This,
then, you might perhaps recover even through Asia, if it seemed good to you;
but the restoration of the whole is yours alone, to whom the gods have given
things of such magnitude. Whenever, then, I look to the greatness of the
disaster, no speech seems to me sufficient, but all fall short of the measure;
but whenever I look to your virtue and nature and your readiness for
benefactions, it comes upon me to fear that I seem to have spoken more than
enough. I do not think this is so; but if it is, you will pardon it, I know. A
man caught in disaster utters many things even against his own character, and
especially a disaster he would never have expected.
