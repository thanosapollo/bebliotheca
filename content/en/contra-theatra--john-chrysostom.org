* Against the Theatres
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

That one must not approach theatres, and that they make men finished
adulterers, and that this is a cause of despondency and of war, and on
Abraham.
=56.541=
1. I think many of those who lately left us and deserted to the theatres of
lawlessness are present today. And I wished to know these clearly, so as to
drive them from the sacred porches, not that they might remain outside
continually, but that they might return again after they have been corrected.
For fathers too often drive erring children from the house, and bar them from
the table, not that they may fall away from these things for good, but that
becoming better by this admonition they may return with the due glory and
honor to the paternal inheritance. Shepherds do this too: they keep the sheep
filled with scab away from the healthy, so that having put off the sickness
they may return in safety to the healthy again, and may not, while still
diseased, fill the whole flock =56.542= with that sickness. For this very
reason we too wished to know these men; but even if we cannot distinguish them
with the eyes of sense, the word will certainly recognize them, and laying
hold of their conscience will easily persuade them to withdraw of their own
will, teaching that he alone is within who furnishes a mind worthy of the
sojourn here; so that the man who with a corrupted way of life takes part in
this sacred assembly, even if he brings the body in here, has been cast out,
and is more driven away than those who have been shut out and are not yet able
to share the sacred table. For those who have been expelled according to the
laws of God, and remain outside, still have good hopes; for if they wish to
correct their faults, =56.543= through the Church from which they fell they
will be able to return again with a clean conscience. But those who have
defiled themselves, and have been commanded not to set foot here until they
have purged the stain that comes from the sins, and then behave shamelessly,
make the wound worse and the sore greater. For it is not so grievous to sin as
the shamelessness after the sin, and the refusal to obey the priests who
command such things. And what so great a sin have these committed, someone
says, that they should be driven from these sacred precincts? And what other
sin greater than this do you seek, when they have made themselves finished
adulterers, and shamelessly, like rabid dogs, leap upon the sacred table? And
if you wish to learn the manner of the adultery as well, I will not tell you
my own word, but that of him who is going to judge our whole life: For he who
has looked at a woman, he says, to lust after her has already committed
adultery with her in his heart. And if a woman met by chance in the
marketplace, and dressed as it happened, has often captured the man who looked
inquisitively by the spectacle of her appearance, those who not simply, nor by
chance, but with such eagerness as even to despise the Church, and who for
this reason went up there and spent the day, and were nailed to the sights of
those dishonored women, how will they be able to say that they did not look
for lust, when even languid words, and whorish songs, and a voice full of
much pleasure, and paintings of the eyes, and cosmetics of the cheek, and a
dress elaborately composed, and a figure full of sorcery, and many other
enchantments prepared for the deceit and bait of the beholders, and slackness
of soul of the spectators, and much dissipation, and the invitation to
wantonness from the place itself, and of the sounds that came before and of
those after these, that through pipes, that through flutes and the other


such things, a melody bewitching, and softening the firmness of the mind, and
preparing the souls of those seated for the plots of the harlots, and making
them easy to capture, having through these things set them at a distance from
Christ? For if here, where there are psalms, and prayers, and hearing of
divine oracles, and fear of God, and much reverence, desire has often stolen
in unnoticed, like some wicked robber: how could those sitting in a theatre,
seeing and hearing nothing sound, but full of much shamefulness, much folly,
and besieged through all things, both through ears and through eyes, become
superior to that wicked desire? And if they cannot, how will they be able to
be freed from the charges of adultery? And those not freed from the charges of
adultery, how will they be able without repentance to set foot on these holy
porches, and to share this fair assembly? Therefore I exhort and beg that
first, having wiped away by confession and repentance and all the rest the sin
from the spectacle there, they thus hear the divine oracles. For it is no
ordinary offense that has been committed by us; and this one may see clearly
even from examples. For if some household slave put into a chest, where the
master's costly and gold-woven garments were stored, a slave's garment full of
filth and of many lice, would you have borne the outrage mildly, tell me? And
what if someone poured dung and mire into a golden vessel that was accustomed
always to hold myrrh: would you not also have laid blows on the one who
committed this? Then shall we take such care of chests, and vessels, and
garments, and myrrhs, and shall we think our own soul cheaper than all these?
And where the spiritual myrrh was poured in, shall we throw in diabolic
processions, and satanic hearings, and songs full of fornication? And how will
God bear these things, tell me? And yet the interval between myrrh and mire,
and between a master's garments and a slave's, is not so great as the interval
between the spiritual grace and this wicked operation. Do you not fear, O man,
looking with the same eyes both at the bed on the orchestra, where the
abominable dramas of adultery are performed, and at this sacred table, where
the fearful mysteries are performed? hearing with the same ears a harlot
speaking shamefully, and a Prophet and Apostle initiating you; receiving with
the same heart the poisonous drugs, and the fearful sacrifice? Is it not from
this that there come overturnings of lives, and corrup56.544 tions of
marriages, and wars, and fights in the houses? For when you have been
dissolved by the spectacle there, and have become more slack and more wanton
and an enemy of all temperance, and returning you see your own wife, you will
look on her more distastefully, whoever she may be. For having been set on
fire by the desire in the theatres, and having been captured by that alien,
bewitched spectacle, you dishonor the chaste and modest partner of your whole
life, you insult her, you surround her with countless reproaches, having
nothing at all to accuse, but being ashamed to name the passion and to show
the wound which you received there and came home with, you weave other
pretexts, seeking irrational occasions of hostility, and overlooking all
things at home, and gaping toward that foul and unclean desire from which you
received the wound; and having the echo of the voice sitting in the soul as if
still sounding, and the figure, and the glance, and the movements, and all the
images of fornication, you look with pleasure on nothing in the house. And why
do I speak of the wife and the house? For you will look even on the church
itself more distastefully, and you will hear with hostility the words about
temperance, about dignity. For what is said will no longer be teaching for
you, but accusation, and being dragged little by little into despair, you will
completely tear yourself away from this teaching that profits all. For this
reason I entreat you all both to flee the wicked sojourns in the theatres
yourselves, and to drag away from there those who have given themselves to
these things. For it is not entertainment, but ruin, and


punishment, and chastisement, all the things that happen there. For what is
the profit of this temporary pleasure, when from it continuous pain is born,
and by day and by night, stung by the desire, you are difficult and
displeasing to all? Examine yourself, then: what you become when you withdraw
from church, and what when from theatres; and compare both days, and you will
need none of our words. For the comparison of each day will suffice to show
the greatness both of the benefit from here and of the harm from there. These
things I have already said to your love, and I will never cease saying them.
For we will both console those who are sick with such diseases, and we will
make the healthy more secure. For the word about these things is useful to
both: to the one, that they may stand off, to the other, that they may not
fall in. But since those who rebuke must also do this in measure, having
stopped this exhortation at this point, we will pay you what was left from the
former subject, returning again to our father Abraham. For it is the custom of
painters, when they are about to make some likeness, to have those who are
going to be painted sit beside them one and two and three days, so that by the
continuity of the viewing they may preserve the accuracy of the form without
fail. Since then it is set before us now to paint, not a type of bodily form,
but beauty of soul, and intelligible comeliness, and the flourishing way of
life of the righteous man, and the meekness, and gentleness, and greatness of
soul, and all his other virtue, we must spend more time with this man, so that
by the continuity of the word's inspection we may not miss the likeness of the
archetype. For if images of a body bring some consolation to those who see
them, much more the images of the soul. And those it is not possible to see
everywhere, but they must be fixed continually in one place; but this you may
carry about wherever you wish, nothing hindering. For having stored it in the
treasuries of the mind, wherever you are, you will be able to look continually
into it, and to reap much benefit from it. And just as those who have diseased
eyes, holding sponges and rags of dark-blue garments, and looking continually
at them, reap some consolation of the disease from that color, so you too, if
you have the image of the patriarch Abraham before your own eyes, and
continually gaze at him, even if ten thousand times anger, or some other of
the unseemly things, trouble and confuse the eye of the mind, looking at that
character of virtue you will receive the health whole, and the philosophy
pure. For the rest, if it seems good, let us lead the word on to the matter at
hand.
2. The blessed Moses, beloved, who by works according to piety was made
intimate with God, who beyond all the prophets after him was counted worthy of
a more particular converse =56.545= of the Creator, so that God spoke with him
mouth to mouth in form, and not through riddles, who by a divine fire that
knew only how to give light, not to burn, was initiated into the vision of
God, who by divine counsel was styled a god to Pharaoh, and by sharing a
venerable title was adorned beyond all who then were, who was appointed a most
fearful minister of the God-sent plagues that came to pass in Egypt, and
appeared a most swift servant of the active power of God, who by the
torchlight of the Holy Spirit had the intelligible eye of the soul
enlightened, and became a late expositor of the ancient works of the Almighty:
this blessed man, after the well-ordered and fair narrative of the making of
the world, proceeded to the history of the men of old, separately of the good,
separately of the wicked, and recorded of the good an end truly
thrice-blessed, and of those opposite to them an accursed one, so that having
from the making of the world the doctrines concerning piety to be taught, and
from those who went before in life both to hate wickedness and to honor
virtue, we may easily accomplish the God-loving and blessed life. Many
achievements, then, of virtue and


nobility of ancient men he recorded, setting these forth as a far-shining
pattern for later generations toward imitation. He teaches us therefore
through Abel to offer firstfruits to God from the abundant gifts of God before
using them, and not to prefer to the honor of God an irrational passion of the
belly. For this blessed Abel sacrificed to the most ancient of beings the
firstborn of the sheep, offering none of the last things to the worship of the
First, but glorifying the first Begetter with the firstfruits of what had come
to be. After him the wondrous Noah teaches toward righteousness, he alone, in
a whole world that was transgressing, having kept unwavering in good deeds the
balance of righteousness. A great lesson of temperance is set before the
young: the blessed Joseph, who in a young body blossomed a white old age of
chastity, and in a slave's figure appeared nobly a master of the pleasures of
the flesh. But these blessed men are perhaps certain partial images of good
works; yet he has made mention of one man, Abraham the forefather, having
raised in one person alone a pillar of all virtue for the whole world toward
imitation. Therefore all the righteous, all the holy boast of the faithful
Abraham as father, and set the kinship with him as a most august glory. For
through this man the zeal of faith belongs to the faithful; to the hospitable
this man is a teacher of hospitality, showing what must be done by works, not
by words; the athletes of piety wear crowns, having emulated his courage; all
the great-souled are images like this archetype; the rivalry toward this man
has brought forth the multitude of so many righteous; from this God-loving
root all the Christ-loving have sprung. And why do I number them by kind? For
all, so to speak, whose light shines before men, were kindled from the
faithful lamp of the forefather; so many good children did the way according
to piety bear him. For he did not lie who said, I have made you a father of
many nations. O blessed soul, that contained so great a multitude of
achievements under one! You are a truly all-bearing land, you a field
all-fruited of works according to piety, you a meadow in bloom abounding with
all kinds of flowers of righteousness, you also a comely crown, having in the
midst the pearl of faith shining forth. God commanded: Go out from your land;
and you did not neglect it, but you held to the way before the command was
completed. He added to Go out from your land the words And from your
kindred; and you were not made soft by affection toward the race, preferring
the Maker of the race. God said, Go out from your land and from your kindred,
and what was heavier than these, And from the house of your father; but you
counted nothing heavy of the things God commands. Neither honor of a father,
nor longing for a mother, nor affection of kinsmen held back the eagerness of
your soul. You did
not consider how an aged mother would lament over you, how an old father would
weep over you. For you did not think you ought to be more humane than the one
who was bringing the call. You had a father worthy of reverence, but not as
the Father of all; you longed for a mother, but piety more. You despised a
father's house, and ancestral wealth. For you knew only the wealth through
faith as fitting for the soul, the immortal possession, the inviolable
treasure. O what a star, brighter than the morning star, you shone from the
east into Palestine, in a life dark with polytheism =56.546= flashing bright
rays of piety! You God cast as a good sweetness of piety into the bitter way
of life of the Canaanites as well. You, like salt of loving-kindness, became a
seasoning of a wicked life. You appeared a calm harbor of hospitality in the
midst of a surge of the inhumanity of the foreigners.
3. How shall I describe, O blessed one, your humane tent? How shall I not
marvel at the oak called Mamre, which you tamed by your good ways? The tent
was a common dwelling for travelers, one abundant hearth of all strangers, an
unpaid inn of the whole inhabited world, not lying in a hidden place, so that
the gain of the rest might escape notice, but in a desert, yet in the middle
of the road, and almost at a common starting-point of all


Palestine. And you lodged all day under the oak, appearing to travelers as a
good angel of rest. Christ appeared to you, O wondrous one, escorted by two
angels, and through hospitality you became tent-fellow both with God and with
angels. O blessed tent, that through a dispensation contained God with angels!
Christ appeared to you in the figure of a man, showing you beforehand the
mysteries of his divine and saving sojourn; yet the glory of the one present
did not escape you because of the slave's figure. For you had other eyes, by
which a Master is recognized. You recognized therefore the mediator of God,
the Son who was going to be known in the midst of two living beings. I marvel,
O blessed one, at your eagerness concerning those who appeared; for though it
was possible to command a servant and to fulfill the need by an order, you ran
to the cattle, an old man, supporting an aged body with a flourishing longing.
And your spouse shared the works with you eagerly, and crowned the table with
a multitude of unleavened loaves; for the eagerness of hospitality did not
wait for the leavening. And all that was done was of fear and reverence, not
as of one entertaining men, but as of one offering a sacrifice to God. Fair, O
blessed one, is the fruit of your hospitality for these things, fair also the
recompense of the love of God. For beyond all hopes the bonds of a barren womb
are loosed, and the dead members of an old man bloom toward the seed of a
genuine child, and you receive from the only-begotten God good tidings
concerning an only-begotten son. For at this season, he says, I will come, and
Sarah shall have a son. How shall I praise your faith according to its worth,
O most blessed? For you did not waver toward the promise, nor limp concerning
the announcement, nor consider your own body as good as dead, being about a
hundred years old, nor look to the barrenness of Sarah's womb, but you were
strengthened in faith, knowing that what God has promised, he is able also to
fulfill. For the craftsman of nature is not a slave to the passions of nature,
because God is not subject to the necessity of nature in the things he works,
being the craftsman of all nature; but nature follows toward every kind of
change the free will of the Maker. Therefore you were not cheated of the
hopes, O blessed one, but you are named father at a stated term, in a dead
age. And first you see fruit from Sarah, and you marvel how beyond hope the
springs of milk flow for the aged woman, and after a little you lisp to the
newborn, teaching the offspring to call you father, asking from the child this
name which grace, going before through a promise, had ratified. How shall I
narrate your achievements after these things, O wondrous one? How shall I
report your high and celebrated faith? Your faith is beyond faith, your
devotion to God beyond all hearing. For who will ever believe that a father
offered his own child as a sacrifice genuinely to God? Perhaps only we believe
who have not become fathers, who have not been taught the affection of
children by nature. But let divine Scripture too come as a witness of what has
been said, like a high herald, crying out the God-loving mind of the blessed
man. After these things, it says, God tested Abraham. And he tested, not so
that he might learn by trial what was unknown, using the outcomes as witnesses
of the man's mind; for God has never yet been ignorant of anything, himself
always being sufficient by his own vision for the knowledge of all things; but
so that men, having learned by trial the one whom he clearly knew beforehand,
might have a fair pattern toward imitation of piety. And come, let us consider
what the words of the trial were. Abraham, he says, Abraham. And he said, Here
am I. He said to him: Take your son, the beloved, whom you have loved, Isaac,
and go into the high land, and offer =56.547= him to me for a whole burnt
offering. O how great a test of the forefather's faith God worked through the
=56.547= words so spoken! For it would have sufficed only to say, Take Isaac,
and offer him for a whole burnt offering, neither saying son, nor moving the
father's bowels by the mention of the beloved. But since for those who are
dissolved in inward goods the sudden onset of evils brings a heavier


pain, for this reason he says, Take your son, and he adds, The beloved, and
again, Whom you have loved, so that having lifted the child-loving old man by
these good titles into hopes of kind promises, then suddenly bringing on the
destruction of the beloved, he might strike the bowels of the begetter more
heavily by commands contrary to hope, not hating the righteous man, but
wishing his faith to appear more splendid to the world. Therefore before he
says, Offer him for a whole burnt offering, from the first fair-sounding words
he makes many good things to be imagined by the righteous man. For having
heard God calling and saying, Abraham, Abraham, at once the God-loving man
rejoiced at the call, was dissolved toward the voice, considering, as is
likely: Does God again count me worthy of the wondrous converse with him? Does
he wish to hallow me with his divine words? Or does he wish to transfer me
again to a better land? Or does he wish to destroy the wicked Canaanites, as
the Sodomites, and not wish his own servant, as in the case of Sodom, to be
ignorant of the will of the judgment? Does he generously promise me good
tidings of a second child after Isaac, wishing to give eyes to my old age with
the eyes of two children? And when he heard, Take, before he heard, The son,
he thought God would converse with him about sacrifices, asking from him an
honor of blessings in return for the gift of the child. And having heard, Your
son, for the time he thought of Ishmael, supposing that Sarah's jealousy had
been loosed, and that for this reason he was being restored again by the
humane one to the paternal hearth. But when he heard clearly, The beloved whom
you have loved, Isaac, at once his heart boiled, he rejoiced in his soul,
thinking that God would arrange with him concerning a betrothal, joining as
God, for the increase of the race, a wife to the young man fruitful for the
growth of a child. And God calls him into the high land. Why? Either wholly so
that from a lookout and elevated place he might show him the whole land as to
the heir of the promise, or wholly he wishes to anoint him as patriarch while
I still live, or to converse face to face, and by a living voice to hand over
laws of a God-loving life, walking in which he will please the Creator.
4. These things then and such the blessed man expected to hear from God; but
God, what? Offer him, he says, for a whole burnt offering. A burdensome
command even for an ordinary man, much more for a father, and that a
child-loving one. But O the unsurpassable faith! O the God-loving soul! God
had not yet ceased calling, and Abraham was ready for the obedience, already
making the end of the command the beginning of the journey. Who then of other
fathers would have borne such a voice, and would not have fallen at once to
the ground, his limbs loosed by despondency? Or perhaps he would have been
destroyed at the command itself, being set on fire by a father's disposition?
For hear the unconsolable nature of the command. God did not ask for the young
man simply for slaughter, so that the old man might have the remains as a
consolation of the passion, sitting beside the tomb; but for a whole burnt
offering, for a burning, for a complete disappearance, so that he might not
even have the pouring out of the heart's pain by weeping over the body. But
yet even if the command was unbearable, and able to strike by being spoken
alone, still it did not drive the man out of the divine mind; for he knew that
everything God commands is expedient and good. Therefore he endures the
command eagerly, and with unspeakable pleasure he works the sacrifice, having
reasoned these things, as is likely: childless from now on, but I become a
child of God through obedience; I lose the heir, but I inherit the things to
come; I shall not see the son, but I shall see God; a child will not tend my
old age, but God will nourish my old age; I shall be blamed by the foolish,
but I shall be accepted by the All-wise; I shall grieve Sarah, but I shall
please the Creator; I have much consolation; his death grieves me, but it
makes him a priest of the Most High; it sets me childless, but a father of a
first martyr. For does there perish


Isaac? The body is purified by the fire, he is loosed from the pleasures of
the flesh, he will go out from =56.548= life, but he will go in to the Lord.
With such reasonings having nerved the collapses of nature, he hastens to the
sacrifice, having saddled the donkey, having sharpened the knife, having made
ready the wood, the fire, leading the son as a lamb, and he told the divine
voice to no one, he did not communicate it to friends, nor even to the partner
of the offspring, not hating the spouse, but fearing the serpent, lest on a
pretext of love of children it blunt the eagerness of piety. O extreme faith!
O unsurpassable wisdom! The patriarch considered how many evils would happen
if the divine command became known, how great a lament there would be, how
great a weeping, how many tears. For this reason he is silent, quenching by
silence a great flame of wailings. For what do you think Sarah would have
done, having heard about the destruction of the child? Would she not at once
have clung around Isaac, contriving, if it were possible, to hide him again
inside in the womb, saying, Dearest child, are you being destroyed, are we
being besieged in sacrifices? Sooner, I think, she would have given up her own
soul than have betrayed the child; and there would have been a necessity of
one of two things, either that the sacrifice not take place, or that the
mother be offered up together with the child. And she would have said also to
Abraham some such words as these: Be sober, O husband, be sober, interpreter
of a humane God. A divine voice commands nothing against a human being; a
wicked demon envies you your blessed parenthood, and wishes to overturn the
grace of God, bringing under a pretext of piety a curse of child-murder. Do
you not know the wondrous Melchizedek, the priest of God Most High? Learn from
him the laws of sacrifices, learn with what sacrifices God delights, although
long ago he initiated you into priesthood when you were returning from the
cutting down of the five kings. Were not bread then and wine for him, holy
offerings, bloodless and clean sacrifices? not a calf? not a sheep? not a
child? For he did not think at all that God is delighted with blood. Will God
eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats? Sacrifice to God a
sacrifice of praise, and pay your vows to the Most High. And if it is truly
God who appeared, understand, husband, the will of the one who appeared. God
is making trial of your disposition toward the child, he wishes you to be
known, how you stand toward offspring: whether you love the son, whether you
are disposed as a father, whether you have been defeated by the bowels of
nature, whether you have chosen to die instead of the child. He wishes you
also to become a fair example of love of children to the Canaanites; therefore
not to obey, rather to speak against the slaughter of the child, to see gray
hairs torn, bitter tears flowing, to appear through these things a lover of
the child. I fear lest you undergo reproaches of child-murder before the
humane one through the consent alone. Husband, be persuaded by me: Be not
righteous overmuch. God will praise this disobedience of yours. And if God
truly wishes Isaac to be offered up, groan bitterly, entreat, fall down, add
tears to the words of the supplication. Good is the one who asks the
sacrifice, far more humane than a father; he will change the vote, he will
change the judgment. The race of human beings would never have perished by a
flood, if those who were transgressing had washed themselves with tears of
supplication. Sodom would still have been, if they had propitiated the judge
with a groan of the heart. If then he pities transgressors, much more you, the
righteous. You once saved Lot from the God-sent fire, having prayed to the
judge; save now also the child from the fire of the mercy-seat, having begged
God. You entreated for Sodomites, unjust men, and men who were often about to
perish justly, and will you not entreat for a blameless child? Use again the
boldness of speech; say, By no means; you who judge all the earth, will you
not do judgment? You will not destroy a righteous man with an ungodly. Be
reminded of the old love of children which you had when you did not have a
child. For before you received Isaac, these were your Master's bright
promises: possession of a sweet land, abundance of money and possessions,
increase of the race so great that it is numbered with the innumerable
natures, sand of the seashore, and stars of heaven;


but yet, so many being the good things in the promises, you counted what was
said a jest, and with boldness you cried out, lamenting childlessness and
saying: What will you give me, Lord? and I perish childless. Having uttered
this voice, you received a child. Become a father, utter this now, that you
may save the child. Say again, What will you give me, Master, destroying my
heir? Woe is me, perhaps then you were a lover of children, when you were not
a father. If Sarah were saying these things, what should Abraham have done?
=56.549= Clearly, leaving the divine command, to make speeches about piety,
and to persuade Sarah that, if God takes this one, he gives you a better. And
how would he have persuaded a woman drunk with the passion? But suppose he has
persuaded her. For the woman is pious, and housemate of a most faithful man;
so then it would wholly follow that, having been persuaded, she would send the
son forth traveling to the sacrifice, perhaps forcing herself to contain the
pain because of what seemed good to God, but being led unwillingly by nature
itself toward laments. How many servants there, bitten by longing for the
young man? how many serving-women sharing the mistress's suffering? How many
Canaanites, acquaintances and neighbors, marveling at what had befallen him?
From these a great tumult, and wailing, and lament, and tears, as over a dead
man being sent forth. And the mother, having cried out greatly, would have
said: Give me, child, last embraces. I hoped to be sent forth by you, child,
and that my death would be adorned with your tears; but since the vote of the
Creator has conquered my hopes, and I lament this one by whom I expected to be
lamented, this alone I ask of you: as you step upon the fire of the sacrifice,
be an ambassador to the Divine on my behalf, that I may depart from life
before the priest of the sacrifice returns. For I cannot see a father's right
