Moabites and Ammonites not even have entrance into the Church of God,
dialectical and mischief-working arguments, who, meddling with the generation
of God and the unspeakable procession, rashly rise up against the Godhead; as
if it were necessary that the things beyond speech either be accessible to
them alone, or else not even exist, because they themselves have not grasped
them. But we, following the divine Scriptures, and loosening the
stumbling-blocks laid in them for those who are blind, will hold to salvation,
daring all things rather than to be rash against God. The testimonies, then,
we will leave to others, already written out many times by many, and by us not
as a side-work. And at the same time it is very shameful for me at least now
to collect the proofs of things long believed. For it is not the best order to
teach first and then to learn, not only in the divine things and those of such
greatness, but not even in anything of the small things and those worth
nothing. And to loosen and articulate the stumbling-blocks from Scripture is
not of the present time, but of a more complete and greater zeal than the
present impulse of the subject. Our teaching, then, to take it in a summary,
is this. And I have gone through these things, not that I may contend against
the adversaries (for we have already often contended, even if moderately), but
that I may show you the character of my teachings, whether I am not a
fellow-combatant of yours, standing against the same, and for the same.
19. This, men, is the defense of my presence; if it is praiseworthy, thanks to
God, and to you who called me; if it is more deficient than the hope, thanks
even so. For it is not altogether blameworthy, I know well, and I do not
distrust you when you say so. Have we at all made a gain of this people? Have
we at all managed our own affairs, which I see the many suffering? Have we at
all grieved the Church? Others perhaps, who think they have taken us by
default, we have opposed with our word; but you in nothing, so far as I am
conscious to myself. I have not taken an ox of yours, says the great Samuel
=36.481=, contending with Israel over the king; nor a propitiation of your
souls, the Lord is witness among you; nor this and that, saying more, that I
myself may not number each thing; but I have kept the priesthood clean and
unadulterated. And if I have loved power, or height of thrones, or to tread
courts of kings, may I not even have any other brightness, or may I throw it
away if I have acquired it.
20. What then is it that I say? For I am not an unpaid worker of virtue, nor
have I arrived at so much virtue. Give me the wage of the labors. What wage is
this? Not what some of those who take all things lightly would suppose, but
what is safe for me to seek. Give us rest from the long labors; reverence this
gray hair; honor the sojourn abroad; bring in another in our place, the one
sought after for your sake, whoever is clean of hands, whoever is not without
understanding in voice, whoever is able to grant you all things, and to bear
with you the ecclesiastical cares; since of these things especially now is the
time. But look at me also, how this body is, spent by time, and by disease,
and by toil. What need have you of a timid and unmanly old man, dying, so to
speak, each day, not in the body only, but also in the cares; I who scarcely
even discuss these things with you? Do not disbelieve a teacher's voice; for
you have never yet disbelieved. I am worn out, being accused of gentleness. I
am worn out, fighting both with word and with envy, both with enemies and with
our own. Some strike the breast, and hit less; for what is openly hostile is
easy to guard against. Others watch the back, and are more painful; for the
unsuspected is more deadly. And if I was also a pilot, and of the very
skilled; then the sea around us was great, and boiling around the ship; and
great was the faction of those sailing in, some yoked in strife with others
over this and that, and beating against one another and against the waves, how
long would I have held out sitting at the tillers, so as to fight both sea and
those sailing in, and to save the ship without danger from a double storm? For
of those with whom, when they were fellow-combatants in every way, salvation
was hard, how is it possible, when they are fighting against us, not to sink?
21. What need to say the rest? But how shall I bear this holy war? For let a
war also be called holy =36.484=, just as also barbarian. How shall I join and
bring into one these who sit opposite and shepherd against one another, and
the people broken off with them and set against them; as in the chasms of
earthquakes, the neighboring and adjoining places; or in pestilential diseases
the attendants and household, the sickness being easily passed from some to
others; and not only this, but also the portions of the inhabited world have
suffered along with those in faction, so that East and West were then
separated into an opposing share, and are in danger of becoming portions of
opinion no less than of the ends. For how long, mine and yours, and the old
and the new, the more learned or the more spiritual, the more well-born or the
more low-born, the richer in multitude or the poorer? I am ashamed of old age,
being called of others, when I have been saved by Christ.
22. I cannot bear your horse-races, and the theatres, and this counterpoised
madness both in expenses and in eager strivings. We unyoke, we yoke against,
we snort against one another, we almost beat the air, as they do, and we throw
dust into heaven, as those who have gone out of their minds; and under other
masks we fulfill our own contentions, we become evil umpires of rivalry, and
unfeeling judges of affairs. Today sharing thrones and of the same opinion, if
those who lead us carry us so; tomorrow opposite in throne and opposite in
opinion, if the wind blows against. With the enmity and the friendship, the
names also; and, what is most terrible, we are not ashamed, using the same
hearers of contrary things; nor have we stood upon the same things, contention
making us different at different times. Certain changes of Euripuses, or
ebb-tides. Just as then if youths were playing and being played with in the
midst of the marketplace, it would be very shameful and not ours, leaving our
own pursuits, to keep company with them (for children's playthings are not
seasonable for old age); so neither, if the others are carrying and being
carried, would I myself, even knowing something better than the many, accept
being one of them rather than, what I am, free with obscurity. For besides the
other things I suffer something of this sort too: I do not for the most part
keep company with the many, nor do I endure to walk the same =36.485= way;
rashly perhaps and ignorantly, yet I suffer it all the same. The pleasant
things of others pain me, and I take pleasure in the painful things of others.
So that I would not even wonder at this, if I were even bound as unmanageable,
and seemed to the many to be out of my mind, which one of those who
philosophized among the Greeks is said to have suffered, being accused of
madness for his sobriety, because he laughed at all things, seeing the things
eagerly pursued by the many as worthy of laughter; or even be thought full of
new wine, as later the disciples of Christ for speaking in tongues; not being
known, that it was a power of the Spirit, not a standing-out of the mind.
23. For consider also our charges. So much time, he says, since you have been
leading the Church, with the turn of the time, and the impulse of the one in
power, so great a matter; what of the change has been signaled to us? How many
insolent men have there been against us before? What terrible thing have we
not suffered? Not insults? Not threats? Not banishments? Not seizures of
goods? Not confiscations? Not burnings of presbyters at sea? Not temples
profaned with blood of saints, and become charnel-houses instead of temples?
Not public slaughters of elder bishops, or more properly to say of patriarchs?
Not that every place was impassable to the pious alone? Not whatever one might
say of the terrible things? Of which what have we given back to those who did
them, since the being permitted to act, doing well, was turned around, and it
was necessary to discipline the insolent? I let the rest go; but our own
things, that we may not speak yours, have we not been persecuted? Have we not
been insulted? Have we not been driven from churches, houses, the deserts
themselves, the most terrible thing? Have we not borne a people gone mad?
prefects insulting? emperors being insulted, and together with the commands?
Then what? We have become stronger, and the persecutors have fled away. For
this: a sufficient vengeance for me against those who do injustice is the
authority to act in return. But to them it does not seem so; for they are
exceedingly complete and just about the requital; and for this they demand the
things of the time. What prefect, he says, has been fined? What people has
been brought to sense? Which kindlers of peoples? What fear have we granted to
ourselves even toward the future? =36.488=
24. And perhaps they would also reproach us with these things (for they have
also reproached): the ambitious display of the table, the venerable character
of the clothing, the processions, the haughtiness toward those who meet us.
For I did not know that our rivalry is toward consuls and prefects, and the
most distinguished of generals, who do not have where they will throw their
own things. And that we must make the belly groan as we luxuriate on the
things of the poor, and use the necessaries for superfluities, and belch forth
from the altars. And to be carried on delicate horses, and to be lifted high
on chariots splendidly, and to be preceded in procession and to be clucked
about, and that all give way to us, as to wild beasts, and split apart, or
even that we be clear from afar as we approach. If these terrible things have
happened, they have passed; grant me this injustice. Set another before you
who will please the many; but give me the solitude, and the country life, and
God, whom alone, and through simplicity, we shall please. It is a terrible
thing if we shall be deprived of words, and gatherings, and festivals, and
these applauses by which we are given wings, and of household, and friends,
and honors, and beauty of a city and greatness, and the lightning shining
round from every side those who look toward these things, but have not bent
inward; but not yet so much as if I shall be thrown into uproar and stained by
the troubles and seethings in the midst, and by the leanings toward the many.
For they do not seek priests, but orators; nor stewards of souls, but keepers
of money; nor pure sacrificers, but strong patrons. I will make some defense
concerning them: thus we have trained them, we who become all things to all, I
do not know whether that we may save all, or destroy them.
25. What do you say? Do we persuade you with these words, and have we
conquered? Or is there need for us of even firmer words toward persuading?
Yes, by the Trinity itself, which we advocate and you advocate, by our common
hope, and by the knitting-together of this people, give me this grace; send us
away with prayers; let this be for me the proclamation of the contest; give me
the letter of release, as the emperors to the soldiers; and, if you wish, with
a right-hand testimony, that I may have the honor; and if not, as you wish; I
will differ in nothing about this, until =36.489= God sees our things however
they may be. Whom then shall we bring in instead? The Lord will see for
himself a shepherd for the protection, as he saw a sheep for a
whole-burnt-offering. This one thing only I seek further: let him be one of
those envied, not of those pitied; not of those who grant all things to all,
but of those who know also to strike against some things for the sake of the
better; for the one is most pleasant here, the other most profitable from
there. You then practice the sending-forth speeches for us; and I will give
you back the farewell.
26. Farewell, Anastasia, named for me of piety. For you raised up for us the
word still being despised; the place of the common victory, the new Shiloh, in
which we first pitched the tent, carried about forty years in the wilderness
and wandering. And you, this great and celebrated temple, the new inheritance,
having received from the Word to be now great, which being formerly Jebus we
have made Jerusalem; and you as many as next after this in beauties, having
taken each some other part of the city, like certain bonds and making the
neighboring your own; whom, with this weakness, not we, but the grace with us,
filled with those despaired of. Farewell, apostles, the fair settlement, my
teachers of the contest, even if I have not often kept festival with you,
perhaps carrying about in the body the Satan of your Paul toward what is
profitable, through whom I am now being settled away from you. Farewell to me,
O throne, this envied and dangerous height, assembly of high priests, honored
by reverence and time of priests, and as much other ministry of God around the
holy table, drawing near to God who draws near. Farewell, choirs of Nazirites,
harmonies of psalmodies, all-night stations, gravity of virgins, good order of
women, companies of widows, of orphans, eyes of the poor, looking toward God
and toward us. Farewell, houses loving strangers and =36.492= loving Christ,
and helpers of my weakness. Farewell, lovers of my words, and courses, and
concourses, and pens seen and hidden, and this lattice being forced by those
thrusting about the word. Farewell, O emperors, and palaces, and as much as is
around the emperor, attendant and household; whether also faithful to the
emperor, I do not know; but to God for the most part unfaithful. Clap hands,
shout sharply, lift your orator to a height. The wicked and talkative tongue
has been silent for you; yet it will not be silent altogether; for it will
fight through hand and ink; but for the present we have been silent.
