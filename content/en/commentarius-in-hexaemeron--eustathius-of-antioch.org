OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS EUSTATHIUS ARCHBISHOP OF ANTIOCH AND MARTYR, A
COMMENTARY ON THE HEXAEMERON
Clement then, and Africanus and besides these Tatian, and of those from the
circumcision Josephus, and Justus, have related that the divine Moses
flourished in the time of Inachus, each privately offering the proof from
ancient history. And Inachus precedes the Ilian war by seven hundred years.
And one of the saints, having made a most accurate [reckoning] of the times by
the times of Inachus, found the most holy Jacob flourishing together; but
Josephus [found] Moses. For having devised a composition of a chronological
canon in parallel, he set beside it the one who ruled among each nation. And
he gathered the times of the kingdom of the Chaldeans, Assyrians, Medes,
Persians, Lydians, Hebrews, Egyptians, Athenians, Argives, Sicyonians,
Lacedaemonians, Corinthians, Thessalians, Macedonians, Latins, those later
called Romans. And having ..... these times ..... of the kings of each nation
he brought together the times, setting beside them the years of the kingdom of
the Assyrian queen, that is of Semiramis, with the years of Abraham. For in
the time of Semiramis the blessed Abraham was known; but Josephus [places
Moses] in the time of Inachus; and Moses in the time of Cecrops, whom before
the Ilian [wars] by three hundred years ......... first of Attica the children
of the Greeks [made king], so that Moses is the most ancient of the ancient
wise men among the Greeks, he saying of Homer and Hesiod, and of the Trojan
[affairs], and of Heracles, and Musaeus, and Linus, and still of Chiron,
Orpheus, the Dioscuri, Asclepius, Dionysus, Hermes, Apollo, and of the Greek
mysteries, and rites, and of the very deeds of Zeus; and still of the flood in
the time of Deucalion, and of the conflagration in the time of Phaethon, and
of the birth of Erichthonius from Hephaestus and earth, and of the rape of
Kore.
For all these 709 existed after Cecrops, whom Homer and Hesiod have fallen far
below in times. And if they are lesser than these in years, they are much
lower still than the age of Moses. This man then was shown as teacher of all
the wise, most divine of the truth. For having spat out the so-called wisdom
of the Egyptians, he went through most excellently concerning this
constitution of the world, having been inspired with the truth clearly by the
God over all and maker of the whole. For he says: In the beginning God made
the heaven and the earth. And many of those among the Greeks who dared to go
through the opposite of these things were plunged into a depth of ignorance.
For the doctrines brought forth from human thought are clearly .... and
unballasted. For no word stands among them unmoved, the second always
overthrowing the one before it; so that there is no work for the faithful to
refute those [doctrines], those men sufficing for one another toward their own
overturning. And of what sort in figure and in substance the heaven is, Isaiah
teaches us saying: Who set the heaven as a vault; hinting that it is thin, and
not solid, nor thick, as he also says elsewhere: Who made the heaven firm as
smoke. And the psalm says the earth is established upon the waters, saying: He
founded it upon seas, and prepared it upon rivers. And how this is one must
not be over-curious, but believe. For the simplicity of faith is stronger than
rational proofs; for unattainable and incomprehensible


are the judgments of God.
On the first day then God made the heaven, and the earth, and the light; and
he lit up all things at a sharp turn of the moment; and this is the
perishable. For before this a dark [thing] held [them] in a shadow, the heaven
having been stretched around, and having made all things that it contained,
and the place left over, unlit. And outside the heaven there is light
unshadowed, and incorruptible, and eternal, in which angels dwell, concerning
which Solomon says: Light is for the righteous always, into which also those
who have lived well rest. And you will understand the economy of this from a
clear example, that an unquenchable light shines round the outer space of the
heaven. And a mist held the inner [space] before the light came to be. For
just as if someone at a settled noon, having fixed a tent of dense material,
makes an improvised darkness, and the contained place can in no way be lit
unless a gleam of light shine forth; so such an unsubstantial darkness held
the world covered by the heaven, which God separated by creating the light,
and naming it day, and the darkness night. For when the light is poured out,
day comes to be, and when it is drawn together, night comes in instead. Whence
also in the measuring of times we count days, and not nights.
712 And on the second day God made the firmament. And this is other than the
heaven made from the beginning. And it has been said by some that it is also
the same; for it is, they say, a working-out of the second, because there the
making of earth and heaven has been handed down as in a summary; but here
Scripture hands down more working-out how it came to be. But we say it is one,
and another; since both another name and a peculiar use of the second heaven
has been handed down. This then is other than the one made in the beginning,
being of a firmer nature. And God divided in the midst of the water which was
under the firmament. For being boundless, he partitioned the nature of the
waters, so that it is both above the firmament, and below. And the aether is
fiery and burning-hot, and would have consumed the whole mainland, if it were
not held by the bound of the one who made it. For this reason there is also
aerial water carried through clouds from the rising of the vapors, which
vapors the rivers, and the springs, and the lakes, and all the seas send
forth, so that the aether may not, having set all on fire, consume [them]. And
it comes to be thus: the ..... the exhalations are gathered about the height,
and the air is thickened by the compressions of the winds, and the moistures
sown in the cloud, having approached one another, become drops, and by the
weight of the things compacted they are let go toward what is below. And the
snow is composed from the waters thus: the moist, having been beaten back by
the force of the winds, is frothed out; and having been beaten back through
and through, having been chilled, it is frozen, and when the cloud is broken,
the snow is carried down. And the thunder according to this account .... comes
to be by resistance, which, being driven in the hollows of the clouds, and
bursting out violently, accomplishes the sound of thunder.
And on the third day by the command of God the water was gathered into their
gatherings, and the dry appeared; And he called the dry earth, and the systems
of the waters he called seas. It was gathered for this reason into one
gathering, so that it might not from the places where it is ........ and going
out cover the mainland. For this reason the sea, often raging from winds, and
rising to a very great height with the waves, whenever it only touches the
shores, having dissolved the onset into foam, has gone back, being bridled by
the sand, weakest of all things. For what hindered the Red Sea from flooding
all Egypt, which is hollower than it, and from being joined to the neighboring


sea, if it were not bound by the command of the Creator? And that this is true
Sesostris the Egyptian and Darius the Mede persuaded us by deed. For having
attempted to join the Egyptian and the Indian sea, they were not able. So much
is Egypt hollower than the Red Sea. He commanded then that it be gathered into
one gathering, and that from this another sea not come to be, but that it
remain in the first collection. For there were many ................... waters
............. hollows ............ 713 collection and the ravines. And the
[saying], and the systems of the waters he called seas, signifies the gulfs
left according to their own shape by the surrounding earth; such as northern
sea, southern sea, eastern, western seas, and of these peculiar names, Pontus,
Aegean, Ionian, Sardinian, Sicilian, Tyrrhenian. And it was not the chance
gatherings of waters that ended in one sea, or system, but the greatest and
outstanding; for one must not think also of the marshy and lacustrine. For
just as fire both is minced into small [bits] and is poured in a mass under
the air, so also in the case of water, even if there are some small divided
systems, yet there is one gathering, which separates the whole element from
the rest. For the water of the sea is the spring of all the moisture about the
earth, and all the earth is underground-channeled, and it is distributed
through unseen passages, as certain cavernous places of the mainland show. For
the flowing sea, running through narrow places, whenever it is left in winding
and steep exits, being pushed by the force of the wind, breaks the surface of
the earth, and having come forth to the outside, becomes drinkable, changed
into sweetness from the filtering. And again whenever it is left in hot places
toward the exit, being drawn by the same cause by the wind it becomes boiling.
This has also been established as the spring of the aerial waters; for its
waters, being drawn up through clouds, and being warmed by the solar ray, and
having been chilled by the shade of the cloud, and having been cleared from
the very height, are sweetened, and sprinkle the mainland.
And on the same day God also commanded the earth to sprout herb of grass;
Sowing seed according to kind, and fruit-bearing wood making fruit, whose seed
is in it. And still even now the order of the things that grow witnesses to
the first arrangement. For the sprouting leads every herb, and every grass;
for the earth having moistures in measure and warmth, the seed is thrown down
into it, and it becomes loose and many-seeded, and having begun from the earth
about it it draws to itself the things proper and akin, and being fattened it
is broadened more; so as to be rooted toward what is below, and to come forth
toward what is above, and to put forth stalks equal in number to the roots.
And when the sprout is always being warmed, the moisture being drawn through
the roots, by the warmth the nourishing [part] of the earth is brought along;
and this is partitioned into stalk, and bark, and the cases of the wheat, and
into the wheat itself, and the awns. And the stalk of the wheat is girt with
joints, so that as certain bonds they may bear the weight of the ears. And the
stalk is also hollow, so that it may become a case of the grain, toward its
not being easy to snatch for the seed-pickers. And it has put forth the awns
instead of points, so that they may keep off the harms of the small animals.
And the oat has been established empty throughout, since it is not weighted in
the head. And if 716 the harmful things sprouted together with the nourishing,
such as hellebore, and aconite, and mandrake, and the juice of the poppy, none
of these has been taken as idle for use. The starlings feed on hemlock because
of the construction of the body; for having thin passages toward the heart,
they are beforehand in sending away what has been swallowed; and quails eat
the hellebore, and they do not undergo harm, having a peculiarity


of temperament. And through the mandrake physicians bring on sleep, and with
opium they put the violent pains to sleep. And with hemlock they waste the
raging appetites, and with hellebore they soothe the chronic sufferings. And
fruit-bearing wood making fruit. And all things in one turn of the moment
sprang up from the earth, not being before, each with its own peculiarity, and
each being known by its own character. The olive, and the date-palm, and the
cypress grow to a height. And of these some are not many-branched, nor
many-twigged, such as fir, and the cypress, and sometimes also the date-palm.
And evergreen are olive, date-palm, and laurel, myrtle, pine, cypress, fir,
juniper, thyia, and what the Arcadians call philodrys; still philyea, cedar,
pine, box, holm-oak, arake, lathyrus. And the rose then sprouted without the
thorn; but later the thorn was yoked beside the beauty of the flower; so that
having the pain laid beside the pleasant we may remember the sin, through
which the earth was condemned to send up thorns and thistles for us. And the
almond first sprouts, but later brings forth the fruit. And the almond that
happens to be bitter, having its stem toward the root bored through, and
having received a fat wedge of pine driven in the middle of the pith, changes
the unpleasant juices into edible sweetness. And the sour pomegranate, having
received the same treatment, is turned into sweetness. And the female
date-palm lets down the branches as if desiring the intertwining of the male;
and the farmers, putting the gall-insects of the male into its branches, set
it into the proper shape of uprightness. And the mulberry sprouts late, but
does not come behind the others into fruit-bearing. And the olive, and the
linden, and the white poplar, and elm turn the leaves supine after the summer
tropic; and in this all recognize that the summer tropic has come to be. And
the field-workers keep the fruit of the cultivated fig from flowing off thus:
having stripped the wild figs of the wild fig, they prepare [the cultivated]
to remain unfalling. For the very small animal that is inside the wild fig,
sitting at the base of the cultivated fig, keeps off the breeze blowing round
from outside, toward the fig not becoming unpleasant and falling off. And
since the fruit of the fig is tender, it has been covered with thickness of
leaves; but of the walnut, since it happens to be hard, it is sheltered by a
light application of leaves. For this reason also the leaf of the vine has
been split, so that the cluster may both turn aside the harms from the air,
and receive the solar ray by the thinness. Thus nothing is idle, but God
arranged all things by unspeakable 717 wisdom. For one water, being carried or
drawn through the root, nourishes the stem in one way, the pith in another,
the bark in another, the wood in another, the root itself in another; and from
the same moisture wine is composed in the vine, oil in the olive, and in
another another. And even in the sweet fruits themselves the variation of the
richness is unspeakable; for other is the sweet in the vine, and other in the
apple-tree, and in the fig and in the date-palm another. And the tears of the
trees themselves are different; for other is the tear of the mastic, and other
the juice of the balsam, which is the finest of all myrrhs, and it abounds
when it comes to be at Jericho. And when the people of the place cut it from
the bushes with a sharp stone, juice would come up. And some relate that amber
also is a juice of plants, freezing over into the nature of a stone.
4. And on the fourth day God created the two great lights, sun and moon in the
firmament of the heaven, for shining upon the earth, to divide between the day
and between the night. This solar light has been prepared as a vehicle for
that first-born light. For as fire is one thing, and the lamp another; for the
one has the power of lighting, and the other that of showing forth to those
who


have need; and as whiteness is something other, and the whitened body other,
so also these things, being different in nature, have been united by the power
of the Creator. And let no one think that day and sun are the same. For first
the maker prepared simple light, which he also called day, then the sun
composed of fire and light. For as we see the earth having fire created in its
very breasts, so also the sun having light; for light having come together
with fire made the brightest sun. But perhaps someone will object: And from
where is this clear, the division not being seen? for the light is not seen
privately, and the fire privately. But let such a one know how many things
fire puts forth in itself, gleam, heat, yellowness, which the all-wise
craftsman, distributing, hints at the composition of these. For when he wishes
to warm the animal, he takes the hot of the fire, and it has the hot of this,
but it does not have the light. But the warmth was divided in the animal from
the brightness, again he takes the yellow. And we see the yellow, but no
longer the hot and shining. And again he partitions off the warmth, and gives
the gleam alone, such as it is possible to see in the case of lightning, that
shining forth it only lights, but does not also warm. And the same example has
also been established in the case of the moon. For this too, being lit by the
sun, becomes more apparent, just as the sun by the day. For the form of the
moon happens to be coal-like, as it is possible to see in the eclipse; for the
sun, having taken away its own brightness from it, makes it dim; but lighting
it, he works it full-shining. For putting on as it were the solar light it is
made more bright; in the same way also the sun, putting on the light of the
day, happens to be more brilliant, that day neither 720 being, nor again
warming, but only lighting. And that the sun has been composed from fire is
clear from its firing, and burning.
To divide between the day and between the night. That is, he appointed the sun
to the measures of the day, and the moon to those of the night. And the night
is a shadow of the earth, the sun being hidden. For as in the day, when
something stands in the way, a shadow comes to be, and the light is present
beside; so the night is composed, the air about the earth being shadowed. And
the sun has this light which it received from the beginning from the maker
always hidden in itself; but the moon is not so, but continuously puts it off,
increasing, and being lessened. And let them be for signs, and for seasons,
and for days, and for years. And sun and moon generate this time. For the
courses being drawn in measure generate the appointed exits of the seasons.
And it is called time, since as upon a certain circle it is rolled with
successive well-ordered successions, first toward second, and third toward the
next. And the [saying], let them be for signs, since they always pre-signify
the presence of winter; for a mist, having come to be in the air, dims the
rays of the sun, and it appears coal-like and bloodshot, not being able to
scatter the air thickened from the vapors of the earth; and from this a sign
of winter is shown. And whenever the so-called halos (and they are certain
round outlines like a threshing-floor, for from this they are so named)
encircle the sun, violence of the winds is signified; and whenever also the
so-called anthelia run together with the course of the sun, it becomes a sign
of aerial accidents; and again when things like rods according to the color of
the Rainbow appearing in the clouds toward dawn are shown, rains, or winters,
or a change of the air for a longer time is signified. And the moon, whenever
on the third day it is shown thin and clean in the horns, promises a settled
fair weather; but appearing thick in the horns, and reddish, it announces
violent water or a violent south wind.


Sun and moon then are established as instruments of times; and time of days,
months, and years; and the stars, of months, and of sowings, and of harvests.
And the Pleiades, rising, show the time of harvest, and setting, of sowing.
And for those who sail they become a sign together with the Wain; for the
stars become signs and pointers of places. For the Bear shows the Bear; and
looking at the sharp [point] of the Pleiad toward the east it shows the Bear;
and of the Bear itself as it turns the horn shows the east, and the shining
part of the moon signifies the east. And before these there was not time, but
an interval, and a certain long and timeless age. It has been said then
concerning the signs. And we think that seasons means the changes of the 721
hours, of winter, spring, summer, autumn. And winter comes to be when the sun
spends time in the southern parts, and accomplishes much of the nightly shadow
about the place according to us, so that the air about the earth is chilled,
and the moist exhalations being composed become causes of rains, and frosts,
and snowfalls; and whenever coming back again from the midday regions it comes
to be upon the middle, equinox comes to be, and by how much it spends time in
the places upon the earth, by so much it brings back the good temperament part
by part, and spring comes to be, providing sprouting to the plants, and
revival to the trees. And from there already running toward the summer tropics
upon the Bear itself the sun presents the great days to us, and because it
converses more with the air, it makes the air itself shiver, and dries the
whole earth, and works every fruit in season being most flame-like, and it
makes the shadows short lighting from on high. For in the shortest days the
shadows become longest. And again when it has come from the northern to the
southern, autumn comes to be, the much warmth being dimmed little by little,
and winter coming on without harm. And the sun does not make the day; let no
one suppose thus; for the day and night are older than this; but it only
begins the day, as also the psalmist declares to us; He set, saying, the sun
for authority of the day, the moon, and the stars for authority of the night.
And this has the authority carrying the light about in itself; whenever it
raises above the horizon according to us, it provides day having scattered the
darkness. And the moon, whenever it has completed its own course twelve times,
happens to be productive of a year, except that it needs an intercalary month
toward the accurate concurrence of the hours. For the solar year is a
restoration from the same sign to the same sign according to the proper motion
of the sun. And when the moon is waning, the bodies of animals are rarefied,
and become somewhat empty; and when it is increasing, they are filled up.
Because it sends in unnoticed a moisture mixed with heat; for those who sleep
under the moon are filled in the heads with excess moisture, and the newly
slaughtered of the meats, and every such thing, is quickly turned by the
application of the moon. And likewise also the affections about the air are
composed of such a cause. For when new moon has come on, there being a settled
calm, disturbances of sudden winds come to be, the clouds being shaken and
falling together with one another; and there come to be reverse currents about
the straits, and about the ocean a running-up of the sea. And if also an
eclipse of the sun comes to be when there is new moon, a more violent one
happens; for the moon having come under from below blocks the rays of the sun.
And this moon too has been established as something great. And silly myths
have been preserved everywhere by drunken old women; that by certain magics it
is moved from its own seat, and is carried down toward the earth. And what
place would have received it when it was brought down? Do not 724 let the
appearance deceive as being something small


being thought. For our sight becoming small has made the things seen also be
thought small, bringing its own affection upon the visibles. For just as if
someone on a ridge should behold plains and oxen grazing upon the same, he
does not seem to see a phantasm as of ants; for the sight having been spent in
the air, and having become faint, does not suffice toward the accurate
understanding of the things seen. And that the body of the moon is very great,
it is possible to learn from proofs. For the cities throughout the inhabited
world, settled very far from one another, those turned with street-plans
toward the east, all equally receive the lunar light, and likewise those who
dwell in the west and the midday and northern parts behold it.
And on the fifth day God commanded the waters to bring forth creeping things
of living souls according to kind; and immediately the sea brought forth all
sorts of different kinds of animals. For of the marine some are amphibious,
such as seals, and crocodiles, and the river horses, and still the frogs, and
the crabs, yet they do not have the swimming [faculty]; for they are creeping
by nature, being dragged by the leading of the water; and some are
live-bearing, such as seals, and dolphins, and torpedoes, and the things like
them. And God only commanded, and it sent forth the pelagic, and the
shore-dwelling, the deep, the rocky, the gregarious, the scattered, the
whales, the oversized, the thinnest of the fishes, having a natural kinship
toward the water. For if they are separated a little from the water, not
having breath, they are destroyed; for the spreading of the gills for them,
and the folding-over, receiving the water, and sending it through, has
acquired this breath. Nothing among them has the teeth by half, but all have
been packed with sharp teeth, toward the food not flowing away by the
lingering chewing. For it was going to be destroyed in the fine chewing by the
water. And nothing else among them chews the cud except the parrot-wrasse. And
the food also is other, suitable to others. For some are fed on mud, and some
on seaweeds, others on the herbs fed by the water; and others are eaters of
one another. And there are also different peculiarities of them. The sawfish
has long fins; and having seen a ship running with a fair wind, it also,
having raised its own fins, imitates the shapes of the motion of the ship, and
contends with those who sail. Having then made forty stades, and having grown
weary, it lets down its fins, and is carried by the waves into its own place.
And the whale is called Aspidochelone among some; and it is by nature most
huge, so as often to produce for those who sail a phantasm of a great island.
For when it has come up from the deep, its body appears as rough stones, and
they, fixing stakes about it, bind the boats, which are immediately sunk, the
whale having been thoroughly warmed by the sun, and having gone down 725 into
the deep; and it has also by nature a heavy voice, and when hungry it opens
the mouth, and sends out a most fragrant exhalation, so that the small fishes
are drawn, and are piled whole into its mouth. And this dwells in the Atlantic
seas because of the size. And to the crab the flesh of the oyster is edible,
and not being strong enough to open the folding of its shells, it watches it
having opened its folds in the windless places, and delighting in the gleam of
the sun, and having taken a pebble it puts it in secretly, and prevents the
folding, and thus easily eats it down. And the sea-urchin foreknows the onset
of winter, and goes under a pebble toward its not being carried about by the
course of the waves. And the octopus, having approached some rock, has become
of the same color with it, and eats down the fishes that come near the rock.
And the remora happens to be very small by nature, and it stops the greatest
of ships being carried from a fair wind, so that the ship seems to have been
fixed in the sea.


And there is a hydris in the river, having the form of a dog, which happens to
be an enemy of the crocodile, and it kills this one sleeping. For the
crocodile sleeps having opened its own mouth; and the hydris, having rolled
itself in mud [φ. ἐγκυλίσασα], leaps into its mouth, and eating down all the
things inside it kills it. And the crocodile is a beast together and a fish,
and great from head as far as tail; yet it does not have the breadth analogous
to the size. And its hide happens to be a wrinkled scale, and the color of its
back is like a black rock, and the belly like a white. And its feet are four,
gently bent toward the side; as of the land tortoise, a tail long and thick;
for it is the end of the spine of one bone. And it has been cut above into
spines, and they are as the edges of saws, and it strikes with it against
those with whom it may wrestle. And it has one head woven together with the
back, and directed into one straight line; and so long as it has not gaped, it
is the head of a beast; but if it gapes, it becomes all mouth, making the gape
as far as the shoulders. And the so-called hippopotamus has the feet of a
horse and the belly, and the hoof split as a cloven hoof, a tail thick and
bare of hairs, because the whole of the body also has thus, a round head a
little different from the cheek of the horse, and it has the nostril gaping,
breathing a fiery smoke, as from a furnace, a broad jaw; for it opens the
mouth as far as the temples. And it has been packed inside with teeth in a
triple way, having besides these two bent teeth as of a swine. And it is most
gluttonous as a whole, making a crop its food. And it is hunted by such a
deceit. They watch its haunts, and they make a pit, and they roof it with
reed, and they heap sand upon it, and they set a wooden house below having the
doors opened into the roof of the 728 pit; and the beast having stepped on it
falls in simply, and is shut into the house being carried down. And the
hunters immediately shut [it] off. Second. .... and the beast has the size of
an ox. And in the fishes there is much heat inside, and sharp; wherefore also
they digest more quickly the things they eat, the edibles immediately rotting
through in their belly. And the sea fox, whenever it cannot run through the
catch, having swallowed the hook, leaping out and running up, gnaws off the
line, and escapes. And the crocodile is a man-eating beast, but when it is
about to make the matings, having swum up it turns the female upon the banks;
for this one alone is by nature to approach supine, and after the intercourse
again lifting her with the hands it turns her together; and whenever it
perceives her having been with another, it pretends to mix with her, and
having turned her in the same way it knows through the smell (for it has a
most sharp sense concerning this) that she has been defiled by another, and
having torn her belly with the claws, it eats down all the things inside her.
For about the belly the animal is tender; for its back has been clothed in
stout scales, so that even the sharpest iron brought against it is blunted.
Let the waters bring forth winged things of living souls, and all at once the
air has been filled with every kind of the birds. And he gave them the
