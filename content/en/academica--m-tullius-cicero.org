** Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2
:END:

When I was recently at my place at Cumae, and our friend Atticus was with me,
word was brought to us from M. Varro that he had arrived from Rome the evening
before and that, if he had not been tired from the journey, he would have come
to us at once. When we heard this, we thought no delay should be put in the
way of seeing a man joined to us both by the same studies and by long-standing
friendship; so we set out at once to go to him. And when we were a little way
from his villa, we saw him coming toward us; and having embraced him, as is
the way of friends (for after a sufficiently long interval * * * ), we led him
back to his villa.

** Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3
:END:

Here first a few things, and those while we asked whether there was perhaps
anything new from Rome.

Then Atticus said, "Leave aside those things which we can neither ask about
nor hear without distress, I beg you, and ask rather whether he himself has
anything new. For Varro's Muses have been silent longer than they used to, and
yet I think he is not idle but is hiding what he writes."

"Not at all," he said; "for I think it is the mark of an intemperate man to
write what he would wish to be concealed; but I have a great work in hand, and
have had it for a long time; and for this very man" (he meant me) "I have
begun certain things, which are indeed of some size and are being polished by
me more carefully."

** Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4
:END:

And I said, "Those things, Varro, though I have long been waiting for them, I
still do not dare to demand;

for I have heard from our friend Libo, whose eagerness you know (for we can
hide nothing of that kind from him), that you are not interrupting them but
treating them more carefully and never putting them out of your hands. But
this other thing never entered my mind to ask of you before this time. But
now, after I have begun to commit to writing those subjects which I learned
together with you and to set forth in Latin letters that old philosophy which
arose from Socrates, I ask why it is that, though you write so much, you pass
over this kind, especially since you yourself excel in it and that study and
that whole matter far surpass the rest of studies and arts."

** Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5
:END:

Then he:

"You ask a matter I have often deliberated and much agitated. So I will answer
without hesitation, but I will say what is ready to me, because, as I have
said, I have thought much and long on this very point. For when I saw
philosophy most carefully set forth in Greek letters, I judged that if any of
our people were held by zeal for it, if they were trained in Greek learning,
they would rather read Greek than ours; but if they shrank from the arts and
disciplines of the Greeks, they would not even care for these things, which
cannot be understood without Greek learning. So I did not want to write things
which the unlearned could not understand and the learned would not care to
read.

** Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6
:END:

You see the same yourself; for you have learned that we cannot be like
Amafinius or Rabirius, who with no art applied discuss things set before our
eyes in common speech, define nothing, divide nothing, conclude nothing by apt
questioning, and in short think there is no art either of speaking or of
arguing; but we, obeying the precepts of the dialecticians and of the orators
too, since our people think each faculty is a virtue, as if they were laws,
are forced to use new words as well, which the learned, as I said, will prefer
to seek from the Greeks, and the unlearned will not accept even from us,

so that all the labor is undertaken in vain.

** Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7
:END:

Now as for physics, if I approved Epicurus, that is, if I approved Democritus,
I could write as plainly as Amafinius. For what is the great task, when you
have taken away the efficient causes of things, to speak of the chance
collision of little bodies (for so he names atoms)? You know our physics;
since they are held together from an efficient force and from that matter
which the efficient force shapes and forms, geometry too must be applied; and
in what words will anyone be able to set that forth, or whom will he be able
to bring to understand it? These very matters of life and character and of
things to be sought and avoided they treat simply, for they think the good of
a beast and of a man is the same; but among our people you are not unaware
what subtlety there is, and how great.

** Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8
:END:

For if you follow Zeno, it is a great thing to make anyone understand what
that true and simple good is which cannot be separated from honor (and
Epicurus denies that he can even suspect what kind of good that is, without
pleasures that move the senses); but if we pursue the Old Academy, which, as
you know, we approve, how keenly that will have to be explained by us, how
shrewdly, how obscurely even we must argue against the Stoics. The whole of
that study of philosophy, then, I take upon myself, both for constancy of
life, as far as I can, and for the delight of the mind, and I think, as it is
in Plato, that no greater or better gift has been given by the gods to man;

** Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9
:END:

but I send my friends in whom there is zeal to Greece, that is, I bid them go
to the Greeks, that they may drink from the fountains rather than chase after
rivulets. But those things which no one had yet taught, and from which
students could not know them, I have, as far as I could (for I do not greatly
admire my own work), made known to our people; for they could not be sought
from the Greeks, and after the death of our L. Aelius not even from Latin
writers. And yet in those old works of ours, which, imitating Menippus, not
translating him, we sprinkled with a certain cheerfulness, much was mixed in
from the heart of philosophy, much said in the dialectical manner, so that the
less learned might understand more easily, invited to reading by a certain
pleasantness; in panegyrics, in these very prefaces of the Antiquities, we
wished to write in the manner of philosophy, if only we have succeeded."

** Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h10
:END:

Then I said, "Those things are so, Varro.

For we, wandering and straying in our own city like foreigners, your books
have as it were led home, so that we could at last recognize who and where we
were. You have opened the age of the fatherland, you the descriptions of
times, you the laws of rites, you of priests, you domestic and military
discipline, you the names, kinds, offices, causes of sites, regions, places,
of all things divine and human; you have brought most light both to our poets
and to Latin letters and words altogether, and you yourself have made a varied
and elegant poem in almost every meter, and you have begun philosophy in many
places, enough to urge,

too little to teach.

** Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h11
:END:

You do indeed bring a probable reason: either those who are learned will
prefer to read Greek, or those who do not know those things will not even read
these. But you do not quite convince me of it; on the contrary, both those who
will not be able to read those will read these, and those who will be able to
read Greek will not despise their own. For what reason is there why men
trained in Greek letters should read Latin poets and not read philosophers? Or
is it because Ennius, Pacuvius, Accius, and many others delight, who have
expressed not the words but the force of the Greek poets? How much more will
philosophers delight, if as those imitated Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, so
these imitate Plato, Aristotle, Theophrastus. Orators at least I see praised
if any of our people have imitated Hyperides or Demosthenes.

** Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h12
:END:

But I, Varro (for I will speak as the matter is), while ambition, while
honors, while cases, while not only care of the republic but a certain
management of it as well, held me entangled and bound by many duties, I kept
these things shut in my mind and, so that they should not grow stale, I
renewed them when I could by reading; but now, both struck by the heaviest
wound of fortune and freed from the administration of the republic, I seek
from philosophy a medicine for grief and I judge this the most honorable
delight of leisure. For either this is most fitted to this time of life, or
this is especially consistent with whatever we have done worthy of praise, or
nothing is more useful even for educating our citizens, or if these things are
not so, I see nothing else that we can do.

** Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h13
:END:

Our Brutus, indeed, outstanding in every kind of praise, so pursues philosophy
in Latin letters that you would not miss Greek on the same subjects; and he
follows the same view as you, for he heard Aristus at Athens for some time,
whose brother Antiochus you heard. Therefore, I beg you, give yourself also to
this kind of letters."

** Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h14
:END:

Then he:

"That I will consider, and not without you. But about you yourself, what is
it," he said, "that I hear?"

"About what matter?" I said.

"That the Old Academy has been left by you," he said, "and that the New is
being treated."

"Why then," I said, "should it have been more allowed to our friend Antiochus
to migrate into the old house from the new, than to us into the new from the
old? For surely the most recent things are the most corrected and amended. And
yet Antiochus's teacher Philo, a great man as you yourself judge, denied in
his books, as we also used to hear from him in person, that there are two
Academies, and he refuted the error of those who thought so."

"It is as you say," he said; "but I do not think you are unaware of what
Antiochus has written against Philo's views."

** Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h15
:END:

"On the contrary, both those things and the whole Old Academy, from which I
have been away so long, I would like to be renewed by you, if it is not
troublesome," and at the same time I said, "let us sit down, if it seems
good."

"That indeed," he said, "by all means, for I am rather weak. But let us see
whether it pleases Atticus that I do the same thing I see you want."

"For my part," he said; "for what would I rather than recall what I heard long
ago from Antiochus and at the same time see whether those things can be said
well enough in Latin?"

When this had been said, we all sat down in view of one another.

** Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h16
:END:

Then Varro began thus:

"Socrates, it seems to me, as is agreed among all, first called philosophy
away from hidden things and from those wrapped in nature itself, in which all
philosophers before him had been occupied, and brought it to common life, so
that he inquired about virtues and vices and altogether about good things and
bad, and thought heavenly things either far from our knowledge or, even if
they were known as fully as possible, still of no use for living well.

** Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h17
:END:

He, in almost all the conversations which have been written out variously and
copiously by those who heard him, argues in such a way that he himself affirms
nothing, refutes others, says he knows nothing except this very thing, and
that he is superior to the rest because they think they know what they do not
know, while he himself knows this one thing, that he knows nothing, and for
that reason he thinks he was called by Apollo the wisest of all, because this
was the one wisdom of man, not to think he knows what he does not know. And
since he said this constantly and remained in that view, all his speech was
spent only in praising virtue and in exhorting men to the pursuit of virtue,
as can be understood from the books of the Socratics and especially of Plato.

** Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h18
:END:

But from the authority of Plato, who was varied and many-sided and copious,
one and agreeing form of philosophy was established under two names, of the
Academics and of the Peripatetics, who, agreeing in things, differed in names.
For when Plato had left Speusippus, his sister's son, as heir, as it were, of
his philosophy, and two men of outstanding zeal and learning, Xenocrates of
Chalcedon and Aristotle of Stagira, those who were with Aristotle were called
Peripatetics, because they argued while walking in the Lyceum, but the others,
because by Plato's institution they were accustomed to have gatherings and
conversations in the Academy, which is the other gymnasium, took their name
from the word for the place. But both, filled with Plato's richness, composed
a certain definite formula of teaching, and that indeed full and packed, and
they left that Socratic custom of arguing doubtfully

about all things and with no affirmation applied. So there came to be, what
Socrates least approved, a certain art of philosophy and an order of things
and a description of a system.

** Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h19
:END:

And this was at first, as I said, one under two names; for there was no
difference between the Peripatetics and that Old Academy. Aristotle excelled,
as it seems to me at least, by a certain abundance of talent, but the source
was the same for both, and the same division of things to be sought and
avoided.

But what am I doing," he said, "or am I in my right mind, teaching you these
things? For even if it is not 'a sow teaching Minerva,' as they say, still
whoever teaches Minerva does it ineptly."

Then Atticus said, "Do go on, Varro; for I greatly love our things and our
people, and those things delight me when they are said in Latin and in that
manner of yours."

"What do you think of me," I said, "who have now professed that I will present
philosophy to our people."

** Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h20
:END:

"Let us go on then," he said, "since it pleases.

The method of philosophizing received from Plato, then, was already threefold:
one about life and character, another about nature and hidden things, a third
about arguing and judging what is true, what false, what right in speech or
crooked, what consistent, what contradictory.

And first they sought that part of living well from nature and said that
nature must be obeyed, and that in no other thing than in nature was that
highest good to be sought to which all things should be referred, and they
established that the last of things to be sought and the end of goods was to
have obtained all things from nature, both in mind and in body and in life. Of
the body they placed some things in the whole, others in the parts: health,
strength, beauty in the whole; in the parts, however, senses unimpaired and
some excellence of the several parts, as speed in the feet, force in the
hands, clearness in the voice, and in the tongue also a distinct impression of
sounds;

** Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h21
:END:

of the mind, those things which were fitted to grasp virtue by native gifts,
and these were divided by them into nature and character. To nature they gave
quickness at learning and memory, both of which were proper to the mind and to
talent; but of character they thought there were pursuits and as it were
habit, which they formed partly by constant exercise, partly by reason, and
among these was philosophy itself; in which what is begun and not completed is
called a certain progress toward virtue, but what is completed is virtue, as
it were the perfection of nature and, of all the things they place in minds,
the one best thing.

** Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h22
:END:

These, then, of minds; but of life (for that was the third) they said those
things were attached which were of use for the practice of virtue. Now virtue
is seen in the goods of the mind and of the body and in certain things which
are attached not so much to nature as to the happy life. For they thought a
man was as it were a certain part of the state and of the whole human race,
and that he was joined with men by a certain human society. And of the highest
and natural good they treat thus; but the rest they think belong to increasing
or holding it,

as wealth, as power, as glory, as favor. So a threefold account of goods is
introduced by them,

** Section 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h23
:END:

and these are those three kinds which most people think the Peripatetics speak
of. That indeed is not false; for this is their division; but it is unwise if
they think that those who were then called Academics were other than the
Peripatetics. This account is common, and to both this seemed the end of
goods: to obtain the things that are first in nature and that are to be sought
for themselves, either all or the greatest; and the greatest are those that
turn upon the mind itself and upon virtue itself. So all that ancient
philosophy held that the happy life is placed in virtue alone, and yet not the
happiest unless there were also joined the things of the body and the rest
that were said above as fitted for the use of virtue.

** Section 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h24
:END:

From this description there was found also a beginning of doing something in
life and of duty itself, which consisted in the preservation of oneself and in
the appetite for those things which nature prescribed. From this arose flight
from idleness and contempt of pleasures, from which the undertaking of many
great labors and pains for the sake of the right and honorable and of those
things which were consistent with the prescription of nature; whence both
friendship arose and justice and equity, and these were preferred to pleasures
and to many conveniences of life. This, then, was among them the institution
of character and the form and description of that part which I placed first.

** Section 24
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h25
:END:

About nature (for that followed) they spoke so as to divide it into two
things, so that one was efficient, the other as it were offering itself to
this, and from it something was produced. In that which produced they thought
there was force, in that which was produced only a certain matter; yet in each
both: for matter itself could not hold together if it were contained by no
force, nor force without some matter; for there is nothing that is not
compelled to be somewhere. But what comes from both they already named body
and as it were a certain quality; for you will surely grant that in unusual
matters, as the Greeks themselves do, from whom these things have long been
treated, we may sometimes use unheard-of words."

** Section 25
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h26
:END:

"We indeed," said Atticus; "why, you may even use Greek when you like, if
Latin perhaps fails you."

"You do well indeed; but I will strive to speak Latin, except in words of this
kind, so that I may call philosophy or rhetoric or physics or dialectic, which
custom already uses as Latin, as it does many others. I have therefore named
qualities what the Greeks call poiotetas, which word itself among the Greeks
is not a word of the crowd but of philosophers, and that in many cases; but of
the dialecticians there are no public words, they use their own. And that
indeed is common to almost all arts; for either new names must be made for new
things or they must be transferred from others. And if the Greeks do this, who
have been engaged in these matters for so many ages already, how much more
must it be granted to us, who are now first attempting to treat them."

** Section 26
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h27
:END:

"You indeed," I said, "Varro, seem to me likely to deserve well of your
citizens, if you increase them not only with abundance of things, as you have
done, but also of words."

"We will dare then," he said, "to use new words with you as authority, if it
is necessary. Of these qualities, then, some are primary, others arisen from
these. The primary are of one kind and simple; those arisen from these are
various and as it were many-shaped. So air (for we use this too as Latin) and
fire and water and earth are first; but from these have arisen the forms of
living things and of those things which are born from the earth. Therefore
those are called beginnings and, to translate from the Greek, elements; of
which air and fire have the force of moving and producing, the remaining parts
of receiving and as it were of suffering, I mean water and earth. A fifth
kind, from which the stars and minds would be, Aristotle thought was something
unique and unlike those four I named above.

** Section 27
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h28
:END:

But they think there is subjected to all things, without any form and lacking
all that quality (for let us by treating it make this word more usual and more
worn), a certain matter, from which all things have been shaped and produced,
which as a whole can receive all things and be changed in all ways and in
every part and therefore even perish, not into nothing but into its parts,
which can be cut and divided infinitely, since there is nothing at all in the
nature of things so small that it cannot be divided. And all things that are
moved are moved through intervals, which intervals likewise can be divided
infinitely.

** Section 28
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h29
:END:

And when that force which we have said is quality is so moved, and when it is
so turned this way and that, they think that matter itself is wholly changed
through and through and that those things are produced which they call
qualified things; from which, in all nature cohering and continued with all
its parts, one world has been produced, outside of which there is no part of
matter and no body. And they think that all things which are in it are parts
of the world, which are held by a sentient nature, in which there is perfect
reason, which is the same everlasting (for there is nothing stronger by which
it might perish);

** Section 29
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h30
:END:

and this force they say is the soul of the world, and that it is the same as
perfect mind and wisdom, which they call god, and as it were a certain
prudence of all things that are subject to it, looking after heavenly things
most of all, then on earth those that belong to men; which they sometimes call
the same necessity, because nothing can be otherwise than as it has been
established by it, sometimes

* * * as it were
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

a fated and unchangeable continuation of everlasting order, and sometimes the
same fortune, because it produces many things unforeseen and unexpected for us
on account of the obscurity and ignorance of causes.

** Section 30
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

The third part of philosophy, then, which consisted in reason and in arguing,
was treated thus by both. Although it arose from the senses, yet the judgment
of truth was not in the senses. They wished the mind to be the judge of
things, and thought it alone fit to be trusted, because it alone discerned
that which always was simple and of one kind and such as it was (this they
called idea, already so named by Plato; we can rightly say form).

** Section 31
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h2
:END:

But they thought all the senses were dull and slow and in no way perceived
those things which seemed subject to the senses,

because they were either so small that they could not fall under sense, or so
mobile and agitated that nothing was ever one and constant, not even the same,
because all things were continually slipping and flowing. So they called this
whole part of things the opinable;

** Section 32
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h3
:END:

but they thought knowledge was nowhere except in the notions and reasonings of
the mind. For this reason they approved definitions of things and applied
these to all that was being discussed; explanation of words was also approved,
that is, why each thing was so named, which they called etymologia; afterward
they used certain arguments and as it were marks of things as guides to prove
and to conclude what they wished to explain. In this the whole discipline of
dialectic was handed down, that is, of speech concluded by reason; to this, as
from the other side, was applied the oratorical force of speaking, the
explainer of continuous speech fitted to persuading.

** Section 33
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h4
:END:

This was their first form, handed down from Plato; whose dispersions, as I
have received them, I will set forth if you wish."

"We indeed wish it," I said, "that I may answer for Atticus too."

"And you answer rightly," he said; "for the authority of the Peripatetics and
of the Old Academy is being excellently explained."

"Aristotle, then, first undermined the forms I spoke of a little earlier,
which Plato had wonderfully embraced, so as to say that there was something
divine in them. Theophrastus, however, a man both sweet in speech and so
disposed in character that he carries before him a certain honesty and candor,
even more violently in a way broke the authority of the old system; for he
stripped virtue of its dignity and made it weak, because he denied that living
happily was placed in it alone.

** Section 34
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h5
:END:

For Strato, his hearer, though he was of keen talent, must nevertheless be
wholly set aside from that school; who, having left the most necessary part of
philosophy, which is placed in virtue and in character, and having given
himself wholly to the investigation of nature, in that very thing differed
most from his own people. But Speusippus and Xenocrates, who first took up
Plato's method and authority, and after them Polemo and Crates, and with them
Crantor, gathered in the Academy, diligently guarded what they had received
from their predecessors. Already Zeno and Arcesilas had heard Polemo
constantly.

** Section 35
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h6
:END:

But Zeno, since he was older than Arcesilas and argued very subtly and was
moved very keenly, tried to correct the system. That correction too, if it
seems good, I will explain, as Antiochus used to."

"It seems good to me," I said, "and you see Pomponius signifying the same."

"Zeno, then, was by no means one who, like Theophrastus, would cut the sinews
of virtue, but on the contrary one who placed all things that belonged to the
happy life in virtue alone and counted nothing else among goods, and called
that honorable which was a certain simple and sole and one good.

** Section 36
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h7
:END:

But the rest, though they were neither goods nor evils, he said some were
according to nature, others contrary to nature; and between these themselves
he counted others interposed and intermediate. Those that were according to
nature he taught were to be taken and to be thought worthy of a certain value,
and the contrary the contrary; but he left neither in the intermediates, in
which he placed nothing of any weight at all.

** Section 37
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h8
:END:

But of those that were to be taken, some were to be valued more, others less.
Those of more value he called preferred, and those of less, rejected. And as
he had changed these things not so much in matters as in words,

so between right action and sin he placed certain intermediates, duty and
contrary to duty, placing right actions alone among good actions, and wrong,
that is sins, among bad; but duties observed and omitted he thought
intermediates, as I have said.

** Section 38
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h9
:END:

And whereas the earlier thinkers said that not all virtue was in reason but
that certain virtues were completed as it were by nature or by habit, he
placed all in reason. And whereas they thought those kinds of virtues I named
above could be separated, he argued that this could in no way be done, and
that not only the use of virtue, as the earlier thinkers said, but the habit
itself was excellent in itself, and yet that virtue was present to no one
without his always using it. And whereas they did not take disturbance of mind
away from man and said that by nature one both grieves and desires and fears
and is carried away by joy, but they contracted these and drew them into a
narrow space, he wished the wise man to be free of all these as of diseases.

** Section 39
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h10
:END:

And whereas the ancients said those disturbances were natural and without
reason and placed desire in one part of the mind, reason in another, he did
not agree even with these; for he thought disturbances were voluntary and were
taken up by a judgment of opinion, and he thought the mother of all
disturbances was a certain immoderate intemperance. This, more or less, about
character.

About natures he thought thus: first, that he did not apply to those four
beginnings of things this fifth nature, from which the earlier thinkers
thought the senses and the mind were produced; for he established that fire
was that very nature which generated each thing, and mind and the senses. He
differed from the same men also because he thought in no way that anything
could be produced by that which was without body,

of which kind Xenocrates and the earlier thinkers had said even the soul was,
and that neither what produced something nor what was produced could be not a
body.

So Arcesilas denied that there was anything that could be known, not even that
very thing which Socrates had left himself, that he knew that he knew nothing;
thus he thought all things lay hidden in secret and that there was nothing
that could be discerned or understood; for which reasons no one ought either
to profess or to affirm or to approve by assent, and always to check and to
keep rashness from every slip, which was then conspicuous when either a false
or an unknown thing was approved, and that nothing was more shameful than for
assent and approval to run ahead of cognition and perception. He did what was
consistent with this method, so that arguing against the views of all he drew
most people from their own, so that when in the same matter equal weights of
arguments were found on contrary sides, assent might more easily be withheld
from either side.
