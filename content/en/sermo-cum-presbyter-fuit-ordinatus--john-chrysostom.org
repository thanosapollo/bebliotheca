* Sermon When He Was Ordained Presbyter
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

A first homily of the same, when he was ordained presbyter, to himself and to
the bishop and to the multitude of the people.
Are the things that have happened concerning us true? And have the things that
have come to pass really come to pass, and have we not been deceived? Are the
present things not night and a dream, but is it truly day, and are we all
awake? And who would believe these things, that while it is day, while men are
sober and awake, a cheap and discarded youth has been raised to so great a
height of office? For in the night nothing of this sort is unlikely to happen.
Already, at any rate, some who are crippled in body and who do not even have
plenty of the necessary food, having fallen asleep, have seen themselves
become sound and beautiful and enjoying a royal table; but the appearances
were sleep and the deceit of dreams. For such is the nature of dreams: it is a
certain cunning and marvelous thing, and it delights to revel in paradoxes.
But these things do not happen in the day, nor would one perhaps see this
coming to pass upon the very truth of the facts.
But all things have now happened and come to pass and have their end, as you
see, these things, yes these things more unbelievable than dreams, that a city
so great and populous, a marvelous and great people, has gaped toward our
cheapness, as if it were going to hear something great and noble from us. And
yet even if springs of words were stored in my mouth like rivers that flow
everlastingly, with so many having run together all at once to the hearing,
the stream would quickly have been checked in me by fear, and the waters would
have been driven back. But when we preserve not even the measure of rivers,
nor of springs only, but of a cheap sprinkle, how is it not a fear that even
this little of the inflow may fail, dried up by fear, and that the same thing
may happen which is accustomed to happen also in the case of bodies? And what
happens in the case of bodies? Often holding many things in the hand and
squeezing them with our fingers, yet having been frightened we have poured
them all out, our sinews having been slackened and the tension of the flesh
having been loosened. This then is the fear, that today too it may happen in
our soul, and that the thoughts which we gathered for you with much labor,
these small and cheap ones, driven by the agony, may go away as nothing and,
leaving our mind empty, may fly off. Therefore I beg you all alike, rulers and
ruled, that as much agony as you cast into us by the running-together for the
hearing, so much courage you breathe into us through the zeal of the prayers,
and that you beg him who gives a word to those who preach the gospel with much
power, to give us also a word in the opening of our mouth. Surely there is no
labor for you, being so many and so great, to tighten again the soul of one
youth dissolved by fear; and you would be just to grant us this request, since
it is also for you that we have thrown this die, for you and your love, than
which nothing is more violent nor more tyrannical, seeing that it even
persuaded us, who are not very experienced in speaking, to speak, and prepared
us to strip for the stadium of teaching, although we had never before touched
these wrestlings, but were always ranked with the hearers and enjoying an
unbusied quiet.
But who is so hard and intractable as to pass by your assembly in silence,
and, having found ardent lovers of hearing, not to address them, even if he
were more voiceless than all men? I wished, then, first, being about to send
forth a word in church, to begin the preludes with God who has given us this
tongue; for so it was necessary. For one must offer firstfruits not only of
threshing-floor and winepress, but also of words to the Word, and of words
much more than of sheaves, inasmuch as this fruit is more proper to us and
more dear to God who is honored. For a cluster and an ear the flanks of the
earth bring forth, and inflows of rains nourish, and the hands of tillers
tend; but a holy hymn is born of reverence of soul, is nourished by a good
conscience, and God receives it into the storehouses of the heavens. And by
how much a soul is better than earth, by so much is this yield better than
that. For this reason also one of the prophets, a marvelous and great man,
Hosea by name, exhorts those who have offended God and are about to make him
gracious, saying: Bring with yourselves, not herds of oxen, nor so many and so
many measures of fine flour, nor a turtledove and a dove, nor anything else of
such things, but what?
Bring with yourselves words, he says. And what kind of sacrifice is a word?
perhaps someone would say. The greatest, then, beloved, and most august, and
better than all the others. And who says these things? He himself who knows
these things most accurately of all, the noble and great David. For once
sacrificing thank-offerings to God upon a victory of war that had come to
pass, he says somehow thus: I will praise the name of my God with a song; I
will magnify him with praise. Then, showing us the superiority of this
sacrifice, he added: And it will please God more than a young calf bringing
forth horns and hoofs. I wished, then, myself also today to lay down these
victims and to stain the spiritual altar with blood from these sacrifices; but
what am I to suffer? A certain wise man stops my mouth and frightens me,
saying: Praise is not seemly in the mouth of a sinner. For just as in the case
of crowns not only the flowers must be pure, but also the hand that weaves
them; so also in the case of the holy hymns not only the words must have a
share of reverence, but also the soul that plaits them. But ours is a certain
unholy one, and without boldness of speech, and full of many sins. And those
so disposed this law not only stops the mouth of, but also another more
ancient than that one and laid down before it. And David, who just now
discoursed to us concerning the sacrifices, brought this one in; for having
said: Praise the Lord from the heavens, praise him in the highest. Praise the
Lord from the earth, and having called both creations, the upper, the lower,
the sensible, the intelligible, the seen, the unseen, that above the heaven,
that under the heaven, and having set one choir from both, and having thus
commanded them to hymn the king of all, nowhere did he call the sinner, but
even here he shut the doors against him. And that what is said may become
clearer to you, I will read the psalm itself to you from the beginning: Praise
the Lord from the heavens, praise him in the highest; praise him, all his
angels, praise him, all his powers.
Did you see angels praising, did you see archangels, did you see the Cherubim
and the Seraphim, the higher powers? For when he says: all his powers, he
includes the whole people above. Nowhere did you see the sinner? For how was
it possible for him to be seen in heaven, he says? Come then, let us lead you
down also to the earth, having transferred you to the other part of the choir;
and not even here will you see him again. Praise the Lord from the earth,
dragons and all abysses, the wild beasts and all the cattle, creeping things
and winged birds. Not in vain, nor at random, did I fall silent while saying
these things in the midst, but the reasoning of my mind was also confounded,
and it came forth to weep bitterly and to groan greatly. For what could be
more pitiable, tell me, than that scorpions and vipers and dragons are called
to the praise of him who made them, but the sinner alone is left out of this
holy choir-standing? And reasonably. For he is an evil and untamed beast, not
displaying the wickedness toward the fellow-slaves of the bodies, but pouring
out the venom of wickedness against the Master's glory. For because of you, he
says, my name is blasphemed among the nations. For this reason the prophet
drove him out, as from a holy fatherland, from the inhabited world, and
settled him in exile. So too somewhere an excellent musician cuts off the
discordant string of a well-tuned lyre, so as not to ruin the harmony of the
remaining notes; so too a skilled physician cuts out the rotten member, so
that the ruin from there may not run upon the soundness of the remaining
members; so also the prophet did, having cut off the sinner as a discordant
string and as a diseased member of the whole body of creation. What then would
there be for us to do? Since we have been thrown away, since we have been cut
out, it is altogether necessary to be silent. Shall we then be silent, tell
me? And will no one allow us to hymn our Master, but have we called your
prayers in vain? Have we fled to your intercessions in vain? Not in vain, may
it not be.
For I have found, I have found also another manner of doxology, your prayers
themselves having appeared in the midst of this perplexity like lightnings in
darkness; for it is permitted also to praise fellow-slaves, and when these are
praised, the glory will altogether pass through to heaven, just as he himself
also says: Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works
and glorify your Father who is in the heavens. See then also another manner of
doxology which it is possible even for a sinner to speak, and not to undo the
law. Whom then, whom of the fellow-slaves shall we praise? And whom else but
the common teacher of the fatherland, and through the fatherland of the whole
inhabited world as well? For just as this man taught you to stand fast unto
death for the truth, so you have taught the other men to withdraw from the
soul rather than from piety. Do you wish then that from this point we should
plait for him the crowns of encomiums? I wished it myself also, but I see a
yawning sea of achievements, and I fear that the word, having been carried
down to the bottom, may be too weak to run up again. It is necessary,
therefore, to narrate old achievements, journeys abroad, vigils, cares,
counsels, battles, trophies joined to trophies and victories to victories,
things greater not only than our tongue but than every human tongue, needing
an apostolic voice moved by the Spirit, by which it is possible both to say
and to teach all things. But passing by this part, we shall come to another
safer one, which it is possible to sail through even in a small boat. Come
then, let us set in motion the words concerning self-control, how he mastered
the belly, how he looked down on luxury, how he laughed at a costly table, and
these things having been reared in a costly house. For there is nothing
marvelous if one who has lived in poverty comes to this dry and hard life; for
he has poverty as a fellow-merchant and fellow-traveler, lightening the load
for him each day. But he who has become master of wealth would not easily
strip off the holds from there; so great a swarm of diseases flies around such
a soul, and a heavy and gloomy cloud of passions, fencing off the eyes of the
mind, does not allow it to look toward heaven, but forces it to nod downward
and to gape toward the earth.
And there is not, there is not any other thing so much a hindrance toward the
departure of the heavens as wealth and the evils from wealth. And this word is
not mine, but the vote has come down from Christ himself: For it is easier, he
says, for a camel to enter through the hole of a needle than for a rich man
into the kingdom of the heavens. But see, this difficult thing, or rather
impossible, has become possible; and what Peter of old was at a loss about
toward the teacher and sought to learn, this we have all come to know through
the experience itself, or rather even more than this. For this man not only
went up into heaven, but also leads in so great a people, having besides
wealth also other hindrances not less than wealth, youth and untimely
orphanhood, which are more able than all men to bewitch a soul; such spells
they have, such drugs they prepare.
But this man mastered these things and laid hold of the heavens and
transferred himself to the philosophy there, and he neither considered the
splendor of the present life, nor looked to the distinction of his ancestors;
or rather he did look to the distinction of his ancestors, not of those bound
to him by natural necessity, but of those belonging to him by choice of piety.
Therefore he also became such. He looked to the patriarch Abraham, he looked
to the great Moses, who, having been reared in a royal house and having shared
a Sybaritic table and having been caught in the midst of Egyptian tumults (and
you know what the things of barbarians are, of how much conceit and display
they are full), having dishonored all those things, deserted to the clay and
the brickmaking, and having desired to be of the slaves and captives, he the
king and son of the king. For this reason, then, he came back again with a
more splendid form than the one he had before he threw it away; for after the
flight and the service at his father-in-law's and the hardship in a foreign
land, he came back as leader of the king, or rather having become a god to the
king.
For I have set you, he says, as a god to Pharaoh. And he was more splendid
than the king, not having a diadem, nor wearing purple, nor driving a golden
pair, but having trampled all that conceit. For all the glory, he says, of the
daughter of the king is within. He came back, then, having a scepter,
wherefore he commanded not only men, but heaven and earth and sea and the
nature of airs and waters and lakes and springs and rivers; for all things, as
many as Moses wished, these the elements became, and in his hands creation was
reshaped again, and like some well-disposed maidservant, having seen a friend
of the master arriving, it obeyed all things and hearkened as to the master
himself. Looking to whom, this man became such even while being young, if
indeed he ever was young; for I at least am not persuaded, so grey was the
prudence in him from the very swaddling-clothes, but even being young
according to the account of age, he laid hold of all philosophy, and having
learned thoroughly our nature, that it is like a plot of land running to wild
growth, he easily cut out the diseases of the soul by the word of piety, as by
some sickle, providing clean fields to the farmer for the sowing of the seeds,
and receiving all things, he sent them through to the depth; so that, having
been rooted from below, they might neither give way toward the assault of the
ray, nor again be choked by the thorns. And the soul he tended thus; but the
leapings of the flesh he checked with the drugs of self-control, throwing upon
the body, as upon some hard-to-rein horse, the bit from fasting, and
stretching it back so far as to make the very mouths of the desires bleed,
with the fitting sparing; for he neither overstretched the body, so that the
horse, having been entangled, might not become useless to him for the service,
nor did he allow it to fall into too great a good condition, lest, having
grown too fleshy, it should again rise up against the reasoning that was
driving it, but he took care at once both of its health and of its good order.
And it is not that, being young, he was such, and that when he passed that age
he also loosed this providence; but even now, when he sits in old age as in a
fair-weather harbor, he holds to the same care again.
For youth, beloved, is like a raging sea, full of wild waves and evil winds;
but grey hair moors the souls of those who have grown old as into a waveless
harbor, granting them to revel in the safety from the age. This man too,
enjoying this now and sitting in the harbor, as I said before, is no less in
agony than those tossed in the midst of the sea; and he received this fear
from Paul, who, having gone up into heaven and having run up into that after
it and having touched a third again, said: I fear lest somehow, having
preached to others, I myself should become unapproved. For this reason this
man also has set himself in continual fear, that he may be continually in
confidence, and he sits upon the tillers, not watching the risings of stars,
nor reefs and rocks, but uprisings of demons and villainies of the devil and
battles of thoughts, and going around the camp in a circle, he sets all in
safety.
For he does not look only that the vessel may not be sunk, but he transacts
all things so that not even one of those sailing in it may experience any
tumult. Because of this man and his wisdom we all sail with a fair wind,
having spread the sails of the ship full. And yet when we lost the former
father, who also begot this man for us, our affairs were in perplexity.
Therefore we also lamented pitiably, as not expecting this throne to receive
another such man. But when this man appeared and came forward into the midst,
he made all that despondency pass by like a cloud and hid all the gloomy
things, not wiping out the mourning for us little by little, but so suddenly
as if that blessed man himself, having risen from the coffin, had gone up upon
this throne again. But we have unawares, by desire of paternal achievements,
stretched the words beyond the measure, not the measure of the things achieved
by this man; for of these we have not yet begun, but of that which is fitting
for us by reason of youth. Come then, let us rest the words again in silence
as in a harbor. But they do not wish to stand away, but they are displeased
and indignant, desiring altogether to have their fill of the meadow. But this
is impossible, O children. Let us cease then pursuing the unattainable; even
the things said are enough for us toward consolation; since even in the case
of costly unguents, not only if one pours the kettle, but even if he touch the
very surface with the fingers, he has both stained the air and filled all
those present with the fragrance; which indeed has happened now also, not
through the power of our words, but through the virtue of this man's
achievements.
Let us go away then, let us go away, having ended in prayers; let us beg that
our common mother remain unshaken and unmoved, and that this father, the
teacher, the shepherd, the steersman, be carried out to a longer life. If
there is any account of us too with you; for we shall not dare to number
ourselves with the priests, since it is not even lawful to count the abortions
with those born complete, but if there is any account of me too with you, as
of some miscarriage, beg that much inclination from above come to be for us.
For we needed safety even before, when we lived by ourselves the unbusied
life; but since we have been led into the midst; and how, I pass over, whether
by human zeal or by divine grace, for I contend with you about nothing, lest
someone say we are even being ironic; nevertheless since we have been led and
have gone under this strong and heavy yoke, we need many hands, countless
prayers, so as to be able to give back the deposit sound to the Master who
deposited it, on that day when those entrusted with the talents are called and
led in and undergo the accounts.
Beg then that we may not become of those who have been bound, nor of those who
are cast out into the darkness, but of those able to obtain at least a
moderate pardon, by the grace and love of mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, to
whom is the glory and the power and the worship unto the ages of ages. Amen.
