LETTER OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA ON THE LIFE OF THE BLESSED
MACRINA
1 The form of the book, so far as it lies in the type of the title, seems to
be a letter, but the bulk is beyond the epistolary limit, stretching out into
a historian's long discourse. Yet the subject apologizes for us, for whose
sake you commanded me to write, being greater than the due measure of a
letter. You are certainly not unmindful of the meeting, when, intending to
visit Jerusalem according to a vow, in order to see in those places the tokens
of the Lord's sojourn in the flesh, I met you at the city of Antioch, and when
all sorts of conversation were being stirred between us (for it was not likely
that the meeting would be in silence, your intelligence supplying many
starting-points to the discourse), as often happens in such cases, the talk,
flowing on, came to the memory of a certain life of good repute. And a woman
was the occasion of the narrative, if indeed a woman: for I do not know
whether it is fitting to name from nature her who has come to be above nature.
And the narrative had its credibility for us not from the reports of others,
but the account went through with accuracy those things of which experience
was the teacher, calling no foreign hearing to witness; for the virgin
mentioned was not a stranger to our family, so that there should be a
necessity of knowing the wonders concerning her through others, but from the
same parents as ourselves, like some first-fruits of the produce, the first to
spring from our mother's womb. Since then you judged that the history of the
good would bring some profit, so that such a life might not escape the time
after this, nor that she who had raised herself through philosophy to the
furthest bound of human virtue might run by unprofitably, covered over by
silence, I thought it well both to obey you and, in few words, as far as I am
able, to relate the things concerning her in an unadorned and simple
narrative. 2 Macrina was the name of the virgin, and there was of old a
certain Macrina of good repute in the family, who had been the mother of our
father, having contended in the time of the persecutions with confessions on
behalf of Christ, after whom the child was named by her parents. But this was
the name in the open, the one by which she was called among those who knew
her, and another had been given her in secret, which was named from a certain
appearance before she came through the birth-pangs into the light. For the
mother too was such in virtue as to be guided everywhere by the divine will,
and especially having embraced the pure and unstained conduct of life, so as
not even to choose marriage willingly. But since she was an orphan on both
sides, and was in the bloom of bodily prime, and the report of her beauty
gathered many to the suit, and there was danger, if she were not joined to
someone of her own will, of suffering some unwanted thing from outrage, those
maddened by her beauty being prepared for seizures: for this reason, choosing
the one known and attested for gravity of life, so as to acquire a guardian of
her own life, she at once in the first birth-pangs becomes mother of this
child. And when the time was present at which the travail must be loosed by
the birth, having fallen into sleep she seemed to be carrying in her hand what
was still contained under the inward parts, and someone appearing in form and
figure more magnificent than according to man addressed the one being carried
by the name of Thecla, that Thecla of whom there is much account among the
virgins. And having done this three times he departed from her sight and gave
ease to the travail, so that she both rose from the sleep and saw the dream as
waking fact. That then was the hidden name. And it seems to me that the one
who appeared did not so much, leading the mother toward the


nominal calling, address her thus, but that he foretold the life of the young
woman and indicated through the sameness of name the likeness of her choice.
3 The child then is reared, having also her own nurse, but for the most part
being nursed in her mother's own hands. And having passed the age of infants
she was apt at the children's lessons, and toward whatever lesson the judgment
of the parents led, in that the nature of the young woman shone. And the
mother was eager to educate the girl, yet not this outside and general
education, which as a rule the first ages of those being educated are taught
through the poems. For she thought it shameful and altogether unseemly that
either the tragic passions, as many as from women gave the poets their
beginnings and subjects, or the comic indecencies, or the causes of the evils
at Ilium, should be taught to a tender and easily molded nature, being in a
way stained by the less seemly narratives concerning women. But as much of the
God-inspired Scripture as seems more readily grasped by the first ages, these
were the girl's lessons, and especially the Wisdom of Solomon, and of this
still more as much as bore toward the ethical life. But of the psalm-sung
Scripture she was ignorant of nothing whatsoever, going through each part of
the psalmody at its own times, both rising from bed and taking hold of her
tasks and resting and receiving food and withdrawing from table and going to
bed and rising for prayers: everywhere she had the psalmody as a sort of good
companion, left behind at no time. 4 Growing with these and with such
practices, and having trained her hand especially toward wool-working, she
advances to the twelfth year, in which most of all the flower of youth begins
to shine. And here it is worth wondering how the beauty of the young woman,
even when hidden, did not escape notice, nor did any such wonder seem
throughout that whole country as to be, in comparison with that beauty and
fairness, such that not even painters' hands could attain to her prime; but
the art that contrives all things and dares even the greatest, so as even to
recast the images of the elements themselves through imitation, was not able
to imitate with accuracy the fortunate beauty of that form. For this reason a
great swarm of those suing for her marriage poured around the parents. And the
father (for he was temperate and trained to judge the good), having set apart
from the rest a certain man of good repute from the family, known for
temperance, just returned from the schools, had resolved to betroth the girl
to him, if she should come to age. Meanwhile the young man was in the better
hopes and brought to the father of the young woman, as a sort of welcome
bride-gift, his good repute in speeches, displaying the power of his words in
contests on behalf of the wronged. But envy cuts short the better hopes,
snatching him from life in pitiable youth. 5 The maiden was not unaware of
what had been resolved by the father; but when by the death of the young man
the decision concerning her was cut off, naming marriage the father's
judgment, as if what had been decided had already come to be, she claimed to
remain by herself for the rest, and the judgment was more firmly set than her
years. For often when her parents brought her the talks about marriage,
because there were many who wished to sue by report of her beauty, she said it
was absurd and unlawful not to be content with the marriage once ratified for
her by the father, but also to be forced to look toward another, there being
in nature one marriage as there is one birth and one death; and she maintained
that the one joined according to the parents' judgment had not died, but that
the one "living to God" through the hope of the resurrection she judged a
sojourner abroad and not a corpse;


and that it was absurd not to keep faith with the bridegroom who was away.
With such words thrusting off those who tried to persuade her, she approved
for herself one safeguard of the good judgment, never to be separated from her
own mother even for an instant of time, so that the mother often said to her
that she had carried the rest of the children for a set time, but that one she
carried in herself always, encompassing her in a way always with her own
inward parts. But the living-together of the daughter was neither toilsome nor
unprofitable to the mother; for instead of many serving-maids there was the
attendance coming from the daughter, and there was a certain good exchange
fulfilled from both toward each other. For the one tended the soul of the
young woman, the other the body of the mother, filling in all other things the
service sought, and often preparing bread for the mother with her own hands;
which was not pursued by her as the principal thing, but since she had used
her own hands for the mystical services, having judged the zeal about this
fitting to the practice of her life, she supplied from what was over to the
mother the food from her own labors. And not these things only, but she also
administered together with her all the care that lay upon her; for she was
mother of four sons and five daughters and paid tribute to three governors,
because her property was scattered among so many provinces. The mother then
being variously divided among her cares for this reason (for already the
father had gone out of life): in all these things she was partner to the
mother in the toils, sharing the cares and lightening the weight of the pains.
And at once by the mother's training she kept her own life blameless, being
always directed and attested in a mother's eyes, and at once she furnished
toward the same aim (I mean the one according to philosophy) a great guidance
to the mother through her own life, drawing her little by little toward the
immaterial and simpler life. 6 And when the mother had arranged what concerned
the sisters toward what seemed 6 to each with propriety, there returns at this
time from the schools, long beforehand trained in speeches, the great Basil,
the brother of the one mentioned. Taking him then, exceedingly lifted up in
conceit about speeches and despising all dignities and lifted in pride above
those splendid in power, she drew him also with such speed toward the aim of
philosophy that, standing off from worldly distinction and looking down on
being wondered at through speeches, he went over of himself to this laborious
and self-working life, preparing for himself through perfect poverty an
unimpeded life toward virtue. But his life and the practices that followed,
through which becoming famous in all the world under the sun he hid by his
glory all those who had shone in virtue, would be of a long writing and much
time; and let my account be turned again toward what lies before us. 7 For
when already the occasion of every more material life had been cut away from
them, she persuades the mother, leaving the life in custom and the more
pompous conduct and the attendances from those under her, to which she had
been accustomed in the time before, to become equal in mind with the many and
to mix her own life with the conduct among the virgins, making sisters and
equals as many as she had with her from slave-girls and subordinates; or
rather I wish to insert a little into the narrative and not to run past such a
matter unrecorded, through which the height of the virgin is more made known.
8 Of the four brothers the second after the great Basil was Naucratius by
name, differing from the others in fortunate nature and beauty of body and
strength and speed and fitness toward everything. Having advanced to the
twenty-


second year and given the proofs of his own labors before a public hearing, so
that the whole theater of the hearers was shaken over him, by a certain divine
providence looking down on all that was in his hands he went away toward the
solitary and propertyless life in a great impulse of mind, bringing nothing
with him except himself; and there followed him also one of the household
servants, Chrysaphius by name, both because that one was well disposed toward
him and because he had set up the same choice concerning life. He lived then
by himself, having taken a certain remote place by the Iris. And the Iris is a
river flowing through the middle of Pontus, which having its beginnings from
Armenia itself gives out its stream through our places into the Euxine Pontus.
About this the young man, finding a certain place thick with deep wood and
hidden in a hollow of the overhung ridge of the mountain, lived in it, having
come far from the city tumults and from the occupations of military service
and of rhetoric in the law-courts. And having freed himself from all the
things that din around human life, he tended with his own hands certain old
men living with poverty and sickness, having judged it fitting to his own life
to have such an occupation in care. Hunting then, because he was fitted toward
every kind of hunting device, he furnished food to the old men and at the same
time tamed his youth with such labors; but also serving eagerly the mother's
wishes, if ever something were commanded by her, he set his life right through
both, mastering youth by the labors and making his way to God through the
divine commandments by his zeal toward the mother. 9 He spent a fifth year
philosophizing in this manner and making the mother blessed by his own life,
both by the things with which he adorned his own life through temperance and
by those with which he offered all his power to the will of her who bore him.
Then a heavy and tragic calamity, by the plotting, I think, of the adversary,
was brought together upon the mother, which sufficed for the whole family
toward disaster and mourning. For he is snatched suddenly from life, no
disease having prepared the suffering to be hoped in advance, nor any other
such of the customary and familiar things having brought death upon the young
man; but having set out to hunt, through which he furnished the necessaries to
those being tended in old age, he is brought back dead to his house, both he
himself and Chrysaphius, the partner of his life. And the mother was far from
what was happening, being a three days' road distant from the disaster, and
someone came to her announcing the calamity. And she was perfect in all things
according to virtue, yet nature mastered her equally as well; for having given
way in soul she became at once breathless and voiceless, reason yielding to
the emotion, and she lay at the blow of the evil hearing like some noble
athlete shaken by an unexpected stroke. 10 In this the virtue of the great
Macrina shone through, how, setting reason against the emotion, she both kept
herself unfallen and, becoming a support of the mother's weakness, drew her up
again from the depth of grief, training even the mother's soul toward courage
by her own firm and unyielding state. Therefore the mother was not swept
toward the emotion nor did she suffer anything ignoble and womanish, so as to
cry out toward the evil or to tear her garment or to wail over the calamity or
to stir the dirges with doleful melodies. But she endured quietly, thrusting
off the assaults of nature by reasonings, both her own and those brought to
her by the daughter toward the healing of the evil. For then most of all the
high and lifted soul of the virgin shone through, because even in her nature
suffered its own; for he was a brother, and of brothers the favored one, who
had been snatched by such a manner of death; yet having become higher than
nature she lifted the mother together with her own reasonings and set her
above the emotion, training her by her own example toward endurance and
courage. And otherwise also her life, always being


raised through virtue, did not give the mother occasion to be distressed over
what was lacking more than to exult in the good that was seen. 11 When then
both the care of child-rearing and the concern for the education and
establishment of the children had ceased for the mother, and the greater
occasions of the more material life had been divided among the children, then,
as was said before, the life of the virgin becomes counselor of the mother
toward this philosophical and immaterial conduct of life, and standing her off
from all things in custom she brought her down to her own measure of humility,
preparing her to become equal in honor with the fullness of the virgins, so as
even to share with them equally one table and bed and all things toward life,
every difference according to rank having been taken from their life. And such
was the order of the life, and so great the height of the philosophy and the
solemn citizenship of the life in the daily and nightly conduct, as to
overpass the description from words. For just as the souls loosed from the
bodies through death are loosed together also from the cares according to this
life, so their life was separated and settled away from all worldly vanity and
was rhythmically ordered toward imitation of the angels' conduct. For in them
neither anger, nor envy, nor hatred, nor disdain, nor any other of such things
was observed, and the desire of vain things, of honor and glory and puffing
and pride and all such, had been cast out; and luxury was self-control, and
glory was not to be known, and wealth was propertylessness and the shaking off
of all material surplus from the bodies as a sort of dust, and of the things
pursued according to this life there was no work, except as a side-work, but
only the study of the divine things and unceasing prayer and unstopping
hymnody, stretched equally through all time of every night and day, so that
this was for them both work and rest from work. What human speech then would
bring such a conduct before the eyes, among those for whom life was on the
border of the human and the bodiless nature? For to have the nature freed from
human passions was greater than according to man, and to appear in a body and
to be wrapped in shape and to live with the sense-organs, in this they had the
less beside the angelic and bodiless nature. And perhaps someone might dare to
say that the difference was not even toward the inferior, because living with
flesh in likeness of the bodiless powers they were not weighed down by the
drag of the body, but their life was upward-borne and aloft, journeying on
high together with the heavenly powers. The time of such conduct was not
little, and the achievements grew with the time, philosophy always advancing
toward the purer by the additions of the goods being discovered. 12 And she
had as the one serving most toward this great aim of life a brother of the
same womb, Peter by name, at whom the birth-pangs of our mother ceased. For
this was the last shoot of the parents, who was named at once son and orphan;
for at the same time as he came into the light the father left life. But the
eldest of the sisters, of whom the account is, having taken a small share of
the breast at the first birth, at once snatched him from the nurse and rears
him through herself and led him upon all the higher education, training him
from infancy in the sacred lessons, so as not to give the soul leisure to
incline toward any of the vain things. But becoming all things to the young
man, father, teacher, pedagogue, mother, counselor of every good, she worked
him such that before he went out of the age of children, still blooming in the
boyish tenderness of his prime, he was lifted toward the high aim of
philosophy, and by a certain fortunate nature was fitted toward every kind of
art worked through the hand,


so that with no one leading he had accomplished through all accuracy the
science of each of the things whose learning comes to the many by time and
labor. This one then, looking down on occupation about the outside speeches,
and having nature as a sufficient teacher of every good lesson, and always
looking toward the sister and making her the aim of every good, advanced in
virtue to such a point that he seemed to have nothing less than the great
Basil in the advantages according to virtue. These things were in the life
after this; but then he was instead of all things to the sister and the
mother, working with them toward that angelic life. Who once also, when a hard
famine had come to be, and many from everywhere were flowing by report of the
well-doing toward the remote place in which they dwelt, so multiplied the
foods through devices that by the multitude of those visiting the wilderness
seemed to be a city. 13 In this the mother, having advanced to a sleek old
age, migrated toward God, resting her own life in the hands of both children.
Of whom it is worth relating the voice of blessing which she used upon the
children, having mentioned each of those not present according to what was
fitting, so that none became disinherited of the blessing, and especially
having committed to God through prayer those present with her. For as these
two sat beside her on either side of the bed, having touched the children with
either hand she said these things to God in last voices: "To you, Lord, I both
offer first-fruits and I tithe the fruit of the birth-pangs. This firstborn is
my first-fruit, and this is the tenth, the last birth-pang. And both have been
dedicated to you by the law and are your offerings. Therefore let the
sanctification come both upon this my first-fruit and upon this tenth," having
pointed by the pointing words to the daughter and the boy. She then, having
ceased from blessing, ceased also from living, charging the children to lay
her body also in the ancestral tomb. And they, having fulfilled what was
commanded, held to the philosophy more highly, always competing toward their
own life and hiding the achievements that had gone before by those that
followed. 14 In this the Basil great among the saints was shown forth as
protector of the great church of the Caesareans; who brings the brother to the
lot of the priesthood in the presbyterate, having dedicated him by his own
mystical liturgies. And in this again their life advanced toward the more
solemn and more holy, the philosophy being increased by the priesthood. And
eight years having passed after this, in the ninth year Basil, famous
throughout the whole inhabited world, is moved from men toward God, becoming a
common occasion of mourning to the fatherland and to the inhabited world. And
she, having heard the disaster from afar by report, suffered in soul at so
great a loss (for how was the emotion not going to touch even her, of which
even the enemies of the truth had perception?). And just as they say the
testing of gold comes to be in different melting-pots, so that if something
should escape the first melting it is distinguished in the second, and again
in the last all the filth mixed in the matter is cleaned away, and the most
accurate trial of proven gold is if, having gone through every furnace, it
should cast off no filth; some such thing happened also in her case, the high
mind having been tested by the different assaults of griefs, the unadulterated
and unlowered of the soul being shown forth from every side, first in the
departure of the other brother, after this in the separation of the mother,
and third when the common good of the family, Basil, was separated from human
life. She remained then like some unconquered athlete, nowhere giving way at
the assault of the disasters.


15 It was the ninth month after this calamity, or a little beyond this, and a
synod of bishops was gathered at the city of Antioch, of which we also took
part. And when again we were each dismissed toward our own, before the year
passed, it comes into the mind of me, Gregory, to cross over to her. For the
time in between was long, in which the circumstances of the trials prevented
the visits, which I endured being driven everywhere from the fatherland by
those presiding over the heresy. And as I numbered the time in between, in
which the trials prevented the meeting face to face, the interval appeared not
little, being measured at nearly eight years. When then, having accomplished
the greater part of the road, I was a day's road distant, a certain sight
appearing to us from a dream made the hopes of what was coming fearful. For I
seemed to be carrying remains of martyrs in my hand, and there was from them a
gleam such as comes from a clean mirror, when it is set face to face toward
the sun, so that my eyes were dulled toward the flashing of the radiance. And
the same night such a sight having come to me three times, I was not able to
guess clearly the riddle of the dream, but I foresaw a certain grief for the
soul and watched to judge the appearance by the outcome. And having come near
the remote place in which she lived, accomplishing the angelic and heavenly
life, I asked first one of those close to her about the brother, whether he
were present; and when he said that he had set out toward us and was spending
the fourth day, understanding what it was, that his setting-out toward us had
come by another road, then I inquired also about the great one; and when he
said she had come to be in a certain sickness I held more eagerly,
accomplishing the rest of the road in haste; for a certain fear also, running
under as a messenger of what was coming, disturbed me. 16 And when I had come
to the place itself and report had announced the presence beforehand to the
brotherhood, the whole company of the men poured forth toward us from the
men's quarters; for it was customary for them to honor those they loved by
meeting them; and the choir of virginity among the women waited our entrance
in good order at the church. And when the prayer and the blessing had an end,
and they, after submitting the head to the blessing, withdrawing becomingly,
went back toward themselves, and none of them was left toward us, guessing
what it was, that the leader was not among them, someone going before to the
house in which the great one was, and spreading the door, I came within that
holy monastery. And she was already strongly held by the sickness, and she was
resting not upon any bed or mattress, but upon the floor, a board being
stretched under the sackcloth and another board again supporting the head,
whose workmanship was of such a sort as to become instead of a pillow for the
head, going under the tendons in a slanting shape and holding up the neck upon
itself as she liked. 17 When then she saw me come near the doors, having
raised herself upon her elbow she was not able to run forward, the strength
already being loosed by the fever; but having fixed her hands upon the floor
and stretching herself as far as she could out from the pallet she fulfilled
the honor of the meeting; and I, running forward and taking up with my hands
the face lying on the ground, both raised her again and gave her back to the
customary shape of lying. And she, stretching out the hand to God: "This grace
also you have fulfilled for me, she says, O God, and you have not deprived me
of my desire, because you have moved your servant to a visit of your
handmaid." And so that she might bring no dejection upon my soul, she softened
the groan and somehow forced herself to hide the constraint of the breath, and
through all things she was reshaped toward the more cheerful, herself both
beginning the talks that pleased and preparing for us the starting-points
through the things she asked. And when the memory of the great Basil fell in
by the sequence of the talk, my soul gave way and


the face fell together in dejection and the tears poured from the eyelids; but
she was so far from being brought low together with our emotion that, having
made the memory of the saint an occasion of the higher philosophy, she went
through such words, both discoursing on human nature and uncovering by the
account the divine economy hidden through the gloomy things, and going through
the things concerning the coming life as if borne by God in the Holy Spirit;
so that my soul seemed to me to be almost outside human nature, lifted
together with what was said and being set within the heavenly sanctuaries by
the leading of the account. 18 And just as in the history of Job we hear that
the man, being wasted throughout the whole body from every side by the
putrefaction of the wounds through discharges, did not incline the reasonings
toward the sense that was in pain, but had the pain in the body, yet he
himself was not dulled toward his own activity nor did he cut off the account
as it entered upon the higher things; some such thing I saw also in that great
one, the fever burning up all her power and driving her together toward death,
cooling her own body as with a certain dew, so she had the mind unimpeded in
the contemplation concerning the high things, harmed in nothing by so great a
sickness. And if the writing were not stretched to infinite length, I would
have related all things in order, how she was lifted in the account,
philosophizing to us concerning the soul and going through the cause of the
life through flesh, and for whose sake man is and how mortal and whence death
and what the return from this again toward life. In all which, as if inspired
by the power of the Holy Spirit, she went through all things clearly and in
sequence, the account flowing in all ease as water from a certain spring being
carried unimpeded toward the slope. 19 And when the account was concluded: "It
is time for you, she says, brother, worn by much toil of the journey, to rest
the body a little." And for me a great and true relief was both to look upon
her and to hear the great words; but since this was welcome and dear to her,
so that I might seem through all things to obey the teacher, finding in one of
the nearby little gardens a certain pleasant lodging prepared, I rested under
the shade of the vines. But it was not possible to have the sense of the
things that gladden, the soul within being confused by the hope of the gloomy
things; for the sight of the dream seemed to me to uncover the riddle through
what appeared. For truly the spectacle lying before us was a remnant of a holy
martyr, which had been put to death by sin, but was shining by the indwelling
grace of the Spirit. And these things I went through toward one of those who
had heard my dream beforehand; and we being more dejected, as was likely, in
the expectation of the things that grieve, she, somehow guessing the thought
in us, sending through to us a certain message of the more cheerful things,
commanded us to take heart and to have the better hopes on her behalf; for she
had perceived the inclination toward the better. And these things were not
said toward deceit, but the account was from the truth itself, even if we were
ignorant toward the present. For in truth, just as some runner having run past
the opponent and already come toward the finish of the stadium, drawing near
to the prize and seeing the victory crown, as already having obtained what
lies before him both exults in himself and announces the victory as good news
to the more well-disposed of the spectators, from such a disposition she also
gave us to hope the better things concerning herself, already looking toward
"the prize of the upward calling" and all but speaking even upon herself the
word of the apostle, that "there is laid up for me henceforth the crown of
righteousness, which the righteous judge will give me," since "I have fought
the good fight and I have finished the course and I have kept the faith." We
then, toward the message of the good things,


having become cheerful, were of the enjoyment of what lay before us: and these
were various and the preparation was filled with all gladness, that great one
thus coming down in zeal even as far as these things. 20 And when we were
again in her eyes, for she did not allow the unoccupied hour to be spent upon
ourselves, taking up the memory of the things lived by her from youth she went
through all things in order as upon a writing, both as much as she had through
memory of the life of the parents and the things before my birth and the life
after these; and the aim of the narrative for her was thanksgiving toward God.
For she showed the life of the parents to be not so much splendid and
looked-at among those then from surplus, as increased from divine
loving-kindness, the parents of the father having been confiscated because of
the confession toward Christ, and the grandfather according to the mother
having been put to death from royal anger and all that belonged having passed
over to other masters; and yet their life was increased through faith to such
a point that there was no one named above them in those times; and again their
surplus having been cut ninefold according to the number of the children, the
portion of each was so multiplied through blessing that the life of each of
the children was above the fortunate estate of the parents. And of that one
herself none of the things named to her according to equal portion toward the

sort from the supply of God that her hands never ceased working toward a
commandment, nor did she ever look toward a man, nor did the occasions toward
seemly conduct come to be for her through any human benefaction, but neither

was, first when the emperor Valens was driving because of the faith, after
this when the confusion in the churches was calling us out toward contests and
labors: "Will you not cease, she says, being disposed unthankfully toward the
divine goods? Will you not heal the ingratitude of the soul? Will you not set
yours beside those of the parents? And yet according to this world we boast
most of all in this, in seeming to be well-born and to have grown from the
well-born. The father, she says, was thought great according to education in
those times, but the glory according to him stood as far as the local
law-courts. After this, leading the rest by his sophistic art, the report did
not go out of Pontus, but the being looked-at in the fatherland was enough for
him. But you, she says, are famous to cities and peoples and nations, and

not see the grace? Nor do you recognize the cause of such great goods, that
the prayers of the parents lift you toward height, you having no or little

longed for the daily measure to be stretched more, so that the sweetening of
our hearing might not cease; but the voice of those singing psalms called out

according to flesh, the fever having spent all the power lying in nature. But
she, looking toward the weakness of our mind, contrived to lead us aside from
the more dejected hope, again with those fair words dispersing the grieving of
the soul, in a thin and constrained breath for the rest. And there most of all
variously


was my soul disposed toward what appeared, nature being weighed as was likely

the separation from life, but with high mind philosophized to the last breath

economically taken on human form, for whom, there being no kinship or intimacy

him, loosed from the bonds of the body. For in truth the course was becoming

awaken them "at the last trumpet." You give to the earth as a deposit our
earth, which you formed with your hands, and again you take up what you gave,
having transformed our mortal and unseemly with incorruption and grace. You
have delivered us from the curse and from sin, having become both on our
behalf. You "have crushed the heads of the dragon" who through the gap of
disobedience took man with the throat. You have made a way for us of the
resurrection, having shattered the gates of Hades and "having brought to
nothing the one having the power of death." You "have given to those who fear
you a sign," the type of the holy cross, toward the taking-down of the
adversary and the safety of our life. Eternal God, to whom "I was cast from my
mother's womb," "whom my soul has loved" from all power, to whom I have
dedicated both the flesh and the soul from my youth and until now, do you
station beside me a luminous angel, the one leading me by the hand toward the
place of refreshment, where "the water of rest" is, beside the bosoms of the
holy fathers. You who cut through the flame of the fiery sword, and gave back
to paradise the man who was crucified with you and fell under your mercies,
"remember me also in your kingdom," because I also have been crucified with
you, "having nailed my flesh from your fear and having been afraid from your
judgments." Let not the fearful gap separate me from your chosen ones, nor let
the envious one stand against my road, nor let my sin be found before your
eyes, if in anything I have stumbled through the weakness of our nature and
have sinned in word or in


work or according to thought. You who have authority upon earth to forgive
sins, "forgive me, that I may be refreshed" and be found before you "in the
putting-off of my body" "having no spot or wrinkle" in the form of my soul,

incense before you." 25 And saying these things at once she set the seal upon
the eyes and the mouth and the heart. And little by little the tongue, burned

toward the gleam she was evident as being eager even to speak the
lamp-lighting thanksgiving; and the voice failing she fulfilled the purpose
through the heart and through the movement of the hands, and the lips were
moved together toward the inward impulse; and when she had fulfilled the
thanksgiving and the hand, being brought upon the face through the seal,
signaled the end of the prayer, having drawn a great and deep breath she ended
life together with the prayer. And when she was for the rest breathless and
unmoved, remembering the commands which she had made at once at the first
meeting, having said that she wished my hands to be laid upon her eyes and the
customary attendance to be brought upon the body through me, I brought upon
the holy face the hand numbed from the emotion, so far as not to seem to
neglect the command; for the eyes needed nothing of those who set in order,
being becomingly taken by the eyelids, just as happens upon sleep according to
nature; and the lips fittingly closed and the hands becomingly laid back upon
the breast and the whole position of the body of itself fitted according to
seemliness needed nothing of the hand of those who adorn. 26 And from two
sides my soul became palsied, both by the things I saw appearing and by the
things with which I was surrounded in hearing through the wailing lament of
the virgins. For until then they endured in quiet, shutting the pain in the
soul, and they stifled the impulse of the wail by fear toward her, as if
fearing even of the face already silent the rebuke, lest perhaps, some voice
having broken from them contrary to what had been commanded them, the teacher
be grieved toward what was happening. And as if some fire were smoldering
their souls within, when the emotion could no longer be mastered through
quiet, suddenly a bitter and ungovernable sound breaks forth, so that my
reasoning no longer remained in its settled state, but as if some

neglecting the things in hand, was wholly of the dirges. And the occasion of
the emotion seemed to me in a way just and reasonable for the virgins. For
they were not lamenting the deprivation of some custom or of the care
according to flesh, nor any other such thing, at which men are impatient
toward disasters, but as having been split off from the hope according to God
itself and from the salvation of the souls they cried these things and in the
dirges bewailed these things. "The lamp of our eyes, they said, has been
quenched; the light of the guidance of the souls has been taken away; the
safety of our life has been dissolved; the seal of incorruption has been
lifted; the bond of like-mindedness has been torn apart, the support of those
without strength has been crushed, the attendance of the sick has been taken
away. Upon you even the night was for us instead of day, being lit by a pure
life; but now even the day will be turned toward gloom." And more harshly than
the others they kindled the emotion who called her mother and nurse. And these
were they whom, in the time of the famine, having taken up as they were thrown
along the roads, she had nursed and reared and led by the hand toward the pure
and incorrupt life.
