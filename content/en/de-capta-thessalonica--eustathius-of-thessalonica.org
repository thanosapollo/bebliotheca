3 Eustathius of Thessalonica, a writing of the capture of the city, would that
it had been later, a capture that had fallen ill in the ill constitution of
the ill-starred reign under Andronicus Comnenus, which that man, administering
evils against the inhabited world, had long been gathering, but very quickly
healed by the liberator, the great emperor Isaac Angelus, who succeeded him
happily and fortunately for the world by the providence and goodwill of God
not many days after the city was taken, in using a swiftness of hand in deeds,
as was most of all needed, which God joined with him, as another discourse,
finding its season, will recount.

Preface of such a writing Captures of cities, whether related as history or
composed as narrative, are for the most part managed by the same methods. The
writer will not be forced to handle all that belong to the task, nor will he
arrange in the same way those that are useful on both sides; but when he is
writing history and without passion he will even theologize in places and will
expand into a discourse of nature and will paint his diction toward beauty
rather unsparingly and will describe places and will be adorned with
ekphraseis and, in short, as one speaking outside of passion, he will arrange
much for the pleasure of the ear, and he will not refrain even from what is
likely, guessing here at least, because he was not present at the evils being
told, so as both to be moved and to speak those very things. And thus the one
who sets forth what belongs to history. But the one who both writes a composed
account and has been colored by the evil will necessarily touch all those
things, yet not to that extent, being bound to abound only in the passion, and
that in proportion to his own personal quality. For if he is of the people,
what blame would he have for being moved to satiety? But if he is relaxed into
the life according to the spirit, and between mourning and giving thanks to
the Greater he sees not a fortified wall but a great chasm, he would refrain
from tragedy without mixture. And the same man would not play, dancing in
mourning, which is also what it is to beautify speeches very much with
cosmetic art in grim sufferings. And the other kinds of composition he will
handle soberly according to a method of his own, neither setting out
paradoxical hearings after the unfeeling histor 4 ian nor the other things
which those outside of passion contrive toward untimely ambition and a display
of much learning. If then the present writing will show me too to be some such
person, it will appear at once. The discourse set out below will of necessity
begin from the passion itself, because it was not possible that one who was in
pitiable things should not first play the tragic part. Then, settling from the
lament, and having somewhat heavily blamed the cause and co-cause of the evil,
he will come to narrate both distinctly and clearly, and in some measure even
grandly; and in places simply, as was needed, and in places more polished, and
having begun from the head, than which nothing is more timely as elder, he
will descend to what follows in sequence and an order not altogether unspoken,
and he will take up again the matters of the capture more widely by every
necessity, since this above all has been laid down as the work before the
writing. And because the divine also foreshows signs in such things, which
here too shone forth plainly in advance, the discourse will touch some such
things also in due measure. Nor will it stand off from setting out the sinful
causes on account of which the terrors came, which those who write composed
accounts also reasonably set among their work. And the writing will rake up
such causes dimly later in the whole discourse according to a teaching method.
For it has not been read and issued at another time, but when the introductory
catecheses of the holy days of fasting are being taught to the ears, so that
the discourse may end in ecclesiastical teaching, having begun otherwise from
the evils which the city suffered.

Beginning of the actual composed discourse of the capture Our present time
also, if any of those of old, has shown forth a subject which a man without
passion and standing far from the danger of it would call great and heavy with
disaster and all-terrible and to be prayed against and not easily



bearable and of many griefs and wanting springs of tears and such things; but
the one who has been coiled, as they say, in a net and wound up with the
matter as we have been would perhaps not have the means to name the evil
according to its worth, being struck away from what he wishes to think by the
variety and succession of the disasters, as many as yawned upon each of the
unfortunate, claiming many and different names as their own; but if he is also
able to hit the evil to the word, he would not unfittingly call it 6 the
vanishing of a great luminary, saying nothing, that man, toward passion (for
one ought here to be moved with other names), yet rightly laying hold of the
grandeur of the misfortune. For is it not some such thing that Thessalonica,
shining very brightly among the cities under heaven, should suffer as she now
has? And she has it as even enemies would pray against, as a fair form
vanished grieves even the one who was once without affection. Alas for the
daemon who so heavily sprang upon the prosperity of so great a city and
overthrew it. He has taken her away completely, then, so that not even a
remnant of old beauty remains. And her walls made useless, and all the holy
places outraged to a degree that not even places daring for all, and seemly
houses put to shame, and the fortunes of citizens, some drained, some poured
out and otherwise scattered, what would they be said, where it is not possible
to go through them as one ought? And the multitude in the city, not only the
military but also the rest, as much as is in the sacred way of life according
to the world and as much as is lay, who would weep it worthily? Yes, and as
much as, having beaten off the roar of life, had arrived as at a harbor at the
unoutgoing of dwelling, all these, as locusts driven by fire through a crop,
were shaken out to destruction by the burning battle. And the enemy,
surrounding, kindled a fire of wrath upon the whole civic body, and those,
some overtaken and somewhere even swaddled by such fire from every side, had
no way to slip through, but had danger of many kinds, and others suffered the
evil from afar as from sparks, the missiles. The sun was newly striking the
fields, but it had not the power to dissolve the deathly night, but the
darkening of those who were falling vaunted itself over the light. Not yet had
anyone finished putting off sweet sleep when the bitter and unawakening took
him over, and a bad dream stood as waking upon the head, and the one seeing it
shut his eyes into death. He was leaving the bed by way of waking, and the
enemy iron laid him down otherwise, as that iron rejoices to bed its victims.
And upon many still half-naked this evil beast was fastening, so that it might
not even weary its teeth by rubbing them on coverings of bodies. And as for
soldiers, and already also those otherwise robust or wholly in their prime, if
war grasped them and tore them, fixing in sharp claws, it was doing nothing
new. For that one delights in such bodies, by which, being also put together,
it is for the most part served. But if it also made it its ambition to carry
off wretched men, already tomb-old and bent toward the earth and somewhere
becoming Charon's even before they were struck, those who were loosed by fear,
and old women most of them and 8 out of their eyes and deafened through deep
old age and neither seeing the lightning of the weapons nor perceiving the
thunder from threats, this was not a sane Ares but a raging spear at work and
a sword without discrimination. These things are most pitiable for mortals.
And most piteous that infants also were laid with those falling in every way,
some pierced through together in the arms that carried them, some by being
thrown down through fear or even murder of those who held them, and the more
of them trampled together. For the necessity of flight also tyrannized such a
blowless murder, one inside the churches as the people were shoved and by the
squeezing suffered a carrying-off of souls, the other about the entrance of
the acropolis, when a war-spear was raised above the eastern tower by the sea
as a signal to the opponents that the city was already worn down, so that
those who wished could also climb in from outside, remaining below was
despaired of, and they raised



their eyes, most of them, as to mountains, the acropolis, where they expected
help would be for them. And what most of all accuses the many-fold destruction
of the evil here was this, that to the corpse-heaps a pile of irrational
animals was also laid beside. For since the people who had flowed together
upward could not be contained by one gate, and they were forcing themselves to
be stuffed in so as to be saved, yet each had no way to slip through, both
those on foot and those on horses, the heavy crowd running behind the one
going before, and he by the falling-on suffering the like from those pressing
from farther back, they too not being free of suffering in this sequence, the
disaster rose to a hill of the dying, all together mixed, men, horses, mules,
asses, on which were packed the things necessary to the many. And such a mound
rose up against the city's tower-work there, almost resembling the earthworks
raised before walls, which war itself also sometimes contrives. And these
things were by the gate of the acropolis, the one that shows in front. But
those whom the inner gate also held, when the useless general untimely dashed
it down, having slipped in to his own ruin in the flight, these are another
tragedy of a new kind. For as if those otherwise fallen were not enough, that
man increased the evil, small in good things, great in wickedness, and as a
kind of finishing crown he too summed up the misfortune, not bearing not to
harm further by a murder of his own hand those whom he had generalled down by
ill counsels, and most of all, to speak the truth, by his slackenings toward
battle and guard, as the discourse going forward will plainly describe, so
that, I think, having made 10 the enemy his own he might soften them, if
indeed he both let so great a city fall thus and became the doer of murders,
himself also sacrificing in addition those whom the time measured out. For he
did not order others to let down that hidden gate, raised aloft by a machine,
but himself, having slackened the device in haste, brought it down and,
shutting it upon those slipping in, trapped those whose lot it was to run
under into destruction, having exchanged life for a squeezed death and lying a
piteous sight, with half the body jutting toward the inside, and with the rest
showing to those outside, so as to be wept over. And he was so brave as to
destroy the whole city that, at the same moment as he came to the tower which
stands above the gates there, he also let himself down by a rope to the enemy
who had run up, leaving the soldiers on the tower to practice empty things
over the defense, and this path of his and this rope to be tracked as accursed
and sung around. Here, being moved, I am led to say something to the man and
to ask him: Why then, O best general, having left the lower city to be cut
down, did you become of the upper portion and, hurrying, hide inside, if you
so quickly had to leave it too subject to the enemies? And why did you not
croak forth the ignoble voice, that of being pitied, before you also choked
those held in the gate and the rest? How is it not ridiculous to run into the
acropolis as a defense and to leap up into a tower as for the good of those
standing above, and at once to seize a rope and to be below sooner than a
bucket on a well-rope down a well? O this laughter, that the brilliant leader,
drawn up before the eastern gates as for a brilliant battle, then seeing some
one enemy lightened and appearing over the battlements, let go the rein to the
horse, running ahead of the whole throng of the army, and having trampled
those found in the way and run in upward, then also having harmed many through
a gate which we set out before, some by that gate itself brought down from on
high, others also by being shut out with those outside, he went up quickly as
if to fight from the tower, and more quickly came down to be enslaved, this
alone being his deed of manhood, that he was not let down more slowly through
a basket, but was almost flung down, as a beast-like bird to pasture; for he
was not, I suppose, as some eagle-like thing to a noble hunt. And a man ought
not, long



having practiced to stretch out his hands womanishly to the pursuers, to slip
into a defense and give toils to those running upon him, lest they rage even
more. But he, his wits damaged, was fleeing 12 as if for a guard above, and
was at once being brought down from there, so that his ignobility might be out
of place and he might not be thought by some to have been taken while
fighting. You went up into the height of the acropolis, vanished, O you; you
took a captivity captive, which even there you betrayed to the enemy. These
things are contrary to what David sings, whose name you bear and have made
useless. And let no one be angry with me here, a small-souled old man, for
recounting such things of a man whose mid-liver I would gladly have, fastening
on it with my teeth, to speak after the Homeric old woman, in return for his
having also brought down the heads of so great a city and loosed the knees of
many men. And may it not be that they are loosed still further, O God the King
and O his servant, our city-guardian, and may the evil not be stretched out to
the longest. And I know that I myself set this man together before the battle
and blessed his things in a time of peace, when the citizens who are dealers
of gain, chattering, pressed upon him as they wished, and we otherwise, as was
fitting, were being fitted. And he himself, turning the friendship back,
showed on our behalf both look and nod and his walking in things to be done.
But now, what are we even to do, having found that man who was to be praised
changing and no longer offering places for good report? Or must one altogether
bear in mind that it is nothing new, just as he, loving us formerly, then in
later things did not love us when we were refuting, as not himself wise
according to the proverb-speaker, so we too then were with him because of what
is holy, because he was not overly bold after the self-willed to harm the
common things, then, seeing destruction from him in advance, to hate him as
cause, and now, being ourselves also destroyed, to wish even some great evil
to be fastened on him, a man who otherwise seemed good, for one must not scare
off the things toward truth, but who was drawn together with a crooked rule,
that of the master Andronicus, and here has fallen out into extreme baseness.
For he neither loved that man, as we shall set out when we come down, nor was
he loved in turn, nor did he come down to any thought of a prize of honor, but
even foresaw a penalty, which things will appear to have ruined us. This then
must be said thus, and that I would never, having set the lovable in front,
put the truth behind, but putting forward the things that have fallen together
into a writing whose virtue is love of truth, I would not, having overshadowed
its light, make it useless. For according to the one who declared rightly, the
man is a friend, but the truth is more a friend. And one must still consider
together also that for those who praise one must pick out as many good things
as there are and somewhere even recolor the base things more in the manner of
advocacy, 14 but for those writing a composed account not so, but one must
handle the words as the truth over each thing demands; where it is not the
season to pick out the things toward blessing by the law of an encomium, but
to hurry to take much care for the memory of those who come after of each
thing itself, which time running has thrown aside. And if this David of ours,
going ahead, was making good the lot of God, let this too be heard, see, but
let it be heard together also that he destroyed it utterly, having sweetened
little and embittered very greatly, in that, having procured some little money
for these or those, he finally fined all into all that was theirs, and
countless even into life. And let no one now bring it in that I have come to
my senses again and at a later time have perceived, as in a moral at the end,
what David turned out to be, namely when it was judged right that he be sent
to the Sicilian together with Maurozomes as first-fruits and as the choice
offering of our destruction. Whom the Harpies ought to have overtaken and so
disposed before he also destroyed us utterly. For I have not now been turned
to mind, but the whole time of his generalship for war the enemies were
pressing us, and I was refuting him, reproaching, showing forth the errors as
they came each time, speaking, even if to no end, what he would have heard
also from others, if they had spoken freely and the city's ill fortune had not
shut their mouths. It has been shown by me sufficiently, I think, that I am
doing nothing new in speaking ill of



David who had changed his mind, whom I had already blessed when he was of
sound mind. And I in this way also, as to the emperor Andronicus, whom when I
come down I would not have it in me to speak well of in the whole, in other
things I wondered at, having said by selection whatever of the better things
shone in him. For I do not write antiphons after the manner of the base among
sophists. But toward this composed history I cannot hide as many things as now
fall in sequence, since the remaining rhetoric, that toward bare praise,
seems, after the wise bee herself, not only from sweet flowers and the rest of
usefulness to put together for herself the perfume-boiling of the sweetness
but somewhere even from things not such. For indeed even from residues not
easy to carry off she stores, picking out what tends to the point, as those
skilled at making hives, having observed, know; and she gathers something from
there too, as far as the combs are to be drawn together and held; of which
sort of things lying under the bees of Hermes both David and Andronicus had,
and most of all Andronicus, that man of every kind and all-various, and this
not after the manner of a chameleon 16 or of an octopus, but rather of
Proteus, and most of all after Empusa, who made terrible things appear. And
the same man was also many-turning after the Euripi, and after the primeval
formless matter he was laid under all forms in many forms, of which some would
be praised, and some would not. And it is possible in his case also to fit
that there was some pity with him, and there was also a great wrath. And it is
possible to bring together also the "all things were together in him," and
that there was with him too some union of things much mixed and a
like-mindedness of those thinking apart, not indeed whence the virtue of
harmony is put together, but a vice of confusion. For the man was a
many-turned thing and much-furnished to those clever at writing on either
side. And one might also be led to liken him to a sphere, not the one taken
over toward generation according to an old marvel-telling, but toward a
variety of characters. And if someone should also say that two jars stand in
him, the one full of goods, the other of evils, from which that man, drawing
and mixing, measured out to those under him, but for the most part from the
jar of evils alone, he would riddle most fittingly; for he was mixed and not
unmixed toward undiluted grace, nor indeed well mixed. But let these things
have been digressed thus in season, I think, and not altogether without
method. And I go up a little to the man who was once, which is far, of some
friendship of David, but now worthy of a world-wide hatred. Quickly you turned
your back, O most noble for fleeing, and more quickly you ran face to face not
into a counter-array, but into enslavement, remaining in the flight only so
far as to show the turning as unmanly, so that no trace of nobility might be
found in you, neither in the siege, which you were even somewhere praying to
be summed up more quickly, as we shall strike upon in what follows, nor after
the capture, in which you belied both the man acting bravely and the one
fleeing, in that, having turned to flee, you gave yourself to those who were
not even pursuing. For other things had been a care to them in their minds,
the things before the work and 18 necessary to them, and of you they would
not, I think, even have had memory, if you had not shown yourself. For those
who are nobly brave know how to make light of a general who is not such, of
which sort I also condemned you, in the things of which they had tried your
silliness. But why, carried aside by the passion and becoming of the things
now and in the eyes, do I lead astray the one who will sometime later hear, so
that he may know in the manner of a composed writing the things of our affairs
from the beginning even to the end, so that following he may know from what
sort of things of old what sort the things now are, and some he may be moved
at, and some he may otherwise wonder at, and some also, if he should wish, he
may learn according to history, which picks out as many things as are useful
to the present coincidences? And why do I not set some head, as upon a body,
over this writing, through which, going down with the discourse, I may step
forward well to where the last is, taking it from there? It appears it was to
be, as it was well pleasing to God, that when the Comnenus emperor Manuel
fell, whatever was upright among the Romans should fall with him, and that, as
when that sun failed, all that is ours should become dim. So he went away
where he ought, leaving a succession of race not such as it ought to have
been. For a child a little past
