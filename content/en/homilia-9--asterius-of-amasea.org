Of Asterius, bishop of Amasea, on the holy martyr of Christ, Phocas
=9.116= The whole roll of the noble martyrs is sacred and divine, having
repaid by suffering the grace for the Passion, and having fulfilled by blood
the recompense of blood to the Savior of all. Yet among these themselves the
glory of all is not one, nor have equal honors been allotted to all by one
measure, but even the roll of the saints does not escape first and second. The
cause of this, I think, is the Judge's extreme exactness and impartiality. For
he looks upon both the magnitudes of the punishments and the stands of
endurance, and, testing the contest, he distributes to the athletes the
rewards according to worth. And it is in no way strange if justice is so
honored by God, when even among us generals and presidents of the games are
not equal toward the champions or the contestants, but they vote for each the
gifts corresponding to the measure of his achievement. I have gone through
these things in order to show that the one who has today given us the occasion
of this gathering is more illustrious than his companions and fellow
contestants. For the others are not all known among all, nor do they have a
proclaimed valor; but there is no one who does not know Phocas. But just as
the ray of the sun is spread over all eyes, so also the fame of this martyr
has sounded round every ear. For as soon as I am in this honored shrine of his
and touch the sacred chest of his body, I am filled from the place with the
memory of all the stories told of him together. I see the gardener by trade,
the artless quality of the =9.118= soul, the hospitality, the outstanding one
of the seacoast, the benefactor of the inland country, and I become a disciple
of a martyr, learning to keep piety even to the last dangers. But come, if it
seems good, leaving aside the common things known to many, I will tell you,
lovers of the martyrs, the outstanding stories of the martyr. Neighboring
Sinope, an ancient and well-known city, rich in noble and philosophical men,
brought forth this holy man, the great benefit of Christians. His occupation
in life was the tilling and care of a garden, which he possessed somewhere
there before the gate of the city at the mouth of the isthmus and worked with
industry, a consolation of life for himself and for those in need. And he
opened very readily to strangers his small and poor hearth. Dwelling by the
highway, he set out his goods as common to those who came that way; and he was
a new Lot, not of Sodomites but of Sinopeans. As time went on he found the
commandment not without wage; for from hospitality he gained the occasion of
the life-giving death. From where and how, listen, as I take it up a little.
When the word of our piety had been proclaimed and the wonderful preaching had
gone out to every ear, making known Christ and the mysteries from him, the
nations raged, according to the psalmist, and the kings and the rulers
gathered together; and the peoples of error were angry because Jesus had begun
to reign. And every Christian was sought as a malefactor, and the one near was
punished and the one far off was tracked down. Therefore neither the cheapness
of the trade nor the gardener hid Phocas, but he too was made known as a
genuine initiate of Christ. And so there came against him those of this
wretched and fleeting life, ordered to lead him away untried, without a
defense. For the charge was piety toward God, a charge which the one who had
done it confessed with a clear voice; and when no one asked, he showed it by
his works, and when someone inquired, he cried out with great joy. So the
executioners of his punishment, who are the cause of our feast, came and
lodged with the very man they were seeking, neither knowing nor being known.
They kept the reason of their coming secret for the time, so that, first being
taught by those outside the city who Phocas was and =9.120= where he lived,
they might suddenly come upon him and seize him, as the Jews once in the
garden with Judas seized the Lord. But they had not noticed that they had the
quarry inside the nets: the dogs the ram, the wolves the lamb; and the kid
rested with the leopards, and the lamb fed with the wolves, and the lions made
a common way of life with the calf. And when, as is wont to happen, table and
familiarity had given them confidence with one another, the martyr inquired of
them who they were and for what reason they had come to his city. And they,
ashamed at the man's ready welcome and hospitality, and charging him to tell
no one what would be said, make known to him the secret and declare the plot
of the conspiracy, and that they are seeking to take Phocas for punishment.
Therefore besides the hospitality they asked him to grant this kindness also,
and to share the labor with them in the hunt for the one sought. The servant
of the Lord heard, and receiving the word with an unshaken soul, he suffered
or uttered nothing low or ignoble; nor, shrinking from the danger, did he look
toward flight, and this though he had much power to do so, because it was not
yet clear that he himself was the one they were seeking; but he did the very
opposite, having chosen rather to stand firm and not to flee. And indeed he
nods to their request: "I too," he says, "will join you in the matter at hand.
For I know the man, and having sought him out I shall easily find him; and I
will show him not after a long time, but tomorrow, even to you yourselves;
only rest at this poor lodging of mine." Having thus guaranteed them a firm
promise, he divides himself between two tasks: the entertainment of his own
murderers and the preparation of the tomb. And having made ready the pit and
the things necessary for burial, he came to them the next day: "I have sought
Phocas out carefully for you," he said, "and the quarry is now ready; and if
it seems good, let him be seized." And when they said in great joy, "And where
is the man?" "Not far," he says, "but with you; I myself am this man for you;
so use the matter and fulfill the purpose of your journey and toil." The men
were struck in their souls and at the =9.122= hearing they froze, ashamed of
the salt and the table and the munificent host in poverty. But he still
exhorted them toward the daring of the killing, and commanded them not to
hesitate even for a little, saying that the murder was not of their hand, but
the attempt of those who had given the order. He spoke, he persuaded, he
suffered, and having his head cut off he was offered as a well-pleasing
sacrifice to God. From that time until now we have this man, O people, as a
pillar and stay of the divine churches of the inhabited world; and he is the
most illustrious of martyrs, himself holding the first place among the best.
This man draws all the people together to his lodging; and the highways are
full of those from every country hastening to the place of prayer. That
magnificent temple, then, which has been allotted also to have the sacred body
of the martyr, is a relief of the afflicted, a healing-place of those who are
ill-treated, a table of those who hunger. For Phocas, now dead, feeds more
abundantly than Joseph formerly did while living in Egypt. For that one
exchanged the grain for silver, but this one grants it freely to those in
need. So tribes of the poor and of wanderers run to the isthmus of Sinope as
to some common treasury. But there it is so. And if anywhere else through
small relics the martyr has established for himself a kind of colony of a
metropolis, this place too is wonderful and eagerly sought by all Christians,
just as this sacred precinct among us is a lodging of those who keep feast.
For precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his holy ones; and the
relics, divided in many places, keep the praise entire everywhere for the
thrice-blessed. So also in the ruling city, the crown of Italy and queen of
the world, there is for the martyr a public escort and honor, and a
distinguished house, adorned for beauty. And the Romans honor Phocas no less
than Peter and Paul. Therefore also, as the account teaches, they eagerly
acquired the head of the martyr, choosing a purpose the reverse of that of the
polluted Herodias. For she, thirsting for just =9.124= blood, demanded a
precious head for insult; but they, for honor and their own benefit, worthily
procured a martyr's head. And sailors and seafarers everywhere, not those who
sail the Euxine sea only, but also those who cut the Adriatic and are borne
over the Aegean and whoever sail the western ocean and go to sea in the
eastern gulfs, have turned the customary chants with which they rest the toil
of the voyage into a new praise of the martyr; and Phocas is wholly sung by
them on the tongue, since he also furnishes clear signs of his help. For he
has often been seen, now by night when a storm was expected, waking the
steersman who had dozed at the tiller; and at another time again stretching
the ropes and tending the canvas and looking out from the prow for the reefs.
Hence it has also become a custom for sailors to have Phocas as a
table-companion. And since it is impossible for the one who is now bodiless to
be a messmate and sharer of the table, learn how a pious calculation has
devised a way for the impossible. For each day they allot to the martyr a
portion of the cooked food in equal share with those who eat. And one of the
diners, buying this portion, lays down the money, and the next day another,
and at another time another; and this lot of the purchase, going round all,
gives each day the buyer of the portion. And when a harbor receives them and
they come to land, the money is divided among the hungry; and this is Phocas's
portion, a benefaction of the poor. Kings also have been struck with awe at
this most enduring and God-loving man, and they adorn with costly treasures
the temple of the renowned poor man. And those who come after strive to outdo
their elders. And it is not yet wonderful if men of the Roman empire, lovers
of piety, brought up with statutes and laws, are so reverent toward the
servant of Christ, when the wonder has reached even to barbarians; and all the
wildest Scythians, whoever inhabit the mainland opposite the Euxine sea,
neighbors of Lake Maeotis and the river Tanais, and whoever dwell at the
Bosporus and stretch as far as the river Phasis, all these escort the
gardener; and though they differ from us in all customs and occupations, they
become of one mind in this alone, the wildness of their manners =9.126= being
tamed by the truth. One ruler, then, from there, even a king, having laid
aside the crown of his head, shining with gold and with flowers of stones, and
having put off the warlike breastplate, which had materials of wealth (for the
panoply of the barbarians is boastful and luxurious), sent both as dedicated
offerings through the martyr to God, consecrating the resources of power and
of dignity. For it is clear that the crown was a thank-offering of the
kingship, and the breastplate of strength in wars. And the benefactions that
continually happen upon these things through visions in dreams and through the
healings of which the sick enjoy, how could anyone even recount them, it being
impossible to let the account run out upon the workings one by one? We then,
stopping here, will offer glory to God the Savior, whose faithful servants
have been counted worthy of such grace that, toward every need and
circumstance, according to the gift given them, they benefit their fellow
servants. And this after the departure from here and the separation from the
body; whose memory, together with the work, runs each day through cities and
villages, proclaiming everywhere the benefactor Lord, to whom belongs all
honor and power, majesty and magnificence, now and always, and unto the ages
of ages. Amen.
