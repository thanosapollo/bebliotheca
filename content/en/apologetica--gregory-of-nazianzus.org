ORATION II.
Of the same, an apologetic on account of the flight to Pontus, and again
of the return from there, after the ordination of the presbyter, in which
what the profession of the priesthood is.
A. I have been defeated, and I confess the defeat; I submitted myself to
the Lord, and I made supplication to him; for let the most blessed David
begin the speech for me; or rather he who spoke in David, and who still
even now speaks through him. Since also the best order for everyone
beginning both a speech and a deed is to begin from God and to rest in
God. But the cause, whether of the first withdrawal and faint-heartedness,
through which I went far away in flight, and lodged apart from you a time
not small perhaps to those who longed for me, or of the present gentleness
and change, through which I have given myself again, bringing myself to
you, let another think and say another thing, of those who either hate or
love us; the one not letting go of charges, the other even adding
approval; for nothing is so sweet to men as to speak the things of others,
and most of all if they happen to be drawn by some goodwill or hatred, by
which the truth is also accustomed to be stolen for the most part. But I
will set the truth in the midst, having been ashamed of nothing, and I
will judge justly for both parts, as many as accuse us, and as many as
defend us eagerly; having in part accused myself, and in part made
defense. =35.409=
B. And that the speech may go forward for us by the way, I will speak
first concerning the cowardice, which is prior; for I do not bear that
some of those who keep all our things carefully, both well and otherwise,
should be struck in me; since God was pleased that our case should be
something to Christians, and those already struck, if indeed there are
any, I will heal through the apology; for it is good that one neither
sinning nor suspected, so far as it is possible and the speech chooses,
should not set a stumbling-block for the many or a scandal; if indeed we
know how inexorable and most heavy the punishment from the unlying one is
for those who scandalize one of the little ones.
C. For I suffered this, O men, neither as uneducated and without
understanding, but rather, that I may even boast a little, nor as
despising the divine laws and ordinances. That just as in a body one part
is ruling and as it were sitting in front, and another is ruled and led,
so also in the Churches God ordained by a law of equality that has what
is according to worth, or also of providence, by which he bound all
things, that some should be shepherded and ruled, as many as this is more
profitable for, being directed by word and deed toward what is needful;
and that others should be shepherds and teachers, toward the perfecting of
the Church, as many as are above the many in virtue and in kinship toward
God, holding the relation of soul to body, or of mind to soul; in order
that both, being composed with one another and blended, the falling-short
and the overflowing, as in members, and being joined and bound by the
harmony of the spirit, may be shown one complete body, and truly worthy of
Christ himself who is our head.
D. I know then that neither for the others is anarchy and disorder more
profitable than order and rule, nor for men; but for these even of all
things not least, by as much as the danger for them is also =35.412= about
greater things; for whom it is a great thing, even if they should not
preserve the first of the speech, to sin nothing, but at least that second
of it, that those who sin should be brought back.


Since this appeared good and just, it is an equal evil to me and likewise
disorderly, both that all should wish to rule, and that no one should
accept it; so that, if all should flee this, whether one must call it a
liturgy or a leadership, the fullness of the Church would limp in the
greatest part, and the fair thing would no longer even remain fair. And
where still, and by whom, would God be worshipped by us in the mystic and
upward-bearing things, which is the greatest of our things and most
honorable, there being no king, nor being a ruler, nor priesthood, nor
sacrifice, nor as many things as, as greatest upon great offenses, the
disobedient of old were condemned?
E. But it is not even some strange thing and out of the way for the many
who philosophize the divine things to go up to ruling from being ruled,
nor outside the accustomed bounds of philosophy, and bringing toward
reproach; just as it is not for a sailor of the well-reputed to take the
prow, and for a lookout who has well watched the winds to be entrusted
with the tillers; or if you wish, for a noble soldier to be a taxiarch,
and for a good taxiarch to be a general and to be entrusted with the
leadership of every war. Nor yet, which perhaps someone of all the absurd
and wretched would suppose, who judge the things of others by their own
passions, was I ashamed of the order of the rank by desire of the greater;
I am not so inexperienced either of the divine greatness, or of human
lowliness, =35.413= as not to think it a great thing for every generated
nature even in any way whatever to draw near to God who alone is most
bright and most brilliant, and who exceeds every material and immaterial
nature in purity.
F. What then is it that I suffered? and what is the reason of my
disobedience? For I did not seem to the many then to remain in myself, nor
to be as I was known, but to have become some other from another, and more
than was well to resist and to be stubborn. Of this then you may already
hear the causes, having long been longing. First of all, having been
struck by the unexpected, as those who are terrified by sudden noises, I
did not hold my reasonings; and because of this I loosed shame, having
been accustomed to it all the time. Then a certain love of the fair thing
of quiet and of withdrawal stole in upon me; of which having become a
lover from the beginning, as I do not know if anyone else of those who
have been eager about speeches, and which I promised to God in the
greatest and most difficult dangers, and having even touched it a little,
so much as to come to be in the forecourts, and for the longing to be
kindled more for me through the trial, I did not bear being tyrannized and
being pushed into the midst of the tumults, and as it were being torn by
force from some sacred asylum of this life.
G. For nothing seemed to me such as, having shut the senses, having become
outside flesh and world, having turned in upon oneself, touching none of
human things, except all necessity, speaking to oneself and to God, to
live above the things seen, and always to bear the divine impressions
pure in oneself, unmixed with the characters below and wandering, being
and always =35.416= becoming truly an unstained mirror of God and of the
divine things, receiving light with light, and a clearer with a dimmer,
already harvesting by hopes the good of the age to come, and to go about
with angels, still being above earth having left the earth, and being set
above by the spirit. If anyone of you is possessed by this love, he knows
what I say, and he will pardon the passion then; for the many I would not
perhaps even persuade by speaking, as many as the thing seems even in
laughter, being badly disposed either by their own folly, or by those
unworthy of the profession; who have put a bad name around a fair thing,
vainglory around philosophy, having taken as fellow-worker envy and the
vice of the many which is readier toward the worse; in order that one
thing at any rate may wholly be sinned by them, either the bad being
worked, or the fair being disbelieved.


H. And besides these I suffered a thing, for I will speak out to you all
the secret, I do not know whether rustic or free, but I suffered it; I was
ashamed on behalf of the others, as many as, being nothing better than the
many, a great thing indeed if not much worse, with unwashed hands, as the
saying is, and with uninitiated souls introduce themselves to the most
holy things; and before they become worthy to approach the sacred things,
they claim the sanctuary, and they crowd and push around the holy table,
as if thinking this rank to be not a type of virtue, but an occasion of
life, nor a liturgy under account, but a rule unexamined. And they are
almost more in number than those over whom they rule; wretched of piety,
and miserable of the brightness; so that they seem to me, as time and the
evil go forward, no longer even to have those whom =35.417= they will
rule, all teaching instead of being taught of God, as the promise says,
and all prophesying, so that Saul also is among the prophets, according to
the old history and proverb. For nothing so much at any time either is, or
has come to be, other things having flourished and ceased at other times,
as now for Christians such reproaches and sins. Of which even if to stop
the rush is greater than according to us, yet at least to hate and to be
ashamed is not the smallest part of piety.
I. And what is last and greater than the things said, for I come already
to the very colophon of the speech, and I will not lie; for it is not
lawful for those who make the speech about such great things; I did not
think it equal, nor do I now think, to rule a flock or a herd, and to
stand over souls of men. For there it is enough to show the herd or the
flock as fattest and most plump; and looking toward this both the
herdsman and the shepherd will inspect the places that have water and
pasture, and will drive in and drive out from pastures and to pastures,
and will rest and move off and call back, a little with the staff, but
the most with the pipe. And there is no other work for the shepherd or the
herdsman except so much as to war a little against the wolves, and
somewhere also to look after something that is sick; but the most will
concern him, oak, and shade, and reeds, and to lie down in a fair place of
the grass, and beside cold water, and =35.420= under the breezes to
improvise a bed, and somewhere also to sing something erotic with the ivy
cup, and to speak to the cattle or to the flock; and of these themselves
to feast or to sell the fattest. But of virtue no one yet has taken
thought of flocks or herds. For what even is virtue of these? or who has
looked to the fair for those before his own pleasure?
J. But for a man, it being hard to know how to be ruled, it is in danger
of being much harder to know how to rule men, and most of all this our
rule, the one in divine law, and leading toward God, of which as great as
is the height and the dignity, so great also is the danger for the one
who has mind. Whom first it will be necessary, just as silver or gold,
being turned from every side, and in all kinds of times and affairs, to
sound nowhere base or copper-mixed, nor to bear in himself any of the
worse matter, and of a hotter fire's worth; or the evil will be so much
greater, by as much as he would rule more; if indeed also greater than the
wickedness that stands about one is that which travels into many.
K. For neither does a fabric so easily take a deep dye, nor the thing that
has drawn near a stench or the contrary, nor does some sick vapor so
readily pour itself into the air, and through the air converse with the
animals, which is a plague and is so named, as the subject is accustomed
most quickly to be filled with the vice of the one who stands in front,
and much more easily than with the contrary, virtue. For this is what
wickedness most of all has as an advantage over nobility, and what I most
of all am distressed at when I consider, that wickedness is a thing easy
to emulate and ready to hand, and nothing is so easy as


to become bad, even if no one should happen to lead us toward this; but
the acquisition of the good is rare and uphill, even if there should be
much that draws aside and calls forth. And this is what the most =35.421=
blessed Haggai also seems to me to have considered and to have come to
that wonderful and most true image: Ask, priests, the law, saying; If holy
flesh in the garment of some food, or drink, or vessel, having touched,
will at once sanctify the thing that has drawn near; and when they denied:
Ask again if also by uncleanness something of these having consorted, it
did not immediately take a share of the pollution; as they would say that
it takes a share, and does not remain clean by the communion.
L. Saying what is this? which my speech is; that the good is hard to
catch for human nature, just as fire also for the wetter matter; but most
are ready and fitted toward the participation of evil, some straw toward
a spark, I think, and a wind, easily kindled and consumed through
dryness; for one would more quickly take a share of a little vice richly
than of deep virtue little by little. Since also a little wormwood most
quickly imparts its bitterness to honey; but honey does not even double to
wormwood of its own sweetness; and a small pebble having been drawn from
under would drag a whole river toward the slope; but to hold or to beat
back even the strongest dam would scarcely be able.
M. First then this, of the things we said, is worthy to beware, lest we
appear bad painters of the wonderful virtue, or rather perhaps of
painters not poor, a poor archetype of the many; =35.424= or lest we run
not far from the proverb, attempting to heal others while ourselves
abounding in sores.
N. And second, even if someone should keep himself pure from every sin, or
as much as possible, I do not know if even this is sufficient for the one
who is going to educate the others toward virtue; for the one entrusted
with this must not only not be bad, for this even of those under hand is
for the many most shameful, but also differing by the good, according to
the commandment that orders to turn away from evil and to do good; nor
only to wipe out the poor types of the soul, but also to inscribe the
better, so as to excel more in virtue than as much as he exceeds in
dignity; and to know no measure of the fair and of the ascent, nor to
think the thing mastered a gain rather than the thing that escapes a
loss; but always to make the thing under foot an approach of the next; and
not to think it a great thing if we differ from the many, but a loss if we
fall short of the worth; and to measure the thing accomplished by the
commandment, but not by the neighbors, whether they are bad, or have come
to some amount of virtue; and not with small weights to weigh virtue,
which is owed to the greatest, and from whom are all things, and toward
whom are all things.
O. Nor to think that the same things fit all, just as neither are ages
the same for all, nor characters of faces, nor natures of animals, nor
qualities of earth, nor beauties and sizes of lamps; but to hold that it
is a vice of a private person to do poor things, and as many as are worthy
of punishment, of which the law also is a heavy master; but of a ruler or
one who stands in front, not to be as best, and always going forward by
the fair, if indeed he is going to draw the many by the excess of virtue
into the moderate, and not to rule by force, but to lead by persuasion.
For the involuntary, toward being tyrannical, is also not praiseworthy,
nor lasting; for the thing forced is accustomed, just as a plant by force
=35.425= being transplanted by hands, when let go, to run back again into
itself; but the thing from choice is both most lawful at once and most
safe, being kept by a bond of goodwill. Whence also most of all to
shepherd the flock willingly, but not by compulsion, our law and
lawgiver command.
P. But let there be someone neither bad, and having come to the peak of
virtue; I do not see what knowledge he has taken, or trusting in what
power, he would dare this


presidency; for in truth this seems to me to be some art of arts, and
science of sciences, to lead man, the most many-turned animal and most
variegated. And one would know this by comparing the healing of souls with
the therapy of bodies; and having learned how laborious that is, and
having further examined how much more laborious ours is, and more
honorable both by the nature of the matter, and by the power of the
science, and by the end of the working. For the one labors about bodies,
and the perishable matter that flows below, which will wholly be dissolved
and will suffer its own, even if now by the alliance of the art it
overpowers the faction in it; for either disease or time has dissolved it,
having yielded to nature, and not overstepping its own bounds.
Q. But the zeal of that about soul is for that which is from God and
divine, and which shares in the nobility from above, and which is eager
toward that, even if it has been bound with the worse; perhaps also
through other causes, which God who bound them alone knows, and if anyone
from God has been made wise in such mysteries; but so far as I know and
those according to me, for two reasons; one, in order that through
contest and struggle against the things below it may inherit the glory
above, having been tested by the things here as gold by fire, and may have
the things hoped for as a prize of virtue, and not only as a gift of God;
and this then was of the extreme goodness, to make the good =35.428= also
ours, not only being sown by nature, but also being farmed by choice, and
by the movements of self-rule toward both; and another, so that it may
also draw the worse toward itself and set it above, having loosed it
little by little from the thickness; in order that, what God is to soul,
this soul may become to body, having trained through itself the servant
matter, and having made akin to God the fellow-slave.
R. Places, and times, and ages, and seasons, and such things the physician
will inspect; and he will give drugs and diet, and he will watch the
harmful things, so that the desires of the sickness may not oppose the
art; and somewhere also with burnings, and cuttings, and the more austere
things of the therapy, there is when he will use them even upon those for
whom; of which nothing is yet so great, even if they appear very toilsome
and hard, as to spy out and to heal habits, and passions, and lives, and
choices, and if there is any other such of the things in us, and having
banished from our pairing all that is beastlike and wild, to bring in
instead and to confirm all that is tame and dear to God, and to award
justly to soul and to body; not having allowed the better to be dominated
by the worse, which is the greatest of injustices; but having subjected to
the ruling and leading part that which is second by nature; just as indeed
a divine law, and holding most fairly upon all his creation, both as much
as is visible, and as much as is above sense.
S. And I consider that also, that each of those things which I numbered
as being watched by the therapist according as it has of nature, so
remains and contrives nothing against itself knavishly, nor plays the
sophist against the things brought by the art; but the healing rather even
sets the matter around; except if somewhere some small disorder of the
one who is ill should fall in, which it is not hard both to guard and to
beat back. But for us intelligence and self-love, and to be easily
conquered neither to know nor to bear, is a greatest hindrance toward
virtue, and as it were some array =35.429= comes to be against those who
fight with us; and as much zeal as one ought to bring in to lay bare the
disease to those who heal, so much we bring in so as to flee the healing,
and we are brave against ourselves, and skilled against our health.
T. For either we have stolen the sin slavishly, covering as some festering
and malignant passion in the depth of the soul, as if we will escape even
the great eye of God and of justice, if we escape men; or we allege
pretexts in sins, finding advocate speeches for the passions; or the


ears having blocked in the manner of a deaf asp that stops its ears, we
are contentious not to hear the voice of those who charm, nor to be
drugged with the drugs of wisdom, by which sickness of soul is healed; or
last, those of us who are bolder and more noble, we even openly are
shameless toward both the sin and those who heal it, with uncovered head,
as the saying is, going toward every lawlessness; O the madness, or if
there is any other more proper name for such a passion! and those whom we
ought to love as benefactors, these we ward off as enemies, hating those
who reprove in the gates, and abominating a holy speech; and we think we
will more war against those well-disposed to us, if we most of all do
ourselves badly, as those who touch their own flesh thinking they consume
the things of the neighbors.
U. These are the things by which I set our medicine far more laborious
than that about bodies, and because of this more honorable; and that for
that, spying out few of the things in the depth, the greater of the
business is about the appearing; but for us the whole therapy and zeal is
about the hidden man of the heart, and the battle is toward the one who
wars against us from within and wrestles against us, who using ourselves
as weapons against us, the most terrible thing, gives us to the death of
sin. Toward these things then there is need of much and complete faith, and
of a greater cooperation from God, and of not a little of our
counter-artifice (as at least I =35.432= persuade myself), that which is
seen both in word and in deed, if we are to be well healed and thoroughly
cleansed, and if the most honorable of the things we have, the souls, are
to be of most worth.
V. And as to the ends of both therapies, for this still remains for us
toward the examination, for the one, health, or good condition of flesh,
either to guard it being, or to call it back having gone, of which it is
not yet clear if it will be of any profit to those who have acquired it;
since even the contraries have often benefited those who have them more,
as poverties and riches, glories and lack of glory, lowlinesses and
brightnesses, and as many things as lie in the middle according to nature,
and incline nothing more this way or that, take the better or the worse
by the use and the choice of those who have acquired them; but for the
other the thing set forth is to wing a soul, to snatch it from the world,
and to give it to God, and either to keep the thing according to the
image remaining, or to lead by the hand that which is in danger, or to
save that which has flowed away, and to house Christ in the hearts through
the Spirit; and the chief point, to make God, and of the blessedness
above, the one of the array above.
W. This the pedagogue law wishes for us; this the prophets between Christ
and law; this the perfecter of the spiritual law and the end, Christ; this
the emptied godhead; this the flesh taken on; this the new mixture, God
and man, one from both, and through one both. Because of this God was
blended with flesh through a middle soul =35.433= and the things standing
apart were bound by the kinship of the mediator toward both; and all
things on behalf of all came into one, and on behalf of one, the
forefather; the soul because of the one that disobeyed, the flesh because
of the one that served and was condemned with it; the one a soul, the
other flesh; Christ because of Adam who came under sin, the one better
than sin and higher.
X. Because of this the new was brought in instead of the old; and through
suffering the one who suffered was called back, and on behalf of each of
our things each of that which is above us was given in exchange; and the
economy about the one who fell through disobedience from love of man has
become a new mystery. Because of this a birth and a Virgin; because of
this a manger and Bethlehem; the birth on behalf of the formation; the
Virgin on behalf of the woman; Bethlehem because of Eden; the manger
because of paradise; the small and appearing things on behalf of the great
and hidden. Because of this angels glorifying the heavenly, then earthly;
and shepherds seeing glory upon the lamb and shepherd; and a star leading,
and magi


falling down and bringing gifts, in order that idolatry may be dissolved.
Because of this Jesus being baptized, and being witnessed from above, and
fasting, and being tempted, and conquering the one who conquered. Because
of this demons being driven, and diseases being healed, and the great
proclamation being entrusted to small ones and being accomplished.
Y. Because of this nations raging, and peoples meditating empty things;
because of this a tree against the tree, and against the hand hands, those
nobly stretched of the one stretched without self-control; of the one let
loose those bound with the nails; of the one that cast Adam out those that
make the ends akin. Because of this height against the fall; and gall
against the tasting, and a crown of thorns against the wicked rule, and
death against death, and darkness on behalf of the light, =35.436= and
burial against the turning back to earth, and resurrection on behalf of
the resurrection. All these things were some education of God about us,
and a healing of our weakness, leading the old Adam back from where he
fell, and bringing him to the tree of life, from which the tree of
knowledge, not in season, nor fittingly having been partaken, alienated
us.
Z. Of this therapy we are servants and fellow-workers, as many as sit in
front of the others; for whom it is a great thing both to know and to heal
their own passions and sicknesses; or rather not yet a great thing, except
that the vice of the many, of those who are upon this rank, has made us
say this; but much greater is to be able to heal the things of the others
and to cleanse them through scientifically, and as would be profitable for
both, those who need the therapy and those who have been entrusted to
heal.
AA. Then the therapists of bodies will have both toils and sleeplessness
and cares, which we know; and to harvest their own griefs upon others'
misfortunes, as one of the wise among them said; and some things
themselves laboring and finding out, and others collecting from others
and contributing they will bring to those in need; and nothing so small
for them either found or having escaped, not even of the least, as not to
be supposed great toward the turn of health, or of the danger the
contrary; and these things on behalf of what? in order that a man may live
more the days upon earth, and this one not even of the reasonable perhaps,
but even of the most wretched, for whom to have died long ago was perhaps
better, being bad, in order that he may be released from the greatest of
sicknesses, vice; and if we should set him even of the fair, for how long
will he live? the whole time? or what will he gain of the life here, of
which to seek to be dissolved is of the fair things =35.437= the first
and most safe, and of a man truly healthy and having mind?
AB. But for us, for whom the thing in danger is salvation of a soul, both
blessed and immortal, and to be punished immortally, or to be praised,
through vice or virtue, how great must the contest be thought to be, or of
how much science is there need to heal well, or to be healed, and to
transfer the life, and to give the dust to the spirit? For it is not of
the same words nor impulses, neither the female to the male, nor youth to
old age, nor wealth to poverty, nor one in good cheer to one despondent,
nor the one who is ill to the one who is healthy, both rulers and ruled,
both wise and unlearned, both cowards and bold, angry and meek,
accomplishing and falling.
AC. And if you still examine accurately, how great is the middle of those
in pairings toward the unmarried, and among these again of those of the
desert toward the social and mixed; of those examined and having gone
through in contemplation toward those who simply go straight, of city
people again and of those from the country, of the more simple and of the
more knavish, of those in affairs toward those who are quiet, of those
struck by change toward those who run well and are unlearned of the worse;
for each of these differ more from one another sometimes in the desires
and the impulses than according to the forms of the bodies; if


you wish, the mixtures and blendings of the elements, from which we have
been composed, have not an easy economy.
AD. But just as for bodies not the same drugging and food are brought,
but others another, either being in good condition or being ill, so also
the souls are healed by a different speech and leading. And witnesses of
the therapy are those whose passions also; some =35.440= a speech leads,
and others are rhythmized by example; some need goads, and others a bridle.
For some are sluggish, and hard to move toward the fair, whom one must
wake by the blow of the speech; and others hotter than the moderate in the
spirit, and hard to hold in their impulses, just as noble colts running
far from the turning-post, whom the speech would make better by checking
and beating back.
AE. Some praise has benefited, and others blame, both with the season; or
the contrary has harmed outside the season and the reason. Some exhortation
sets right, and others rebuke; and this, some being convicted in the
common, and others being admonished secretly. For some are accustomed to
despise the private admonitions, being sobered by the condemnation of a
multitude; and others to be shameless toward the freedom of the rebukes,
being trained by the mystery of the rebuke, and giving in return for the
sympathy the easy persuasion.
AF. Of some it is necessary to keep all things carefully even as far as
the smallest, as many as the thinking to escape (since they contrive this)
has puffed as wiser; and of others there are things which it is better
even to overlook, so as seeing not to see, and hearing not to hear,
according to the proverb, in order that we may not provoke them toward
desperation, drowning them by the labor-loving of the rebukes, and at last
make them bold toward all things, having dissolved shame, the drug of
persuasion. And indeed one must also be angry at some, not being angry; and
overlook, not overlooking; and despair, not despairing, of as many as this
nature seeks. And others one must heal by fairness and lowliness, and by
sharing eagerness about the more useful hopes. And some =35.441= to
conquer, and of others to be defeated often is more profitable; and of
some abundance and power, of others poverty or ill fortune, either to
praise or to pray against.
AG. For it is not as it holds upon virtue and vice, the one to be fairest
and most beneficial always and for all, and the other worst and most

AK. For there being three of the sicknesses now about theology, atheism,
and Judaism, and polytheism, of which of the one Sabellius the Libyan

corrupted for us, being Father of a true God and equal in honor in nature;
nor bringing in three principles against one another or arranging them,

AL. It being necessary neither thus to be some father-lovers, as even to

AN. For as having the contest about God and the greatest of the things

AO. And what would one say about those who through vainglory or love of

AP. Or as many as bringing no supposition from home, nor any type of the

unreason!) they are distressed equally toward every speech, and they

AQ. And because of this it is easier just now to stamp truth upon a soul,
just as wax not yet marked, than to write upon writings, I mean wicked

confused and disordered with the former. For it is better to walk a road

speech has let the more pass, in order that it may not be more excessive
than what is needful.
AR. Just as then if someone should attempt to lead and to tame a beast
composed of many beasts, many-formed and many-shaped, both of greater and
smaller, both tamer and wilder, it would wholly be a work for him, and not
a small contest, thus to stand over an uneven and strange nature, neither
with the same voices, nor foods, nor touches of hand, nor hissings, nor
with the manners of the other leading, each of the beasts rejoicing, and
others being pleased or burdened with other things, as the nature of each
and the custom; and what would the overseer of such a beast need to do?
what else at least than to be many-formed and variegated in the science?
and to bring to each the corresponding therapy, so that the beast may be
led well by him and be saved? thus of this common body of the Church being
composed of many and different both habits and speeches, just as of one
composite and unlike animal, there is every necessity that the president
also be both simple =35.453= the same according to the rightness in all
things; and as much as possible of every kind and variegated according to
the kinship toward each, and the fitness of converse toward all both
suitable and serviceable.
AS. For some need to be fed with milk, with the simpler and more

AZ. But since we have remembered Paul and those according to that one,

BA. I let go to say the toils, the sleeplessness, the fears, the
sufferings in hunger and thirst, those in cold and in nakedness, those
plotting from outside, those acting against from within. I let go the
persecutions, the councils, the prisons, the bonds, the accusers, the

the beatings with rods, the circuit, the dangers according to land, those
according to sea, the deep, the shipwrecks, dangers of rivers, dangers of
robbers, dangers from race, dangers in false brothers, the life from
hands, the Gospel without expense, as he was a theatre both to angels and
to men, standing in the middle of God and men; on behalf of the one
contending, and to the other bringing and making akin =35.464= a peculiar
people, besides the things without. Of these who would worthily go through
the daily overseeing, the care of those according to each, the anxiety of
all the Churches, the sympathy toward all and brother-loving? Someone
stumbled, and Paul was weak; and another was scandalized, and Paul was the
one burning.
BB. And the labor-loving of the teaching? and the variegated of the
therapy? and the love of man? and the austere again? and the mixture and
blending from both? so as neither to soften by kindness, nor to roughen by
harshness. He legislates for slaves and masters, rulers and ruled, men and
women, parents and children, marriage and unmarriedness, self-control and
luxury, wisdom and lack of learning, circumcision and


uncircumcision, Christ and world, flesh and spirit. On behalf of some he
gives thanks, and others he takes hold of. Some he names joy and crown,

shares eagerness as they cut straight, and others he beats back as they
travel badly. Now he separates, now he confirms love; now he laments; now
he is glad; now he gives milk to drink, now he touches mysteries; now he
comes down with, now he lifts together with himself; now he threatens a
rod, now he holds out a spirit of meekness; now he is lifted with the

the others. =35.465=
BC. He boasts in the weaknesses and the afflictions; as with some other

the more moderate, he accepts to suffer something on behalf of them, and
as impious, first from Christ, only if these should be saved.

CZ. And third what is also greatest, and having said this still, I will be

standing as far as the pleasure. These things are myths of those who play

soul with the clever of the fictions and the greedy of the expressions.
DA. But we, who draw the accuracy even as far as the chance tittle and

DB. What then is the history and from where the counsel? for it is perhaps
not worse for the sake of safety of the many to go through. Jonah also was
fleeing from the face of God, or rather he thought to flee; but he was
caught by sea, and storm, and lot, and belly of a sea-monster, and a
three-day burial, having a type of a greater mystery. But that one, in
order that he might not announce the gloomy and absurd message to the
Ninevites, and after this be caught a liar, the city having been saved by
the repentance; for he was not distressed at the salvation of the wicked,
but he was ashamed of the ministry of the falsehood, and as it were was
jealous of the trustworthiness of the prophecy, which there was a danger
of being dissolved in him, the many not being able to see together the
depth of the economy of God about these things.
DC. And as I heard some wise man about these things, not absurdly helping

threat according to his great wisdom, and according to his unsearchable

he understood, not to follow God, who through whatever manner he wished

DD. But Jonah knew this more than anyone, says the one who spoke the
speech, and I am persuaded, toward what the proclamation was bearing for
the Ninevites, and that, counseling the running-away, he was changing
place, but he was not fleeing God; for neither is any other of men, not

been lightened with wings, if there is some device, and having run up into
air, not having consorted with the lowest of Hades, not having put around

unfleeable and most unfightable, God, having wished to hold and to take
under hand, outstrips the quick, trips the intelligent, overturns the
strong, contracts the lofty, tames boldness, presses power.
DE. Jonah then was not ignorant of the mighty hand of God, he who was
threatening this to the others, nor was he thinking as going to flee the
divine into all, let us not believe this; but since he saw the falling-out
of Israel, and perceived the prophetic grace passing over into the
nations, because of this he withdraws from the proclamation, and postpones
the command; and having let go the lookout of the joy, for this Joppa is
able for Hebrews, I mean the old height and the dignity, he threw himself
into the sea of the grief; and because of this he is stormed, and he
sleeps, and he is shipwrecked, and he is awakened, and he is allotted, and
he confesses the flight, and he is sunk, and he is swallowed by the
sea-monster, but he is not consumed; but there he calls upon God, and, the
wonder, he is given out together three-day with Christ. But the speech
about this let it wait, a little later, if God should grant, going to be

DG. For if, what alone is strong in these things to say, someone should

DH. Because of this I was weary in the reasonings seeking out what is
needful, and standing in the middle of two fears, both of the one dragging
down and of the one leading up. And having been at a loss in these for the
most, and having weighed myself from both, or just as a stream by unstable
winds, having been leaned this way and that, at last I became of the more
vehement, and having conquered me, the fear of the disobedience has me,
having dragged me aside. And consider how rightly and justly I judge the
fears, neither to desire the presidency not being given, nor to push away
that which is being given. For the one is of the bold, and the other of
the disobedient, and both of the uneducated. And I am some middle of both
the overly daring and of the very cowardly; more cowardly than those who
leap upon all, and more courageous than those who flee all. Thus I know
about these things.

DJ. But for I am carried again upon the history, and looking up the most
well-reputed of the old, I find as many as ever grace put forward into
overseeing or prophecy, some having yielded eagerly to the call, and
others having postponed the gift; and of neither is blameworthy, neither
the cowardice of those who withdrew, nor the eagerness of those who
started. For the one revered the greatness of the ministry; and the others
having trusted the one who calls followed. Aaron was eager; but Moses
fought off; Isaiah obeyed readily; but Jeremiah feared the youth, and did
not first dare the prophecy, until he took from God both a promise and a
power greater than the age.
DK. With these reasonings I charm myself, and little by little my soul

have entrusted all my life. Because of this I do not disobey, nor do I
speak against, says my Master, not being called upon presidency, but as a
sheep being led upon slaughter; but I even fall before and I humble myself
under the mighty hand of God, and I ask pardon of the former idleness and
disobedience, if this is some charge for me. I was silent, but I will not
always be silent. I withdrew some little, as much as to inspect myself, and
=35.513= to give consolation to the grief; but now I have accepted to
