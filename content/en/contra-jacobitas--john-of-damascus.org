11 For if there is no nature without hypostasis, nor indeed an essence without
person, nor again a hypostasis and person without essence (for there is not),
yet essence and hypostasis are not the same, nor nature and person. For
essence and what is in-essence are not the same, nor what is enhypostatic and
hypostasis. For what is in something is other, and that in which it is is
other; for what is in-essence is what is contemplated in the essence, that is
the aggregate of accidents, which makes plain the hypostasis, not the essence
itself. But the enhypostatic is not the hypostasis, but what is seen in a
hypostasis. And essence is that which exists in any way, whether by itself or
with another or in another; by itself as the essence of fire, with others as
soul and body (for these have the hypostasis with one another), in another as
fire in a wick and as the flesh of the Lord in his beginningless hypostasis.
For the hypostasis makes plain the someone or this that is shown at once with
the stretching of the finger, but the enhypostatic makes plain the essence.
And the hypostasis defines a person by the characteristic properties, but the


enhypostatic makes plain that it is not itself an accident, which has its
existence in another. For if what is in something and that in which it is are
the same, it is time for you to say that virtue and the virtuous are the same,
and by the reasonable conversion that vice and that in which vice is are the
same, and according to you the devil himself will be vice and his maker a
maker of vice, and, since the accident is in the essence, the accident will be
essence and the essence accident, or since the body is ensouled, I mean the
human, according to you the body will be soul. And who will bear the madness
of this confusion?

12 That there is no essence without hypostasis, we know clearly, but we do not
say that enhypostatic and hypostasis are the same, nor indeed essence and what
is in-essence; but we call the hypostasis in-essence, and the essence
enhypostatic. For we know the essence of the holy Godhead to be enhypostatic
(for it is in the three hypostases) and each of the hypostases likewise
in-essence (for they are completed in the essence of the holy Godhead). And in
the ineffable economy of the Lord that lies above all mind we say the
hypostasis is in-essence as being completed in the essences from which it is
also composed, and each of his essences enhypostatic; for they have his one
hypostasis in common, his Godhead eternally, as also that of the Father and of
the Spirit, but his ensouled and rational flesh, that is to say his humanity,
having recently come to subsist in it and having been allotted that very
hypostasis. Thus neither of the natures of Christ is without hypostasis, nor
is each by itself a hypostasis or possesses a hypostasis of its own and apart,
but both have the same and one.

13 These things having been thus predefined, let us come to the question. And
this is, whether Christ is two natures and in two natures or one, as you
fabulously claim. Therefore one who syllogizes about something must make the
principles or the premises from common and true notions (and I mean by common

14 It has been confessed then by all the holy fathers that the union has come
to be from Godhead and humanity and that Christ is perfect in Godhead and the
same perfect and lacking nothing in humanity. Tell us then: Do these things
seem so to you also? You will confess together, as at least it seems to me.
And what is Godhead and what is humanity, essence or accident, nature or
hypostasis? I think you will say essence and nature. For that it is not an
accident is clear to everyone; and that it is not a hypostasis has been
sufficiently shown in what went before. For if the name of Godhead and that of
humanity signify hypostasis, it is time for you to say also three Godheads
because of the triadic number of the hypostases and infinite humanities
because of the infinite number of the hypostases, or a hypostasis in
hypostases, which no one who is wise, but even one very crooked in mind, has
ever dared to say. Each name then is significant of nature, that of Godhead
namely and that of humanity. Is Godhead then and humanity one nature, not I
mean in the case of the Lord, but absolutely? No, one not mad will say; for
the created is not the same in essence with the uncreated and the creator, nor
the body with the bodiless, nor what has begun with the beginningless. Another
nature then is that of the Godhead and another that of the humanity, and they
are two natures. Being then from Godhead and humanity, Christ is from two
natures. If then you say Christ from Godhead and humanity and from two
natures, tell us: Are these two, that is Godhead and humanity, in Christ or
not? You will say yes, if you do not choose to be altogether impious. How then
do you not two natures in Christ


say? For if in saying Christ from Godhead and humanity you confess him from
two natures, and among you "from Godhead and humanity" has equal force with
"from two natures," how, saying Christ Godhead and humanity even after the
union and in Godhead and humanity, will you not confess him two natures and in
two natures?

15 If Godhead and humanity are not the same as in natural quality, but other
and other, different and different, let everyone of sound mind consider
whether the other according to nature and the other are one according to
essence and not two, but if two and not one, natures namely; for the "other"
makes plain a nature, as those wise about these things have distinguished. And
is nature and nature one or two? Two, clearly; for one and one, even to
infants it is clear, are two.

16 And if you say that the things united are one; but hear that the things
united are not one in every respect, but insofar as they have been united. For
if the things united are one in every respect, since man is composed from
unlike natures, it is necessary to say that soul and body are one in every
respect, and the bodiless will be body, and the body bodiless; and since
Godhead and humanity have been united, they will be one according to the
essential difference, and either the divine will be created or the human
uncreated.

17 If the things united are one in every respect, and the body is composed
from opposite qualities, we shall say that heat and cold are the same and
dryness and wetness likewise the same, and Peter and John, since they have
been united by nature, therefore the two are also one in hypostasis. But it
does not seem so to those of sound mind and to the truth; rather the things
united, insofar as they have been united, are one; Peter and John, since they
have been united according to nature, being consubstantial, are one according
to nature, but soul and body, since they have been united according to
hypostasis, are one according to hypostasis. For no one sober in reasoning
would say that soul and body are one according to nature.

18 If the union has come to be from two natures, they were two either before
the union or in the union or after the union. And if they subsisted before the
union, why do we blame Nestorius? And if the flesh has also existed after the
union, why do you blame us? And if you say in the union, and the union
remains, both now and unto the age undivided, then now unto the age they are
two and will be. And if you say the one at the time of the Word's becoming
flesh is in the union, before they were united, were they two or having been
united? For if both existed before the union, it is necessary to say the union
is from two hypostases; and if you say two in the union, and number according
to you altogether introduces division, the union is a cause of division, and
in the union you divide the union.

19 If each of the things from which Christ is keeps even in the union its own
definition and account of the essence, and the definition of Christ's Godhead
is other and that of his humanity other, why do you call the things not ranked
under one and the same definition one nature and not two?

20 If according to you the flesh of God the Word is consubstantial with his
uncreated Godhead, you are sick with co-essentialization; and why do you not
break forth with what has long been in labor among you? And if it is not
consubstantial, how would things not consubstantial be of one nature, or how
would things of one nature not also be consubstantial? For if we are not
drunk, the definition of consubstantiality is the things that have the same
essence.

21 If Christ is consubstantial with the Father and consubstantial with us
according to the same nature, we too are in every way consubstantial with the
Father; for the things consubstantial with the same according to the same
nature are also consubstantial with one another. And if he is consubstantial
with the Father according to the Godhead, but with us according to the
humanity, tell, are Godhead and humanity one nature? But if they are not one,
they are altogether two and Christ is two natures and in two natures; and if
one, how do they differ? For things that differ differ either essentially or


accidentally; but if accidentally, they would be hypostases and not natures;
and if essentially, natures. For the things that differ are at least two; for
the one, insofar as it is one, does not differ. For difference knows how to
bring in number of necessity.

22 Things consubstantial admit the same account. If then Christ is one
incarnate nature and this, as you say, is the definition of his essence, and
he is consubstantial with the Father and consubstantial with us, then the
Father also and we shall be one incarnate nature of Godhead.

23 Difference introduces at least two things that differ, either natures or
hypostases, or one hypostasis differing from itself. And if natures,
essentially; and if hypostases, accidentally; and if one hypostasis, being
altered and changed according to the separable accident. Choose therefore one
of these; either say Christ two hypostases after the union, or that his
Godhead differs from his humanity according to the separable accident, and the
uncreated and beginningless and impassible and almighty and such things, and
likewise also the created and what has begun and the passible and such things,
will be accidents, and these separable and changeable and alterable, the
things from which Christ is, or with us and the truth proclaim Christ two
natures, whose difference you also confess. For saying a difference of
essences you will be forced to say also of essences that are and are
preserved; for of what is not there would not even be a difference. For how do
the things not preserved differ? And if they are truly preserved, let them
also be numbered.

24 And if you say that from two natures one composite nature has come to be,
tell us: How are the things composed naturally composed? For one composite
nature is constituted from different natures when, natures being united, the
thing completed comes to be something other besides the things united, and is
thought and said to be neither this properly nor that, but other, as from the
four elements, I mean fire and air and earth and water, a body would be
constituted, the four being united namely and mixed, and the thing completed
neither is nor is called fire nor air nor water nor earth, but from these
something other besides these; likewise from horse and ass a mule neither is
nor is called horse nor ass, but from these something other besides these. But
our Lord Jesus Christ, being from Godhead and humanity, is in Godhead and
perfect in humanity, and is and is called God and man, and whole God and whole
man, which is not to be found in the case of a composite nature; for the whole
body is not fire, nor is the whole man soul, nor is the mule wholly horse.
Therefore Christ is not one composite nature, but one composite hypostasis,
not other from others, but the same from others; for from Godhead and humanity
he is God and perfect man, two natures however in one composite hypostasis and
in two natures one composite hypostasis.

25 If Christ is of the same nature and consubstantial with the Father who is
of a simple nature, and he also has a composite nature, he will have two
natures, one simple and one composite.

26 If the Godhead of Christ is uncreated and beginningless, but his humanity
created and having begun, how will the beginningless and what has begun be of
one nature?

27 If Christ is one composite nature, and the simple and the composite are not
consubstantial, and the Father is of a simple nature, Christ is not
consubstantial with the Father.

28 If Christ is one composite nature from Godhead and humanity, and his mother
is not from Godhead and humanity, he is not consubstantial with the mother
either; and you have moved everlasting bounds, which our fathers set, having
said Christ consubstantial with the Father according to the Godhead and the
same consubstantial with us according to the


humanity.

29 And if you say the natures of Christ according to conception and
contemplation, tell us, you inventors and contemplators of the natures: Is
conception of true and existing things, as it were articulating and
distinguishing by thought the things that seem simple to sense, as in the case
of a man recognizing together with the appearing body also the invisible soul,
or a fiction of the mind according to a combination of sense and imagination
composing and fashioning from the things that are the things that in no way
are, as the myth-making of the hippocentaurs and goat-stags (it signifies a
thing then), or is it a mere and meaningless sound like skindapsos and
blityri? If then the second, the mystery of the economy is fantasy and
trickery; and if the first, and you contemplate the two natures according to
conception (and contemplation is of things that are), why do you not number
the things that are, seeing that the holy fathers said not the natures, but
the standing-apart of these according to conception? "For when the natures,"
he says, "stand apart by the conceptions." If then the natures stand apart by
conception, it is clear that what is by conception is not in act nor in
existence; for if what is by conception is in existence, and the Father said
the standing-apart by conception, he says the standing-apart in act and in
existence, which is impossible. You therefore, saying the Godhead and the
humanity in Christ after the union by conception, confess these neither in act
nor in fact. Therefore according to you he will be neither God in truth and
existence nor man, but something other besides these.

30 And again, if you say the two natures of Christ by contemplation, and
contemplation is about the things that are, as those skilled in these things
have distinguished, the natures of Christ also altogether are; and if they
are, why do you flee the number as some bugbear?

31 And if you say that he who says two natures divides the economy and says
two Christs, then also he who says the union from two natures will say the
union also from two Christs.

32 If to say two natures divides Christ, and you say two natures in the union,
in the union you divide the union and according to you the union will be
division.

33 Nature and nature will never be said of one nature, but perhaps of one
hypostasis, nor hypostasis and hypostasis of one hypostasis, but of one
nature. Saying then the things from which Christ is of one nature, you would
confess these hypostases and not natures. And if preferring the true to the
false you said the things from which Christ is after the union of one
hypostasis, you would confess these natures.

34 If you confess the difference of the natures even after the union, how,
speaking of the natures in the plural, do you not confess two natures? For if
according to you there is one composite nature after the union, you ought to
say not a difference of natures, but a difference of one nature toward itself,
which is yours to say and think, not ours; for the same nature will never have
an essential difference toward itself.

35 If there is one nature of Christ, tell us the name of this; for what is
must also be named and named properly, as the nature of God is Godhead and of
man humanity. How then shall we name the nature of Christ? Christ-hood? But
none of the God-inspired has said this. But Godhead-humanity? But not this
either. Why then do you bring in to us a nameless nature? And if you say that
natures are innovated and that by the strangeness of the mystery something
strange comes to be, yet nothing is more incomprehensible or higher than the
nature of God, and nevertheless the only incomprehensible has endured to be
named by us. Either then say the nature drafted by you higher and better than
the Godhead, and it is enough for us, or make plain the name of this, or with
the heralds of the truth


say the natures of Christ Godhead and humanity and not the nature; for Godhead
and humanity is not one nature.

36 If from Godhead and humanity there is one nature of Christ, the Godhead in
Christ is a part of a nature and not a perfect nature, and likewise the
humanity.

37 If your composite nature is consubstantial neither with the Father at all
nor with the mother, and the whole is Christ, Christ, insofar as Christ, is
consubstantial with no one. And if this, when he is consubstantial with
someone, he is not Christ. Being then consubstantial with the Father he is not
Christ. It is necessary then of two the one, either being consubstantial with
the Father not to be Christ, or being Christ not to be consubstantial with the
Father.

38 Things composed are composed from opposites, but according to the
physicists there is nothing opposite to essence. It is impossible then for an
essence to be composite from essences.

39 The essential differences constitute the natures, that is essences. If then
one nature has been constituted from Godhead and humanity, Godhead and
humanity will not be natures, but essential differences.

40 Things that are of one essence are altogether of other hypostasis, and
things of one hypostasis of other essence. Therefore either saying the Godhead
and the humanity of Christ of one essence say of other and other hypostasis,
or saying of one hypostasis confess two natures not one. Where there is
difference of essence there is not one nature. Either then say the flesh
consubstantial with the Godhead of the Word, or saying of other essence
confess two natures.

41 Those who say one nature of the Godhead of Christ and of his flesh, tell
us, in which nature did Christ suffer? In the composite, clearly, you will
say; for saying one you will of necessity attach the passion to it also, and
according to you both the Godhead and the humanity of Christ will be passible.

42 It is customary for philosophical speech to set the account concerning
quantity before that concerning quality, and it adds the cause, that where
quality is, quantity also will altogether be contemplated; "for the difference
is a difference of things that differ." How then, saying a difference of
essential qualities, do you not confess the number of the things that differ
essentially?

43 If there is one composite nature of Christ, it will be either passible only
or impassible or passible-impassible and according to the same both, or
changeable and at one time passible, at another impassible, or part of it
passible and part of it impassible. Choose then which of the impieties you
will. For if passible only, Christ is not God. And if impassible only, not
man. And if at one time passible, at another impassible, he is neither by
nature, but rather according to accident; for what is naturally disposed does
not change. And if part of it is passible and part impassible, and each is by
nature that which it is, the parts will not be of the same nature and thus
Christ will be two natures, who is not one nature, but one composite
hypostasis from two natures and in two natures and two natures. For the same
nature to be passible and impassible at once is impossible; for one and the
same nature cannot be receptive of opposite essential differences.

44 In every union the things united, whether they are confused or mixed,
remain not departing from their own nature. For even if wine is mixed with
water, the two natures are in the mixture; and wax and pitch, even if they
have been confused, yet neither has departed from its own nature, but in the
mixture the two natures remain. For even if they have been united and each
does not display its own property pure and unharmed because of the confusion
and mixture toward what was united, yet nevertheless each according to nature
is this which it was


and they are reckoned two natures, not one, by those who know how to judge
such things; for who does not know that in the mixture from wine and water
there are two natures? If then these things are so, how do you, both knowing
the difference and confessing the properties of the natures, proclaim these
one and not two, and do not even grant the things of confusion to the
unconfused? But it is clear that you are in labor with change and
disappearance and co-essentialization, but you do not wish to bring it into
the open in the change of impiety.

45 The things united become one by the union not after the union, and so long
as it remains in the union and the union is preserved, they are one, insofar
as they have been united. How then do you say two in the union, but after the
union dare to say that one nature has come to be, though the blessed Cyril
took "after the union" instead of "in the union"? For when has the union,
having come to be, ceased, or what has made this cease, so that the result of
this after it and not in it should be suspected as other besides the things
from which it is? For the union is not as the nature of the things that have
come to be and no longer are, such as time and dancing and such things.

46 If after the union something other has come to be besides the union, it is
confusion namely and not union.

47 If confusion makes the two natures one, and according to you the unconfused
does the same, tell, what is the difference according to you of the unconfused
and of confusion.

48 If

50 And if you say that the dyad brings in division, learn well being taught
that philosophical speech does not define number as division, but a heap of
monads or a pouring of monads. So that it makes plain union rather than
division. For division of the monad is half and half, but the dyad is an
addition of a monad; for to say the two as one and one is rather of division,
but to take one and one under one and say two is of conjunction and union.
Either then number gathers, but does not divide, since, if the dyad is a
cutting of the monad, and what is cut is cut in two, number is cutting and not
addition, and if this, just as the monad being cut makes the dyad, in the same
way the two being cut ought to make the tetrad. And from where did the three
come? For only the even ought to be numbered, the odd being excluded (or
rather to say the true, that number is said in two ways; for one is by itself
as the nature itself of number, and the other contemplated in relation and in
fact, just as white is both whiteness and what has been made white. This
nature then of number neither divides nor joins, but admits both; for example
the dyad, if you look at the monads from which it is constituted, is divided
into these (for the two are divided into one and one), and if you contemplate
the group of these, it is rather joined from these; for one and one are two
and two and two four. And again number is not division, but a sign making
plain the quantity of the underlying things, whether united or divided,
another account and not number dividing or joining these; for saying ten
cubits of this wood perhaps being continuous we do not divide together with
the number also the unity in the ten-cubit wood; and again saying ten palms we
know these in division; and saying one measure of wheat perhaps or of palms we
know the grains contained by the measure in division. And if the number


is applied to natures, it presents not their quantity primarily, but the being
of another kind; for saying perhaps two natures of an ox and a horse we
signify not their division according to quantity, but the variation according
to form. And saying three horses or men, Peter perhaps and James and John, we
make plain rather their being divided and that they are not of such a kind,
but rather so many. And in the case of the holy and consubstantial Trinity,
proclaiming three hypostases, we confess one essence and nature of these. But
in the case of the saving economy, saying two natures we signify their being
of another form, not their being separated, as there justly rejecting the
number of the essences, even if no hypostasis would be without essence, so
also here that of the hypostases, even if no nature would be without
hypostasis.

51 If the dyad altogether introduces division and on this account you flee the
number of the natures, why do you not decline the number of the properties?

52 You confess one incarnate nature of God the Word, and the opinion is common
to you and to us; for it is a saying of the fathers. But if you wish through
this to make plain the two natures, your war against the truth is vain. And if
you hold this out simply as expressive of one, contradictorily against the
two, "incarnate" has been thrown in by you in vain. And if it presents neither
one simply nor two united, altogether whatever fractional something between
the one and the two is taken, this "incarnate" will make plain for you,
whether two-thirds, or a half; and of the quantity you would be masters,
seeing that the blessed Athanasius and Cyril here improperly and not properly
by the name of nature made plain the hypostasis as by a more universal. For
the more universal are predicated of the particular, and it is not altogether
the same (for the hypostasis is not nature, for the hypostasis is not
universal), yet it admits both the name and the definition of the universal,
but it does not convert. For the hypostasis, that is the individual of the
nature, is nature, but not only nature, but with properties, and the nature is
not hypostasis, that is individual. Thus improperly and not properly by the
name of nature they signified the hypostasis, so that what is said is one
incarnate hypostasis of God the Word; for the nature of God the Word has not
become flesh, but the hypostasis. And the divine evangelist is witness crying
out here clearly: "The Word became flesh." And the Word is not nature, as has
been made plain above, but hypostasis. And the God-bearing Cyril himself says
elsewhere these things: "The nature of the Word, that is the hypostasis, which
is the Word himself." For if the nature of the Word, that is the Godhead, has
become flesh (for the Word did not have another nature besides the Godhead,
and the Word is of the same nature with the Father and the Spirit, and all
things are common of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit except
the mode of existence), the Father also and the Spirit have become flesh. We
confess therefore a union of natures according to hypostasis and a
becoming-flesh of the Word and a deification of the flesh; for becoming-flesh
is to partake of flesh and of the things of the flesh. The in-essence
hypostasis of God the Word then, that is God the Word, becomes flesh and is
thickened and becomes hypostasis to the flesh, and being formerly God he later
becomes flesh, that is man, and one composite hypostasis of the two natures
comes to be, and in it through the becoming-flesh the two natures of the
Godhead and of the humanity are united and interpenetrate in one another. And
the interpenetration comes from the Godhead; for this imparts to the flesh of
its own glory and brightness, not partaking of the passions of the flesh.
Therefore the nature of the flesh is deified, but it does not make flesh the
nature of the Word. And the nature of the Word deifies the assumption, but is
not made flesh; for the worse is benefited by the better. The better is not
harmed by the worse; for as


iron is fired, but the fire is not made iron, and as the flesh is ensouled,
but the soul is not made flesh, so the divine nature deifies the flesh, but
itself is not made flesh. For the divine nature is not receptive of any
addition or subtraction whatever, and it is imparting, but not partaking;
therefore not even one composite nature has come to be. It is the same then to
say in-essence hypostasis and nature of God the Word; for as has been said,
the blessed Athanasius and Cyril indicated the hypostasis by the name of
nature. For the hypostasis is also nature, but the nature is no longer also
hypostasis; for it does not convert. For the universals are predicated of the
particulars, but the particulars are not affirmed of the universals. And if
when some iron is fired the nature of the iron is said to have been fired, yet
one must know that this is because the nature of iron is receptive of firing
and because the firing is a work of nature; but in the becoming-flesh of the
Word the becoming-flesh is not a work of nature, but a mode of economic
condescension. It is not possible then to say that one of the hypostases of
the Godhead has something which not all the hypostases possess, except the
mode of existence. And the becoming-flesh also is a mode of a second existence
naturally fitting only to the only-begotten Son and Word, so that the property
may remain unmoved.

53 But you will say perhaps: How then do we say that the nature of the flesh
has been deified and has suffered? Do we, not affirming these of all the
hypostases of the humanity, and saying that the nature of the Word has become
flesh, refer the becoming-flesh to the Father and the Spirit? Because all the
nature of men is receptive of the things according to nature and contrary to
nature and above nature not by a change of nature, but the divine nature is
unreceptive of every alteration and addition, and not only this but also
because that flesh was a first-fruit of our lump and had not come to be a
