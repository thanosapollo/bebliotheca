#+BEGIN_QUOTE
To the Bath of Pallas edited by Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff
#+END_QUOTE

All you bath-pourers of Pallas, come out, all of you, come out; I have just
heard the snorting of the sacred mares, and the goddess is ready to go; haste
now, O fair-haired Pelasgian women, haste. Never did Athena wash her mighty
arms before she had driven the dust from the horses' flanks, not even when,
carrying all her armor stained with gore, she came from the unjust earth-born,
but first of all, loosing the necks of the horses from under the chariot, she
washed away in the springs of Ocean the sweat and the flecks, and cleansed all
the clotted foam from the mouths of the bit-eaters. Come, Achaean women, and
do not bring myrrh nor alabastra (I hear the sound of the axle-pipes), do not
bring myrrh, bath-pourers, for Pallas, nor alabastra (for Athena does not love
mixed unguents) nor a mirror; her face is always fair, not even when the
Phrygian was judging the strife on Ida did the great goddess look into
orichalc or into the reflecting eddy of Simois; nor did Hera; but Cypris,
taking the shining bronze, often rearranged the same lock twice. But she,
having run twice sixty double-courses, like the Lacedaemonian stars beside the
Eurotas, skillfully anointed herself, applying simple unguents, the produce of
her own plant; O maidens, the blush ran up, such a color as an early rose or a
pomegranate seed has. Therefore now bring only some male oil, with which
Castor, with which Heracles too is anointed; bring her also a comb all of
gold, that she may comb her hair, having smeared her shining lock. Come out,
Athena; a band after your heart is here, virgin daughters of the great sons of
Arestor; O Athena, the shield of Diomedes too is being carried, as this is a
more ancient custom of the Argives;

Eumedes taught it, a priest dear to you; who once, knowing that the people
were preparing a plotted death against him, went off in flight carrying your
holy image, and settled on Mount Creion; Creion mountain; and he set you,
daemon, among the sheer rocks, which now have the name Pallatides. Come out,
Athena, city-sacker, golden-helmed, delighting in the clash of horses and of
shields. Today, water-carriers, do not dip; today, Argos, drink from the
springs and not from the river, today the slave-women shall carry the pitchers
either to Physadeia or to Amymone, daughter of Danaus. For Inachus, mixing his
waters with gold and with flowers, will come from the pasture mountains
bringing Athena a fair bath. But Pelasgian, take care lest unwillingly you see
the queen. Whoever sees Pallas the city-guardian naked will look upon Argos
this last time. Lady Athena, you come out; meanwhile I will tell something to
these. The tale is not mine, but of others. Maidens, Athena once in Thebes
loved one nymph very much and far beyond her companions, the mother of
Teiresias, and she was never apart from her; but even when toward the ancient
Thespians

But Teiresias still alone with his dogs, his beard just darkening, was ranging
the holy place; and thirsting unspeakably he came to the stream of the spring,
wretched man; and unwillingly he saw what is not lawful. And Athena, though
angered, nevertheless addressed him:
/Who led you, who will no longer carry your eyes away,/
/O son of Everes, what daemon led you on a hard road?/
She spoke, and night took the boy's eyes. He stood speechless, for pains glued
his knees and helplessness held his voice. And the nymph cried out:
/What have you done to my boy, lady? Are such the friendships of goddesses?/
/You have taken the eyes of my child. Accursed child,/
/you saw Athena's breasts and flanks, but you will not see the sun again./
/O wretched me, O mountain, O Helicon no longer to be passed by me,/
/truly you have taken much in return for little; having destroyed gazelles/
/and not many roe-deer, you have the eyes of my child./

/Noble woman, take back again all that you said in anger;/
/I did not make your child blind./
/For it is not sweet to Athena to snatch the eyes of children;/
/but the laws of Cronus speak thus:/
/whoever sees any of the immortals, when the god himself does not choose it,/
/this man sees at a great price./
/Noble woman, the deed cannot become revocable hereafter;/
/since thus the thread of the Fates spun it, when first you bore him;/
/but now receive, O son of Everes, the due gift./
/How many burnt offerings will the Cadmeian woman burn hereafter,/
/how many Aristaeus, praying to see their only child,/
/the youth Actaeon, blind./
/And he will be the running-companion of the great Artemis;/
/but neither the running nor the joint far-shootings in the mountains will/
/save him then, whenever unwillingly he sees the lovely baths of the goddess;/
/but the dogs themselves will then dine on their former master;/
/and the mother will gather the bones of her son going through all the/
/thickets; she will say that you are most blessed and of good life,/
/having received a blind child from the mountains./
/O companion, do not murmur at this; for in return for this many other/
/honors from me will await him for your sake./
/Since I will make him a seer, sung of by those to come,/
/yes, far more than the others,/
/and he will know the birds, which is auspicious and which fly idly/
/and of what kind the wings are not good./
/And he will give many oracles to the Boeotians, many to Cadmus,/
/and later to the great Labdacids./
/I will give also a great staff, which will guide his feet as needed,/
/I will give also a long-lived end of life./
/And he alone, when he dies, will go among the dead with understanding,/
/honored by the great Hagesilas./
Thus speaking she nodded; and that is fulfilled to whom Pallas nods, since
Zeus gave this to Athena alone of his daughters, to bear all the father's
things, bath-pourers, and no mother bore the goddess, but the head of Zeus.
The head of Zeus does not nod falsehoods αι daughter. Athena is coming now in
truth; but receive the goddess, O maidens, as many of you as have the work at
heart, with fair speech and with prayers and with ritual cries. Hail, goddess,
and care for Inachian Argos. Hail both as you drive out, and as you drive the
horses back again, and save the whole lot of the Danaans.
