* Comparison of the King and the Monk

COMPARISON OF ROYAL POWER AND WEALTH AND PREEMINENCE, WITH A MONK LIVING IN
THE TRUEST PHILOSOPHY ACCORDING TO CHRIST.
=47.387=
1. Seeing the many among men loving and admiring the things that seem to be
good more than those that are by nature excellent and truly good, I think it
necessary to make a brief discourse concerning both, and to
set beside one another now both those things that are neglected by many, and
those that meet with much zeal, so that, having learned the difference of
each, we may make much of the former as worthy of zeal and of salvation, and
be trained to despise the latter as worthy of nothing. Therefore wealth and
power and office and glory are loved, and the many count blessed the rulers of
the nations, borne on splendid chariots, enjoying the cry of heralds and a
great bodyguard; but the life of those who philosophize, and of those who have
chosen the solitary way of living, is despised. And those, when they appear,
turn the people toward themselves, but these, when they appear, draw no one's
eyes, or those of very few indeed, toward themselves. And no one would desire
to become like these, but all would desire to become like those; and yet to
acquire power, and to receive rule over a nation, is hard and for the many
impossible, and those who love office would need much money; but to choose the
solitary life, and to live together with the worship of God, is equally
feasible and easy for all. And the possession of office is destroyed together
with this life, or rather it even leaves its lovers while they are still
living, and already it has brought some even into great danger or dishonor;
but the solitary life both now fills the righteous with many goods, and after
the end of life leads them bright and rejoicing to the tribunal of God and the
Father, when most of those who have ruled will be seen paying a great penalty
for what they have lived. Come then, setting beside one another both the goods
of =47.388= philosophy, and those things that seem to be excellent in the
present life's power and glory, let us learn thoroughly the difference of each
of these goods; for they will be clearer when set in parallel. Or rather, if
it seems good, setting the summit of goods, I mean kingship, beside
philosophy, let us consider the fruits of each possession, learning accurately
of what things the king rules, and of what the philosopher. Therefore the king
is set over cities and lands and many nations, and leads generals and prefects
and armies and peoples and councils by his own nods; but the one who has given
himself to God, and has chosen the solitary life, rules anger and envy and
love of money and pleasure and the other evils, always watching and taking
thought how he may not allow the soul to come under shameful passions, nor the
reason to be enslaved to a bitter tyranny, but may always keep the mind above
all things, having set the fear of God over the passions. Such then is the
rule and power the king possesses, and such the monk, so that one would more
justly call this man a king than the one who shines in purple and a crown, and
sits upon a golden throne.

2. For a king in truth is the one who masters anger and envy and pleasure, and
brings all things under the laws of God and keeps the mind free, and does not
allow the lordship of pleasures to hold sway in the soul. Such a man I would
gladly see ruling peoples and earth and sea, and cities and peoples and
armies. For the one who has set reason over the passions of the soul would
easily be set over



men also with the divine laws, so as to be in a father's place to those ruled,
conversing with the cities with all gentleness. But the one who seems to rule
men, yet is a slave to anger and love of office and pleasures, would first
seem =47.389= ridiculous to those ruled, because he wears a crown set with
stones and gold, yet is not crowned with temperance, and his whole body shines
with purple, yet he has the soul unadorned. Then he will not even know how to
handle the rule; for he who could not rule himself, how could he be able to
guide others by the laws? And if you wish to see the war of each as well, you
will find the one fighting demons and mastering and conquering and being
crowned by Christ; for he comes to the war with the divine favor, fenced with
heavenly weapons, so that his victory must come to pass; but the king
fighting barbarians. And by as much as demons are more fearful than men, by so
much is the one who masters these more splendid than the one who conquers
those. And if you should wish to learn thoroughly the cause of each war as
well, you will find the inequality great. For the one wars against the demons
for piety and the worship of God, longing to snatch cities or villages from
error; but the other fights barbarians for places or boundaries or seized
money, or because greed and love of unjust rule call him to the battle; where
indeed many kings often, having desired greater things, have lost even what
they had besides. So then the rule and the wars have shown the king and the
one who has been zealous to live with the worship of God to differ so much
from one another; and one would know them accurately by examining the life of
each and the daily deeds. For he would truly find the one conversing with
prophets, and adorning the soul with the wisdom of Paul, and leaping
continually from Moses to Isaiah, from him to John, from him to another; but
the king continually conversing with commanders and prefects and bodyguards;
and to those with whom one continually converses, to these his character is
made like. Therefore the one living as a monk is molded in his mind toward the
manners of the apostles and prophets, but the king toward those of generals
and bodyguards and shield-bearers, men enslaved to wine and yielding to
pleasures, and spending the greater part of the day in drinking, knowing
nothing timely or fair because of the wine. So then even for this reason it is
fitting to count the solitary life more blessed than the one in power and
kingship and scepters.

3. And if we should wish to examine the time of night as well, we shall see
the monk adorned with the worship of God and with prayers, singing far earlier
than the birds, living with angels, speaking with God, enjoying the heavenly
goods; but the one set over many nations and peoples and armies, ruling much
earth and much sea, stretched out on the bed and snoring. For the monk is fed
with so little food as would not even call for deep sleep; but luxury puts the
other to sleep, and the drinking keeps him on the bed
until day itself. Therefore the monk has both a moderate cloak and table, and
table-companions who are athletes of the same virtue; but the king must adorn
himself with stones and =47.390= gold, and set out a splendid table, and use
table-companions, if he is foolish, worthy of his own vice, but if he has
sense and is temperate, perhaps good and just men, yet falling far short of
those men's virtue. Thus even if a king philosophizes, he will not be able to
come even a little near the nobility of the one living as a monk. For even on
journeys he is a burden to his subjects, both when he dwells in a city, and
when he enjoys peace, and when he takes hold of wars, collecting taxes,
assembling armies, leading captives, conquering, and being defeated; for being
defeated he fills those ruled with his own evils, and conquering he becomes
unbearable, adorning himself with trophies, and



thinking highly of himself, and giving the soldiers license to seize, to
plunder, to wrong travelers, to besiege the cities while they sit in them, to
sack the houses of the poor, to exact from those who have received them each
day what no law permits, on the pretext of some ancient custom that is
unlawful and unjust. And the king with such evils has harmed the rich man not
at all, but he has injured poverty, as if he were truly ashamed before the
well-off. Not so the monk, but as soon as he has appeared, he has come
bringing some grace both to rich and to poor, alike to these and to those,
using one cloak through the year, and drinking water more sweetly than others
drink marvelous wine; asking from the rich no favor for himself, neither small
nor greater, but many and continual ones for those in need, profitable to
both, to those who give and to those about to receive. Thus he is a common
physician of both rich and poor alike, freeing the ones from sins through good
admonition, and for the others undoing their want. But a king, when he
commands the tributes to be lighter, has benefited the rich more than poverty;
and doing the contrary, he has harmed those who possess little. For the
harshness of the tributes would harm the rich man little, but it sweeps away
the houses of the poor like a torrent, filling the villages with wailing, and
neither pitiable old age moves those who exact the taxes, nor the widowhood of
women, nor the orphanhood of children, but they revel the whole time, some
common enemies of the land, exacting from the farmers what the earth has not
brought forth.

4. Come then, let us examine this also, what the monk grants to his subjects,
and what the king. Therefore the king gives gold, and the monk the grace of
the Spirit; and the king undoes poverty when he is good, but the monk frees
souls tyrannized by demons by prayer. And if it happens that someone is
storm-tossed by such misfortunes, he passes by the king as if by lifeless
things, and takes refuge at the house of the monks, as one fleeing a wolf
takes refuge with a hunter carrying a sword in his hand; for what a sword is
to the hunter, that prayer is to the one living as a monk. For the sword is
not so fearful to wolves as the prayers =47.391= of the righteous are to
demons. Therefore not only do we take refuge with the holy monks in our needs,
but the kings themselves take refuge with these in fearful times, as those who
are beggars in famine take refuge at the houses of the rich. Did not Ahab, the
king of the Jews, in famine and want of grain have the hope of salvation in
the prayers of Elijah? Did not Hezekiah, having the same office and power,
when he was sick and about to die, seeing death standing over him, take refuge
with the prophet, as one more powerful than death and a giver of life? And
indeed when war had broken out, and Palestine was in danger of being torn up
from its very foundations, the kings of the Jews, leaving the army, both
infantry and archers and cavalry and generals and commanders, took refuge in
the prayers of Elisha; for they thought the servant of God would be to them in
place of many myriads. And this same thing Hezekiah also, when the Persian war
had arisen, and the city had been brought low into the greatest danger
concerning its very foundations, and those on the wall were trembling and
fearing and shaking, as in expectation of thunder, or of an earthquake that
moves all things, the king set the prayers of Isaiah against the many myriads
of the Persians; and he was not cheated of his hope. For as soon as the
prophet lifted his hands to heaven, God also dissolved the Persian war with
arrows from heaven, training the kings to regard his own servants as common
saviors of the earth, so that they might be trained, even when called by the
righteous to every good and humane deed, to respect the counsels, and to obey
the good admonitions. Therefore not only from these things is it possible to
see the difference of both, but



even if it happens that both fall, the monk deprived of virtue, the king
of kingship, the monk could easily recover himself, and quickly, having wiped
away his sins through prayer and tears and mourning and care of the poor, he
easily runs back again to his former rule; but a king who has fallen would
need many allies, many hoplites and cavalry and horses and money and dangers;
and the king has the hope of salvation wholly in others; but the monk will
have salvation following immediately upon the will and the zeal and the change
of mind: For the kingdom, he says, of heaven is within you. And
indeed death is fearful to the king, but painless to the one who
philosophizes; for the one who looks down on wealth and =47.392= pleasure and
luxury, for the sake of which the many desire to live, must also bear the
departure from here easily. And if it should happen that monk and king be
slaughtered, the monk will draw dangers upon himself for piety, procuring
through death an immortal and heavenly life; but a king will have a tyrant as
his slayer, a lover of the rule, becoming a pitiable and terrible spectacle
after the slaughter; but to see a monk slaughtered for piety is a sweet and
saving spectacle. And the monk will have many lovers of his own goods, both
imitators and disciples, praying that they may appear like him; but the king
would spend many words in prayers, begging God that no lover of the kingship
appear. And no one would dare to kill the monk, thinking it impiety toward God
if he should destroy such a man; but many slayers stand over the king, lovers
of tyranny. Therefore the king is guarded by soldiers, but the monk walls
cities with prayers, fearing no one; but the king always lives with fear and
expectation of slaughter; for the king has a dangerous greed, the monk a safe
salvation. Concerning the affairs in the present life, then, I think enough
has been said by me. And if we should wish to examine the contest that is to
come as well, we shall see the one, bright and conspicuous, caught up in
clouds to meet the Lord in the air, according to the leader and trainer of
this saving life and of all virtue; but the king, if he should appear to have
administered the rule justly and humanely, and this is very rare, will have a
lesser salvation and honor; for it is not equal, it is not equal, a good king
and a monk who lives to the utmost with the worship of God. But if he should
appear wicked and perverse, having filled the earth with many evils, who could
tell the misfortunes by which he will be seen constrained, burned with fire,
scourged, racked, suffering such things as are neither speakable in word, nor
bearable in deed? Reckoning and learning all these things, we must not admire
those who are rich; for he who has appeared as their master cannot come even a
little near the virtue of the monk. When therefore you see a rich man adorned
with clothing, decked with gold, borne on chariots, going out in splendid
processions, do not count the man blessed; for wealth is temporary, and what
seems to be fair is destroyed together with this life; but seeing a monk
walking alone, humble and meek and quiet and gentle, emulate the man, appear
as an imitator of his philosophy, pray to become like the righteous man; For
ask, he says, and it shall be given you. For these things are truly fair and
saving and lasting, through the loving-kindness and providence of Christ; to
whom be the glory and the power unto the ages of ages. Amen.
