Funeral Oration for Eteoneus edited by Wilhelm Dindorf

:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The oration is in no way fortunate, nor according to our hopes, but it
is necessary for the sake of the city and of the family of Eteoneus, and
moreover for our own consolation. And I think that even if dirges had
not yet been established among men before, now at least over this one
they would justly begin. For what would one not lament? Whether the age
at which he has gone and the manhood that has been buried ill, or the
temperance of which one would not easily find a pattern, or the hopes of
which he himself is bereft, and of which household and friends and city
and all that belongs to Asia as it now is are bereft? What Simonides
will lament these things? What Pindar, having found what song or such a
speech? What Stesichorus will utter a thing worthy of such a suffering?
And what Dyseris of Thessaly mourned so great a mourning over Antiochus
at his death, as the mourning now laid before this man's mother? Yet it
will not

:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

suffice to lament in silence, nor only so far as crying out the name,
but some adornment too will be added to the dirge. For what shall we
even fear, that in speaking we may lie, of one whose family first of all
is so great both in the city and in Asia that not even one man could
dispute it; for all, so to speak, hold first place each one. And indeed
the mother's side is a match for the father's. And the parents
themselves: he the most distinguished of men, she the most temperate of
women, and even in the care of her own children better than a woman.
Nurture and nature worthy of his birth: for him the mother was both
nurse and guardian, and body and soul were fitting to one another. To
look upon, the most beautiful and tallest and most perfect of those in
his age, striking the greatest pleasure upon the beholder; in character
the most orderly and most liberal, distinguished by magnificence
together with simplicity, so that one could not guess whether he was a
child or a youth or an old man. For the uncontrived was a child's, the
prime a youth's, the prudence an old man's. And one might admire in his
intelligence that it was nowhere bold nor rash nor arrogant, but that
the quickness of mind was in a leisurely character; and in his
temperance again that it was nowhere sluggish nor supine nor numb, but
that, as of a well-tempered spring season, sharpness stood equally with
gentleness, and neither temperance nor grace injured one another. He
clung to his mother as children at the breast, embraced his brother as a
child, and clung to his studies as if it were not possible for him to
live otherwise. What he heard he knew at once, and seeing a man he would
immediately know what sort he was and whether one ought to welcome or be
on guard. And thinking that the

Homeric saying is well, /The rule of many is not a good thing,/ and that
many teachers tend rather toward ignorance, he chose a teacher from all,
whichever one he did choose, for it is not most becoming for me to say,
and to this one he applied himself so that, doing all that befits the
most studious and most affectionate, he never seemed to himself to be
near what was worthy. And when he was with him he so rejoiced as if this
alone of his life were livable for him; and if something prevented, he
was distressed, but he never blamed.

Listening, then, he was so intense upon the speeches that he had no
leisure to praise, but as the thirsty drink in silence, so it was enough
for him to receive what was said, showing by posture and nod and
brightness the joy he had in the words. And you would always find him
either handling a book, or composing speeches, or delighting his mother
with what he related, or with what he displayed, and in every way in
this posture doing everything which it would be most beautiful even for
a painting to imitate. And what is played among those who have come to
this time of life, by day and by night each, these were to him as
certain myths. But of women one was his table-companion, his mother; of
boys, one brother; and friends those who had chosen the same things and
came together according to the same studies; and he was distinguished
through all. And one might have said he was a statue of Modesty, for
whom it was enough for the most part to be silent, and if he uttered
anything, he had to burn. Therefore you would not otherwise hear his
voice, but he had to either speak having blushed, or blush having
spoken; so unseen, unhearing, and inexperienced of all the most shameful
things he passed his life. He looked upon speeches and education alone,
he who even in dying died into these things, crying out a panegyric and
declamations. O you most beautiful of children in all things, O you who
of the

age that belonged to you did not take hold, yet of the age you had
appeared more solemn and more elder, you the choruses of age-mates long
for, and the elders long for, and the city longs for, which hoped the
greatest things of you, which you lately gladdened first and last. And
for the mother, what nights, or what days remain, she who before was
sung as blessed in her child, but now has been proven as having given
birth in misery. O circles of those eyes, closed now for all time, and
the head formerly most graceful now in the dust. And hands vanished. O
feet, how, as you bore your master, you gave way. O more pitiable than a
bridegroom just now burning, and more fitting for crowns than for
dirges, what untimely end you stirred in your season, so that before a
wedding-song was sung for you the time overtook you for singing dirges.
O most beautiful form. O voice growing common to the Greeks, you have
gone having only made a prelude, having gladdened so much as to grieve
the more. It comes to me to add the saying of Pindar: Stars and rivers
and waves of the sea call upon your untimely end. O the second fall,
what a one you lie upon what a youth. O the addition, what a second
shaking it gave us again. O the tragic daimon, who having just now shown
forth council-chambers and speeches and zeal and joy, far from these in
a little closed the drama. O common misfortunes of the gods of speech
and of the gods below. What ever am I even to answer to the decrees? Or
this, that Eteoneus has gone to the greater ones? O most gifted of
children, what a book I send you, of what speeches you now take your
enjoyment.

But indeed, as in some tragedy truly, in the midst of the wailings I
seem to hear a voice from the machine of some god turning the dirge into
fair speech, saying thus: Cease, O men, the child is not pitiable, or
rather the man, nor is he justly to be pitied for the

:END:

journey which he has set out upon, but if anyone else, he has human
affairs well indeed. For neither Cocytus nor Acheron have taken this one
at least, nor will a tomb hide him having received him, but renowned and
ageless already for all remaining time he will go about as a hero,
assessor of Cyzicus, honored by ancestral Apollo after the manner of
Amyclas and Narcissus and Hyacinthus and if anyone else upon a beautiful
form acquired a virtue greater than according to man. Therefore he must
now be honored with other honors and escorted in another way, as being
better than tears and as having made his colony not without a divine
portion. At all events death is set forth as a common bound for all, but
to have thought himself worthy of the fairest things and to have ended
with glory, blameless to gods and men. This has not been given to all,
but to some few of all. And every life of man is short and not worth
much in respect of number, but whether one names Arganthonius or
Tithonus or Nestor of Pylos who lasted through three generations,
putting all these times together you will find them a lesser part of the
whole of eternity than one can say. One must not then cling to life, nor
measure happiness by this, if someone had as many affairs as possible,
nor if someone enjoyed long old age, such things as men are accustomed
to enjoy, but consider that he has it most beautifully who fulfilled the
portion of life given him among the fairest things, and who, like a
poet, while they still longed to hear and to see, closed the drama. And
this one goes forth both happy and enviable to all, both younger and
old, having enjoyed so much of life, for which things alone it was to be
prayed that one be born, untouched by evils, inexperienced in troubles,
renowned, reared in speeches and studies and praises, from the dear
mother who shared his tent, having set out to the ancient mother. And if
to anyone he seems not to have enjoyed fame sufficiently, one must now
fill it up, adorning him as a hero. And of those who are gone, in safety

are the praises now; already one must think to hear these things from
the greater ones, and holding so, believe that one would the more attain
the truth and do what is pleasing to him. And it is fair even in
drinking-songs, as they sing of Harmodius, to say /you have not died at
all/, but if anyone else he moves about in the midst of those who know
him, both citizens and strangers. O you who lived as in holy places and
died better than according to man. O ornament to friends and to family
and to city, O you who have the first of virtue according to your age.
These are the things from us to you; the rest the city will care for in
common.
