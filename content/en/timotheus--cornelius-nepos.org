* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Timotheus, son of Conon, an Athenian. This man increased the glory
received from his father by many virtues: for he was eloquent,
energetic, laborious, skilled in military affairs and no less in
governing the state. Many things of this man were done brilliantly, but
these are most illustrious. He subdued the Olynthians and Byzantians by
war. He took Samos: in attacking which town in the former war the
Athenians had consumed one thousand two hundred talents, that town he
restored to the people without any public expense. He waged wars against
Cotys and from him brought back one thousand two hundred talents of
booty into the public treasury. He freed Cyzicus from siege. He set out
as aid to Ariobarzanes together with Agesilaus, from whom when the
Laconian had received counted money, he preferred that his own citizens
be increased by land and cities rather than take that of which each
could carry a part to his own home. And so he received Crithote and
Sestus.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
The same man, set over the fleet, sailing around the Peloponnesus,
having laid waste Laconia, put their fleet to flight, reduced Corcyra
under the command of the Athenians and then joined as allies the
Epirotes, the Athamanes, the Chaones, and all those peoples who lie next
to that sea. By which deed the Lacedaemonians desisted from the long
contention and of their own will conceded to the Athenians the
principate of maritime empire, and they established peace on these
terms, that the Athenians should be leaders by sea. Which victory was of
so great joy to the Attics that then first altars were publicly made to
Peace and a pulvinar was instituted for that goddess. That the memory of
this praise might remain, they publicly set a statue for Timotheus in
the forum. Which honor before that time fell to this man alone, that,
when the people had set a statue for the father, it should give one also
to the son. Thus the recent one of the son, placed next, renewed the old
memory of the father.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
This man, when he was of great age and had ceased to hold magistracies,
the Athenians began to be pressed by war from all sides. Samos had
defected, the Hellespont had revolted, Philip already then strong was
attempting many things: against whom when Chares had been set, not
enough of a defense was thought in him. Menestheus is made praetor, son
of Iphicrates, son-in-law of Timotheus, and it is decreed that he set
out to the war. To this man two men outstanding in experience and wisdom
are given into counsel, of whose counsel he might use, the father and
the father-in-law, because in these there was so great authority that
there was great hope that through them the lost things could be
recovered. When they had set out to Samos and Chares, their arrival
having been known, was setting out to the same place with his own
forces, lest anything seem to have been done with himself absent, it
happened, when they were approaching the island, that a great storm
arose: which the two old commanders, having judged it useful to avoid,
held back their fleet. But he, having used a rash plan, did not yield to
the authority of those older by birth, as if fortune were in his own
hand. He arrived where he had been striving, and sent a messenger to
Timotheus and Iphicrates that they should follow to the same place. From
this, the thing having been managed badly, several ships having been
lost, he withdrew himself to the place from which he had set out and
publicly sent letters to Athens, that it had been easy for him to take
Samos, if he had not been deserted by Timotheus and Iphicrates. The
people, sharp, suspicious and on that account changeable, hostile,
envious (for indeed power was being called into a charge) recalls them
home: they are accused of treason. By this judgment Timotheus is
condemned and his fine is estimated at one hundred talents. He, driven
out by the hatred of an ungrateful state, betook himself to Chalcis.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
After the death of this man, when the people was repenting of its
judgment, it took away nine parts of the fine and ordered Conon, his
son, to give ten talents toward repairing a certain part of the wall. In
which the variety of fortune was observed. For the same walls which the
grandfather Conon had restored to the fatherland from the booty of the
enemies, the grandson was compelled with the highest ignominy of the
family to repair from his own private property. But of the moderate and
wise life of Timotheus, although we can bring forth very many
testimonies, we will be content with one, from which it will easily be
able to be conjectured how dear he was to his own. When as a very young
man he was pleading a case at Athens, not only friends and private
guest-friends came together to defend him, but even among them Jason,
tyrant of Thessaly, who at that time was the most powerful of all. This
man, although in his fatherland he did not think himself safe without
bodyguards, came to Athens without any guard and made so much of his
guest-friend that he preferred to go into danger of his head rather than
fail Timotheus contending about fame. Against this man nevertheless
Timotheus afterward waged war by order of the people: for he judged the
rights of the fatherland to be more sacred than those of
guest-friendship.

This was the last age of the Athenian commanders, of Iphicrates, of
Chabrias, of Timotheus, nor after their death was anyone a leader in
that city worthy of memory.

I come now to the bravest man and of the greatest counsel of all the
barbarians, except two Carthaginians, Hamilcar and Hannibal. About whom
we will report more on this account, because both most of his deeds are
more obscure and those things which turned out prosperously for him
happened not by the greatness of forces, but of counsel, in which he
then surpassed all: unless the reason of which has been explained, the
things will not be able to appear.
