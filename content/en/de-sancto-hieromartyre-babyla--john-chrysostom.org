ON THE HOLY HIEROMARTYR BABYLAS

=50.527=

αʹ. I for my part wished to pay the debt today which, when I was here the
other day, I promised you; but =50.528= what am I to do? Blessed Babylas
appearing in the midst called us to himself, not by uttering a voice, but by
turning us with the =50.529= brightness of his appearance. Do not then be
vexed at the postponement of the payment; in any case, the longer the time
becomes, the more the interest also grows for you. For we shall lay down this
money with interest, since the Master who deposited it commanded it so. Being
confident then concerning what has been lent, as both the principal and the
yield remain for you, let us not pass by the gain that has fallen in today,
but let us feast upon the achievements of blessed Babylas.

How then he presided over the Church among us, and saved this sacred ship in
storm and swell and waves, and how great a freedom of speech he showed toward
the emperor, and how he laid down his life for the sheep, and received that
blessed slaughter: these things, and such as these, we shall leave to the
elders among the teachers and to our common father to tell. For the older of
the events the aged can narrate well to you; but as many as have happened
recently, and in our own lifetime, these I, the young man, will narrate to
you: I mean the things after death, the things after the burial of the martyr,
the things when he spent his time in the suburb.

And I know that Greeks will laugh at our promise, if after death and burial we
promise to tell the exploits of him who was buried and dissolved into dust;
yet we will not be silent on this account, but for this very reason we will
speak all the more, so that, having shown this paradox to be true, we may turn
the laughter upon their own head. For of a mere man there would be no
achievements after death; but of a martyr there would be many and great ones,
not so that he may become more illustrious (for he has no need of the glory
that comes from the many), but so that you, the unbeliever, may learn that the
death of martyrs is not death, but the beginning of a better life and the
prelude of a more spiritual citizenship, and a removal from the lesser things
to the better.

Do not look at this, that the body of the martyr lies bare, empty of the
soul's activity; but consider that another power greater than the soul itself
sits beside him, the grace of the Holy Spirit, which makes a defense to
all on behalf of the resurrection through the wonders it works. For if to dead
bodies, even those dissolved into dust, God has granted a power greater than
that of all the living, much more will he grant them a life better than the
former, and more blessed, at the time of the crowns.

What then are his achievements? But do not be disturbed if we take the
discourse a little further back. For those who wish to display pictures well,
having first set the viewers a little away from the small panel, then uncover
them, making the sight clearer for them by the distance. Bear with us then,
you too, as we draw the discourse backward.

For when Julian, who surpassed all in impiety, mounted the imperial throne and
laid hold of the master's scepters, at once he also lifted his hands against
God who made him, and =50.530= he failed to recognize his benefactor, and
looking from below, from the earth toward heaven, he barked like the mad among
dogs, who cry out equally against those who do not feed them and those who do;
or rather he raged with a madness even wilder than theirs.

For they turn away from and hate both their own and strangers alike; but this
man fawned on the demons, who are strangers to his salvation, and served them
in every manner of service; but the Benefactor and Savior, who did not even
spare the Only-begotten for his sake, he turned away from and hated, and he
mocked the cross, a thing which raised up the inhabited world as it lay on its
face, and drove the darkness away from every side, and brought in for us a
light brighter than the rays. And not even here did he stop his madness, but
he promised that the nation of the Galileans would be snatched from the midst
of the inhabited world; for so he was accustomed to call us.

And yet if he thought the name of Christians to be a defilement, and the thing
to be full of much shame, why did he desire to shame us not by that name,
but with a foreign name? But he knew clearly that to be named from kinship
with Christ is a great ornament not only to men, but also to angels and to the
powers above. For this reason he set everything in motion, so as to strip us
of this ornament, and to destroy the preaching. But this was impossible, you
wretched and miserable man, just as it was impossible to overthrow heaven, and
quench the sun, and shake and throw down the foundations of the earth.

And Christ proclaimed these things beforehand, speaking thus: Heaven and earth
shall pass away; but my words shall not pass away. But you do not endure
Christ when he speaks; then receive the voice that comes from the facts. For
I, having been counted worthy to know what a decree of God is, how strong and
invincible a thing, have believed this to be more trustworthy than the natural
sequence and than the experience of all events; but you who are still dragged
on the ground, and who flutter toward the examination of human reasonings,
receive the testimony from the facts; I do not contradict, nor do I contend.

βʹ. What then do the facts say? Christ said that it is easier for heaven and
earth to perish than for any of his words to fall; the emperor spoke against
these things, and threatened that he would destroy the doctrines. Where then
is the emperor who threatened these things? He has perished, and is destroyed,
and now is in Hades awaiting the inescapable punishment. And where is Christ,
who declared those things? In the heavens, at the right hand of the Father,
occupying the highest throne of glory. Where are the emperor's blasphemous
words, and the unbridled tongue? They have become ashes, and dust, and food
for worms. Where is the decree of Christ? It shines from the very truth of the
facts, flashing as from a golden pillar by the outcome of the works.

And yet the emperor then omitted nothing, when he was about to raise war
against us; but he also called seers, and assembled sorcerers, and everything
was full of demons and evil spirits. What then were the returns of this
service? Overturnings of cities, and a famine bitterest of all famines. For
you surely know and remember how empty of wares the marketplace was, and the
workshops full of tumults, each man striving to snatch first what appeared and
to go away. And why do I speak of famine, when even the very springs of waters
were failing, springs that by the abundance of their stream hide rivers?

But since I have mentioned springs, come now let us go up to Daphne, and let
us drive the discourse toward the martyr's achievements. And yet you still
desire to parade the Greek indecencies; but even so, being myself as I am, let
us go off; for in any case, where there is remembrance of martyrs, there also
is shame of Greeks. This emperor then, going up to Daphne, frequently
troubled Apollo, begging, entreating, imploring, that he might divine
something concerning the things that were to come for him.

What then did the seer, the great god of the Greeks, say? The dead hinder me
from speaking, he says; but break open the tombs, dig up the bones, remove the
dead. What could be more unholy than these commands? The demon introduces
foreign laws of tomb-robbery, and invents novel methods of expelling
strangers. Who ever heard of the dead being driven out? Who saw soulless
bodies commanded to migrate, as this one ordered, overturning from their
foundations the common laws of nature?

For they are common laws of nature among all men, that the one who has
departed be hidden in the earth, and be given over to burial, and be laid to
rest in the bosom of the earth, the mother of all. And these laws neither
Greek, nor barbarian, nor Scythian, nor anyone wilder than they, ever
disturbed; but all revere and keep them, and they are so sacred, and venerable
to all.
But the demon, having lifted the mask, with bare head stands against the
common decrees of nature: For the dead, he says, are a pollution.

The dead are not a pollution, most wicked demon, but a wicked choice is a
defilement. And if one must say something even marvelous, the bodies of the
living rather than those of the dead are polluted, being more full of
wickedness; for the former minister to the commands of the soul, while the
latter lie motionless; and what is motionless and empty of all sensation would
also be free of all accusation. Yet I for my part would not even say that the
bodies of the living are by nature polluted, but that everywhere the wicked
and perverted choice is liable to accusations from all.

A dead body is not a pollution, Apollo, but to pursue a girl who wishes to be
chaste, and to undermine a virgin's dignity, and when failing of the shameless
deed to lament: this is worthy both of accusation and of punishment. Many then
among us were prophets, wonderful and great, and having foretold many things
concerning the future, and nowhere did they command those who asked to dig up
the bones of those who had departed; but Ezekiel, standing near the very
bones, was not only in no way hindered by them, but even putting flesh and
sinews and skins around them, he brought them back to life again. And the
great Moses did not =50.532= stand near dead bones, but carrying Joseph
himself, a whole dead man, thus he foretold the things to come; and very
reasonably.

For the words of those men were the grace of the Holy Spirit; but those of
these were deceit, and a lie that can nowhere be disguised. For that these
things were a pretext and an excuse, and that he feared blessed Babylas, is
clear from what the emperor did; for leaving all the other dead, he moved that
martyr alone. And yet if he did these things because he abhorred him, and not
because he feared him, he ought to have ordered the coffin to be smashed, to
be sunk in the sea, to be taken away into a desert, to be made to vanish by
some other manner of destruction. For this was the act of one who abhors. Thus
God did, when he spoke to the Hebrews concerning the abominations of the
nations; he ordered their pillars to be smashed, not to bring the pollutions
from the suburbs into the cities.

γʹ. The martyr then was being moved, but the demon not even so enjoyed freedom
from fear; but he immediately learned that it is possible to move a martyr's
bones, but impossible to escape a martyr's hands. For at the same time the
coffin was being drawn toward the city, and a thunderbolt from above was
coming upon the head of the image, and it burned everything up. And yet, if
not before, then at least it was likely that the impious emperor would be
angry, and would vent his wrath against the martyr's shrine; but not even then
did he dare; so great a fear held him; but although seeing the burning to be
unbearable, and knowing the cause exactly, he kept quiet.

And this is not the only marvel, that he did not dig down the martyrium, but
that he did not even dare to put the roof back on the temple. For he knew, he
knew that the blow was heaven-sent, and he feared lest, if he planned anything
further, he should call that fire upon his own head. For this reason he
endured seeing the temple of Apollo brought down into such desolation. For
there was no other cause why he did not set right what had happened, except
fear alone, through which he kept quiet unwillingly, and this though he knew
how great a reproach he was leaving to the demon, and how great an ornament to
the martyr.

For the walls now stand as trophies, sending forth a voice clearer than a
trumpet; to those in Daphne, to those in the city, to those arriving from
afar, to those present, to the men who will be hereafter, they narrate all
things through the sight: the wrestling, the combat, the victory of the
martyr. For the one standing at a distance from the suburb, and seeing the
shrine of the saint empty of the coffin, and the temple of Apollo deprived of
its roof, is likely to seek the cause of both of these; then, having learned
the whole history, thus to go away from there.

Such are the martyr's achievements after death. Therefore I also bless your
city, because you showed much zeal concerning this saint. For then, when he
was coming back from Daphne, the whole city poured out into the road, and the
marketplaces were empty of men, the houses empty of women, and the chambers
empty of virgins. So both every age and both sexes =50.533= sprang out of the
city, as if about to receive a long-absent father returning from a long
sojourn.

And you gave him back to the choir of those of like zeal; but the grace of God
did not allow him to remain there continually, but again transferred him
across the river, so that many of the places might be filled with the martyr's
fragrance. And not even when he came here was he going to be alone, but he
quickly received as neighbor and tent-fellow the one of like manner. For he
shared with him the same office, and showed equal freedom of speech for the
sake of piety. Therefore this wonderful emulator of the martyr also obtained
the same tent as he, not in vain, as it seems.

For so long did he toil there, continually writing to the emperor,
troubling magistrates, and contributing bodily service to the martyr. For you
surely know and remember that in summer, when the ray held the middle of
heaven, with those who sat attending him, he walked there every day, not as a
spectator only, but as one who would also be a partner of what was being done.
For he often laid hold of a stone, and pulled a =50.534= rope, and when
someone had need of building-work, he answered the need before the workmen;
for he knew, he knew how many the wages of these things would be laid up for
him.

And for this reason he continued serving the martyrs, not only with splendid
buildings, nor with successive festivals, but by the manner better than these.
And what is this? He imitates their life, he emulates their courage, through
all things according to his power he preserves in himself the image of the
martyrs; for see: they gave over their bodies to slaughter; this man put to
death the members of the flesh that are upon the earth; they stood against a
flame of fire, this man quenched the flame of desire; they fought against the
teeth of beasts, but this man too put to sleep the hardest of the passions in
us, anger.

For all these things then let us give thanks to God, that he has granted us
both martyrs so noble, and shepherds worthy of martyrs, for the perfecting of
the saints, for the building up of the body of Christ, with whom to the Father
be glory, honor, power, together with the Holy and life-giving Spirit, now and
always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
