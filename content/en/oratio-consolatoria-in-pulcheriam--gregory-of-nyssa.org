OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA, A CONSOLATORY ORATION ON PULCHERIA
I do not know how I shall use the word; for I see the subject double and
gloomy in each part, so that it is not easy for the discourse to come forth
outside of tears, whichever of the two I choose. The present circuit of the
year, as this was announced to us yesterday by the shepherd, contains the
memory of the gloomy events that once befell the neighboring city through the
earthquake. And who would recount these without tears? But this great and
illustrious fair city, set first of all under the sun, has suffered another
earthquake and has been stripped of no small ornament, having been suddenly
deprived of the luminary that shone in her as an addition to the imperial
good-fortune, and for this reason sharing the gloom of the emperors. And what
is said you who fill the assembly do not at all fail to know, seeing both this
very place in which we have been gathered and the dejection in the place. I do
not know then toward which earthquake I shall turn the discourse, toward the
one now or the one that happened of old. Or is it well to stand toward the
excess of the suffering and to soothe the city's own pain, treating it with
certain reasonings as with drugs? For even if not all who share in the evil
are present at the assembly, yet perhaps through those who are present the
word will be passed on also to those who are not present. For among physicians
those too are wise who by their art stand =9.462= against the excess of the
pains, postponing the treatment of the less grievous. And as those skilled in
these things say, if two pains should fall at once upon one body, the
sensation comes to be of the exceeding one alone, the lesser being stolen away
in the excess of the prevailing pain, so also I see in the present case; for
the new and our own grief is more prevailing than the things that pain us
through memory. For how would one not suffer at what has happened? Who is so
passionless in soul?

Who is so iron in character as to receive what has happened without pain? You
have known altogether this young dove that was being reared in the imperial
nest, that was just now growing her wings in bright plumage, and had
outstripped her age by her graces, how having left the nest she is gone, how
she has flown outside our eyes, how envy suddenly snatched her from our hands,
whether one must call her a dove, or a newly blooming flower, which had not
yet wholly shone out from the calyxes, but part already shone, and part was
hoped to shine, and yet in the small and incomplete it outshone; how it was
suddenly quenched in the calyx and before coming to its prime and spreading
the whole flower upward, it withered upon itself and became dust, which no one
either plucked or wore as a crown, but nature toiled in vain; whose good was
in hopes, but envy, falling aslant like a sword, cut off the hope. An
earthquake, brothers, was the thing that happened, outright, an earthquake no
more humane than any of the hard disasters; for it did not ruin the lifeless
beauty of buildings, nor cast to the earth flowering paintings or the fair
spectacles of stones, but this earthquake, falling suddenly, dissolved the
building of nature itself, brilliant in beauty, flashing beyond =9.463= with
graces. I saw also the high sapling, the tall-leaved palm (I mean the imperial
power) that with the imperial virtues as with certain branches stood above the
whole inhabited world and took in all things; I saw him ruling the others, but
bent by nature and leaning toward the loss of the flower. I saw also the noble
vine-shoot that was entwined about the palm, that had travailed this flower
for us, what birth-pangs she suffered a second time again in soul, not in
body, when this shoot of hers was being plucked away. Who passed by the
suffering without a groan? Who did not lament the loss of life? Who did not
let fall tears upon the suffering? Who did not mix his own voices with the
common chorus of the dirge? I saw an unbelievable spectacle, which would not
come to belief for those who receive wonders by hearing; I saw a sea of men,
by the density of those standing together appearing to the eyes like some
water in every part; the temple was full, the forecourt of the temple was
full, the receiving square, the narrow streets, the wards, the Mese, the
sides, the spaciousness upon the roofs; everything that was seen was a
fullness of men, as if the whole inhabited world had run together to the same
place at the suffering. And a spectacle was set before all, that holy flower
being borne upon a golden couch. How downcast were the faces of all who looked
on. How tearful the eyes. Hands striking against one another. Groans besides
declaring the pain in the heart. It did not seem to me at that hour (and
perhaps not to the others of those then present) that the gold was flashing
its natural grace. But both the gleams of the stones and the weavings of gold
and the sparkles of silver and the light from the =9.464= fire, much and
abundant, stretched along the sides on either hand by the continuity of the
lamps of wax, all were blackened together with the mourning and all shared in
the common dejection. Then also the great David used his own hymnodies for the
dirges and, exchanging the bright choir-standing for the wailing and mournful,
he called forth the dirges by the melodies, and when every pleasure of souls
had been driven away at that time, the tear alone was in pleasure for men.

Since then reason has been so overcome by the suffering, it would be time for
the exhausted part of the mind to be strengthened as far as possible through
the counsel of reasonings. For it is no small danger, if in this we disobey
the voice of the Apostle, to be condemned together with those who have no
hope. For he says, as we have just now heard from the one reading, that we
must not grieve over those who have fallen asleep; for this suffering belongs
only to those who have no hope. But someone, I think, of the more
faint-hearted would say that the divine Apostle commands impossibilities and
oversteps nature with his injunctions. For how is it possible for one living
in nature to be lifted above the suffering and not to be mastered by grief at
such a spectacle, when death has not fallen in season in old age, but in the
first age the bloom is quenched by death, and the ray of the eyes is covered
by the eyelids, and the redness of the cheek changes into paleness, and the
mouth is mastered by silence, and the flower upon the lip is blackened, and
this seems hard not only to those who begot, but also to everyone looking upon
the suffering? What then shall we say to these things? We shall not =9.465=
speak, brothers, a word of our own, but we shall set beside you the saying
that was read to us from the Gospel. For you heard the Lord saying: Suffer the
children and do not hinder them from coming to me; for of such is the kingdom
of the heavens. Therefore even if the child has departed from you, yet she has
run to the Master. She closed the eye to you, but she opened it to the eternal
light. She stood away from your table, but she was added to the angelic
table. The plant was pulled up from here, but it was planted in paradise. She
was transferred from a kingdom to a kingdom. She put off the flower of the
purple, but she put on the vesture of the kingdom above. Shall I tell you the
matter of the divine garment? It is not linen nor wool nor the threads from
silkworms. Hear David, from where he says the garments are woven for God: You
put on confession and magnificence, wrapping yourself in light as in a
garment. Do you see of what sort she has exchanged for what sort? The beauty
of the body no longer appearing grieves you, for you do not see the true
beauty of her soul, with which she now exults in the festival of the heavenly
ones. How fair is that eye that sees God. How sweet the mouth that is adorned
with the divine hymnodies.

For out of the mouth of infants and sucklings you have perfected praise. How
fair the hands that have never worked evil. How beautiful the feet that have
not stepped upon wickedness, nor set their own track upon the way of sinners.
How fair is the whole appearance of that soul, not adorned with the gleams of
stones, but shining forth with simplicity and harmlessness. But perhaps it
grieves you =9.466= that she did not come to old age.

What, tell me, do you see as fair in old age? Is it fair that the eyes grow
dim, that the cheek is wrinkled, that the teeth fall from the mouth and
produce a stammering in the tongue, that the hand trembles and one stoops
toward the earth, and limps with the foot and leans upon those who lead by the
hand, and is beside oneself in the heart and speaks amiss with the voice, such
sufferings as necessarily happen to this age? And are we indignant over this,
that she did not come to the experience of such things? And yet it is fitting
to rejoice with those whose life did not admit the experience of the gloomy
things, and who neither perceived the painful things here nor will come to
know any of the gloomy things there. For such a soul, which has nothing for
which it should come to judgment, does not fear Gehenna, does not dread
judgment, remains fearless and unstruck, no evil conscience bringing on the
fear of the judgment. But she ought, he says, to have come to the measure of
age and to have been gladdened in a bridal chamber. But the true Bridegroom
will say to you toward this, that the heavenly bridal-room is better, that
chamber is more honorable, in which there is no fear of widowhood. Of which of
the good things then, tell me, has she been deprived, having put off this
fleshly life? Shall I tell you the good things of life? Griefs and pleasures,
angers and fears, hopes and desires. These and such things are those with
which we have been interwoven according to the present life. What evil then
has she suffered who has been set free from so many tyrants? For each passion,
whenever it prevails over the soul, becomes a tyrant of us, having enslaved
the reasonings. Or does it grieve us that she was not worn down through
birth-pangs, that she was not crushed through the cares =9.467= of
child-rearing, that she did not receive the like pains which those who begot
her endured over her? But such things are worthy of blessings, not of
wailings. For to come to be in no evil is better than according to human
nature. Thus also the wise Solomon in his own writing blesses the one who has
departed before the one who remains, and the great David says that the sojourn
in the flesh is worthy of dirge and lamentation. And yet both were illustrious
according to the kingdom, sharing by authority in all the pleasant things
according to life, and they were not bent toward the present enjoyment, but
having the desire of the unspeakable good things set forth in the bodiless
life they made the life in the flesh a misfortune. I have heard David in many
places in the sacred psalmodies desiring to come outside of such necessity, in
which he says now that My soul longs and faints for the courts of the Lord,
and now, Bring my soul out of prison. Likewise also Jeremiah judges that day
worthy of a curse which began such a life for him. And it is possible to find
many such voices of the ancient saints borne in the divine Scripture, who
through desire of the true life were weighed down by the sojourn in the flesh.
Thus also the great Abraham once was eagerly bringing the beloved son to God
through sacrifice, knowing that the transfer would be for the child toward the
better and more divine. And as many of you as are experienced in the history
do not at all fail to know =9.468= the narratives concerning him. For what
does the Scripture say? That while Abraham is young a promise concerning the
child comes from God, but when he had passed his prime and had already been
withered by time, when nature ceases increasing herself, old age no longer
obeying the impulses, then against human hopes the promise is brought to
fulfillment, and the child Isaac is born; and when a measured time had passed,
having run up like some sapling into beauty and stature, he was sweet to the
eyes of the parents, shining with the beauty of youth. Then the testing and
trial of the soul is brought to Abraham, whether he discerns accurately the
better in the nature of the things that are, whether he does not look toward
the present life, and God says to him: Offer up your son through sacrifice for
a whole burnt-offering. You know altogether, as many of you as are fathers and
have children and have been taught by nature the affection toward offspring,
how Abraham would reasonably have been disposed, if he looked only toward the
present life, if he were a slave of nature, if he reckoned the sweetness of
life in the present life. And why do I speak of him, leaving the woman, the
weaker part of human nature? If she too had not been educated by the husband
in the more divine things, if she did not know that the hidden life is better
than the apparent, she would not have permitted the husband to work such
things against the child; for she would altogether, moved together with the
maternal bowels, have poured herself about the child and, having entwined him
with her arms, would have received the mortal blow before him. Would she not
have spoken these words to Abraham? Spare the child, husband, do not become an
evil tale of life, let us not become a myth for the time =9.469= after this,
do not begrudge the son his life, do not deprive him of the sweet ray; a
bridal-chamber, not a tomb, is what fathers make it their care to provide for
children, a wedding crown not a murderous sword, a marriage lamp not a funeral
fire; these things robbers and enemies work upon children, not the hands of
fathers; but if the evil must altogether come to be, let Sarah's eye not see
Isaac being put to death; see, drive the sword through both, beginning from me
the wretched one; one blow will suffice for the two, let the mound become
common over both, let one stele recite the common misfortune. These and such
things Sarah would altogether have gone through, if she did not see with her
eyes those things which are unseen to us. For she knew that the end of the
life in the flesh becomes a beginning of the more divine life for those who
have been transferred; he leaves shadows, he takes hold of truth, he lets go
deceits and wanderings and tumults, and he finds those good things which are
beyond eye and ear and heart; neither will eros pain him nor will a filthy
desire distort him, nor will pride puff him up, nor will any other passion of
those that grieve the soul trouble him, but God becomes all things to him.

Therefore he eagerly gives the child to God. And what of the great Job? When,
having been stripped all at once of all that he had, before he could gather up
his soul after the preceding blows, the last blow was announced to him, how
did he receive the misfortune concerning the children? He had three daughters,
and seven sons. He was blessed for his goodly offspring; for being so many
they were all one by the affection with one another, not living divided each
=9.470= by himself, but all going to one another through the circular kindness
they continued gladdening one another in turn and being gladdened. And indeed
then also by rotation the banquet was at the elder of the brothers; the
mixing-bowls were full, the table of the eatables was full, the cups were in
hands, spectacles as was likely besides these and hearings and all
drinking-party cheer; toasts, kindnesses, sports, smiles; all that it is
likely should be luxuriated in a gathering of young people at a feast.
What then upon these things? At the height of the enjoyment of the sweetest
things, the roof having been shaken down upon them, the banquet becomes a tomb
of the ten children, and the mixing-bowl is mixed with the blood of the young,
and the eatables were defiled with the gore from the bodies. When such a
misfortune had been announced to Job (behold for me in the word the athlete,
not only that you may wonder at the victor, for small is the gain from wonder,
but that you may emulate the man in like things and the athlete may become a
trainer for you, anointing the soul by his own example toward endurance and
courage in the time of the grappling of the trials), what then did the man do?
Did he say anything ignoble and faint-hearted by word, or show it through
posture, either tearing the cheek with the nails or plucking the hairs of the
head or sprinkling himself with dust or scourging the breast with the hands or
casting himself upon the earth or setting dirge-singers about himself or
calling the names of the departed and wailing at the memory? There is none of
these. But the messenger of the evils related the misfortune concerning the
children, and he both heard and at once philosophized concerning the nature of
the things that are, saying from where the things that are come and by whom
they are brought into generation and what it is likely should preside over the
things that are: The Lord has given, the Lord has taken away. From God, he
says, is generation =9.471= for men, and toward him is the dissolution; from
where it has been brought in, toward that it is also dissolved. God then who
has the authority of giving, the same has also the authority of taking away;
being good he counsels good things, being wise he knows what is expedient. As
it seemed good to the Lord (and it seemed good altogether well), so also he
has done; May the name of the Lord be blessed. You see how great is the height
of the athlete's greatness of nature; he transferred the time of affliction
into an examination of the philosophy concerning the things that are. For he
knew accurately that the true life is laid up through hope, and the present
life is as it were a seed of the one to come. And the things expected differ
much from the present, as much as the ear differs from the grain from which it
grows. The present life is analogous to the grain, and the expected life is
shown in the beauty of the ear; For this corruptible must put on incorruption,
and this mortal must put on immortality. Looking toward these things Job
rejoices with the children in their good-fortune, as having been loosed more
quickly from the bonds of life.

And a proof is that when the promise of God promised the double in place of
all that had been taken away, while in all the other things the recompense was
doubled, of the children alone he did not seek the doubling, but ten only have
been given in place of those taken away. And since the souls of men remain
forever, for this reason he receives the double recompense of the things that
perish, but in the case of the children those who came after are numbered
together with those born before as all living to God, and the timely death in
no way hindering the departed from =9.472= being. For death in the case of men
is nothing else but a purification of wickedness. For since our nature was
constructed at the beginning by the God of all as a certain vessel receptive
of good things, but when the enemy of our souls had poured the evil into us
through deceit the good had no place, for this reason, so that the implanted
wickedness might not be eternal for us, by a better providence the vessel is
dissolved for a time by death, in order that when the wickedness has flowed
out the human may be reshaped and restored unmixed with wickedness to the life
from the beginning. For this is the resurrection, the reconstitution of our
nature into the ancient. If then it is impossible for nature to be
reconstituted toward the better without resurrection, and resurrection cannot
come to be unless death has preceded, death would be a good, having become for
us a beginning and a way of the change toward the better. Therefore let us
cast out, brothers, the grief concerning those who have fallen asleep, which
only those endure who have no hope. And hope is Christ; to whom be the glory
and the power unto the ages of ages. Amen.
