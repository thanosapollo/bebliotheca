* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Isaeus, who was teacher of Demosthenes and became especially famous for this,
was by race an Athenian, as some relate, but as others write, a Chalcidian. He
flourished after the Peloponnesian War, as I infer from his speeches, and
extended as far as the dominance of Philip. I am not able to state an exact
time of the orator's birth and death, nor indeed anything about the man's
life, what sort of man he was, nor anything about his choice of public
affairs, whether he chose any office or a political career at all, nor
anything at all about such matters, because I have met with no such history.
For not even Hermippus, who catalogued the pupils of Isocrates and was
accurate in other things, has said anything about this orator beyond these two
points, that he heard Isocrates and that he taught Demosthenes. And he
associated with the best of the philosophers.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

It remains, then, to speak about his choice and power and what style he used.
He was a practitioner of one kind of speeches, the forensic, and he was
especially eager about this. He emulated the style of Lysias for the most
part, and unless someone were very experienced in the men and had noteworthy
practice with both, he would not easily distinguish many of the speeches, to
which of the orators they belong, but would be misled by the titles, which are
not so accurate, as is shown to me through a single writing. Yet the likeness
of style is not indistinguishable, but has certain differences neither small
nor few, both in expression and in the subject matter, about which it would be
timely to speak, as we have supposed. And since in diction he is more like
Lysias, making the beginning from this I will state the likenesses and the
differences which he has toward that one.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Pure and precise and clear, both proper and vivid and concise, and besides
this persuasive and fitting to the subjects, compact and forensic, the diction
of Isaeus is no less than that of Lysias, and in these things one would not
distinguish it. But it would seem to differ from that one in these: for the
one is more simple and ethical, and is composed more naturally and figured
more simply, and is furnished with much pleasure and charm. But that of Isaeus
would seem to be more technical and more precise than that of Lysias, and the
composition somewhat more elaborate and divided by varied figures, and by as
much as it falls short of that charm, by so much it excels in the force of
construction, and it is truly a kind of spring of the power of Demosthenes.
One would distinguish the diction in this way, then, but in the subject matter
he will find some such differences. In Lysias he will not see much artifice
either in the divisions of the matters or in the arrangement of the enthymemes
or in the working-out of them; for the man is simple. But in Isaeus he will
find these things already becoming more precise and more technical. For he
uses approaches and preliminary preparations and more technical divisions, and
he places each where it gives use, and he carries the working-out of the
arguments to a great length, and he varies the speeches with changes of
agonistic and pathetic figures. And toward the opponent he plays the knave,
and he outgenerals the judges, and he tries from every side to help the
matters for which the speech is.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

And that the difference of the men may become more evident, I will use an
image from some of the things seen. There are certain ancient paintings,
worked in colors simply and having no variety in the mixtures, but precise in
the lines and having much charm in these. And those after them are less
well-lined, but more worked out, varied with shadow and light and having their
strength in the multitude of mixtures. To the more ancient of these Lysias is
like in simplicity and charm, and Isaeus to those that have been labored and
are more technical. And there was about him among those then a reputation of
wizardry and deceit, as a man clever at crafting speeches toward the worse,
and he was slandered to this. And this is shown by one of the ancient orators
in the accusation of Demosthenes, Pytheas, as it seems to me. For having said
that all wickedness and vice from men dwells in Demosthenes, he adds in this
part as a kind of slander, that he has fed on the whole of Isaeus and the arts
of that man's speeches. And by Zeus this slander was not off the mark for
each. At least to me the speeches of Isaeus and of Demosthenes, even if they
are composed about true and just hypotheses, seem suspect because of the much
artifice, while those of Isocrates and Lysias are most of all just and true,
even if the matters in them are not such, because they show nothing knavish in
the construction, but are free and simple.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

These seemed to me to be the things that differ, from which one would be able
without difficulty to distinguish the speeches of Lysias and of Isaeus. And if
I have supposed rightly, it will be possible for whoever wishes to examine,
making the test upon the examples themselves. I will begin from the
observations about diction. There is a certain speech of Isaeus for Eumathes,
a metic among those banking at Athens, whom, being led into slavery by the
heir of the man who had freed him, one of the citizens takes away and makes
the defense concerning him. And the prooemium of the speech is of this sort:
/Gentlemen of the jury, I was formerly useful to this Eumathes justly, and
now, if there is anything in my power, I will try to join in saving him with
you. But hear a little from me, that none of you may suppose that I approached
the affairs of Eumathes from rashness or some other injustice. For when I was
trierarch in the archonship of Cephisodotus and a report was brought to my
household that I had died in the sea-fight, I having a deposit with this
Eumathes, Eumathes sent for my household and friends and showed the money
which I had with him, and he gave back all rightly and justly. In return for
which, having been saved, I both used him still more and, when he was setting
up the bank, I supplied him with silver. And after this, when Dionysius was
leading him, I took him out into freedom, knowing that he had been released in
the court by Epigenes. But about these things I will hold back./

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

There is also in Lysias one making the defense for a foreigner who is
defendant in a suit about an estate. Callimachus titles this speech
/Concerning Pherenicus for the estate of Androcleides/, and it was contested
many years earlier than the other. In which the one making the speeches about
the foreigner first shows the cause, just as the one taking the metic out into
freedom. And the prooemium of the speech is this: /It seems to me necessary,
gentlemen of the jury, to speak first to you about my friendship and that of
Pherenicus, that none of you may wonder that, having never spoken before for
any of you, I now speak for this man. For to me, gentlemen of the jury,
Cephisodotus the father of this man was a guest-friend, and when we were in
exile I lodged in Thebes with him, both I and any other of the Athenians who
wished, and having suffered many good things both privately and publicly from
him we returned to our own. When therefore these men met with the same
fortunes and arrived at Athens as exiles, thinking that I owed them the
greatest gratitude I received them so familiarly that none of those entering
would know, unless someone knew beforehand, which of us owned the house.
Pherenicus also knows, gentlemen of the jury, that many are more clever at
speaking than I and more experienced in such matters, but nevertheless he
thinks my familiarity the most trustworthy. It therefore seems to me shameful,
when this man bids and begs me to help him in what is just, to look on while
he is deprived, so far as I am able, of the things given by Androcleides./

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

How then do these prooemia differ from one another? In Lysias the opening is
pleasant and for no other reason more than that it has been spoken somehow
naturally and simply: /It seems to me necessary, gentlemen of the jury, to
speak first to you about my friendship and that of Pherenicus./ And what is
added to this appears still more unconstructed, and what has been said as a
private man might be able to speak: /that none of you may wonder that, having
never spoken before for any of you, I now speak for this man./ But in Isaeus
what seems to be simple has been constructed and it has not escaped notice
that it is rhetorical: /I was formerly useful to this Eumathes justly, and
now, if there is anything in my power, I will try to join in saving him./ For
they are loftier and less simple than those, and still more the things that
follow: /But hear a little from me, that none of you may suppose that I
approached the affairs of Eumathes from rashness or some other injustice./ For
the rashness and the injustice and the approaching the affairs of Eumathes are
more like things made than things growing of themselves. And again in Lysias
the pretext is spoken without artifice: /For to me, gentlemen of the jury,
Cephisodotus the father of this man was a guest-friend, and when we were in
exile I lodged in Thebes with him, both I and any other of the Athenians who
wished./ And the things after this are added pleasantly and in a defining way:
/and having suffered many good things both privately and publicly from him we
returned to our own./ But in Isaeus it has been phrased more elaborately and
is not far from the construction of Demosthenes: /For when I was trierarch in
the archonship of Cephisodotus and a report was brought to my household that I
had died in the sea-fight, I having a deposit with this Eumathes./ For the "a
report having been brought" and the "that I had died" and the "I having a
deposit" I at least would not say have been spoken simply. For spoken in that
way they would be more unmade: /For when I was trierarch and it was reported
to those here that I had died, this Eumathes having my deposit./

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And the remaining parts of the prooemia too one would find spoken more simply
in the one, more rhetorically in the other. And again in another contest
Isaeus, having composed a defense for a certain guardian being judged by his
own nephew, has used such a beginning: /I would have wished, gentlemen of the
jury, that Hagnotheus were not so excessively shameful toward money as to plot
against what belongs to others and to bring such suits, but that being my
nephew and master of the paternal property, not small but sufficient even to
perform liturgies, which was handed over to him by us, he would take care of
this and not desire mine; so that he would have seemed better to all, saving
it and making it more, and would have presented himself a more useful citizen
to you. But since he has destroyed it and sold it and shamefully and badly
ruined it, as I would not have wished, and trusting in clubs and preparations
of speeches has come against mine, it is necessary, as it seems, to think it a
misfortune that he is such while being a kinsman, and to defend against the
things he has charged and the slanders outside the matter with which he has
attacked me, as eagerly as we are able toward you./ And Lysias, having written
a speech for a man accused of bad guardianship by the brothers of his own
wife, has used such a prooemium: /It is not enough, gentlemen of the jury, for
guardians, as many troubles as they have through the guardianship, but even
while they save the properties of their friends many are vexatiously
prosecuted by the orphans. Which has now happened to me also. For I, gentlemen
of the jury, having been left guardian of the money of Hippocrates and having
administered the property rightly and justly and having handed over to the
sons, when they passed scrutiny, the money of which I was left guardian, am
now vexatiously prosecuted by them unjustly./

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

I think few words are needed, that this has been spoken simply and pleasantly
and shows a character not fabricated but natural. For: /It is not enough,
gentlemen of the jury, for guardians, as many troubles as they have through
the guardianship/ no one would say belongs to an orator, but to any private
man who has come into an unjust contest. But all would say that that of Isaeus
has been made rhetorically and has been more solemnly beautified in speech,
for: /I would have wished, gentlemen of the jury, that Hagnotheus were not so
excessively shameful toward money as to plot against what belongs to others./
And still more in Lysias it has been spoken very charmingly and simply: /For
I, gentlemen of the jury, having been left guardian of the money of
Hippocrates and having administered the property rightly and justly and having
handed over to the sons, when they passed scrutiny, the money./ But the other
more distinctly and not as a private man would have composed it: /but that
being my nephew and master of the paternal property, not small but sufficient
even to perform liturgies, which was handed over to him by us, he would take
care of this./ And I will still mention one kind, from which the difference of
the men will be most evident.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Each supposes a private man and one who avoids business and young, forced
contrary to his own choice and nature to speak in court, Lysias in the speech
against Archebiades in this manner: /As soon as Archebiades brought this suit
against me, gentlemen of the jury, I went to him saying that I was young and
inexperienced in affairs and had no need to enter a court. I therefore ask you
not to think my age a windfall, but taking my friends and your own to recount
about the debt, from where it has come. And if you seem to those men to speak
the truth, you will need no trouble, but taking your own you will go away. And
you are just to omit nothing, but to say all, since I am younger than the
contract, so that having heard about things we do not know we may deliberate
about what you say; if somehow it becomes clear whether you are unjustly
aiming at mine or justly seeking to recover your own. Though I challenged him
in these things he never yet was willing to come together nor to make a speech
about the things he charged nor to submit to arbitration, until you
established the law about the arbitrators./ And Isaeus, in a dispute about a
plot of land occupied by the demesmen to whom the land was mortgaged, brings
him in using this beginning: /I would have wished most of all, gentlemen of
the jury, not to be wronged by even one of the citizens, and if not, to meet
with such opponents toward whom I would have cared nothing in differing. But
now the most painful of all things has happened to me. For I am wronged by the
demesmen, whom it is not easy to look on while they rob, and it is unpleasant
to be at enmity, with whom there is necessity both to sacrifice together and
to make common associations. It is hard, then, to contest against many; for
their number contributes a great part toward their seeming to speak the truth;
nevertheless, because of trusting the facts, though many and difficult things
fall upon me, I did not think I ought to shrink from trying through you to
obtain what is just. I therefore beg you to have pardon, if being younger I
have dared to speak in court; for because of those who wrong me I am forced
contrary to my own manner to do some such thing. And I will try to tell you
from the beginning, as I am able, in the fewest words about the matter./

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

Who then would not agree that the young and private and business-avoiding man
of Lysias is a kind of archetype of the truth, differing from that in nothing
at all, but the other a kind of copy and not escaping notice that it has been
fabricated by rhetorical art? For both the words and the thoughts show in that
one the self-grown, but in this one the constructed. The one at least has used
a beginning, that he was young and inexperienced in affairs and had no need to
enter a court. And he adds very ethically: /I therefore ask you not to think
my age a windfall./ And what is after this he says as it was natural both to
happen and to be spoken: that he thought they should go to the common friends
as arbitrators /and if you seem to those men to speak the truth, you will need
no trouble, but taking your own you will go away./ And having gone through the
rest in a good character he finally adds: /Though I challenged him in these
things he never was willing to come together./ But Isaeus places this part,
the /having been forced contrary to his own judgment to speak in court while
being young/, at the end of the prooemium. And he begins from a thought not
paltry, by Zeus, nor private, saying that it is more grievous to be at enmity
with such people, with whom there is necessity to share in the most honored
things. Then he acquits himself of something that is going to pain him, namely
that the demesmen being many contest against him. And he composes the words
not paltrily, by Zeus, nor as a private man would: /For I am wronged by the
demesmen/, he says, /whom it is not easy to look on while they rob, and it is
unpleasant to be at enmity, with whom there is necessity both to sacrifice
together and to make common associations./ For the unpleasant enmity and the
common associations are composed more technically rather than more simply, and
still the /though many and difficult things fall upon me, I did not think I
ought to shrink from trying through you to obtain what is just./ For least of
all would a private man speak thus: /I did not think I ought to shrink/ nor
yet the /to try through you to obtain what is just/, but somehow more in that
way: /with so many difficult things falling upon me I have been forced to take
refuge with you, in order that I may obtain what is just through you./

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

I think then that even from these things the difference of the men is not
unclear. Nevertheless it will be more evident also from the things about to be
said, and most of all from the demonstrative and pathetic speeches, in which
Lysias is somewhat simpler both in the composition of the words and in the
commonness of the figures, but this man more varied. For one looking would
find many things in him [lost] as in this: /From where ought the things said
to be believed, by the gods? Not from the witnesses? I think so. And from
where the witnesses? Not from the tortures? Likely so. And from where ought
the speeches of these men to be disbelieved? Not from their fleeing the tests?
A great necessity. I therefore appear as one pursuing these things and
bringing the matters to tortures, but this man as one setting them upon
slanders and speeches, which anyone wishing to have the advantage would do.
And he ought, if he had any just thought and was not seeking to cheat your
judgments, not by Zeus to do these things, but to come to the accounting with
witnesses and to examine each of the things in the speech in this manner,
inquiring of me: how many eisphorai do you reckon? So many; brought in
according to how much silver? According to so much and so much; according to
what decrees? These; who have received these? These men; and witnessing these
things to examine the decrees, the number of the eisphorai, the things brought
in, those who received them, and if they were eute , to trust the account, and
if not, now to produce witnesses, if anything of what I calculated for them
was false./

:PROPERTIES:

These are disconnected and from questioning. Which Lysias has used least, but
Demosthenes, who took the starting-points from this man, more unsparingly, for
example: /Do you not then speak of pay? someone will say. Yes, and at once the
same organization of all, men of Athens, so that each, receiving his share of
the common things, may provide whatever the city needs. It is possible to keep
quiet; remaining at home you are better, freed from doing something shameful
by necessity through want. Something such happens, as also the present things;
being himself a soldier from these same receipts, as is just, on behalf of the
fatherland. There is someone of us outside the age; as much as this man now
receiving without order does not help, these things receiving in equal order,
overseeing and administering all that ought to be done. And wholly, neither
taking away nor adding except some little, having removed the disorder, I
brought the city into order, having made the same order of receiving, of
campaigning, of judging, of doing this, whatever each has according to age and
of which there is occasion./ And those things according to compression and
ventured by being composed briefly and crookedly and from the unexpected, and
not recognized by all nor readily: /And this man, the most cruel of all men,
when they do not provide witnesses to give, before whom they say they have
given back to us, pretends to trust those men more, that they have given back
to us, than us, that we have not received. And yet it is clear to all, as it
seems, who also robbed this man's father when he was in possession of his
rights, that they would not have given back to us willingly, and being so we
would not have been able to exact it./ For this is also the figure which
Demosthenes has often used: /Do you then think that those who would have been
able to do him no harm, and would themselves perhaps have guarded against
suffering, he chooses rather to deceive than to force by foretelling, but to
make war on you from a proclamation?/ And still such things: /For he to whom,
the things that existed outside the mortgaged properties had been spent on
liturgies, and to whom borrowing no one would still have given anything more
upon them, it being fitting for me having paid the rents to have them without
dispute, these men having brought so great a suit and claiming they are their
own prevented me from making the repair from them./ And why must one lengthen
by setting out more? For one would find many of Isaeus both in composition and
in the figures changed from the diction of Lysias, but like the force of
Demosthenes.

:PROPERTIES:

And having said also about the subject matter that he is more clever than
Lysias at managing both whole speeches and their parts, and doing nothing
outside the art which Demosthenes afterward used much, I wish also to furnish
the proofs for these things. And the speech will be summary and as toward
those who have read the man; for it is not possible to set examples of all.
For instance, the narrations he sometimes places unprepared and concise and
anticipating none of the demonstrative things in the fitting place, as he does
in the speech against Medon and in that against Hagnotheus and in the dispute
against the demesmen about the plot of land and in many others. And sometimes
having divided them by heads and setting the proofs beside each of them he
both lengthens more and goes out of the form of narration, using what is
expedient. Of this form are both the defense against Hermon about the surety
and the dispute against Eucleides about the redemption of the plot of land and
the appeal for Euphiletus against the deme of the Erchieis. For in these
speeches, the narrations being longer, he does not place them all at once, but
dividing them by parts he both brings in the witnesses at each head and
furnishes the other proofs, fearing, at least so far as it seems to me, lest
the speech become hard to follow through the multitude of heads and go to sea,
and lest the proofs, all gathered into one place, being many and about many
matters, confuse the clarity. Therefore after the narrations thus managed he
no longer confirms the preceding demonstrations with many speeches, nor is he
like the young writers of arts, but he thinks he ought to destroy the proofs
of the opponents.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

And sometimes he prepares certain matters before the narrations in advance and
anticipates the things that he thinks will make them more trustworthy or more
useful in some other way, as he is found to have done in the suit against
Aristogeiton and Archippus, in which someone disputing an estate, being
brother of the deceased, challenges the one who has the hidden money to a
production of visible property, and the one holding the estate enters a
special plea against the summons, saying that the money has been given to him
according to a will. And there being a double dispute, one about whether the
will has come to be or not, and the other, the will already being disputed,
who ought to hold the estate, first having given the speech about the laws and
having proved in this part that the estate under litigation ought not to be
held before a trial, he thus comes to the narration, through which he proves
that the will has not even come to be from the deceased. And not even this
narration does he place having somehow simply compressed it and without
construction, but this too, being longer, he divides with certain cuttings and
at each kind he brings up witnesses and reads challenges and furnishes
contracts and uses all the proofs and signs and the tests from probabilities.
And I would be able to furnish many other narrations also, managed toward the
expedient by the orator with preliminary preparations, preparations,
divisions, exchanges of places, transfers of matters, by the heads having been
reversed, by the things done not having been spoken according to the times, by
their not all nor at once being said as it was natural for them to have been
done nor as a private man would speak, by countless other such ways. But I
have neither sufficient time to speak about all, setting out at once on each
the art, as I would have wished, nor does the speech toward those who know the
matters have its trust in the multitude of examples, but for such people even
the brief showing is enough.

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

And I will speak also summarily about this form, what opinion I have and in
what I think Isaeus differs from Lysias. One reading the narrations of Lysias
would suppose nothing to be spoken according to art or knavery, but as nature
and truth bring it, being ignorant of this very thing of the art, that to
imitate nature was its greatest work. But upon the narrations of Isaeus he
would suffer the opposite, to suppose nothing spoken self-grown and without
trouble, not even if some things that happened by chance have been said, but
all from construction and engineered toward deceit or some other knavery. And
he would trust the one even when speaking false things, but to the other, not
even if he speaks truth, would he attend without suspicion. And in the
demonstrative parts Isaeus would seem to differ from Lysias both by speaking
not according to some enthymeme but according to an epicheireme and by
amplifying not briefly but at length, not simply but precisely, and making the
matters more terrible and making the passions more noble. For in these things
he is no less evident as having given the starting-points to the art of
Demosthenes, and not pursuing the simplicity of Lysias, as it is possible to
infer from many speeches, or rather from all those written by him. And if it
is necessary also to use examples, lest we seem to speak things unproved, I
will do this too, taking in hand the speech for Euphiletus, in which one of
those voted out summons the deme of the Erchieis into court as being driven
from the citizenship unjustly. For a certain law was written by the Athenians
that there be an examination of the citizens by demes, and that the one voted
out by the demesmen not share in the citizenship, but that those unjustly
voted out have an appeal to the court, having summoned the demesmen, and if
they are convicted the second time, they be sold and the money be public.
According to this law Euphiletus, having summoned the Erchieis as having voted
against him unjustly, arranges this contest. The matters have been stated
precisely beforehand and have been confirmed through the witnesses, and the
things by which he wishes to make the testimonies firm are these, as I have
the opinion, all precisely worked out. And let whoever wishes judge whether I
have recognized the fitting things about them:

* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:

/That, then, gentlemen of the jury, this Euphiletus is our brother, you have
heard not only us but also all the kinsmen testifying. And consider first our
father, for the sake of what he would lie and introduce this man as his son
not being so. For you will find all who do such things either having no
legitimate children or being forced through poverty to introduce foreign men,
in order that they may be helped somewhat from them through their having
become Athenians. Neither of these belongs to our father. For we two sons are
legitimate to him; so that he would not have introduced this man through want
of children. Nor indeed needing nourishment and abundance from this man. For
he has a sufficient livelihood and besides this it has been testified to you
that he has reared this man from childhood and trained him and introduced him
into the phratry members; and these are not small expenses. So that it is not
likely, gentlemen of the jury, that our father, being helped in nothing, would
attempt so unjust a deed. Nor indeed would any man suppose me so completely
mindless as to testify false things for this man, in order that I may divide
the paternal things among more. For I would not even have the power afterward
to dispute that this man is not a brother. For none of you would endure
hearing my voice, if now, putting myself under penalty, I testify that he is
our brother, and afterward I should appear contradicting these things. Not
only us, then, gentlemen of the jury, is it likely to have testified the
truth, but also the other kinsmen. For consider first that those who have our
sisters would never have testified false things about this man. For this man's
mother had become a stepmother to our sisters. And stepmothers and the
daughters of a former wife are somehow accustomed for the most part to differ
with one another. So that if this man were from some other man to the
stepmother and not from our father, our sisters would never, gentlemen of the
jury, have allowed their own husbands to testify. And indeed neither would the
uncle, being on our mother's side, and belonging in no way to this man, surely
have been willing, gentlemen of the jury, to testify a false testimony for
this man's mother, through which a clear harm comes to us, if being a
foreigner we introduce this man as a brother to ourselves. Still then,
gentlemen of the jury, besides these things, how would any of you convict of
false testimony this Demaratus and Hegemon and Nicostratus? Who first will
never appear to have practiced anything shameful, and then being kinsmen to us
and knowing us all have testified to this Euphiletus each his own kinship. So
that I would gladly even ask of the most solemn of those contesting against
us, whether he could show himself an Athenian from anywhere else than from
these things, from which we also show Euphiletus. For I do not think he would
say anything else than that the mother is a citizen and a wedded wife and the
father a citizen; and that he speaks these things truly he would furnish for
himself the kinsmen as witnesses; then, gentlemen of the jury, if these men
were in danger, they would have thought it right that you trust their own
household testifying rather than the accusers. But now, when we furnish all
these things, will they think it right that you obey their own speeches rather
than the father of Euphiletus and me and the brother and the phratry members
and all our kinship? And indeed these men, risking nothing for anyone, do
these things for the sake of private enmity, but we testify putting ourselves
all under penalty. And besides the testimonies, gentlemen of the jury, first
the mother of Euphiletus, whom these men agree is a citizen, wished to swear
an oath before the arbitrator at Delphinium, that this Euphiletus is truly
from herself and from our father. And yet whom was it more fitting than that
woman herself to know this? Then, gentlemen of the jury, our father, whom it
is likely after this man's mother to know his own son best, this man both then
and now wishes to swear that this Euphiletus is truly his son from a citizen
and wedded wife. Besides these things then, gentlemen of the jury, I happened
to be thirteen years old, as I also said before, when this man was born, and I
am ready to swear that this Euphiletus is truly my brother of the same father.
So that, gentlemen of the jury, you would justly also think our oaths more
trustworthy than the speeches of these men. For we, knowing precisely, are
willing to swear about him, but these men say these things having heard them
from those at variance with this man or themselves fabricating. And besides
these things, gentlemen of the jury, we furnish the kinsmen as witnesses both
before the arbitrators and before you, whom it is not right to disbelieve, but
these men, when Euphiletus brought the former suit against the common body of
the demesmen and against the then demarch, who has now died, though the
arbitrator had the arbitration for two years were not able to find any
testimony that this man is of some other father than ours. And to the
arbitrators these were the greatest signs that these men were lying, and both
of them gave judgment against them. And take for me the testimony of the
former arbitration. -- Testimony. -- That they then lost the arbitration,
then, you have heard. And I think it right, gentlemen of the jury, just as
these men would have said this to be a great sign that he is not of
Hegesippus, if the arbitrators had given judgment against them, so now such a
witness for us that we speak the truth, since they themselves were judged to
wrong this man, being an Athenian and having been first enrolled with
authority, by later striking him out. That therefore this Euphiletus is our
brother and your citizen and was unjustly outraged by those who combined in
the deme, I think you have heard sufficiently, gentlemen of the jury./

This is the character of the speeches of Isaeus and these the things that
differ from the manner of Lysias. And nothing prevents, having also taken them
summarily through the shortest showing, from saying the most evident things,
that Lysias seems to me to pursue the truth more, but Isaeus the art, and the
one to aim at the charming, the other at the forceful. And if someone should
look aside at these things as small and paltry, he would no longer become a
sufficient judge of them. For the likenesses will confuse his judgment, so as
not to distinguish the proper character of each. And about these things, what
opinion I have has been shown by me.

And I wish now also to give the account about the other orators, that no one
may think that through ignorance I have left them out, being eminent and
thought worthy of a name not moderate, or that by flight from the labor,
choosing the easiest of the works, I have let go the examination about them.
For I neither was ignorant of those whom all know, nor would I have hesitated
to write about them, if the writing was going to bring out something useful.
But considering that no one became better than Isocrates at the poetic
construction and this so-called elevated and processional thing, I left out
willingly those whom I knew to succeed less in these forms, seeing Gorgias of
Leontini falling out of the mean and becoming childish in many places, and
Alcidamas his hearer being thicker in diction and emptier, and Theodorus of
Byzantium some ancient one and neither precise in the arts nor having given a
sufficient examination in the agonistic speeches, and Anaximenes of Lampsacus
wishing to be a kind of square in all the forms of speeches (for he has
written histories and has left compositions about the poet and has brought out
arts, and he has also touched deliberative and forensic contests), yet not
complete in any of these forms but weak and unpersuasive in all, as I observe.
I therefore thought I ought not, Isocrates excelling all these in all things,
to make any account about those, nor yet about any of those who lived with
Isocrates and imitated the character of that man's expression, I mean
Theodectes and Theopompus and Naucrates and Ephorus and Philiscus and
Cephisodorus and many others. For neither are those men fit to be judged
against the power of Isocrates. Having thought that man the most distinguished
of those adorned according to this manner, I no longer

but he is wholly in the technical-writing and epideictic, and he has not left
forensic or deliberative speeches, and one would be able to say the same
things also about Critias and about Zoilus except insofar as in the characters
Zes of expression they differ from one another; of these men, I say, and of
those near to these, thinking Lysias to differ and to excel, copying him as an
archetype, I made that man the canon of this choice of speeches. And if
someone should ask me for the sake of what I added the third, Isaeus, being an
emulator of Lysias, I would say to him this cause, that he seems to me to have
furnished the seeds and the beginnings of the force of Demosthenes, which
there is no one who does not think to have become the most complete of all.
For these causes, then, I took up these men only. And if I thought it right to
write about all, the speech would have fallen out for me into emptinesses and
besides having nothing or not much useful would have been lengthened into a
writing somehow endless and not commensurate. And about these things enough.
And I will make another beginning of the speech, speaking about Demosthenes
and Hyperides and as third Aeschines. For the most complete rhetoric and the
power of agonistic speeches seem to be in these men.
