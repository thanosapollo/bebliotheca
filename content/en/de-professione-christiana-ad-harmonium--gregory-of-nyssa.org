OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA, TO HARMONIUS, ON WHAT THE PROFESSION OF A
CHRISTIAN IS

Just as those who are subject to the daily taxes do with those in power: if
the debt is left outstanding for several days, they pay off at once the sums
due in particular, in a lump sum; so, if I should happen upon some resource,
this too will I do toward your Magnificence. For I owe you the continual
sending of letters (since for Christians the promise is a debt), and I wish
now to make good the deficiency of writing that I have made in the past
involuntarily, stretching out the measure of the letter so much that, divided
into the usual measures of letters, it may be counted as many instead of one.
And that I may not idle to no purpose at the length of the letter, I think it
well to imitate through this epistolary voice our face to face meeting
together. For you remember surely that the occasions of our words with one
another were always meditations toward virtue and exercises 8,=1.130= toward
godliness, you ever critically weighing what was said and accepting nothing of
the things said without examination, and we resolving, as time advanced, the
things that each moment were sought from the sequence of the discourse. If
then it were possible that this should happen now also, that the starting
point of the word be given from your intelligence, that would be by all means
better; for the gain would thence arise for both of us, both from seeing one
another (what among the things of life is sweeter to me?) and from our aged
lyre being stirred, struck as it were by a plectrum from your wisdom. But
since the necessity of life prepares to separate us in body, even though we
join our souls together continually, it would be necessary also to be deprived
of your countenance, if ever some objection to the word should appear among us
in what follows. And it would be well first to set forth some hypothesis of
the things profitable to the soul for the scope of the letter, and then to
occupy ourselves with the word proposed.

Let it then be sought from us, as in a problem: What is the profession of the
Christian? For perhaps the inquiry about this will prove not without profit.
For if what is signified by this name were found accurately, we should receive
much cooperation toward the life of virtue, truly being what we are called
8,=1.131=, striving after this through an exalted manner of life. For just as
if one should desire to be called a physician or an orator or a geometrician,
he would not accept to be convicted of the name by his untrainedness, not
being found upon trial to be what he is named; but one who wishes in truth to
be addressed as some one of these, lest the calling be convicted of a false
name, establishes the address by the very pursuit: in the same way we too, if
in examining we should find the true scope of the Christian profession, would
not choose not to be that which the name promises on our behalf, lest the tale
about the ape, which is current among the outsiders, apply to us as well.

For they say that a certain one of the 8,=1.132= jugglers in the city of
Alexander, having trained an ape, made it take dancing postures through some
cleverness, and put on it a dancing mask and clothing suited to the
performance, and, having set a chorus about it, was ambitious over the ape as
it writhed itself to the rhythm of the song and hid its nature through
everything it did and appeared to be. And when the theater was possessed by
the novelty of the spectacle, one of the wittier ones who were present, by
some jest, showed to those who had gathered to the spectacle that the ape was
an ape. For while all were shouting and applauding the turns of the ape,
moving in rhythm to the song and the music, they say he threw upon the
orchestra those sweetmeats that draw the greediness of such beasts; and the
ape, hesitating at nothing, when it saw the almonds scattered before the
chorus, forgetting both the dance and the applause and the adornments of its
costume, ran at them and seized what it found with the palms of its hands;
and, lest the mask be a hindrance to its mouth, it eagerly stripped from
itself with its claws the artificial form, tearing at it, so that it moved the
spectators all at once to laughter instead of the praise and the wonder,
appearing ugly and 8,=1.133= ridiculous through the remnants of the mask
showing through. As then the artificial getup did not suffice for that one to
be counted a man, his nature being convicted in the greediness for sweetmeats,
so those who have not truly fashioned their own nature by faith will easily be
convicted in the greedinesses of the devil, being something else than what
they profess. For instead of a fig or an almond or some such thing, vainglory
and love of honor and love of money and love of pleasure, and whatever other
things of such a sort the evil market of the devil sets before the
greedinesses of men instead of sweetmeats, easily leads the apelike souls to
conviction, those who playact Christianity through a formed imitation, making
away with the mask of temperance or of meekness or of some other virtue in the
season of the passions. It is necessary then to understand the profession of
Christianity; for perhaps we should become what the name wills, so as not,
being transformed by the bare confession and the outward token of the name
only, to be something else than what appears, and to be exposed to him who
sees the secret things.

Let us then first examine what Christianity is understood to be from the very
appellation. There is indeed from the wiser ones some greater and certainly
more 8,=1.134= magnificently conceived understanding to be found, joined to
the loftiness of the name. But what we are able to compass concerning this
name is this: the name of Christ, if it were transferred to the clearer and
more familiar word, signifies the king, the holy Scripture showing forth the
royal dignity by this word with a certain special usage of its own. But since,
as the Scripture says, the divine is unspeakable and incomprehensible, above
every grasping conception, the prophets and apostles, borne by the Holy
Spirit, of necessity lead us by many names and meanings toward the
understanding of the incorruptible nature, one directing us to one, another to
another of the thoughts befitting God; so that the authority over all is made
plain through the name of kingdom, and the unmixed with every passion and
every evil, the free, is named by the names of virtue, each being understood
and spoken with reference to the better; so that it is the same thing to be
righteousness and wisdom and power and truth and goodness and life and
salvation and incorruption and the unchangeable and the unalterable, and every
lofty thought whatever signified through such names, for all to be and to be
called 8,=1.135= Christ.

If then the comprehension of every lofty meaning is understood in the name of
Christ (for in the higher of the things signified the rest also are contained,
as each of the others is contemplated in the concept of the kingdom), perhaps
the understanding of the interpretation of Christianity would come to be for
us accordingly. For if we are coinamed with the interpretive names that
surpass the incorruptible nature, we who are joined to him through faith in
him, it is necessary that all the meanings contemplated about that
incorruptible nature together with this name be ours also, and that our
homonymy take place with respect to those accordingly. For as through the
participation of Christ we have received the appellation of Christian, so it
is fitting that accordingly we be drawn also into the communion of all the
lofty names. And just as in a chain, the one who pulled the clasp at the end
would draw through the one those fastened naturally to one another, so, since
the name of Christ has fastened to it naturally the rest of the interpretive
names of that ineffable and many-formed blessedness, it would be necessary
that the one who grasped the one be drawn together with the one into the rest.

If then one should put on the name of Christ but not show in his life the
things contemplated together with this name, such a one bears false witness to
the name, according to the example set before us, having put upon the ape a
lifeless mask molded to a human  8,=1.136= likeness. For it is not possible
for
Christ not to be righteousness and purity and truth and estrangement from
every evil; nor is it possible for one to be a Christian (a true Christian, I
mean) without showing in himself the communion of those names. We will then
say, as one would interpret the thought as a definition of Christianity, that
Christianity is an imitation of the divine nature. And let no one assail the
word as burdensome and exceeding the lowliness of our nature; for the
definition has not overstepped nature. For if one reckon the first
constitution of man, he will find through the scriptural teachings that the
definition has not gone beyond the measures of our nature. For the first
making of man was according to an imitation of the likeness of God, as Moses
philosophized concerning man, in which he says that God made man, after the
image of God made he him; and the promise of Christianity is that man be
brought back to his ancient good fortune.

If then man of old was the likeness of God, perhaps we have not missed the
mark in declaring as our definition that Christianity is an imitation of the
divine nature. Great then is the promise of the appellation. But it would be a
time to investigate whether it is without danger for the one who bears the
name not to be such according to 8,=1.137= his life. And what is sought would
become clear from examples. Let one be supposed who professes a scriptural
art, and let there be given him by the ruler a command to paint in places
settled far away the figure of the king. If then, having stamped upon the
board some ugly and ill-formed shape, he should call this disgraceful painting
an image of the king, would not the authority likely be indignant, the
archetypal beauty being insulted through that bad painting among those who do
not know? For as upon the image the form is shown, of such a sort the
archetypal form must be reckoned to be. If then the definition says that
Christianity is an imitation of God, one who has not yet received the word of
the mystery, whatever sort of life he sees in us believed to be guided aright
according to the imitation of God, such a one will think the divine to be. So
that if he sees examples of every good, he will believe the divine, which is
revered by us, to be good; but if some passionate and beastlike one, being
transformed at different times to different passions and putting on many forms
of beasts in character (for beasts may plainly be seen being formed in the
misleadings of our nature), and then such a one should call himself a
Christian, it being plain to all that the promise of the name proclaims an
imitation of God, he will make the divine believed by us blameworthy in his
own life among the unbelievers. Wherefore the Scripture also announces against
such some more fearful threatening, saying: Woe, because of whom my name is
blasphemed among the nations. And it seems to me that the Lord, leading us
especially toward this thought, said this to those able to hear 8,=1.138=:
Become perfect, as also your Father in heaven is perfect. For, having named
him in truth the Father of those who have believed, he wills that they be
likened to the perfection of the goods contemplated in him, and that those
begotten through him have the like.

You will say to me then: And how could human lowliness be stretched out to the
blessedness contemplated in God, the perplexity showing itself of itself in
the command? For how could it be possible for the earthly to be likened to the
one in the heavens, the very difference of nature showing the unattainableness
of the imitation? For it is equally beyond words both to set before the sight
the heavenly greatness and the beauties in it, and to liken the man from earth
to the heavenly God. But the account of this is plain: the Gospel commands not
that nature be compared with nature, the human I mean with the divine, but
that the good energies be imitated in the life as far as may be possible. What
then are the energies among us that have likeness to the energies of God? To
be estranged from every evil, as far as may be possible, in work and word and
thought, being pure from the pollutions from it: this is truly an imitation of
the divine and of the perfection about the heavenly God.

For it does not seem to me that the Gospel calls the element of heaven some
separated dwelling place of God, in which it commands us to be perfected
according to the Father above the heavens, because the divine is equally in
all and extends through 8,=1.139= all creation likewise, and nothing, if
separated from being, would remain in being, but the divine nature touches
each of the beings alike, encircling all within itself by the encompassing
power; as the prophet also teaches this, saying that even if I should come to
be in heaven in my mind, even if I should dive down to the underworld in my
thought and search it out, even if I should stretch out the intellective part
of my soul to the ends of things, I see all things held within your right
hand. And the passage has this form: If I go up into heaven, you are there; if
I go down into Hades, you are present. If I should take up my wings at dawn
and dwell at the farthest parts of the sea; for even there your hand will lead
me and your right hand will hold me. From these then one may learn that the
heavenly dwelling is not set apart exceptionally for God. But since the lot
above knows how to be pure from evil, and this the holy Scripture has marked
to us in many places through riddle, while to this lower and more material
life the passions that work according to malice are committed, here, the
serpent, the inventor of evil, writhing and creeping about the earthly life,
as the word says of it in riddle, that it goes upon its breast and belly and
feeds on earth continually, this both interprets to us the form of its motion
and the kind of its feeding: that this life is earthly and lying below, and
receives upon itself the creeping course of the many-formed evil 8,=1.140= and
becomes food of the beast that creeps upon it. Therefore the one who commands
us to imitate the heavenly Father commands purity from the earthly passions,
whose apostasy is worked not by local removal but by choice alone. If then the
estrangement from evil is by nature fitted to be accomplished in the single
impulse of the mind, nothing laborious is enjoined on us by the evangelical
word. For toil is not yoked to the impulse of the mind, but it is permitted
us, without labor, wherever we will, to be present through thoughts; so that
the heavenly manner of life is easy for the one who wills even upon earth to
have, as the Gospel teaches, in thinking the things above and depositing the
wealth of virtue in the treasures there.

Do not treasure up, he says, treasures upon the earth, but treasure up
treasures in the heavens, where neither moth nor eating destroys nor thieves
dig through and steal. By these it is shown that no power that corrupts
blessedness trades with the life above. For the one who, toward the ruin of
the life here, works manifoldly his variegated evil against human life, either
in the manner of a moth breeds in the minds, marring the part in which it has
grown in, through its consuming and 8,=1.141= wasting power, if it not quickly
be shaken off from the part, creeping to the things next it, and the marks of
corruption, on which it has come near, being stamped through the motion; or,
if it have the things within safe, it plots through the outward circumstances.
For either through pleasure it has tunneled the wall of the treasure of the
heart, or through another passion it has made the vessel of the soul empty of
virtue, having stolen under the thought by anger or grief or some other such
passion. Since then in the treasures above the Lord says that neither moth
comes to be nor eating, nor the malice from the thieving evil that teaches
these things, which we have surmised, thither must the trade be transferred,
[in] which what is treasured remains not only safe and without diminution
forever, but also, like seeds, works the increase upon the manifold. For the
recompense must needs be magnified by the nature of the one receiving the
deposit. For as we act according to our own nature, offering what is poor,
because we are such by nature, so it is likely that the one who is rich in all
things requites with the things he has by nature those who gave before. Let no
one then be fainthearted, contributing what is in his own power to the divine
treasures, as if he were receiving according to the measure of what he has
given; but let him await the promise of him who said that the great things
shall be given in exchange for the small, heavenly for earthly, everlasting
for the soon dying, exchanging; which things are of such a nature as to be
able neither to be taken by thought nor to come into the interpretation of a
word, concerning which the God-breathed Scripture teaches that 8,=1.142=
neither has the eye seen nor the ear heard nor has it come up upon the heart
of man, the things God prepared for those who love him.

These things to you, O precious head, we have made good not only for the
letters left undone, but we have also gone through in advance, by this
hypothesis, the deficiencies that will come to be hereafter. And do you for me
act in the Lord with longing. And may this be for you always an object of
longing, which is both pleasing to God and to us a thing longed for.
