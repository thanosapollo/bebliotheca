many years having passed, they have suffered nothing such as our bodies
have, nor have they become weaker by old age, nor have they been
softened by disease and any infirmity, but they remain preserving their
own prime and the beauty which, as I said before, God allotted to them
from the beginning, and neither has the light of the sun been worn away,
nor has the bloom of the stars become dimmer, nor has the brightness of
heaven flowed off, nor have the bounds of the sea been moved, nor has
the power of the earth been quenched that is about the births of the
yearly fruits. And that these things are corruptible we proved both from
reasonings and from the divine Scriptures; but that they are beautiful
and bright, and have kept their bloom in its prime, the daily sight of
those who look bears witness, which is especially a thing to marvel at
in God who made them thus from the beginning. But since when we were
saying these things then some were opposing, saying: Then man is more
dishonored than all things seen, if the body of heaven and of earth and
of the sun and that of all the stars has lasted through so great a time,
but this one after seventy years is dissolved and perishes; first we
would say this, that the living being is not dissolved entire, but the
more sovereign and more necessary part of it, the soul, remains
continually immortal, yielding to none of these passions, and the
corruption happens about the lesser part; second, that even by this very
thing we are honored more. For not simply, nor without some cause, but
both justly and for a useful end we endure old age and diseases; justly,
because we fell toward sin; usefully, that through this want and the
passions we may correct the madness that comes to us from sloth. God
then did not allow this to happen as dishonoring us. For if he were
dishonoring, he would not have left our soul to be immortal. Nor, being
weak, did he make our body such. For if he were weak, he would not have
been able to hold heaven and the stars and the body of the earth through
so great a time; but for the sake of making us better and more
temperate, and more obedient to himself, which is the ground of all
salvation. For these reasons he did not make heaven, for the present, to
be caught by old age, nor by any other such infirmities. For what is
deprived of choice and of soul could neither sin nor succeed; hence it
has no need of such correction. But for us who have been honored with
reason and soul, the temperance and humility that come through these
passions have necessarily come to be, since even at the beginning,
before all the rest, the first man that came to be leaped off into
madness. And otherwise, if heaven were going to be constructed as our
bodies are and to grow old, many would have condemned much =54.636=
weakness in the Maker, as not being able to hold one body through many
circuits of years; but now this pretext too has been taken from them,
the works remaining through so great a time.
3. And besides what has been said, our things will not even stop at
this, but when we have been well chastened in the present life, he will
raise the bodies with more glory, and they will be brighter than heaven
and the sun and all the rest, and will pass over to the portion above.
One way of knowing God then is that through the whole creation; another
not less is that of conscience, which this too we then set out


through more words, showing how self-taught in us is the knowledge of
the good things and of those that are not such, and how from within the
conscience sounds all these things in us. For these two teachers have
been ours from the beginning, creation and conscience; and neither of
them sending forth a voice, they were educating men in silence. For
creation, striking the spectator through the sight, sends the one who
looks at all things on to the wonder of him who made it; and conscience,
sounding within, suggests all that must be done; and we grasp its power
and the judgment of its vote through the things of the sight. For when
it accuses the sin within, it confuses the face without and fills it
with much despondency. Again it makes men pale and full of fear, when we
are caught in some of the most shameful things, and we do not hear the
voice, but through the outer face we behold the indignation that is
happening within. And besides these two the word was showing us a third
teacher too, added by the care of God, no longer voiceless, as the
former ones, but by word and exhortation and counsel shaping our mind.
And who is this? The father allotted to each. For this is why God
arranged that we be loved by those who begot us, that we may have
trainers of virtue. For it is not begetting alone that makes a father,
but training well; nor does bearing make a mother, but rearing well. And
that this is true, and that not nature but virtue makes fathers, the
parents themselves would agree with us. At any rate their own sons,
often, when they see them become worthless and leaping off into
wickedness, they cut off from their own kinship, disowning them, making
others sons who often belong to them from nowhere. Could anything be
more paradoxical than this, when they cast out those they begot, and
bring in those they did not beget? These things have not been said to us
simply, but that you may learn that choice is stronger than nature, and
this more than that is accustomed to make both sons and fathers. And
this too became a work of the care of God, neither to let the children
come to be stripped of the natural disposition, nor again to entrust the
whole to that. For if the parents were not going to love their own sons
at all, moved by natural necessity, but only from the man =54.637=
ners and the successes only, you would have seen many coming to be
outside the paternal house through their own neglect, and our race being
torn apart. Again if he had entrusted the whole to the tyranny of
nature, and had not allowed them to hate even when they are wicked, but
the fathers, even when insulted and suffering ten thousand terrible
things from them, through the necessity of nature remained serving
children who insult and riot against them, our race would have slipped
out into the last of vice. For if now the children, not having the whole
to trust to nature, but knowing that many, having become worthless, have
fallen out of both the house and the paternal substance, nevertheless in
many places, trusting the affection of the parents, insult those who
bore them; if God had not granted them also to be indignant, and to go
after them, and to cast out the children when they become bad, where of
wickedness would they have stopped? For these reasons God entrusted the
affection of the parents both to the necessity of nature and to the
manner of the children, that they may also forgive the children in
measure when they sin, nature calling them to this, and that they may
not, when they become wicked and are sick incurably, train them on
toward vice by the wickedness, nature again convicting and being able to
compel them to tend the children even when they have become worthless.
Of how much providence are these things, tell me, both the commanding to
love, and the setting a measure on the affection, and again the
appointing a recompense for the good rearing of children? For that a
recompense is laid up, and not for men only but also for women, hear how
in many places the Scripture converses with these too about these
things, and not less with these than with the men. For when Paul said,
But the woman, having been deceived, has come to be in transgression, he
added: But she will be saved through the childbearing. Which


he means is of this sort: You are in pain, he says, that the first woman
threw you into pangs and toils and a long pregnancy? But do not be
vexed; for you have not been so much harmed from the pangs and the
toils, as you gain, if you will, from the rearing of children, taking a
pretext of successes. For the children that are born, if they enjoy the
fitting tendance, and are led on to virtue by your providence, become
for you a starting-point and pretext of much salvation, and besides your
own successes you will receive a great recompense also of the tendance
about these things. 4. And that you may learn that it is not the bearing
that makes a mother, nor is a wage laid up for this, elsewhere too Paul,
conversing with a widow, spoke thus, If she has reared children; and he
did not say, if she has made children, but If she has reared children.
For the one is of nature, the other of choice. And for this reason here
too, having said, She will be saved through the childbearing, he did not
stop at this, but wishing to show that it is not the begetting of
children, but the rearing of children well, that brings us this wage, he
added: If they continue in faith and in love and in sanctification with
temperance. Which he means is this: Then you will receive a great wage,
if the children who have been begotten continue in faith and in love and
in sanctification. If then you lead them on to these things, if you
exhort, if you teach, if you coun =54.638= sel, much recompense of this
care will be laid up for you from God. Let not the women then think it
alien to them to take care of both females and males. For he did not
distinguish the sex here, but there too he said simply, If she has
reared children, and here, If they continue in faith and in love and in
sanctification. So that we must take care of both the children, and
especially the women, inasmuch as they also sit at home the more. For
the men both journeys and the cares of the marketplace and the affairs
of the city often drag about; but the woman, having exemption from all
such care, would be able more easily to take care of those who have been
born, enjoying much leisure. Thus the women of old used to do; for not
to men only but also to women this debt is necessary; I mean, to take
thought for their own children, and to lead them on to philosophy. And
that this is true, I will narrate to you a certain ancient history.
There was a woman among the Jews, Anna. This Anna was sick with
childlessness for a long time, and what was harder, that her rival was
mother of many children. And you know how by nature and in itself the
thing is unbearable to women; but when a rival also is present who has
children, it becomes much harder; for in that woman's prosperity she
learns her own misfortune more exactly; just as those who live with last
poverty then are pained more, when they think of those who are rich. And
this was not the only terrible thing, that she herself did not have
children, but that one did, but that she was also a rival, and not only
that she was a rival, but that she was provoking her through despising
her. But God, seeing all these things, was forbearing, and The Lord, it
says, did not give her a child according to her affliction, and
according to the despondency of her soul. What is, According to her
affliction? It is not possible to say, it says, that seeing her bearing
the misfortune mildly, he was holding back her childbirth, but even
though he saw her being cut through, being in pain, being afflicted, he
did not loose the despondency, managing some other thing much greater.
Let us not hear these things as a side matter, but from here too let us
learn a very great philosophy, and when we fall into some terrible
thing, even if we are in pain, even if we lament, even if the evil seems
to us to be unbearable, let us not hurry, nor be restless, but let us
wait for the providence of God. For he knows clearly when he must loose
what is making the despondency for us; which then also happened in her
case. For not hating her, nor turning away, did God shut her womb, but
that he might open for us the doors of the philosophy in the woman, and
that we might look down upon the wealth of her faith, and know that from


this he was making her more splendid. And hear also what follows. And
thus she used to do, it says, year by year from a sufficient time, when
she went up into the house of the Lord. And she was despondent, and she
was weeping, and she was not eating. The pain was stretched, much the
=54.639= length of the grief, not two and three days, nor twenty and a
hundred, nor a thousand and twice as many; but, From a sufficient time,
it says, for many years the woman was grieving and in pain; for this is
the, From a sufficient time; and yet she did not despair, nor did the
length of the time convict her philosophy, nor the taunts and the
revilings of the rival, but she was praying continually and entreating;
and what is greater than all, and what especially shows her longing
toward God, that she was not simply desiring this very thing, to get the
child, but to dedicate fruit to God, and to offer firstfruits of her own
belly, and to receive the wage of this good promise. From where is this
clear? From the words after these. For you all know, I suppose, that to
women childlessness is especially unbearable because of the husbands.
For many of men are so unreasonably disposed as to blame the women, when
they do not bear, not knowing that bearing has its beginning from above,
from the providence of God, and neither the nature of a woman, nor
intercourse, nor anything else is sufficient toward this. But yet, even
though they know that they blame unjustly, they taunt, and often turn
away, and are not well disposed toward them. 5. Let us see then also in
the case of this woman whether this was happening. For if you see her
despised, dishonored, insulted, not having boldness toward the husband,
nor enjoying much goodwill, you will be able to guess that for this
reason she was desiring a child, that she might have boldness and much
freedom, and become more desired by the husband. But if you find the
whole contrary, her being loved more than the one who has the children,
and enjoying more goodwill, it is quite clear that not for some human
thing, nor that she might draw the husband more, was she desiring a
child, but for the cause that has been said. From where then is this
clear? Hear the writer himself saying it; for he has not set it down
simply, but that you may learn the virtue of the woman. What then does
this one say? For Elkanah loved Anna above Phennana. Then after these
things, seeing her not eating, but weeping, he says: What is it to you,
that you weep? and why do you not eat? and why does your heart strike
you? am I not better to you than ten children? Do you see how he was
hung upon her, and was in pain for her more, not because of not having
children, but because of seeing her despondent and held by exceeding
pain? But yet he did not persuade her to stand off from the
despondency. For she was not seeking the child because of him, but that
she might show some fruit to God. And she rose up, it says, after they
had eaten in Shiloh, and after they had drunk, and she stood before the
Lord. Nor has this been said simply, that After eating and drinking; but
that you may learn that the time which others make a time of ease and
license, this she was making a time of prayer and tears, because of
being exceedingly sober and awake. And she stood before the Lord; and
Eli the priest, it says, was sitting upon the seat upon the doorposts of
the temple of the Lord. Nor has this been said simply, that Eli the
priest was sitting upon the doorposts of the temple of the Lord; but for
the sake of showing the heat of the woman. For just as a widow woman
often, being without a protector and desolate, being despitefully used,
much =54.640= wronged, when a king is about to drive in, with
spear-bearers, shield-bearers, horsemen, and much other attendance
coming to meet him, is not struck, nor needs a protector, but cutting
through all of them she meets the king with much boldness, making a
tragedy of her own misfortune, necessity leading her by the hand; so
then also this woman did not blush, was not ashamed, the priest sitting,
herself through herself to ask and with much boldness to come forward to
the king; but winged by the longing, and having gone up to heaven in her
understanding, as seeing God himself, thus with all heat she was
conversing with him. And what does she say? Or rather nothing


does she utter first, but she makes the preludes from lamentations, and
sends forth warm springs of tears. And just as, when rains are coming
down, even the harder earth, soaked and softened, easily stirs itself
toward the births of the fruits, so also it happened in the case of this
woman. For as if by rains of the tears the womb was being softened, and
being heated through the pain, it was beginning to be stirred toward
that good childbearing. But let us hear also the words themselves, and
this good supplication. Weeping she wept, it says, and she prayed a
prayer to the Lord, saying: ADONAI LORD ELOI SABAOTH. Terrible are the
words, and full of shuddering; and well did the writer do in not
transferring them toward our voice; for he was not able with their own
virtue to set them over toward the Greek tongue. And the woman did not
call him from one word, but from many of those that belong to him,
showing the longing about him, and the warm disposition. And just as
those who write petitions to the king do not insert one name only, but
the trophy-bearer, the augustus, the autocrat, and many others of these,
having set them above first, then make the supplication; so also she,
bearing up some petition to God, sets many names in the prelude of the
petition, both showing her own disposition, as I said before, and the
honor toward the one being called upon. And the pain itself dictated the
petition; therefore it was also heard quickly, she having written it
with much understanding. For such are the prayers that come from pain of
soul. For in place of paper her understanding was, in place of a reed
the tongue, and the tears in place of ink; therefore even until the
present day the petition has remained. For such letters become
inerasable, which have been dyed with that ink. And the preludes of the
petition are such; but what are the things next? If looking you look, it
says, upon the humiliation of your servant. Thus she has received
nothing, and from a promise she made the beginning of the prayer. She is
already repaying God, holding nothing in her hands. Thus she was boiling
and in travail toward this more than toward that, and for this she
prayed to receive the child. If looking you look upon the humiliation of
your servant. I have two just claims, she says, the servitude, and the
misfortune. And give me your servant seed of a man; I will give it
before =54.641= you as a gift. What is, Before you as a gift? Given over
and a complete slave. I stand off from all authority. For I wish to
become a mother only so much, as for the child to take the beginning
from me, and for the rest I withdraw and yield. 6. And consider the
piety of a woman. She did not say, If you give me three, I give you the
two, and if two, I give you the one; but, if you give only one, I
dedicate to you the fruit complete. And wine and strong drink he shall
not drink. She has not yet received the child, and already she is
forming a prophet, and she converses about his rearing, and she makes
covenants with God. O the boldness of a woman! Since to put downnor even
then did she have, because of not yet having received, from the things
to come she puts down the price. And just as many of the husbandmen
living in last poverty, and not having money so as to buy a calf or a
sheep, take them from the masters upon a half share, promising to put
down the price from the fruits to come; so then also she, or rather she
did even much more. For she does not take the son from God upon a half
share, but so as to give him back to him again complete, and to reap the
rearing. For she thought it a sufficient recompense to toil about the
priest of God. And wine and strong drink he shall not drink, it says.
She did not think toward herself, saying: But what if, being tender, he
is destroyed by the drinking of water? but what if he falls into
infirmity? but what if he dies, given over to a hard disease? But having
thought that he who gave him will himself also be able to take thought
for the health, from the very swaddling-clothes and the pangs she
embarked him into the holiness, having thrown the whole upon God, and
before the pangs her belly


was being sanctified, having a prophet, and carrying a priest, and
bearing the dedicated thing, a living dedicated thing. For these reasons
God was forbearing when she was despondent, for this he was giving
slowly, that he might make her more splendid by the manner of the birth,
that he might show the philosophy. For even having stood to the prayer
she did not remember the rival, she did not speak her revilings, she did
not bring the taunts into the midst, she did not say: Avenge me from
that polluted and wicked woman; which many women do; but not even having
remembered those taunts, she was praying only about the things
profitable to her. Do this also, O man, and when you see an enemy
grieving you, toward that one send out no bitter word, nor pray against
him who is hostilely disposed toward you; but having gone in, and bent
the knees, and sent forth tears, call upon God to loose the
despondency, to quench the grief; which then also she has done, and she
reaped the greatest things from the enemy woman. For that one joined in
working toward the birth of the child. And how, I say. Since she
taunted, she afflicted, and worked the pain greater, from the pain the
prayer became more attentive, the prayer drew God, and made him nod, and
thus Samuel was born. So that, if we are sober, not only will the
enemies be able to harm us nothing, but they will even benefit us the
greatest things, making us more earnest toward all things, only if we
lead ourselves not toward revilings and insults, but toward prayers
=54.642= from the despondency that comes to us from them. And having
borne the child, she called him Samuel, that is, Hearing of God. For
since from being heard, and from prayer, she received him, and not from
nature, she then deposited the memory of the work, as on a bronze
pillar, in the name of the child. And she did not say, Let us call him
by the name of his father, or of the uncle, or of the grandfather, or of
the great-grandfather; but he who has given him, he himself also, she
says, let him be honored by the name of the child. Zeal after this
woman, O women, let us imitate this one, O men, and let us make so great
care about the children, let us rear those who have been born thus, both
in all the other things, and in the matter of temperance. For nothing
must one be so eager about and take thought for in the young as for the
sake of temperance and gravity. For this passion especially troubles
those ages; and what we do in the case of lamps, this let us also guard
in the case of the children. Often at any rate we command a maidservant
lighting a lamp not to carry the lamp past, wherever there is straw, or
grass, or some such thing, lest, we not knowing, some spark falling out,
and laying hold of that matter, burn the whole house; this providence
let us have also in the case of the children, and let us not carry their
sights past, where there are wanton maidservants, where there are
profligate girls, where there are unbridled slaves, but let us command,
and charge, even if we have such a maidservant, even if a neighbor, even
if simply some other such, neither to come into sight, nor into
companionship of words with the young, so that a spark falling out from
there may not kindle the whole soul of the child, and the misfortune
become inconsolable. And not from spectacles only, but also from soft
and broken hearings let us lead them away, that their soul may not be
bewitched through these; nor let us lead them up to theaters, nor to
symposia and drunkennesses, but more than maidens kept in chambers let
the young be guarded for us. For nothing is so naturally fitted to adorn
that age as the crown of temperance, and to come to marriage purified of
all wantonness. Thus also the wives would become desired to them, when
the soul has not practiced fornication beforehand, nor been corrupted,
when the young man has known that wife only who has been joined to him
by marriage. Thus also the loves become warmer, and the goodwill more
genuine, and the friendship more exact, when with such a guard the young
walk to the marriages. As the things now happening are not marriage, but
simply a


traffic of money, and huckstering. For when even before the marriages
the young man is corrupted, and after the marriage again he looks toward
another woman, what is the profit of the marriages, tell me? Greater
then is the punishment, unforgivable the sin, when a wife being within
he shames himself toward harlots, and works adultery. For after a wife,
even if the one who is being corrupted with the married man is a harlot,
the thing is adultery. And these things happen, =54.643= and after
marriage they run toward the women who play the companion, because
before the marriage they did not practice to be temperate. From here
fights, and revilings, and overturnings of houses, and daily wars; from
here the love toward the wife flows under and withers, the intercourse
in the brothels dissolving it. Whereas if he learns to be temperate, he
will think his own wife more desired than all, and he will see her with
much goodwill, and he will keep much concord toward her, and peace and
concord being, all the good things will come into that house. In order
then that both the things here may be well managed for us, and with
these we may also attain the kingdom of the heavens, let us take thought
both for ourselves and for the children, especially for the sake of this
commandment, that we may not, having put on filthy garments, go into
that spiritual marriage, but with much boldness may enjoy the honor laid
up there for the worthy; which may it be that we all attain, by the
grace and love of man of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the Father
glory, honor, power, with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the
ages of the ages. Amen.

HOMILY II. On the faith of Anna, and on the philo sophy, and on her
gentleness, and on the honor toward the priests, and on the need both
when beginning breakfast, and when ending, to pray.
1. Nothing then is equal to prayer, beloved, nothing more powerful than
faith. Both Anna showed us the other day; for having come forward to God
with these gifts, she accomplished all things whatever she wished, and
she corrected a maimed nature, and opened a shut womb, and took away
shame, and dissolved the taunts of the rival, and brought herself back
to much boldness, having harvested from a barren rock an ear waving.
And you all heard how she prayed, how she asked, and persuaded and
received, and bore and reared and dedicated Samuel. So that one would
not err in addressing this woman at once mother and father of the child.
For even if the husband sowed, yet the prayer furnished this power to
the seed, and made the beginnings of the birth of Samuel more august.
For not, as in the case of the rest, sleep and unions of those who begot
only, but prayers and tears and faith became beginnings of this birth,
and a prophet, having come to be through the faith of the mother, had
the begettings more august than the others. Therefore also about this
woman one would say in season: Those who sow in tears, in gladness will
they harvest. Let us zeal after this one, O men, let us imitate this
one, O women; for the woman is a teacher of either sex. As many as are
barren, let them not give up; as many as are mothers, thus let them rear
the children who have been born; and let us all zeal after the
philosophy of the woman before the birth, the faith in the birth, the
earnestness after the birth. For what could be more philosophic than
that woman, who bore a misfortune so unbearable mildly and nobly, and
did not stand off first, until she loosed the terrible thing, and found
a wonderful and paradoxical end of the evil, having taken no ally and
helper from below? For she knew the love of man of the Master; therefore
she also came forward alone, and accomplished what she wished. For not
of human help, =54.644= but of the divine grace did the correction of
that despondency have need; for she was not in a loss
of money, that someone bringing gold might loose the despondency; not in
a body's


infirmity, that she might call upon the sons of physicians, and drive
the disease away. It was nature being despitefully used, needing the
hand from above. Therefore having left all things upon earth, she ran
to the Master of nature, and she did not stand off first, until she
persuaded him to loose the childlessness, and to open the womb, and to
make the barren a mother. Blessed then also for this, not that she
became a mother, but that not being one she became one. For that was
common of nature, but this was an exceptional success of the woman.
Blessed then also for those pangs, and blessed not less also for all the
things before the pangs. For you all know, I suppose, both women and
men, how nothing more unbearable than childlessness could come to be for
a woman; but even if she enjoys prosperity ten thousand times, she would
never beat off the pain that comes to her from this blow. And if now it
is so unbearable, when we have been called to a greater philosophy, and

drawing near am I, and not a God from far), do you put off and shrink?
And what defense will you have? of what pardon will you enjoy, when
being able to find both easy and from every side ready deliverance of
the evils laid on, you are slothful, and betray your own salvation? For
not only barrenness is this physician able to heal, but also any disease
whatever both of soul and of body, having willed only. And this is not
the only wonderful thing, that without toil, and sojourn abroad, and
expense, and mediators, but that even without pain he makes the healing.
For he does not stand toward the passion with iron and fire, as the sons
of the physicians do; but it is enough to nod only, and all despondency,
and all pain, and every passion runs away and is put to flight. 2. Let
us not then neglect, nor put off, even if we are poor and have fallen
into last want. For it is not even money that one must put down, that we
may put forward poverty. For this physician does not demand silver as
the wage, but tears, and prayers, and faith. And if having these you
come to him, you will receive altogether, whatever you ask, and you will
go away with much gladness. And this it is possible to learn from many
places, and not least also from this woman; for not having put down gold
and silver, but prayer and faith


and tears, having received what she asked, thus she went away. Let us
not then think the narrative to be without gain for us; For these things
were written, it says, toward our admonition, upon whom the ends of the
ages have come; but let us come forward to her, and let us learn, how
the disease was loosed, and what after being loosed she did again, and
how she used the gift given from God. She sat, it says, and she suckled
Samuel. See how she was thereafter looking at that child not as a child
only, but also as a dedicated thing; and the affection was becoming
double for her, the one from nature, the other from grace. And it seems
to me she was even reverencing her own child; and reasonably. For if
some, being about to dedicate bowls and golden cups to God, when they
receive them having been prepared, and before the day set them away at
home, no longer look at them as common vessels, but as dedicated things,
and they do not even dare simply and at random to touch these, as also
the rest; =54.655= much more the woman attending to the child with such
a mind, even before bringing him into the temple, both was loving more
than as a child, and was tending as a dedicated thing, thinking to be
sanctified through that one; for her house also became a temple, having
the prophet within and the priest. And not from what she promised him
only, but also from what she did not dare to set foot in the temple,
before leading him away from the milk-rearing, it is possible to see her
piety. For she said, it says, to her husband: I will not go up, until
the little child goes up with me; and when I wean it, it will be seen to
the face of the Lord, and it will sit there unto the age. Do you see?
To leave him at the house, and to go up, she did not think to be safe;
for after the giving of the gift, she was not enduring to appear without
the gift; and having brought him up again, and having taken it, she was
afraid to go down. For this she remained so great a time, so as to
appear with the gift. And she brought him up then, and she left him, and
neither was that one vexed, having been torn from the breast; and you
know how children are accustomed to be distressed being led away from
the milk-rearing; but neither was that one distressed being torn from
the mother, but he was looking toward the Master, who also made that one
a mother, nor was she in pain being separated from the child; for the
grace mediating conquered the sympathy from nature, and they seemed to
be with one another. And just as a vine settled in one place leads the
branches forward to a long way, and from much interval the cluster
hanging is with the root; so then also it happened in the case of this
woman. For remaining in the city, she stretched her branch as far as the
temple, and there she hung the ripe cluster; and nothing did the
interval of the place hinder, the love according to God joining the
child to the mother. For even if the age was unripe, yet the virtue was
ripe for him, and to all going up into the temple he was becoming a
teacher of much piety. For being busybodies and learning the manner of
his birth, they were taking a sufficient consolation, the hope toward
God. And no one seeing the child was going down in silence, but all were
glorifying the one who had given it beyond hope. For these reasons God
put off the birth, so as to stretch this pleasure, so as to make the
woman more conspicuous. For those who knew her misfortune were becoming
witnesses of the grace of God; so that her remaining a long time in
childlessness both made her more known to all, and prepared her to be
called blessed and marveled at by all, and God to be thanked through
her. And these things I say, that even if we see holy women being in
childlessness, or in some other such terrible thing, we may not be
vexed, nor be distressed, nor say toward ourselves: Why ever did God
overlook a woman living with so great virtue, and did not give her a
child? For these things are not of one overlooking, but of one knowing
what is profitable for us more exactly than we ourselves. She was going
up then into the temple, and she was bringing in the lamb into the
flock, and the calf into the herd, and into the meadow the rose freed
from thorns, a rose never


withering, but continually blooming, and able to run up toward heaven
itself, of whose fragrance all those throughout the inhabited world take
=54.656= hold until today. So great a number of years then has passed,
and the things of the virtue according to this fragrance are stretched,
and it has not become weaker by the multitude of the time. For such is
the nature of the spiritual things. 3. She went up then transplanting
this good shoot, and just as the labor-loving of the husbandmen first
put down seeds of cypresses and other such into the earth, then, when
they see the seed having become a tree, they do not leave it upon the
same earth, but having pulled it up from there, they transplant it upon
another land, so that the earth, having received it newer in its bosoms,
may display its own power unmixed and complete into the rearing of that
root; so also this woman did. For the child sown beyond hope in that
belly, she transferred from the house, and planted into the temple,
where there are springs and spiritual and continual irrigations. And it
was possible to see that prophetic thing being fulfilled upon them,
which David was saying singing thus: Blessed is the man who has not
walked in the counsel of the impious, and in the way of sinners has not
stood, and upon a seat of pestilent men has not sat; but either in the
law of the Lord is his will, and in his law he will meditate day and
night; and he will be as the tree that has been planted beside the
outlets of the waters, which will give its fruit in its season. For not
after the trial of vice did he come upon the deliverance from vice, but
from the very swaddling-clothes he chose virtue; he did not share
councils that have lawlessness, nor did he have communion with gatherings
filled with impiety, but from first age from the breast of the mother he
came upon another spiritual breast. And as a tree enjoying continual
irrigation runs up toward a great height; so then also this one arrived
at the peak of virtue, being watered by the hearing of the divine
oracles continually. But let us see how she planted him; let us follow
the woman, let us go into the temple with her. She went up, it says,
with him into Shiloh in a calf of three years. Double thereafter the
sacrifice was becoming; and there was a calf, the one without reason,
the other with reason; and the one the priest slaughtered, but this one
the woman dedicated; or rather the sacrifice of the woman was better
than the sacrifice which the priest brought up; for she too was becoming
a priestess of her own inward parts, and she imitated the patriarch
Abraham, and she set the contest toward that one. But that one, having
taken the son, brought him down; but she left him to remain in the
temple continually; or rather that one also dedicated him wholly. For do
not look at this, that he did not slay, but that in the mind he
completed the whole. Did you see a woman competing toward a man? did you
see her hindered by nothing from nature from zealing after the
patriarch? But let us see how she dedicates him. Having come forward to
the priest, she said to him: In me, lord. What ever is, In me? With
exactness attend to the things being said, she says. For since much time
has passed, she wishes to remind again of the =54.657= things said the
other day; therefore she says: In me, lord; your soul lives; I am the
woman who stood before you in the praying toward the Lord for this
little child. I prayed toward the Lord, and he gave me my request, which
I asked from him. And I also lend him to the Lord all the days which he
himself lives, to be a loan to the Lord. She did not say: I am the
woman whom you taunted, whom you reviled, whom you jeered into riot and
drunkenness; therefore God showed you that I am not drunken; but you
were simply charging this. She said none of these harsh words, but with
much gentleness she answers; and yet having the outcome from the affairs
witnessing to her, and being able now to taunt the priest, as having
blamed her then at random and in vain, she does none of these things,
but she speaks the benefaction of God only. And consider the
good-mindedness of a servant. When she was suffering badly, she unveiled
the misfortune to no one, nor did she say toward the priest: I have a
rival woman, and that one taunting me and being reviled has a chorus of
children, but I living in gentleness have not even today


been able to become a mother, but God has shut my womb, and seeing me
afflicted he has not had mercy. She said none of these, but having been
silent of the form of the misfortune, she made clear that she is
despondent only, having said, that A woman in a hard day am I; and she
would not even have uttered this, if the priest had not compelled,
having suspected her to be drunken. But when he relaxed that blow, and
God gave the request, then she makes the benefaction manifest to the
priest, wishing to take him as a partner of the thanksgiving, as then
she took him of the prayer; and she says: For this little child I
prayed, and the Lord gave me my request, which I asked from him. And now
I lend him to the Lord. See how she is moderate. Do not think me to be
doing some great and wonderful thing, she says, that I dedicate the
child; I did not begin the success, but I give back a debt. For I
received a deposit, and this I restore to the one who has given. And
saying these words, she was dedicating herself too with the child, as
with some chain, by the sympathy of nature, having bound herself up
toward the temple. 4. For if where the treasure of the man is, there
also is his heart, much more where the child of the woman is, there also
was the understanding of the mother, and her belly was being filled with
blessing again. For when she said these words, and prayed, hear what the
priest says toward Elkanah: May the Lord give back to you other seed
from this woman, in place of the debt, which you lent to the Lord. At
the beginning he was not saying, May he give back to you; but what? May
he give you every request of yours. But when she set God as a debtor,
May he give back to you, he says, stretching under good hopes about the
things to come. For if not owing he gave, much more after taking he will
give back. And the first then from prayer, and those after that one from
blessing took the beginnings; and thus thereafter the whole fruit of the
woman was being sanctified. And the firstborn was a success of the
woman, but the second a partner of her and of the priest. And just as a
fat and rich earth, having received the seeds, =54.658= shows us the
cornfields waving; so also the woman, having received the words of the
priest with faith, brought us other ears waving, and she changed the
ancient curse, bearing from prayer and blessing. And you then, O woman,
zeal after this one, and if you are barren, display also such a prayer,
and call upon the priest to take hold with you of the embassy. If
altogether you receive the words with faith, the blessing of the fathers
will end into good and ripe fruit. And if you become a mother, dedicate
your son too. That one brought him up into a temple, you construct
yourself a royal temple. For your members, he says, are a body of
Christ, and a temple of the Holy Spirit in you. And again: I will dwell
in you yourselves, and I will walk about. For how is it not absurd to
correct a house that has grown old and is about to fall, both spending
money, and gathering builders, and doing all business, but the house of
God (for a house of God the soul of the young would come to be) not even
to think worthy of the chance providence? See that you do not hear the
same, which also Jews once heard. For those, since they saw this
sensible temple neglected after returning from the captivity, and were
beautifying their own houses, so provoked God, as to send the prophet,
and to threaten famine, and much scarcity of the necessities, and to
speak the cause of this threat; and this was, that You dwell in houses
with coffered ceilings, but my house has been left desolate. And if the
neglect about that temple raised so great anger of God, much more the
sloth of this temple will provoke the Master; for this is much more
honorable than that, inasmuch as it also has greater symbols of the
holiness. Do not then let the house of God become a cave of robbers,
that you may not also hear another rebuke, which Christ made toward the
Jews saying: The house of my Father is a house of prayer; but you have
made it a cave of robbers. And how does it become a cave of robbers?
When


we allow ignoble and slavish desires, when all wantonness, to go in and
to dwell in the souls of the young. For harder than robbers are such
reasonings, having enslaved the freedom of the children, and they make
them slaves of the irrational passions, stabbing them from every side,
and filling their understanding with many wounds. Therefore let us look
around at these things each day, and using the word as a scourge, let us
drive all such passions from their soul, that our children may be able

report of the peoples? tell me. For the evening having overtaken, at

passed, as having lived in luxury as a dream, thus they are set down
stripped of all gladness, and neither the cheer that comes to them from
the crown, nor that from the bright robe and from all the other display,
seeking they would be able to find, all things having run past them
quicker than every wind. But the citizenship of the heavens is the whole
contrary; without expense it brings us much gain and lasting. For not

hindrance, but it is possible even for the poor man to liturgize this

there be harm of poverty, if it has the wealth within stored. Let not
male children only, but also daughters liturgize this liturgy for us;
for not as upon the outer citizenship these things have been commanded

men, and young, and slaves, and free. For where the display is of soul,
neither nature, nor age, nor worldly dignity, nor anything else is
naturally fitted to hinder. For this then, I exhort all of you, from
first age to give out both sons and daughters to such liturgies, and to
store up beforehand for them the wealth that fits such a citizenship,
not burying gold, nor gathering silver, but depositing gentleness,
temperance, gravity, all the other virtue in their soul. For that

HOMILY IV. Toward those who leave the gatherings, and go up to the
theaters, and that not only more useful than the pastime in theaters is
the spending time in church, but also sweeter; and both on the second
saying of the prayer of Anna, and on the need to pray continually, and
in every place, even if we are in a marketplace, even if in a road, even
if upon a bed.
1. I do not know with what words I shall use today. For seeing the
gatherings being lessened, prophets being insulted, apostles being
overlooked, fathers being despised, the insult passing through the
slaves to the Master, I wish to bring a charge, but I do not see those
who ought to hear the accusation present, but you who have no need of
this exhortation and the admonition. Yet not even thus must we be
silent; for both we will prepare the pain upon those to breathe a little
into the things outside, casting it out through the words, and we will
make those themselves be ashamed and blush, having sent upon them so
many accusers, all you who hear. For if they had come to be here, they
would have heard us only rebuking; but now fleeing the rebuke from us,
from you they will hear all these things. Thus friends also do; when
they do not find those liable, they meet with the friends of those, so
that having gone away they may say the things said. This God also did;
for having left those who had sinned toward him, he meets with Jeremiah
who has been wronged nothing, saying: Did you see what the senseless
daughter of Judah did to me? For this then we too super-meet with you
against those, that having gone away you may correct them. For who would
bear so great neglect? Once in the week we are gathered here, and they
do not even endure this day to overlook the worldly cares; and if
someone charges, at once they put forward poverty, and the necessary
food, and compelling businesses, having practiced a defense more hard
than all accusation. For what would come to be worse than this
accusation, but or when some other thing appears to you more necessary
than the affairs of God and more compelling? Especially then even if
this were true, the defense was an accusation, as I said. But that you
may learn that these too are a pretext and an excuse and curtains of
sloth, nothing of me saying, the day after tomorrow will convict all
those who put forward such things, when the whole city shifts toward the
hippodrome, and houses and marketplaces are emptied into that lawless
spectacle. And here it is not even possible to see the sovereign place
of the church being filled; but there not the hippodrome only, but also
upper rooms, and houses, and roofs, and cliffs, and ten thousand other
places from above they oc =54.661= cupy; and neither poverty, nor
business, nor weakness of body, nor infirmity of feet, nor any other of
such things holds the ungovernable
madness; but men who have grown old run there more vehemently than young
men in their prime,


shaming the gray hair, making an example of the age, making the old age
itself ridiculous. And here coming in, as if seasick and thinking they
are being troubled, they have fallen back in the hearing of the divine
words, putting forward straitness, and choking, and such things; but
there even receiving sun with the head naked, and being trampled, and

surfeit of that most joyless spectacle? how will you be able to shape
the son, how to punish the household slave sinning, how to counsel
another who neglects the needful things, yourself being unseemly in last
old age in such things? And if a young man insults the one who has grown
old, at once he puts forward the age, and he has ten thousand sharing
the indignation; but when there is need to make the young man temperate,
and for him to become some rule of virtue, nowhere is there account of
the age for them, but more madly than those themselves they walk upon
the lawless spectacle. And these things I say, and I fasten upon those
who have grown old, not delivering the young from accusation and
slander, but through those securing these. For if the one who has grown
old must not, much more the young. For there much is the laughter, and
greater the unseemliness; but here the destruction is harder, deep the
pit, inasmuch as also the things of desire are more in their prime for
the young, and more vehement is the flame for them, and if it lays hold
of even small matter only from outside, it burns all things. For also
toward anger and toward desires the young man is more easily fallen;
therefore he also needs more guard, a more vehement bridle, a safer wall
and hindrance. 2. For do not say this to me, O man, that the spectacle
has pleasure; but if it does not have harm with the pleasure, teach me

danced with the devil, and have heard men blaspheming and reviling, and
have spent the time at random and in vain, and have nothing to carry
home, neither bodily nor spiritual gain from there. So that for the sake
of pleasure especially one must meet here. For there at once condemnation,
accusation of the =54.662= conscience, repentance upon the things that
have come to be, shame, and taunt, and having the eyes without boldness;
but here the whole contrary, boldness, and speaking freely, and with

throw yourself into a marketplace, and see all running upon that

swept away with the many you go away there, having been a little
entertained, through all the day you will be in pain, both of the one
coming, and of many others, condemning yourself; but if you hold
yourself a little, through all the day you will have to live in luxury.
For not upon this only, but also upon all the other things it is
accustomed thus to happen. For vice has some temporary pleasure,
continual the pain; but virtue, short the toil, continual the gain with
the gladness. For example, someone prayed toward God, wept, was in pain
a little


time according to the prayer; another lived in luxury through all the
day, then gave alms, fasted, worked some other of the good things, or
having been insulted did not revile in return; in one turn then of a
moment having endured and held the anger, through all he is glad and
rejoices, reminding himself of those successes. But upon vice the
contrary; someone insulted, reviled in return; after these things having
come to be at home, he eats himself, reckoning the words, which often
have brought much harm. So that if you pursue pleasure, Flee the
youthful desires, and take care of temperance, and attend to hearing of
divine oracles. These things we say toward you, that you saying toward
those, and continually pumping these words upon them, may draw them away
from all wicked habit, and may persuade them to do all things by the
fitting reasoning. For of such men even being borne simply and at random
one would not find even the earnestness to be praiseworthy; and this
from the coming gathering I make clear. For when the holy Pentecost is
being accomplished for us, so great a multitude will run, as for all the
things here to be straitened for us. But yet not even this collection do
I accept; for it is of habit, not of piety. What then would come to be
more wretched than those, when both their sloth is full of so many
charges, and the seeming to be earnestness is stripped of praises? For
the one sharing this divine gathering by zeal and longing and a temperate
reasoning must do these things continually, but not with those appearing
in a feast only, to stand off again with those, being led simply after
the manner of cattle. 3. I would have been able then also to stretch the
prelude of the word toward more length; but since I know clearly, that
even before our exhortation doing the things fitting for yourselves, you
will also say more than the things said, so as not to seem to annoy in
the rebukes against those, having left all the remaining things to you,
I will touch the accustomed teaching, having brought the word back upon
the his =54.663= tory of Anna. And do not marvel, if we have not yet
been delivered of this subject. For I am not able to cast this woman out
from my understanding; so do I marvel at the well-formedness of her
soul, and the beauty of the understanding. For I love eyes weeping in
prayers, practicing through all, and lips, and a mouth not being made
red with some paints, but being beautified with thanksgiving toward God,
such as was hers; whom I marvel at that she philosophized, and I marvel
more, that being a woman she philosophized, a woman, of whom many have
often accused. For from a woman, it says, is a beginning of sin, and
through her we all die; and again: Small is all vice toward vice of a
woman; and Paul: For Adam was not deceived, but the woman having been
deceived has come to be in transgression. For this I especially marvel
at her, that she beat off these charges, that she put away the
accusation, that being of the sex that is slandered and accused, she
rubbed off all the taunts, training through the works, that those too
have not become such from the nature, but from the choice and their own
neglect, and that in this sex it is possible to lay hold of extreme
virtue. For contentious is this living being and well-strung, and if it
inclines into wickedness, it works great evils; and if it lays hold of
virtue, it will give over the soul first, rather than stand off from the
purpose. Thus at any rate she too conquered the nature, and forced the
necessity, and by the sitting at the prayer she prepared a child to
sprout from a maimed womb. For this she flees for refuge again upon the
prayer, and after receiving thus saying: My heart was made firm in the
Lord, my horn was raised in my God. That then, what is, My heart was
made firm in the Lord, I said the other day toward your love, is clear
to you; but now it is necessary to interpret the next saying. For having
said: My heart was made firm in the Lord; she added: My horn was raised
in my God. What is, My horn? For continually the Scripture has used this
word, as when it says: His horn was raised, and, The horn of his anointed
was raised. What then does it say horn? The power, the


glory, the conspicuousness, setting it from a metaphor of the irrational
animals; for to those also in place of glory and a weapon God set in the
horn only, and if they throw this away, they have thrown away the more
of the power; and just as an unarmed soldier, so also a bull without
horns is easily caught. Nothing else then through this does the woman
say, but or, that My glory was raised. And how was it raised? In my God,
she says. For this the height is also safe, having a firm and unmoved
root. For the glory from men imitates the cheapness of those who glorify,
whence it also shifts easily; but that of God is not thus, but remains
unmoved through all. And making both these clear, both the easy-shifting
of that, and the lasting of this, the prophet was saying thus: All flesh
is grass, and all glory of man as a flower of grass. The grass was dried,
and the flower fell out. But about that of God not thus, but how? But
the word of God remains unto the age. And this is clear also from this
woman. For kings and generals and dynasts, =54.664= having often done
much business, so that their memory might become unforgettable, and
having built splendid tombs, and having set up statues, and many images
in many places, and having left ten thousand memorials of successes,
have been silenced, and they are not even known to anyone from a bare
name; but this woman is sung everywhere of the inhabited world now; and
if you go away into Scythia, and if into Egypt, and if into Indians, and
if into the very ends of the inhabited world, you will hear all singing
the successes of this woman, and simply all the earth, as much as the
sun looks upon, the glory of Anna occupies. And this is not the only
wonderful thing, that the woman is sung everywhere of the inhabited
world, but that so great a time having passed, not only have the things
of her good report not been quenched, but they also grow and are
stretched much more, and all know her philosophy and her endurance and
her perseverance, and in cities and in fields, and in houses, and in
camps, and in ships, and in workshops, and everywhere you will hear this
woman being praised. For when God wishes to glorify someone, even if
death comes upon, even if a multitude of time, even if anything else
whatever, the things of that glory remain blooming continually, and no
one will be able to overshadow this conspicuousness. For this she too,
training all who hear, that one must not flee for refuge to the perishable
things, but from where the good things remain firm and unmoved for us,
spoke the cause of the glory. For having said, that My heart was made
firm in the Lord, she added, My horn was raised in my God, hinting here
at double good things for us, which it is hard to happen quickly. For
both of the surge, she says, I have been delivered, and I have put away
the dishonor, and I have enjoyed safety, and I have shared glory. And
both these one would not easily see running together with one another.
For many are delivered of dangers, but do not have a glorious life;
others again enjoy glory and conspicuousness, but are compelled to be in
danger through this glory. What I mean is of this sort: many often
having dwelt in the prison, adulterers and sorcerers and tomb-robbers,
and those who have sinned some other such things, then from some kingly
love of man were released from the dwelling; these were delivered of the
punishment, but they did not rub off the taunts, but they have the shame
following them. Other noble soldiers pursuing the glorious and
conspicuous life, having given themselves over to the enemies
recklessly, often took many wounds, and last underwent an untimely
death; these having desired glory, fell out of safety. 4. But in the
case of the woman both these happened, and she enjoyed safety and
attained glory. Thus also it happened in the case of the three
children; for they were both delivered of dangers having escaped the
fire, and they became conspicuous, having conquered the power of the
element beyond nature. Such are the successes of God; for together he
grants a splendid and safe life. Which then both she also hinting


was saying. My heart was made firm in the Lord, my horn was raised in my
God. She did not say simply, In God, but, In my God, snatching the
common Master of the inhabited world toward herself. And this she did,
not lessening his =54.665= mastership, but showing and consoling her own
love. For such is the custom of those who love; they do not endure to
love with many, but exceptionally and privately they wish to show their
affection. Thus David also does saying: O God, my God, toward you I rise
early. For having spoken his common mastership, he spoke also the
private one upon the saints. And again, O God, my God, attend to me; why
have you left me? And again: I will say to God: You are my helper; for
of a boiling and warm soul and being burned by the longing are these
words. Thus also this woman did. But that men do this, is no wonder; but
when you see God doing this, then be struck. For just as these do not
call him commonly with the many, but also wish him to be their God
privately; so also he himself, does not say he is their God commonly
with the others only, but also privately of them. For this he says, I am
the God of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob, not contracting his mastership,
but stretching it more; for not so does the multitude of those ruled, as
the virtue of those being kinged, show his rule; nor does he so rejoice
being called God of heaven and earth, and of sea, and of the things in
these, as he rejoices being called God of Abraham and Isaac and Jacob.
And what does not happen upon men, this it is possible to see happening
upon God. What I mean is of this sort: upon men the slaves are called
from the masters, and thus it is custom for all to say, The so-and-so
steward of the so-and-so, the so-and-so manager of the so-and-so, of a
general and a prefect; but no one says, The so-and-so the prefect of
this steward, but we are always accustomed to call the lesser from the
greater. But upon God the contrary has happened; for not only is Abraham
said of God, but also God of Abraham, and the Master is called from the
slave. This then itself also Paul being struck was saying: For which
cause God is not ashamed to be called their God. He is not ashamed, he
says, the Master to be called from the slaves. Why is he not ashamed?
speak the cause. That we too may imitate. But those were strangers and
sojourners, he says. And yet even for this one must be ashamed; for the
stranger seems to be cheap and easy to despise. But those saints were
not strangers in this manner, which we think, but some other and
paradoxical. For we call those strangers who have left their own
fatherland, and have come into another land; but those were not thus
strangers, but having overlooked the whole inhabited world, and having
thought the earth to be small, they were looking toward the city in the
heavens, not through arrogance, but through greatness of soul, not
through madness, but through philosophy. For since they considered all
the things upon earth, and saw that it flows and perishes, and nothing
here is firm and unmoved, not wealth, not power, not glory, not the life
itself, but each has an end, and is hurried toward its own limit, but
the things in the heavens are not such, but endless and immortal, they
chose to become strangers of the things that flow and run past, that
they might lay hold of those that remain. Strangers then they were, not
by not having a fatherland, but by longing for the fatherland that
always remains. Which then also he himself =54.666= making clear was
saying: But those who say these things, make it appear that they seek a
fatherland. Of what sort, speak, a fatherland? is it the former, which
they left? Not, he says; For if they were remembering that, they would
have had a time to bend back; but now they reach after a better, that
is, a heavenly, of which God is craftsman and creator. For this God is
not ashamed to be called their God. 5. Let us then imitate these too, I
exhort; let us overlook the present things, let us desire the things to
come, let us take this woman as a teacher, and toward God always


let us flee for refuge, and from that one let us ask all things. For
nothing is equal to prayer; for this also makes the impossible things
possible, and the hard things easy, and the difficult things it sets
straight. This the blessed David also was succeeding in; therefore he
was also saying: Seven times in the day I praised you upon the judgments
of your righteousness. And if a king man, being baptized with ten
thousand cares, and being dragged about from every side, so many times
in the day was calling upon God, what defense or pardon would we have,
having so much leisure, and not continually entreating him, and this
being about to reap so great a gain? For it is impossible, impossible,
for a man praying with the fitting eagerness, and calling upon God
continually, ever to sin. And how, I say. He who has heated his
understanding, and has raised up the soul, and has migrated himself
toward heaven, and thus has called his own Master, and has been reminded
of the sins, and has conversed with him about the forgiveness of these,
and has called upon him to become propitious and tame, from the spending
time in these words puts away all worldly care, and is winged, and
becomes higher than the human passions; and if he sees an enemy after
the prayer, he will no longer see him as an enemy; and if a well-formed
woman, he will not be broken toward the sight, the fire according to the
prayer remaining within, and thrusting away every out-of-place reasoning.
But since being men it is likely also to fall into sloth, when one and
a second hour and a third have passed after the prayer, and you see the
heat that has come to be for you about to flow under little by little,
run quickly again upon the prayer, and heat your understanding that has
grown cold. And if you do this through all the day, heating the intervals
between by the density of the prayers, you will not give the devil a
starting-point and an entrance against your reasonings. And what we do
when breakfasting and being about to drink, when we see the water that
has been heated having grown cold, we set it upon the hearth again, so
as to be heated quickly; this let us do here too, and as upon coals, of
the prayer, setting our mouth, thus again let us kindle our understanding
into piety, and let us imitate the builders. For those too, being about
to build bricks, because of the rottenness of the matter bind the
building with long woods, and they do not even do this through much
interval, but through a little, that by the density of these woods they
may work the composition of the bricks safer. Do this also you, and all
the worldly =54.667= actions, as with some bindings of woods, taking
them by the continuity of the prayers, fence your life from every side.
If then you do thus, even if ten thousand winds blow thereafter, even if
trials even if despondencies, even if some unpleasant reasonings, even
if any terrible thing comes upon, it will not be able to throw down that
house bound together with prayers so dense. And how is it possible, he
says, for a worldly man, nailed to a court, to pray according to three
hours of the day, and to run out into church? It is possible, and
exceedingly easy; for even if to run to church is not easy, standing
there before the doors, and nailed to the court, it is possible to pray;
for there is not so much need of a voice, as of understanding, nor of a
stretching of hands, as of a soul that has been strained, nor of a
schema, but of a mind; since this Anna too, not because she sent forth a
splendid and great voice, for this was being heard, but because she was
crying great things within according to the heart. For her voice was not
being heard, it says, and God was hearing her. This also many others
have often done, and the ruler crying from within, threatening,
stretching himself, being mad, standing before the doors having sealed
themselves, and having prayed few words according to understanding,
having gone in they changed him and tamed him, and from wild set him
down tame; and nothing either from the place, or from the time, or from
the silence was hindered toward this prayer. 6. This then also you do:
groan bitterly, be reminded of your sins,


look up into heaven, say according to understanding: Have mercy on me, O
God, and your prayer has been completed. For he who has said, Have
mercy, has displayed a confession, and has recognized his own sins; for
it is of those who have sinned to be mercied. He who has said, Have
mercy on me, has received forgiveness of the things done amiss; for he
who has been mercied is not punished. He who has said, Have mercy on me,
has attained a kingdom of heavens; for whom God will mercy, he does not
deliver from punishment only, but also thinks worthy of the good things
to come. Let us not then put forward saying, that a house of prayer is
not near; for us ourselves, if we are sober, the grace of the Spirit of
God has made temples, so that much from every side is the ease for us.
For our worship is not such, as among Jews it was formerly, having much
the sensible, and needing much business. For there the one praying
needed both to go up into the temple, and to buy a turtledove, and to
handle woods and fire, and to take a knife, and to stand beside the
altar, and to accomplish many other commands; but here nothing such, but
wherever you are, you have the altar with yourself, and the knife, and
the victim, yourself being both priest, and altar, and victim. For
wherever you are, you are able to set the altar, having displayed a
sober choice only, and nothing hinders a place, nor impedes a time, but
even if you do not bend knees, even if you do not strike breast, and
stretch the hands into heaven, but display only a warm under =54.668=
standing, you have completed the whole of the prayer. It is possible
also for a woman holding a distaff, and working at the loom, to look up
into heaven in the understanding, and to call God with heat; it is
possible also for a man throwing himself into a marketplace, and walking
by himself to make earnest prayers; and another sitting at a workshop
and sewing hides, to dedicate the soul toward the Master; it is possible
for a household slave, both buying, and going up and going down, and
standing beside a kitchen, when it is not possible to come into church,
to make an earnest and awakened prayer. God is not ashamed of a place;
one thing only he seeks, a warm understanding, and a temperate soul. And
that you may learn, that there is not need of a schema and of places
altogether and of times, but of a noble and awakened mind, Paul lying
on his back upon the prison, and not standing upright (for the wood, by
which the feet were bound, was not allowing), since he prayed with
eagerness lying, shook the prison, and made the foundations totter, and
bound the jailer, and after these things led him by the hand toward the
holy mystic initiation. Again Hezekiah not standing upright, nor bending
knees, but lying on his back upon the bed through the infirmity, having
turned himself upon the wall, since he called God warmly and with a
temperate soul, both called back a sentence that had been brought out,
and drew much goodwill, and came back to the former health. And not upon
holy and great men only, but also upon wicked ones one would see this
happening. For the robber too, not having stood in a house of prayer,
nor having bent knees, but having been stretched upon the cross, from
few words attained the kingdom of the heavens; another in mire and a
pit, another in a pit and beasts, another in the very belly of the
whale, having called upon God, dissolved all the things laid on, and
drew the goodwill from above. And saying these things, I exhort to haunt
the churches continually, and in a house to pray with much quiet, and at
leisure bending knees, and stretching the hands; but if either through
time, or through place we should be caught between many others, not for
this to leave the accustomed prayers, but in this manner, which I have
said toward your love, pray and call upon God, as going to have nothing
less from this prayer. These things I have said, not that you may clap
and marvel, but that through the works you may display them, and taking
the time of the night and that of the day, and the time of the work in
prayers and petitions. If thus we manage the things according to
ourselves, both the


present life we will accomplish with safety, and we will attain the
kingdom of the heavens; which may it be that we all attain, by the grace
and love of man of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom to
the Father the glory, together with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and
unto the ages of the ages. Amen.

=54.669= HOMILY V. Toward those who are gathered in feasts only, and
what ever a feast is; and toward those who charge the providence of God,
because of there being rich and poor in the life; and that poverty is
most useful and that it has more pleasure than wealth everywhere and
safety; and on Anna.
1. In vain, as it seems, we called upon those who came to us at the
former gathering, persuading them to remain upon the paternal house, and
not with those who appear to us in a feast only to stand by and stand
off again; or rather not in vain. For even if none of these was
persuaded by the things said, yet the wage has been completed for us,
and the things of the defense toward God have been filled. Therefore the
one who speaks, even if someone attends, even if he does not attend,
must put down the seeds, and deposit the silver, that no longer toward
him, but toward the bankers the word may be for God. Which then also we
have done, having convicted, having rebuked, having called upon, having
admonished. For we also reminded of the son who had eaten up the
substance, and had come back toward the paternal house, and we added all
the misery, and the famine, and the shame, and the taunts and the other
things, whatever he endured upon the foreign land, wishing by this
example to make them more temperate; and not even here did we set the
word, but we also displayed the affection of the father about them, not
having demanded of them accounts of the sloth, but having received these
with hands on the back, and having given pardon for the things done
amiss, and having opened the house, and having set the table beside, and
having thrown around them the robe from the teaching, and having
furnished them all the other tendance. But those did not imitate that
son, nor did they condemn the former withdrawal, nor did they remain
upon the paternal house, but they leapt away again. It would be yours
then, of you who are always with us, to bring them back, and to persuade
them to share with us the feast according to each gathering. For even if
the Pentecost has passed, yet the feast has not passed; for every
assembly is a feast. From where is this clear? From the words themselves
of Christ, through which he says: Where two or three are gathered into
my name, there I am in the midst of them. And when Christ is in the
midst of those who have been collected, what other proof of a feast do
you seek greater than this? Where teaching and prayers, where blessings
of fathers and hearing of divine laws, where an assembly of brothers and
a bond of genuine love, where converse toward God, and a dialogue of God
toward men, how would it not be a feast and a festival? For the feasts
not the multitude, but the virtue of those being collected is accustomed
to make; not the costliness of the garments, but the beautifying of the
piety; not the abundance of the table, but the providence of the soul.
For a very great feast is a good conscience. And just as upon the outer
festivals, the one who neither has a garment to throw around that is
splendid, nor to enjoy a more abundant table, but living in poverty and
famine and the last evils, does not =54.670= perceive the time of the
feast, even if he sees the whole city dancing, but so much more he is in
pain and is bitten, inasmuch as he also sees the others in enjoyment,
but himself in want; but the one who is rich and living in luxury, and
having robes to change according to each, and enjoying much prosperity,
even if there is not present


a time of a feast, thinks to feast; so then also upon the spiritual
affairs, the one living in righteousness and successes, even if a feast
is not present, keeps a feast, reaping the clean pleasure from the
conscience; but the one being fed in sin and wickedness, and knowing
many evils in himself, even when a feast is present, is unfeasted most
of all. So that it is possible for us, if we wish, according to each day
to keep a feast, if we take care of virtue, and cleanse out our
conscience. For what does the gathering that has passed have more than
the present? or is it not in the tumult and the trouble only, but in
nothing else? For when both the enjoyment of the holy mysteries, and the
communion of the other spiritual things, such as I mean of prayer, and
of hearing, and of blessings, and of love, and of all the rest, is the
same also today, this day will be nothing less than that, neither for
you, nor for me who speak. For those who heard us then, these will also
hear now; but those not present now, were not even present then, even if
they seemed to be present in the body; they do not hear now; or rather
they were not even hearing then; and not only were they not hearing, but
they were even annoying those who hear, putting in tumult and trouble.
So that equal to me is the theater both then and now, and the collection
of the hearers, the same, and this is nothing less than that. But, if
one must also say something wonderful, this has something more than
that, inasmuch as also the discourse is untroubled, and the teaching
without tumult, and the hearing with more understanding, no tumult of us
troubling the hearing. 2. And these things I say, not dishonoring the
multitude of those then collected, but persuading you not to be gloomy,
nor to be downcast through the fewness of those now collected here. For
we do not wish to see a multitude of bodies upon the church, but a
multitude of hearers. Since then the same diners both then and now are
present to us, with the same eagerness today too I will set the feast
beside you, having come back upon the subject, which the time of the
feast cut through. For just as when Pentecost was present, it was
unseasonable, having left speaking about the good things given to us at
that time, to hold to the former sequence; so now Pentecost having
passed, in season we will take up the sequence of the history, holding
to the subject according to Anna. For one must not consider if the
things said are many, and in many days, but if we have come toward the
end of the subject. For also those who have found a treasure, even if
they take ten thousand moneys from there, do not stand off first, until
they have drawn out the whole; for not the taking many things, but the
leaving nothing, this especially is naturally fitted to restore them.
And if those who are mad about the moneys use so great earnestness about
the things that perish and do not remain =54.671= , much more must we do
this upon the divine treasures, and not stand off first, until we have
drawn out all that appears. And I said that which appears, since to draw
out all things is impossible. For a spring continually bubbling of the
divine thoughts is the power, never failing, nor being spent. Let us not
then grow weary; for the word is not even for us about the chance
things, but about prayer, our hope; and a prayer, through which the
barren became a mother, and the childless many-childed, and the one in
pain in good cheer; through which the maimed nature was being corrected,
and a womb that had been shut was being opened, and all the impossible
things were becoming possible. Therefore let us search through all
things little by little, having unfolded according to each saying, so as
not even the smallest, as possible, to run past us. For this then we
spent two whole discourses upon two sayings only, both upon the first
that says, My heart was made firm in the Lord, and the second that after
that, My horn was raised in my God. It is sequential then today to come
upon the third. And what is this? My mouth was widened upon my enemies,
she says; I was gladdened in your salvation. Attend to the exactness of
the word. She did not say: My mouth was sharpened upon my enemies; for
she was not prepared toward revilings and jeers, not toward taunts and
accusation, but toward


exhortation and counsel, toward correction and admonition. For this she
did not say, My tongue was sharpened upon my enemies, but, It was
widened. I have enjoyed ease, she says, I am able to speak freely. Now I
have beaten off the shame, I have come back toward boldness. And not
even here did she speak by name the rival woman, but simply by the
undefined of the appellation, as with a mask she hid the one who had
grieved so much. She did not say, what many women say: God shamed her,
he dashed down and threw down the polluted and arrogant and
great-speaking one; but simply, My mouth was widened upon my enemies; I
was gladdened in your salvation. See how she keeps the same law through
the whole prayer. For just as in the beginning she was saying, My heart
was made firm in the Lord, my horn was raised in my God, my mouth was
widened upon my enemies; so also here, I was gladdened in your
salvation. Not simply, in salvation, but, In your salvation; For not
since I was saved, she says, but since through you I was saved, for this
I rejoice and exult. Such are the souls of the saints; more than the
gifts they rejoice upon the God who has given; for they do not love him
through his things, but those through him. This of well-minded household
slaves, this of thankful servants, to set their own Master before all
their own things. Thus, I exhort, let us too be disposed; and if we sin,
let us not be in pain, because we are punished, but because we have
provoked the Master; and if we succeed, let us not rejoice through the
kingdom of the heavens, but because we have pleased the king of the
heavens. For to the one who has a mind, more fearful than all gehenna is
the having offended God, just as also more desired than all a kingdom is
the having pleased God. And do not marvel if upon God one must be
disposed thus, where even upon men many are thus disposed. Sons at any
rate we often have genuine; and if unwilling we set some evil upon them,
we avenge ourselves and =54.672= punish; and upon friends too we do
this. And if upon friends and sons we think the having grieved those to
be much harder than the having been punished, much more upon God one
must be disposed thus, and think it harder than all gehenna to do some
of the things not seeming good to him. Such was the blessed Paul; for
this he was saying: I am persuaded that neither angels, nor rules, nor
powers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor height, nor depth,
nor some other creation will be able to separate us from the love of
God, that in Christ Jesus our Lord. And we too when we call the holy
martyrs blessed, we first call them blessed from the wounds, and then
from the prizes; from the blows, and then from the crowns laid up. For
from the wounds the prizes, not from the prizes the wounds, take the
beginning and the subject. 3. Thus also the blessed Paul before the good
things laid up was rejoicing upon the painful things happening to him
through Christ, and was crying saying, I rejoice in the sufferings for
you; and again, And not only, but we also boast in the afflictions; and
again, That it has been granted to us from God, not only the believing
into him, but also the suffering for him. For it is truly a very great
grace, to be thought worthy to suffer something through Christ, and a
completed crown, and a recompense not less than the coming exchange; and
this those know as many as know how to love Christ genuinely and warmly.
Such also was this woman, having a warm longing about God, and a burning
affection; for this she was saying, I was gladdened in your salvation.
For nothing was common to her with the earth, but she was overlooking
all human help, and she was winged by the grace of the Spirit, and she
was lifted, and toward God in all things she was looking, and the
loosing of the terrible things laid on she was seeking from there. For
she knew, she knew clearly, that the human things, of whatever sort they
are, imitate the nature of those who have given; and we need everywhere
the turn from above, if we are going to ride at a safe anchor. For this
she also in all things was fleeing for refuge toward him, and having
received the grace, she was rejoicing more upon the one who has given,
and


giving thanks she was saying: There is none holy, as the Lord, and there
is none righteous, as our God, and there is none holy except you. For
ungraspable is his judgment, she says, clean and not to be cheated the
vote. Did you see a thought of a well-minded soul? For she did not say
toward herself: For what great thing has come to be for me? and what
more than the others? What the rival received of old and with much
abundance, this after a long time I, after labor, and tears, and
supplication, and petition, and much toil. But since she was well
persuaded about the providence of God, she does not demand of the Master
accounts of the things that come to be, which many of men do, judging
God according to each day. And if they see the one being rich, and the
other being poor, they move ten thousand words against his providence.
What do you do, O man? Paul did not allow you to judge the fellow-slave
=54.673= saying thus: So as not to judge anything before time, until the
Lord comes; and do you drag the Master into a court, and demand a word
of the things that have been done, and do you not shudder, nor fear? And
of what sort of pardon will you hit, and of what defense, tell me,
taking so many proofs of his providence according to each day and hour,
but from the seeming to be irregularity according to the wealth and the
poverty voting against the good order in all things, and this unjustly?
For if with the fitting mind, and a toiled understanding you were
wishing to examine these things too, even if there was nothing else that
establishes the providence of God, the wealth and the poverty would have
especially shown this clearly. For if you take away the poverty, you
have taken away all the constitution of the life, and you have corrupted
our life; and neither sailor, nor pilot, nor farmer, nor builder, nor
weaver, nor shoemaker, nor carpenter, nor bronze-striker, nor leather-
cutter, nor bread-maker, nor any other of these craftsmen will there be;
and these not being, all things will be gone for us. For now, as some
best teacher, the necessity of poverty sitting upon each of these,
pushes them toward the works even unwilling; but if all were going to be
rich, all were going to live in idleness; and thus all things would have
been destroyed and corrupted. And apart from what has been said it is
also otherwise easy to stop their mouths again from the things
themselves which they charge. For for the sake of what, tell me, do you
accuse the providence of God? because the one has acquired more, and the
other fewer moneys? What then? if in the most necessary things, and much
greater, and those that hold our life together, we show there is an
equal share for all men, will you vote for the providence of God? All
necessity. For if the not all enjoying equally one thing, I mean moneys,
you make a proof of there being no providence, if they appear all
enjoying equally, not of one thing, nor of one so cheap, but of more and
much greater, it is quite clear that even unwilling from there you will
be compelled to vote for the providence of God. Come then upon the
things that hold our life together let us go with the word, and let us
search them through with exactness, and let us see if the rich man has
more than the poor in those. For example, the rich man has Thasian, and
many other such drinks that have been drugged, and prepared with much
sauce; but the springs of the waters are set forth commonly to all, both
to rich, and to poor. Perhaps you laughed hearing this equality. Then
learn, how much more honorable than all wine is the nature of water, and
more necessary and more useful; and then you will condemn yourself, and
you will know the true wealth of the poor. For wine having been taken
away, no great harm would come to be to anyone, except to the weak only;

most necessary things and those that hold our life together the poor man
is nothing taken advantage of, but, if one must also say something
wonderful, he even has more than the one who is rich. For many rich it
is possible to see, by the infirmity of the body that through the luxury


comes to be for them, abstaining from the drinking of water the most;
but the poor man =54.674= through all the life enjoys these streams
without fear, as upon springs of honey, thus running upon the springs of
the waters, and reaping from there the unmixed and clean pleasure. And
what the nature of fire? is it not more necessary than ten thousand
treasures and all human wealth? And this again equally to rich and poor
is the treasure of the need set forth. And the benefit that comes from
the air to our bodies, and the light of the ray, does it come to be more
for those well off, and less for the poor, and do those look at it with
four, but these with two eyes only? But not even this would anyone be
able to say; for both to rich and to poor an equal measure of the
enjoyment has been appointed, or rather here too one would see the poor
having more than those who are rich, inasmuch as they also have the
senses clearer, and the eye sharper, and all the perceptions more exact.
Therefore they also reap a more genuine pleasure, and they live in
luxury more and enjoy in the contemplation of the creation. And not upon
these elements only, but also upon the other things that have been
brought in to us from nature, you will see much equality, or rather the
advantage being with the poor. For also sleep, sweeter than all luxury
and more necessary, and more useful than all food, is easier for the
poor, than for the rich; and not easier only, but also more unmixed. For
for those the much luxury, and the neither eating being hungry, nor
drinking being thirsty, nor sleeping being drowsy, is accustomed to
dissolve the pleasure in all things; for not so the nature of the
affairs, as the necessary of the need, makes each of these sweet for us.
Not so at any rate the drinking wine sweet and flower-scented, as the
drinking water being thirsty is accustomed to gladden; not so the eating
cakes, as the eating being hungry; not so the sleeping upon a soft bed,
as the sleeping being drowsy; which all things are more with the poor,
than with those who are rich. And the things of the health of the body
and of all the other good condition, are they not set forth common both
to rich and to poor? Can anyone say or show, that poor only are infirm,
but rich remain through all in clean health? The contrary rather it is
possible to see, poor not easily being caught by the incurable diseases,
but everywhere in the bodies of those who are rich these blooming. Gouts
at any rate, and heavinesses of head, and palsies, and incurable
distortions of nerves, and wicked and corrupted fluxes of all sorts,
these especially are accustomed to trouble those who live in luxury,
those who smell of unguents, not those who toil and are miserable, and
from the work according to each day furnish the necessary food. 5. For
this also than the beggars those who live with luxury are all more
wretched; and this not even the luxurious themselves would speak
against. Often at any rate a rich man lying upon a soft bed, household
slaves and maidservants being present, enjoying much tendance from all,
having heard a poor man through the alley crying, asking bread, wept,
and groaned, and prayed to become such with the health, rather than with
the wealth to have the infirmity. And not upon the health only, but also
upon the making of children again one will see the rich man having
nothing more than the poor; but similarly both with these, and with
those both many-childedness =54.675= and childlessness; or rather here
too one would see the rich man being less. For the poor man, even if he
does not become a father, does not perceive much of the pain; but the
rich, inasmuch as he sees the substance being increased, so much more
he is bitten by the childlessness, perceiving no pleasure through the
desolation of the heir. And the lot of the poor man, even if he goes
away childless, through the cheapness not being fought over, has passed
over into friends and kinsmen; but that of the one who is rich, drawing
many eyes toward itself from every side, has often come into the hands
of the enemies of the one who has died; and this that one living, and
seeing upon


others happening, he will live a life harder than all death, and
expecting this to happen also upon himself. But the things of death, are
they not common? do not untimely deaths come to be both upon rich and
upon poor, and after the death too is not similarly both this and that
body dissolved, and does it become ash and dust, and bear worms? But the
things of the burial-clothes are not common, he says. And what is the
profit? For when you heap upon the rich man much robe and costly and
shot with gold, nothing else or more do you procure for him but the
hate, and greater the accusation, and you open the mouths of all against
the one who has died, and you draw ten thousand curses upon him, and you
kindle a more vehement slander of the greed, each being burst, and being
choked, and cursing the one who has died, because not even having died
did he dissolve the madness about the moneys. And this is not the only
terrible thing, but that he also opens the eyes of the wall-diggers. So
that the more tendance becomes a pretext of more unseemliness for him.
For the body of the poor man no one would choose to strip thereafter;
for the cheapness of the garments becomes a guard for the wrapping of
the body. But here bolts and bars and doors and guards =54.676= , and
all things at random and in vain, the desire of the moneys persuading
those who know how to work such evils to dare all things. So that the
more honor brings on more insult to the one who has died, and the one
who has taken cheap burial-clothes lies enjoying the honor; but the one
costly is stripped and dishonored; and if none of these come to be, not
even thus will there be something more for him, but or the furnishing a
more abundant table to the worm, and the rottenness becoming more. These
things then, tell me, are they worthy of blessings? And who is so
wretched and miserable, as from these to think a man to be zealous-after?
And not these only, but also the other things going upon according to
each, and searching through with exactness, we will find the poor having
much more than those who are rich. These things then all looking at with
exactness, and going after all the others (For give, he says, a starting-
point to a wise man, and he will be wiser), and remembering that
continually, that not even from the very abundance of the moneys will
there be something more for those who have acquired, but cares, and
agonies, and fears, and dangers; let us think we have nothing less than
those who are rich. For if we are sober, we will also have more, both in
the affairs according to God, and in all the present things. For also
pleasure and safety, and a good glory, and health of body, and
philosophy of soul, and good hopes, and the not sinning quickly, one
will find more with the poor, than with those who are rich. Let us not
then according to the ill-minded of the household slaves murmur, nor
accuse the Master, but in all things let us give thanks, and let us
think one thing only to be terrible, sin, and a good, righteousness. For
if we are thus disposed, neither disease, nor poverty, nor dishonor, nor
any other of the things that seem to be painful will bite us; but having
reaped a clean pleasure through all things, we will attain the good
things to come, by the grace and love of man of our Lord Jesus Christ,
with whom to the Father the glory, together with the Holy Spirit, unto
the ages of the ages. Amen.
