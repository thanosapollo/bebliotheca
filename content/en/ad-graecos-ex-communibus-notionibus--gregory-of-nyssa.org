GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA
How, though saying three persons in the divinity, we do not say three gods, to
the Greeks from the common notions
If the name God were indicative of person, saying three persons we would of
necessity say three gods; but if the name God is significative of essence,
confessing one essence of the holy Trinity we reasonably dogmatize one God,
since of one essence the one name is God. Wherefore also, consistently with
both the essence and the name, there is one God and not three. For we do not
say God and God and God, as we say Father and Son and Holy Spirit, since with
the names that are significative of the persons we intertwine the conjunction
"and" because the persons are not the same, but rather other and differing
from one another according to the very signification of the names; but with
the name God, which is indicative of the essence from some property belonging
to it, we do not join the conjunction "and" so as to say God and God and God,
since it is the same essence, of which the persons are and which the name God
signifies; wherefore also it is the same God; and with the same, and for the
indication of the same, the conjunction "and" is never intertwined 3,=1.20=.
If we say Father God and Son God and Holy Spirit God, or God the Father and
God the Son and God the Holy Spirit, we join the conjunction "and" in thought
to the names of the persons, as for example to Father, Son, Holy Spirit, so
that it is Father and Son and Holy Spirit, that is person and person and
person, wherefore also three persons. But the name God is predicated
absolutely and in the same way of each of the persons without the conjunction
"and", so that we cannot say God and God and God, but we understand the name
as said <second and> third in utterance because of the underlying persons, yet
applied in the seconding and the thirding without the conjunction "and"
because God is not other and other. For it is not inasmuch as the Father
preserves otherness toward the Son that according to this the Father is God;
for thus the Son would not be God; for if, because the Father is Father, for
this reason the Father is also God, then since the Son is not Father, the Son
is not God; but if the Son is God, it is not because he is Son. Likewise also
the Father is not God because he is Father, but because he is such an essence,
of which is Father and Son and through which Father is God and Son is God and
Holy Spirit is God. And since the essence is not divided in each of the
persons so as also to be three essences according to the persons, it is clear
that neither will the name be divided, which signifies the essence, that is
God, so as to be three gods. But just as the Father is essence, the Son is
essence, the Holy Spirit is essence, and not three essences, so also the
Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God, and not three gods. For
there is one God and the same, since there is also one essence and the same,
even if 3,=1.21= each of the persons is said both to have essence and to be
God. For either it is necessary to say three essences of Father and Son and
Holy Spirit, since each of the persons is essence, which is most irrational,
since we do not even say that Peter and Paul and Barnabas are three essences;
for of such persons the essence is one and the same; or, saying one essence,
of which is Father and Son and Holy Spirit, although we know each of the
persons to have essence, we justly and consistently say one God, even if we
believe each of the persons to be God, because of the commonality of the
essence.
For just as, because the Father differs from both the Son and the Holy Spirit,
we say three persons of Father and Son and Holy Spirit, so, since the Father
does not differ from both Son and Holy Spirit according to the essence, we say
that the essence of Father and Son and Holy Spirit is one. For if, where there
is difference, there is a triad because of the difference, where there is
identity, there is a monad because of the identity; and there is identity of
the persons according to the essence; therefore there is a monad of them
according to the essence. And if according to the essence there is a monad of
the holy Trinity, it is clear that also according to the name God. For this is
indicative of the essence, not presenting the what of it (clearly, since the
what of the divine essence is unthinkable and incomprehensible), but taken
from some property belonging 3,=1.22= to it it intimates it, just as "capable
of neighing" and "capable of laughing," being properties of natures, when
said, signify the natures of which they are properties. There is then a
property of the eternal essence, of which is Father and Son and Holy Spirit,
to oversee and contemplate and know all things, not only those that come to be
in act, but also those taken in thought, which belongs to that essence alone,
inasmuch as it is also cause of all things, having made all things and ruling
over all as its own products, and administering all human affairs by some
beneficial and ineffable reason. Taken from this, the name God, said properly,
signifies that essence which truly rules over all as creator of all. Since
therefore the essence, of which is Father and Son and Holy Spirit, is one, and
the name intimating it is one (I mean, of course, God), there will be one God
properly and consistently with the account of the essence, no account
compelling us to say three gods, just as we do not say three essences. For if
in the case of Peter and Paul and Barnabas we do not say three essences
because they are of one, how much more in the case of Father and Son and Holy
Spirit shall we justly not do this; for if the essence is not to be divided
into three because of the persons, it is clear that neither is God, since God
does not indicate person, but the essence. For if God indicated person, one
and only 3,=1.23= of the persons would be called God, the one signified by
such a name, just as also Father alone is called the Father because this name
is indicative of person.
But if someone should say that we call Peter and Paul and Barnabas three
particular essences [it is clear that this is individual]; for this is more
proper to say, let him know that when we say particular essence, that is
individual, we wish to signify nothing other than the individual, which is
person. Wherefore even if we should say three particular essences, that is
individual, we say nothing other than three persons; but God does not follow
upon persons, as has been shown. Therefore neither upon the particular, which
is individual, essence; for individual essence said of individuals is the same
as person. What then is to be said to this, that we call Peter and Paul and
Barnabas three men? For if these are persons, and persons are not signified by
the significative name of the common essence, similarly neither is the
so-called particular or individual essence, since this is the same as person:
for what reason do we call them three men, they being of one essence, of which
man is indicative, if we utter this neither because of the persons nor because
of the being called particular or individual essence? We say that we say this
catachrestically and not properly, because of a certain custom that has
prevailed from necessary causes, which are not observed in the case of the
holy Trinity, so that we might do this same thing also in its case. And
3,=1.24= these are the causes: the definition of man is not always observed in
the same individuals or persons; for when the former die, others are
constituted in their place, and again while the same often remain others
besides come to be, so that the definition of the nature, that is of man, is
observed sometimes in these, sometimes in those, and sometimes in more,
sometimes in fewer. For this reason then, of the addition and the subtraction
and of the dying and birth of the individuals in which the definition of man
is observed, we are compelled to say both many men and few, the common custom
having been driven aside by the change and alteration of the persons even
contrary to the very account of the essence, so as to number with the persons
in a certain way also essences. But in the case of the holy Trinity nothing of
this sort ever happens; for the same persons and not other and other must
always be said, remaining according to the same and in the same way, receiving
neither any addition into a tetrad nor diminution into a dyad; (for neither is
another person begotten or proceeds from the Father or from one of the
persons, so that the Trinity should ever be a tetrad; nor does one of these
three persons ever die even as in the twinkling of an eye, so that the Trinity
should become a dyad even in thought;) and since no addition and diminution,
change and alteration comes about in the three persons of Father and Son and
Holy Spirit, there is nothing that knocks our mind aside toward saying,
besides the three persons, also three gods. Again, all the persons of man
3,=1.25= do not have their being from the same person immediately, but some
from this one, others from that one, so that besides the caused there are also
many and various causes. But in the case of the holy Trinity it is not so; for
there is one person and the same, that of the Father, from whom the Son is
begotten and the Holy Spirit proceeds. Wherefore also we properly and
confidently say that the one cause together with his caused is one God, since
he also coexists with them. For the persons of the divinity are divided from
one another neither by time nor by place, not by will, not by occupation, not
by activity, not by passion, not by any of such things as are observed in the
case of men; except only that the Father is Father and not Son and the Son is
Son and not Father, and likewise the Holy Spirit is neither Father nor Son.
Wherefore no necessity knocks us aside to say that the three persons are three
gods, as in our case we call the many persons many men because of the causes
stated. And that it is because of the causes stated and not according to a
necessary account that we call the many persons of man many men, would become
clear from this: the same according to the same cannot be one and many; but
Peter and Paul and Barnabas are confessedly according to "man" one man;
therefore according to the same, that is according to "man," they cannot be
many. But they are called many men, catachrestically of course and not
properly; and what is said catachrestically is not worthy nor sufficient among
3,=1.26= those who think well to destroy what both is and is said properly.
Therefore one must not say, in the case of the three persons of the divine
essence, three gods, since according to "God" there is one God and the same
because of the identity of the essence, of which God is significative in the
manner stated.
But if someone should say that Scripture numbers three men, trying from a
surplus out of our own to refute us, such a one has been shown neither a right
nor a pious hearer of Scripture; for this one would not even have moved
arguments against us concerning whether one must say that the three persons of
the divine essence are three gods, perceiving especially that Scripture,
giving Father and Son and Holy Spirit and God the Word, God not Word (that is
God the Father), handing down God Holy Spirit as God, altogether declines to
say three gods, considering polytheism impiety and throughout proclaiming one
God, neither confusing the persons nor dividing the divinity, but rather
preserving identity of divinity in the particularity of hypostases, that is of
three persons. If then such a one were right and took account of what is just,
perceiving these things from Scripture he would not have hastened to confirm
his own, but would rather have inquired, asking to learn the cause why
Scripture says three men although knowing the whole nature as one man,
according to "Man as 3,=1.27= grass are his days," uttering the common of the
nature in the singular; for declaring what belongs to the common of the nature
it said it of one, because it knows the whole man as one man and not many; for
he would have heard, asking as a lover of learning, that Scripture also, as a
good nurse knowing men as her own infants, sometimes lisps with them, using
certain of the names in the same way, clearly not wounding the perfect and not
wronging those able to partake of solid food; for not defining the lisping as
dogmas does it harm the perfect, but condescending to the infants through
compassion and imitating their things it thus leads them up to itself,
bringing them to the perfection of age. Yet it also defines and dogmatizes the
perfect things according to what is fitting to itself and proper for those
being discipled to be taught. Indeed, that in saying God has ears and eyes and
mouth and the remaining bodily parts it does not hand down such a thing as a
dogma, defining the divine as composite of various members, but taking such
things by metaphor from ours in the manner stated, toward the leading-up, as I
said, of those not able to go immediately to the incorporeal, it sets forth
the dogmas with certain solid and most distinct expressions, saying that God
is spirit and is present everywhere, wherever one might go, wisely educating
these in his simplicity and uncircumscribedness 3,=1.28=. Thus also, saying
three men because of custom, so as not to make strange what is common and in
use among the many, it also says one because of accuracy, so as not to shake
what is perfect and observed in the nature of things; and the one we consider
a condescension that has come about for the useful and beneficial of the more
infantile, and the other we define as a dogma set forth for confirmation and
tradition of perfection.
But some, being disputatious and aroused toward leaving nothing
uncontroverted, in order, as I at least would say, to exercise laboriously
both those who speak and those who hear, overlooking what has been said by us
and taking what is not granted as if confessed, and using a paralogism, they
construct what seems good and say that just as we say hypostasis differs in
nothing from hypostasis inasmuch as hypostasis, that is insofar as hypostasis,
and we do not for this reason say that all hypostases are one hypostasis; and
again essence differs in nothing from essence inasmuch as essence, and we do
not for this reason say that all essences are one essence, so we might say God
differs in nothing from God inasmuch as God, and not for this reason are the
three hypostases, of which God is predicated, one God; and again, saying man
differs in nothing from man inasmuch as man, we do not take away that Peter
and Paul and Barnabas are three men; for essence differs from essence not
insofar as essence, but insofar as such-and-such an essence, and hypostasis
from hypostasis insofar as such-and-such a hypostasis; 3,=1.29= likewise also
man from man, inasmuch as such-and-such a man, and again God from God,
inasmuch as such-and-such a God; and "such-and-such" or "such-and-such" is
customarily said of two or even more. But these things, as we said, those men
say; and we shall show that the whole is a sophism and that what has been said
is nothing else, prevailing in no other way than by using the very things said
and showing that one must not say such-and-such a God and such-and-such a God,
or such-and-such a man and such-and-such; but if at all, such-and-such a
hypostasis of God and such-and-such a hypostasis of man; for we justly say
many hypostases of the one man and three hypostases of the one God. Now what
is called "such-and-such" wishes to distinguish something from something
sharing in that name to which "such-and-such" is added; for example we say
that man is such-and-such an animal, wishing to distinguish him from the
horse, for instance, which shares with him in the name of animal, but differs
by the rational and the irrational. And something is distinguished from
something either by essence or by hypostasis or by essence and hypostasis; and
by essence man is distinguished from horse, and by hypostasis Paul from Peter,
and by essence and hypostasis this hypostasis of man from this hypostasis of
horse. But when the account of those differing simply according to essence and
of those differing according to hypostasis and not according to essence has
been made clear, the account also concerning those distinguished according to
essence and hypostasis together will be evident. Let the account then be
examined by us concerning these; for that those differing according to essence
are called two or three essences, and those differing according to hypostasis
are likewise called two and three hypostases, 3,=1.30= both they themselves
and we confess; but we differ, in that in the case of Peter and Paul they say
one must say <two> men, but we, not properly however and according to the
scientific account; for we have now not even one account concerning common and
catachrestic usage; for this has force neither toward the taking away of
something nor toward the establishing. Let it therefore first be made clear to
us why we say that man and horse, or horse and dog, differ inasmuch as
such-and-such essences. Or is it evident that it is because they differ from
one another according to the things accustomed to characterize essences, as
for example by rational and irrational, capable of neighing, capable of
barking, and if there is anything of this sort. For in saying such-and-such an
essence we say nothing other than an existence sharing in life, in
contradistinction to that which is not such, an existence naturally able to
reason, toward distinction from one differing by irrationality, an existence
having the capacity to neigh as characterizing, and if there is anything of
this sort. For in place of such differences and properties, "such-and-such"
and "of such a kind" is added to the essence or also to every genus toward
distinction of the species under it, as for example such-and-such an essence
instead of sensitive or insensitive, such-and-such an animal instead of
rational or irrational. And again we say: Paul differs from Peter, inasmuch as
there is such-and-such a hypostasis of each of them, since they differ from
one another according to some of the things naturally able to constitute
hypostasis and not essence, as for example by baldness, tallness, fatherhood,
sonship, and if there is anything 3,=1.31= of this sort; for it is evident
that species and individual are not the same, that is essence and hypostasis;
for when someone says individual, that is hypostasis, he immediately sends the
mind of the hearer to seek curly-haired, grey-eyed, son, father, and if
anything similar; but when he says species, that is essence, toward knowing of
course rational animal, mortal, receptive of intellect and knowledge,
irrational animal, mortal, capable of neighing, and such things. And if
essence and individual, which is hypostasis, are not the same, neither are the
things characterizing this and that the same. And if these also are not the
same, they cannot be joined to the same names; but some to those laid down
according to essences or of essences, and others to those said according to
individual. There are then three names concerning which the inquiry is:
essence, individual, man. And to essence we join "such-and-such" toward
distinction, as I said, of the species under it that differ from one another
according to essence; and to hypostasis again likewise we yoke "such-and-such"
toward division of persons that share with one another in this name, that is
of hypostasis, and differ from one another not by the things that characterize
essence, but by the so-called accidents. In what manner then do they wish to
join "such-and-such" to the name man? (for from the things commonly confessed
the disputed receive their solution.) As to the essence? Then the things under
it will be distinguished from one another by essential difference? Which is
not so; for Paul differs in nothing according to essence from Peter, of whom
man is predicated. But as to the hypostasis? Then man is indicative of person
3,=1.32= and not of essence, which is absurd to say; for man signifies the
common of the essence and not an individual person, of Paul, for instance, or
of Barnabas. Therefore in no way is "such-and-such" joined to man, at least
according to the scientific account. But if common usage is at a loss for this
and makes catachrestic use of the names of the essence for indication of
person, this is nothing toward the accurate canon of logical science. But why
do I run down the customary catachresis, being at a loss in such things, not
noticing that even those scientific of accounts, often not having plenty of
fitting words to phrase what is thought, themselves also use other
catachrestic names toward presentation of what is said? Only let this be clear
to us, that if we said in the case of Peter and Paul that man differs in
nothing from man inasmuch as man, but inasmuch as such-and-such a man, and
essence differs in nothing from essence inasmuch as essence, but inasmuch as
such-and-such an essence, we would have been able to say this of them. But if
it is not possible to say this, since the essence of Peter and Paul is one and
the same, then neither that, since the name man is indicative of essence; and
if it is not consistent to join "such-and-such" and "such-and-such" to the
name man, then neither do we properly say two or three men. And if these
things have been shown in the case of man, how much more in the case of the
eternal and divine essence will it more properly fit that each of the
hypostases not be called such-and-such a God and such-and-such, nor that the
Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit be uttered as God and God and God, nor
that three gods be dogmatized, even in thought. 3,=1.33= It has therefore been
established by the account from us that is both just and consistent and most
scientific, that we say one God, the creator of all, even if he is observed in
three persons or hypostases of Father and Son and Holy Spirit.
