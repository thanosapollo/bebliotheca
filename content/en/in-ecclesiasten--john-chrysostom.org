OF THE SAME JOHN CHRYSOSTOM, INTERPRETATION ON ECCLESIASTES Many of the more
reasonable are at a loss about this book, while the more rash even mock it. So
that we may dissolve the passions of both, come, let us establish it from its
end. The end of the word, hear all: fear God and keep his commandments, for
this is every man; because God will bring every work into judgment in every
thing overlooked, whether good or whether evil; which is what Christ says: he
will render to each according to his works. And he names both work and God.
What need of many words? Knowing these things then, even if we do not find
some of the things set down, let us not condemn the words; for those perhaps
have a great and powerful meaning. For the book is lofty and most ethical and
most musical and full of much philosophy concerning the things according to
life. Therefore I exhort you to attend. 1 Words of Ecclesiastes, son of David.
He does well to call himself Ecclesiastes here; just as then assembling a
church, so he discourses to the inhabited world, having set himself to speak
about the common affairs, as being a slave of the common Master. For he does
not discourse toward Jews, as the rest do, but toward all <the> creation and
the inhabited world. Come then, let us compare with these whatever of the
philosophers you wish, and we shall find those things of the outsiders to be
slight. Words of Ecclesiastes, son of David. Reasonably he names both the
father and the place of the kingdom: Israel in Jerusalem; for after this man
the kings of Israel were not in Jerusalem, but in Samaria. Having called
himself thus, he does not discourse to us about small things, but about the
great affairs. Vanity of vanities, said the Ecclesiastes, <vanity of
vanities>, all things are vanity. He does well to set himself in the middle,
and he does not say: God said, but the Ecclesiastes said. For he did not need
faith here, so as to bring in the trustworthiness of God, but someone who is
clear from the thing itself and who teaches from reasonings and from the
experience of the affairs, as if he said: all ought indeed to learn all things
from God; but since you did not wish it, but were fluttered, hear at least
from me; therefore then let us come together, that we may learn that vanity of
vanities, all things are vanity. And very much so; for it is no small thing
toward the finding of the truth to know the falsehood and the deceit and the
phantasms. But what all things does he mean? and what ever is vanity of
vanities? For he has used much of the refrain; just as it is the custom of
Scripture to call vain that which is not according to some thing that is, if
it is thought to be, but is not. The idols of the nations are vain; not
because idols are not (for they [are not]), but because they are not [vain]
gods. When the name is present, but the underlying thing has sat down, it is
no longer a name, <but> falsely named; as if someone in contention should say
that the man in the shadow is a man; for he is not; for he does not admit the
account that belongs to a man; therefore that one is vain. For what reason
then vanity of vanities? He speaks an intensification of the thing; as when we
say glory of glories, we mean the surpassing glory, or king of kings the
surpassing king, so vanity of vanities. And from the contrary, if you wish,
let us try to move the argument; for you must learn this expression exactly.
Truth of truth we call great. Light or light of light; for the light in
darkness would not be great; but when it appears in light, then we also say a
great light, as the sensible sun, light of light. But the sun of righteousness
is not light of lights, but light



true. And do you want also concerning a body? I will say that too: the body is
corruptible, but the dust is corruption of corruption, or as we said kingdoms
of kingdoms. When you also have the comparison toward things of the same kind,
a beautiful of beautifuls would be very beautiful. So it is not simply vanity,
but such that after it there would not be another vanity. Think of a shadow
for me: this is vanity of vanities beside a painting. For just as there is
truth of truth, as that of God and ours, so vanity of vanities, and not simply
vanity, but vanity highest of all. All things vanity. What all things does he
mean? Heaven, earth, sea, this world? God forbid. For what was said would be
blasphemous; for the works of God would not be vain, but true; for all your
works, he says, are true and all his ways judgments. Concerning what things
then does he say: vanity of vanities? or is he not content with saying it
once, but always adds it thus? He speaks from disposition by doubling them.
Let us not fear, beloved, let us not be troubled, let us not be led astray,
let us not lead the argument around everywhere. The proof is near at hand.
What then does he say? What surplus is there for a man in all his toil in
which he toils under the sun? Did you see what all things he means? The works
of men that belong to life, tradings, retailings, businesses, glory, wealth,
luxury. And see how he threw the matter down, and see how first having called
it toil, which especially strikes the hearer, he then said the toil is also
unprofitable. And he does not declare here, but asks: what surplus for a man?
Which was both of one very confident in the proofs and showing much gravity
and condemning them as mad and doing all things in vain. So-and-so built a
dwelling, made it splendid. What profit for this man? None. He gathered
craftsmen, spent money, fulfilled the desire, took courtesans, endured
illnesses, cares, collisions, asked for favors, everything ran together. What
is the surplus? tell me. What small or great thing was he able to enjoy?
Another again, having received a paternal inheritance, fed prostitutes,
mounted office, did great things. What is the end, tell me, in all his toil? I
do not say that of the toil one part is good and one bad. Bring me all things
into the middle and you will find nothing. For the things of piety are not of
toil; for the momentary lightness of the affliction works for us an exceeding
weight of glory; and come to me all who labor; take my yoke, for it is kind,
and my burden is light. But this is toil, inasmuch as it neither has God
cooperating, nor is lightened by the hopes. And if you wish to call the man
himself, being convicted, a toil, you wish it and I do not decline. This toil
is under the sun, that toil is not under the sun, but above the sun. For our
citizenship exists in the heavens. Do you see how immediately in the beginning
and in the prefaces he defined the things about which he was speaking? About
the affairs under heaven, about these toilsome and laborious things concerning
which the men <toil> *** 2 *** to be disposed and to set them as nothing;
which Paul also says: not in any honor toward the filling of the flesh. But
the wicked † to whom a penalty he says, the gathering † Because to the good
man before his face he gave wisdom and knowledge and gladness; and to the
sinner he gave distraction to be distracted and to gather. The not breathing,
the toiling for others; do you see how he leads away from gathering money, how
he stops the desire, how he cuts out the fantasy, how he leads out toward
spending and consumption? For he who from himself hinders, how will he give to
another? But he who provides for himself, much more also for another; for he
is trained to despise money. Let us learn then that to despise is a work of
God, but to live in luxury is superfluous; for he will spend on necessary
needs. For perhaps someone would not have accepted hearing that to have mercy
is a work of God, as the one hearing that to live in luxury is a work of God.
Do not fear to spend; God gives to you; he rejoices in these especially more
than in those; since where



are the present monies useful? He compares this man toward the one who stores
up. And he will show good to his soul. It seems to me he means almsgiving; for
this is truly good, this is a soul of a man, when he has mercy on the body of
the neighbor. He begins from that and ends into this. He does not simply say
good, but toward the sparing and the stingy, the small-souled, then again he
says: and indeed this too is vanity. Why the gathering? For another will take
it. 3 To all things a time, and a season to every matter under heaven. Here we
are trained neither to be pained at the terrible things, nor to rejoice at the
good. For it is not, as we are troubled and say that "such a thing happened,
thus it happens from our sin", but life has such a nature; and one must not be
angry at nature. Just as we are not displeased at winter, nor at summer, so
neither at the present affairs; for each thing must come to be and each has a
season. Do not be pained as at paradoxes; just as the evils, so also the goods
must happen; or rather not evils, but things appearing so, as one must
rejoice, but also be pained. And see from where he confirms the argument; and
this from the things that happen among us, showing that they come to be of
necessity. Building is not always good, planting is not always, but there is a
place where even the contrary and not only the good. And he begins from the
things that hold together and which is especially a cause of pleasures and of
despondency. A time to give birth and a time to die. Why are you pained at
death? For did it happen contrary to expectation? Contrary to hopes? It came
in season, perhaps necessary for coming to be. Dying more quickly, how and in
what way perhaps well-timed? A time to plant and a time to pluck up what has
been planted. Then he confirms it from the plants; must one not always plant,
nor stand? For just as you do not allow the aged vines to remain, but you take
them away, so also God; it is a sequence of affairs, do not be pained. See how
useful the book is also toward good cheer. A time to kill and a time to heal.
And these are contrary. And what sort of time for killing? As the wicked. With
God there is a time of this and of this. A time to pull down and a time to
build, a time to weep and a time to laugh. And what sort of time is untimely?
When laughter is untimely and useless. For if there is a time of those generic
things, much more of these because of those; are these not simply accidents?
And God has defined these too; not always to laugh, lest we be dissolved, not
always to weep, lest we fall. A time to embrace; concerning marriage. A time
to seek and a time to lose. And yet it comes from our own slackness. Do not
think so, he says. There is a time when you must lose. Either he says this
that we may not mourn, or that we may also willingly let go; there is a place
where not seeking is well-timed, but it is better to lose. A time to love and
a time to hate; therefore also to be loved and to be hated. All things that he
made are beautiful in their time. Why then do you toil? All things are
wonderful, all things that have come to be in their time are beautiful. Do not
name grief simply, but show it to me with the time, and I show it to you as
better than pleasure and than laughter. Therefore he also says: better to go
into a house of mourning than into a house of laughter. None of these is in
itself beautiful, nor evil; dying is better than being born, as in old age.
For neither is anything else beautiful without the time, even if you name
food, even if drink. And indeed he has given the whole age in their heart, so
that man may not find the work that God made from beginning even to end. God
does not wish, he says, his works to be manifest to men, checking their
curiosity. Thus, he says, he constructed the times wisely, so that men are not
able to find the things that have come to be; for the multitude of the days
perhaps overshadows and produces forgetfulness. Or he says this, that,
although



knowing the time according to which the works come to be, it is not possible
to see them together. I knew that there is no good in them, except to be glad
and to do good in his life. This is a good work, to have mercy. For intending
to exhort to well-doing, first he legislates it, to fare well; for the one who
is kind to himself will also be so to another. This is a gift of God. Even if
he says a gift of God, God has defined this alone to be beautiful, wishing to
lead us away from the toil of the present life and from toiling in these
things. I knew <that> all things, as many as God made, they themselves will be
forever; upon them there is no adding and from them there is no taking away.
Here again he admires his wisdom, that his work is so sufficient and without
lack as to suffice everywhere. And God made, that they may fear from his face.
See these things too; not only that they may be shaken, but that they may also
admire him; it is likely then that the things that have come to be toward the
subject of knowledge of God are some great and wonderful things. What has come
to be already is, and as many things as belong to coming to be have already
come to be. But the things of men are not such, but always come to be. And God
will seek the one who is pursued. See how in the middle he inserts words of
philosophy and does not say here "vanity" concerning those who suffer badly;
for since he said that he made the works for us, so that he may be known, and
this is a cause of knowledge of God. What then? he says, how does he overlook
the injustices? He comes then also to the argument of providence, that this
too shows God to be. God will seek the one who is pursued; even if not now,
yet after these things; from experience I know these things, he says. And
still I saw under the sun the place of judgment, there the ungodly, and the
place of the just, there the pious. I saw also those being punished; which his
father also says. And I said in my heart: God will judge with the just and
with the ungodly. And that one will receive the prize of virtue and this one
of vice. I saw the place; he does not mean a place of punishment; for under
the sun, he says; I do not tell you the things to come, I saw these things
happening in many places. That there is a time for every matter and upon every
work. So as not to seek; God knows the times, when and when those must be
punished. There I said in my heart concerning the speech of sons of men, that
God will distinguish them. There I said; do not take these into doctrines,
which he said thus being at a loss, since David also introduces someone
saying: and I said; so then in vain I justified my heart and washed my hands
among the innocent, and: I said in my ecstasy; I have been cast away from the
face of your eyes, and: I said in my prosperity; I shall not be shaken
forever. Concerning the speech of a son of man that God will distinguish them;
since many accuse God and mock in vain, then he will train them, then he will
teach them. And to show that they themselves are cattle and indeed to them.
See then what also happens now. Many accuse God, many say he is unjust, many
that he does not provide; how do these differ from cattle? When then God
assigns to each according to desert, when the time calls, then he will show
their folly, then he will distinguish them. That the meeting of sons of man
and the meeting of the cattle; as the death of this one, so the death of this
one. He shows that death is one for both, body one, fashioning one; and if
they are ignorant of this they are reasonably cattle, that there is one spirit
for all. And who knows the spirit of sons of man [and the meeting of the
cattle; as the death of this one, so the death of this one. And who knows the
spirit of sons of man] whether it goes up above, and the spirit of the cattle
whether it goes down below into the earth? And he says this concerning those,
that they are cattle. For if they saw these things and philosophized about
resurrection, they would not have uttered these things, they would not have
said; since that he himself was not so disposed is clear from the things after
these: that God will bring them into judgment. That who will bring him to see
in what he may become after him? Since many wish to leave money, who



will be able to bring him, so as to see the things that have come to be? he
says. See how continually he still throws them into despising money and not
seeking after the departure from here. 4 And I turned and I saw all the
calumnies. I turned, what is it? I reckoned, he says, the good things of life
and I saw them vain; then I changed myself and I reckoned its painful things
and I saw them surpassing. This is "I turned". When then its good things are
vain, and its terrible things hard, what is the gain? tell me. I saw a tear of
those being calumniated and there is none who comforts them. They weep without
consolation; for calumny is terrible. Terrible; and yet this ought not to have
been terrible, but the contrary. For when a judge does not condemn, for what
reason do you lament? But nonetheless the race of men is so pained, as to
lament even at this. And I praised all the dead, those already dead, above the
living, as many as live until now; and better than these two is whoever did
not come to be. This is not his vote, but of the confused mind; for we utter
many things in despondency, which it is not necessary to bring into authority
of doctrines. For this reason he says: I said in my ecstasy; every man is a
liar; so that the declaration is not mine, but the vote of the despondent
soul. See this man hearing and seeing and looking, lamenting those; so the
passion troubled him. How is it likely they are disposed? Of how much
punishment would the calumniators be worthy? Reasonably also the laws of the
outsiders subject them to the same penalties. And I saw all the toil and all
the manliness of the work, that a man's jealousy is from his companion and
indeed this is vanity. That is, this youthful thing of theirs. When they fare
well, then again envy, and envy even from the own; this is the terrible thing,
so that, even if they were not vain *** But from another side again see two
contrary vices distinguished, and marvel how being nothing he envies as
remaining and always being. Why do you envy things that are not? he says. The
fool folded his hands and ate his flesh. That is, he spares nothing, but if it
should be needed even to eat himself, he is unsparing and savage; that is, he
bound himself and ate him up. A good fullness of a handful of rest above a
fullness of two handfuls of toil. The two are good above the one. So also
Christ: where two or three are gathered into my name, there I am in the midst
of them. He gathers us and teaches us to love one another, even if not kin,
because of the necessary need; for since he spoke about one enjoying wealth,
it is better to be two, and if also three much better. And he teaches from the
pitiable and living affairs; whether one must rest or suffer badly, the
sympathy is great. The two are good above the one, who have a wage in their
toil; because if they fall, the one raises his partner, and woe to the one,
when he falls <and there is not a second to raise him. And indeed if two
sleep, they also have warmth;> and the one, how shall he be warmed? And <if>
the one is overpowered, the two will stand against him, and the threefold cord
will not quickly be broken. The one, he says, will not even be able to stand
up, nor to rest; but the two even in calamities are great. But the threefold
cord will not quickly be broken. Upon a rope, he says, the joining is a great
safety; is not much more upon a man concord and love? A poor and wise child is
good above an old and foolish king; because from a house of bonds he will come
out to be king, because even in his kingdom he became poor. That one, he says,
even in a kingdom is poor, but this one from a bond will be wonderful. The
bonds hindered that one in nothing, the kingdom benefited this one in nothing.
There is no end to all the people, to all as many as came to be before him.
That is, the things already come to be were surpassing, to dissolve



terrible things; but this one is able also to give much license to those after
these; just as those are not pained because of this one, so these [do not]
rejoice because of this one. For the wicked even pains those after him, but
the good even gladdens those before him. And indeed the last will not rejoice
over him. Or again that one says that the wicked will pain both those before
him and those after him. Because indeed this too is vanity; as a man, if he
is. Guard your foot in whatever you go into a house of God. That is, you shall
be clean of robbery, of greed; their feet are swift to shed blood. Or he says
this: do not run with the same feet both into vice and into a house of God,
since even in that time also draw near to hear; that is, obey God; for this
would be above a sacrifice. Because they are not knowing how to do evils. Such
are those who hear, they will not even know vice. For how? Just as those who
feed on good things do not even know what illness ever is. 5 Do not be hasty
upon your mouth, and let your heart not be quick to bring out a word before
the face of God. Again here he commands not simply nor as it happens to
promise to God. Because God is in heaven and you upon the earth; therefore let
your words be few. That is, he will be able to punish you. For if often
conversing with a man one will sin, much more with God. Because a dream comes
in a multitude of trial and a voice of a fool in a multitude of words. Here he
means the fantasy; he calls false things dreams; for it happens that you also
lie. Do you see how many things he says about a promise? As you vow a vow to
God, do not delay to pay it; there is no will in fools. You then, as many
things as you vow, pay. It is good that you not vow rather than that you vow
and not pay. Do not give your mouth to make your flesh sin. Do not counsel all
counsels as coming to be to destroy the flesh, such as adulteries,
fornications. Do not say before the face of God that: it is ignorance. Do you
see how great an argument of providence for you, lest you also pay a penalty
for this and the works of your hands be destroyed, because in a multitude of
dreams and vanities and many words. Your works: the false and fantasized and
having nothing true. If you see calumny of a poor man and seizure of judgment
and of justice in a country, do not wonder at the matter. Do not wonder, he
says, at the injustices. High above high. For of the one doing injustice there
is another enemy. And high ones over them. [And surplus of earth.] Just as
this one is not alone high, but also another. And surplus of earth. Just as
this is much, so also the revenue is high. Or that he has to give to the one
wronged. For lest you say: and what is the profit? After being wronged you do
not see him being rich. Then also from another side: He who loves silver will
not be filled with silver; and who has loved produce in his multitude? That
is, to stop their longing is impossible. Even if he takes all, he desires
more, so that the one who is going to be filled with silver must not surround
himself with many things, but dissolve the longing about money. Whether one
loves money or produce, nothing is superfluous. This is superfluous, that many
do not want their produce. And indeed this is vanity. In a multitude of
goodness those eating it were filled; and <what> manliness to him from it?
Because a beginning of seeing with his eyes. What is the surplus? he says.
Manliness having been made, he said above; that is, what is the gain from the
fruitfulness? The continually looking in, but nothing more, and taking a
beginning of looking at them. Then from another side he strikes it again.
Sweet is the sleep of the slave, if he eats little, if much. Because there is
nothing surplus, pleasure comes together both in this and in that. Do we not
do all things for this, for pleasure? And for the one filled with becoming
rich there is none who lets him sleep.



But the one who enjoys more cannot sleep in pleasure. Then he says that the
wealth is not even sent on to them. There is a sickness which I saw under the
sun, wealth being kept by its owner for his vice, and that wealth will perish
in a wicked distraction, and he begot a son and there is nothing in his hand.
As he came out from his mother's belly naked, he will return to go as he came.
For when perhaps he arrives just as he came naked, what is the gain? As many
things as God legislated, he does not let him go away with them. Often even
while they are here he left them; often not being left, he threw the rest
around with more evils. 6 If a man begets a hundred and will live many years,
and his soul will <not> be filled, and indeed a burial did not happen for him,
I said: the miscarriage is good above him. Let us choose this one above this
one today. The miscarriage, what does he say? Rather let this one rest above
that one. All the toil of man is into his mouth. See how he secretly slanders
luxury. As many things as you toil, does it not end there? For what reason do
you surround yourself with many things? For what reason do you spare? For what
reason do you not spend? For what reason do you fear and desire more? Will not
one mouth receive the whole? What more do you acquire by stinginess? Will you
not go away into the same place as the one who lived in luxury? Because there
is a surplus for the wise above the fool? So then this is from understanding.
Because the poor man knows how to go opposite life. For when a poor man lives
in luxury, but a rich man no longer, is this not from wisdom and folly? A good
vision of eyes above surplus for a soul. It is better to be gladdened, he
says, with the eyes than to have those things stored which an eye does not
see. And indeed this is vanity and a choice of spirit. If anything has come to
be, already its name has been called. That is, they are known to men. And it
was known what a man is: nothing. He will not be able to be judged with the
strong above him. With God, he says; he will not be able to demand the account
itself. Because there are many words multiplying vanity. Because in many words
he is convicted, or that he himself also does many vain things. He cannot
accuse concerning nature, that it is corruptible; for his life too is much
worse. Because who knows what is good [in] man in his life? No one knows. Is
it not then an accusation? Very much; for this very reason that they do not
know the expedient, but as they dwell in darkness, so that they can neither be
judged toward God, nor know how to answer anything. He will say it quickly.
But those who are ignorant of their own expedients, how will they be able to
go to law with God? A number of days of his life of vanity; and he made them
in a shadow. He made them unseen; for this is as in a shadow. Because who will
announce to a man what will be behind him under the sun? For God judges the
affairs throughout the whole life; often he gave the end of the affairs in
another generation, so that the one who is going to learn his judgment would
live always a time. And what he says is of this sort: if nothing else, the
short-timed takes us away. For what? If you are going to accuse God that he
snatched you quickly from the world, then after these things terrible things
happened to be, not having to know the things after you, you have nothing to
say, but you are caught. 7 A good name above good mercy. That is, it is better
to enjoy a good report and to be saved, than from mercy; for that one is
liable. How then do you say: who knows the really good? We do not know. But
for now the things we know, these they are. And a day of death above a day of
his birth. For the one is rest, the other a preface of labors. It is good to
go into a house of mourning. That is, easy; for the good here he means this,
the easy. Or to go into a house of drinking, inasmuch as this is the end of
every man. † And he had to give back no one who is not able to pay this
return, but that one by grace entirely; † from there envy and jealousy. You
have a sufficient consolation that no one escapes; from there they will
receive gladness.



You see all sharing with you in the terrible things; *** from here grief often
and blasphemy; for especially the things that are not universal pain, being
good, the terrible things gladden. And the living will give good in his heart.
He will give toward his heart consolation, not to the soul, not *** the one
who has gone; but you have honored<s> the kin by comforting, but rather
procuring the consolation for yourself. The goods are double; when having gone
he rested, when still remaining he was not pained at the terrible things.
Because as a voice of thorns under the cauldron, so is the laughter of fools;
and indeed this is vanity. Unmarked, unpleasant, unprofitable, useless. Do not
say: what has happened that the former days were good above these? because you
did not ask about this in wisdom. For if you examine also the useful things of
these, you would have seen much the counterweight; or, if not this: it had to
happen thus. And if this is not in wisdom, much more the other things which
they examine. Wisdom is good with an inheritance. That is, in abundance.
Because in its shadow wisdom is as a shadow of silver. That is, you will not
rest from here only, but you will also be in much splendor. See the works of
God; because who will be able to order him whom God twists? He speaks
concerning punishment. Who will be able to save him whom God punishes? See
Sodom. His works: he does not mean the creation, but the economies. In a day
of goodness live in good, and see in a day of vice; see, and indeed together
with this in agreement God made this concerning speech. That is, understand
the things coming on, examine exactly that God saves the whole that has been
made; whether good or evil, it meets toward one end. Concerning speech, that
nothing may be found behind a man. Thus however in agreement, as not even to
know the concord and the end. There is a just man perishing in his just thing,
and there is an ungodly man remaining in his vice. Again another difficulty
and ignorance; righteousness did not benefit the one, vice did not destroy the
other. Do not become just much, nor be wise excessively, lest you ever be
