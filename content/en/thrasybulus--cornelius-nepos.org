* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Thrasybulus, son of Lycus, an Athenian. if virtue without fortune is to be
weighed by itself, I doubt whether I should put this man first of all. that
without doubt: I prefer no one to this man in faith, constancy, greatness of
spirit, in love toward the fatherland. for what many have wished and few have
been able, to free the fatherland from one tyrant, it has befallen this man that
he vindicated from servitude into liberty one oppressed by thirty tyrants. but I
know not how, although no one went before him in these virtues, many ran ahead
by nobility. first in the Peloponnesian war this man did many things without
Alcibiades, that man no thing without this: which that man made all together a
gain by a certain natural good. but yet all those things are common to
commanders with soldiers and fortune, because in the running-together of battle
the thing goes from counsel to the turns of things and the virtue of those
fighting. therefore by his own right the soldier claims some from the commander,
but fortune very many, and can truly proclaim that she has prevailed more by
these. but that most magnificent deed is proper to Thrasybulus. for when thirty
tyrants set over by the Lacedaemonians held Athens oppressed by servitude, they
had partly expelled from the fatherland partly killed very many citizens, whom
fortune had spared in war, they had divided among themselves the publicated
goods of very many, not only as a first man, but even alone at the beginning he
declared war on them.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
For this man, when he had fled to Phyle, which is a most fortified castle in
Attica, had with him not more than thirty of his own. this was a beginning of
the safety of the Attics, this a strength of the liberty of the most famous
city. nor indeed was this man not despised at first by the tyrants and his
solitude. which thing indeed was both a destruction to those despising and a
safety to this despised one: for it made those sluggish toward pursuing, and
these more robust, time having been given for preparing. wherefore more that
precept of all ought to be in minds, that nothing in war ought to be despised,
nor without cause is it said that the mother of a timid man is not accustomed to
weep. nor yet according to the opinion of Thrasybulus were the resources
increased: for already then in those times the good spoke more strongly for
liberty than they fought. from here he crossed into the Piraeus and fortified
Munichia. this the tyrants twice set upon to attack, and from it having been
shamefully driven back they immediately fled back into the city, arms and
impediments having been lost. Thrasybulus used not less prudence than courage.
for he forbade those yielding to be violated (for he thought it equal that
citizens spare citizens), nor was anyone wounded except who first wished to
attack. he spoiled no one lying of a garment, he touched nothing except arms, of
which he needed, and which pertained to living. in the second battle Critias
fell, leader of the tyrants, when indeed he was fighting most bravely against
Thrasybulus.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
This man having been thrown down Pausanias came as aid to the Attics, king of
the Lacedaemonians. he made peace between Thrasybulus and those who held the
city on these conditions: that none except the thirty tyrants and ten, who
afterward having been created praetors had used a custom of the former cruelty,
be afflicted with exile nor their goods publicated: the procuration of the
republic be given back to the people. this also of Thrasybulus is famous, that
peace having been reconciled, when he could most in the state, he carried a
law, that no one be accused of things done before nor punished, and they called
that of oblivion. nor indeed did he only care that this be carried, but he also
effected that it have force. for when certain of those who had been together
with him in exile wished to make a slaughter of those with whom a return into
grace had been made publicly, he prohibited it and furnished what he had
promised.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
To this man for such merits a crown was given by the people for the sake of
honor, made of two olive twigs. which because love of the citizens and not force
had expressed, it had no envy and was of great glory. well then that Pittacus,
who is held in the number of the seven wise, when the Mytilenaeans gave him many
thousands of iugera of field as a gift, said 'do not, I pray you, give me that
which many envy, even more also desire. wherefore from those I do not wish more
than a hundred iugera, which may indicate both my evenness of spirit and your
will.' for small gifts are accustomed to be lasting, rich ones not proper.
content then with that crown Thrasybulus neither required more nor thought
anyone had gone before him in honor. this man in the following time, when as
praetor he had put a fleet to Cilicia and the watches were not done diligently
enough in his camp, an eruption having been made by barbarians from a town by
night he was killed in a tent.
