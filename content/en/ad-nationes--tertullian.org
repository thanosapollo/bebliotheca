** Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

The proof of your ignorance, which while it defends injustice convicts it, is
ready at hand: that all who formerly were ignorant with you and hated with
you, as soon as it has befallen them to know, cease to hate when they cease to
be ignorant; nay, they themselves become what they hated and begin to hate
what they had been. So every day you groan over the growing number of
Christians, you cry that the city is besieged; Christians in the fields, in
the forts, on the islands; you grieve as at a loss that every sex, every age,
every rank in short is crossing over from you. And yet by this very thing you
do not move your minds to the estimate of some hidden good. You may not
suspect more rightly, you do not care to try more closely; here alone human
curiosity grows numb. You love to be ignorant of what others rejoice to have
found; you prefer not to know, because you already hate, as if you were sure
you would not hate once you knew. Yet if there will be no debt of hatred, it
will surely be found best to depart from the former injustice; but if a cause
is established, nothing will be taken from the hatred, which will even be
heaped higher by the conscience of justice. Unless you are ashamed to be
corrected, or sluggish to be excused. I know clearly with what answer you are
accustomed to meet the proof of our overflowing: a thing is not at once
prejudged good, you say, because it converts a great many and snatches them to
itself. I know the change of mind toward evil parts. How many deserters of a
good life? How many runaways into the perverse? Many in good faith, nay
already more because of the extremities of the times. But the matching of that
comparison fails. For of evil it is so agreed among all, that not even the
guilty themselves, who pass over to evil and turn from goods to perverse
things, dare to defend evil as good. They hold base things as matter for fear,
impious things for shame. In short they are eager to hide, they avoid
appearing, they tremble when caught, they deny when accused; not even when
tortured do they confess easily or always; at any rate when condemned they
mourn. For they reproach themselves for what they were; they even impute the
passage of an evil mind from innocence to fate. So they refuse to have it as
their own, because they cannot deny that it is evil. But what of this sort
happens to the Christian? No one is ashamed, no one repents, except only of
former things. If he is pointed out, he glories; if he is dragged, he does not
hang back; if he is accused, he does not defend himself; when questioned he
confesses; when condemned he glories. What evil is this in which the nature of
evil comes to a halt?

** Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2
:END:

In which you yourselves also judge contrary to the form of judging evildoers.
For when the guilty are brought in, if they deny what was done, you press them
with tortures toward confession; but Christians who have confessed of their
own accord you squeeze with tortures toward denial. What so great perversity,
that you fight against confession, change the offices of the tortures,
compelling the accused to get off for nothing, forcing him against his will to
deny? You presidents for the extorting of truth seek a lie from us alone, that
we may say we are not what we are. I suppose you do not want us to be evil,
and therefore you are eager to shut us out from this name. To be sure, you
stretch and butcher the rest for this, that they may deny what they are said
to be. Yet when they deny you do not believe them; us, if we have denied, you
believe at once. If you are sure that we are the most guilty, why even in this
are we handled by you otherwise than the guilty? I do not say that you grant
no space either to accusation or to refusal (you are accustomed not rashly to
condemn the unaccused and undefended); but, for example, if a murderer is
under inquiry, the cause is not at once struck off, nor is the investigation
satisfied, by his having confessed the name of murderer. Although you believe
the confessed with difficulty, you even demand the consequences besides: how
often he has done the slaughter, with what weapons, in what places, with what
spoils, partners, receivers, lest anything at all of the evil man should lie
hidden, or anything be wanting to the truth that is to instruct the sentence.

Further, concerning us, whom you assign to more atrocious and more numerous
crimes, you compose shorter and lighter indictments. I believe you do not want
those loaded whom you want destroyed by every work, or you do not think things
must be inquired into which you know. This then is the more perverse, if you
compel denial of things of which you most certainly know. Nay, how much more
it suited your hatred, setting aside the form of judging, to strive by your
own zeal not toward denial, lest you should set free those you hate, but
toward confession of the several crimes, so that enmity might be the more
sated by the heaping up of punishments, while it is recognized how many times
each had already celebrated those banquets, how often he had rushed upon
incest in the dark. What? The inquiry for the rooting out of the race ought to
have been spread, the question stretched to partners and accomplices. Let the
baby-nurses and the cooks be brought in, the very dogs that were groomsmen;
the matter would have been corrected. Even the spectacles would have had a
favor added. For with how much zeal would men gather into the arena when some
one was about to fight who had swallowed a hundred infants? For if things so
horrifying and so monstrous are reported of us, they surely ought to have been
dug out, lest they should seem incredible and the public hatred against us
grow cold. For most men even temper their belief of such things, shuddering
that nature should seek a wild-beast food which it has shut off from the human
race by the very act of lying together.

** Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3
:END:

You therefore, otherwise most diligent and most persistent examiners of far
smaller crimes, when you desert that diligence in the face of things so
horrifying and gone beyond all impiety, neither by receiving confession, which
must always be worked at by judges, nor by laying out an inquiry, which must
always be consulted by those who condemn, it is already clear that every
charge against us is directed not at some crime but at a name. So, if the
truth of the crimes were established, the very names of the crimes would
condemn, if they fitted, so that it would be pronounced against us: It is our
pleasure that that murderer, or incestuous man, or whatever we are tossed as,
be led, be fixed up, be given to the beasts. Further, your sentences mark
nothing except a confessed Christian. No name of a crime stands, unless it is
the crime of a name. For this is in truth the account of the whole hatred
against us. The name is in the cause, which a certain hidden force attacks
through your ignorance, so that you refuse to know for certain what you are
certain you do not know for certain, and therefore you neither believe things
that are not proved, and, lest they be easily disproved, you refuse to
inquire, so that a hostile name may be punished under the presumption of
crimes. So, that we may recede from the hostile name, we are compelled to
deny; then, denying, we are set free, with total impunity of past things, no
longer bloodstained nor incestuous, because we have lost that name. But while
this account is shown in its own place, do you set forth what you pursue for
the overthrow of the name: what crime of the name, what offense, what fault?
For it is prescribed to you that you cannot object crimes which neither the
indictment directs nor the publication assigns nor the sentence enumerates.
What is offered to the president, what is inquired of the accused, what is
answered or denied, what is read out from the council, that I recognize as
making a man accused. Therefore concerning the desert of the name, if there is
any guilt of names, if any accusation of words, I think there is no complaint
against a word or a name, except when something either sounds barbarous or
tastes unlucky or unchaste, or otherwise than becomes the speaker or delights
the hearer. These are the crimes of words or names, just as of words and of
speech barbarism is a fault, and solecism, and a more tasteless figure. But
the Christian name, as far as the meaning is, is interpreted from anointing.
Even when we are corruptly pronounced by you Chrestians (for you are not
clearly sure even of the name itself), even so it is modulated from sweetness
or goodness. You therefore detain in harmless men even our harmless name, not
inconvenient to the tongue, not harsh to the ears, not evil to a man, not
hostile to the country, but Greek with others, both sonorous and pleasant in
interpretation. And surely names are not to be punished with the sword or the
cross or the beasts.

** Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4
:END:

But you say a sect is punished by the name of its author. First indeed it is
surely an honest and usual right that a sect be marked from the appellation of
the author, while philosophers also are named from their authors Pythagoreans
and Platonists, as physicians Erasistrateans and grammarians Aristarchians.

Therefore if because of an evil author an evil sect, the offshoot of an evil
name is beaten. Yet it would be presumed by rashness. It was prior to know the
author, so that the sect might be known, than to keep from the sect an
inspection of the author. But now, necessarily by being ignorant of the sect,
because you are ignorant of the author, or by not reviewing the author,
because you do not review the sect either, you dash against the name alone, as
if in it detaining the sect and the author, whom you do not know at all. And
yet for philosophers there lies open the liberty of crossing over from you
into a sect both of authors and of their own name, and no one stirs hatred
against them, when they bark out openly and publicly all the bitterness of
speech against your morals, rites, worship, and manner of life, with contempt
of the laws, without respect of persons, so that some even hurl their liberty
against the princes themselves with impunity. But the truth, most laborious to
the age, philosophers indeed affect, yet Christians possess, and therefore
those who possess displease more, because he who affects mocks, he who
possesses defends. In short Socrates was condemned from that part in which he
had more nearly attempted the truth, by destroying your gods. Although the
Christian name was not yet then on the earth, yet truth was always being
condemned. Therefore you will not deny him a wise man, to whom even your
Pythian had given testimony. Of men, he said, Socrates is the wisest of all.
Truth conquered Apollo, so that he himself pronounced against himself; for he
confessed that he was not a god, but also affirming as wisest him who refused
the gods. Further among you he is so much the less wise, because refusing the
gods, though he is wise for this reason, because ab(??)ing the gods. In which
manner you are also accustomed toward us:

A good man, Lucius Titius, only that he is a Christian. Likewise another: I
wonder that Gaius Seius, a grave man, has become a Christian. For the
blindness of folly they praise what they know, they blame what they do not
know, and they spoil that which they know by that which they do not know. It
occurs to no one that a man is perhaps good and prudent because he is a
Christian, or a Christian because he is prudent and good, since it is more
human to judge hidden things from manifest than to prejudge manifest things
from the hidden. Others whom they had known before this name as wandering,
cheap, dishonest they suddenly wonder at as corrected, and yet they know how
to wonder rather than to follow; others strive with such obstinacy that they
fight against their own advantages, which they can take from commerce with
this name. I know one husband and another, anxious before about his wife's
morals, who could not bear even mice creeping into the bedroom without a groan
of suspicion, when he had learned the cause of the new diligence and the
unwonted captivity, offered all patience to his wife, denied that he was
jealous, preferred to be the husband of a she-wolf rather than of a Christian;
it was allowed him to change his own nature into the perverse, he did not
permit the woman to be reformed into the better. A father disinherited a son
of whom he had ceased to complain. A master put into the workhouse a slave
whom he had besides felt to be necessary. As soon as anyone has understood a
Christian, he prefers him guilty. For the discipline itself is traduced by
itself, and we are betrayed from nowhere else than from our good, if the evil
also shine from their own evil. Or are we alone the worst against the
institutions of nature, marked from the good? For what badge do we carry
forward, except the first wisdom, by which we do not adore the frivolous works
of the human hand, abstinence, by which we keep from what is another's,
chastity, which we do not contaminate even with the eyes, mercy, by which we
bend over the needy, truth itself, by which we offend, liberty itself, for
which we know how to die? He who wants to understand who Christians are must
use these indicators.

** Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5
:END:

What therefore you say: The worst and most disgraceful in avarice, luxury,
dishonesty, we will not deny that there are some. Even this suffices for a
testimony of our name, if not all, if not the more. It is necessary that in a
body, even as whole or pure as you please, some mole should sprout or a little
wart rise or a freckle grow dirty. The sky itself no serenity so strainedly
purges that it is not signed by the flake of some little cloud. A moderate
stain on the brow, seen in some exemplary part, from which the whole is clean.
The greater portion of good uses a moderate evil for a testimony of itself.
When however you prove some of ours evil, by this very thing you do not prove
them Christians. Seek the sect to which malice is assigned. You yourselves in
conversation, if ever against us, Why is that man a cheat, you say, if
Christians are abstinent? why savage, if merciful? So you render testimony
that Christians are not such, while you twist back why those who are called
Christians are such. There is much distance between crime and name, between
opinion and truth. For names too are so instituted that they have their bounds
between being said and being. How many finally are called philosophers and yet
do not fulfill the law of philosophy? All bear a name from professions, but
they lead the name without the excellence of the profession who by the surface
of a word defame the truth. They are not at once because they are said, but
because they are not, they are said in vain, and they deceive those who bind
the thing to the name, though the status of the name should compete from the
thing. And yet of this sort they neither gather nor share with us, made yours
again through offenses, since we do not mix even with those whom your force
and savagery have driven to denial. For surely unwilling deserters of the
discipline would more easily be contained among us than willing ones. For the
rest, without (??)ause you call Christians those whom the Christians
themselves deny, who do not know how to deny themselves.

** Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6
:END:

By these our propositions and answers, which truth supplies from its own, as
often as your conscience is pressed and cramped, a silent witness of its own
ignorance, you flee heated to a certain little altar, that is the authority of
the laws, that they would surely not beat this sect unless it had been
established concerning merits among the founders of the laws. What then
forbade it to be likewise established among the executors of the laws also,
when concerning the other crimes which are similarly fenced off and punished
by laws, unless there is first an inquiry, the penalty ceases? For example a
murderer, an adulterer by law: yet it is discussed concerning the order of
what was done, and yet the kind of the deed is known to all. The laws punish a
Christian. If there is any deed of a Christian, it ought to be dug out. No law
forbids inquiry; nay, inquiry works for the laws. For how will you observe a
law by taking care against what is forbidden by law, when the diligence of
taking care has been taken away through the failure of recognizing what you
observe? No law owes to itself the conscience of its own justice, but to those
from whom it courts obedience. For the rest a law is suspect if it refuses to
prove itself. Rightly therefore they are judged just against Christians and
reverend and to be observed so long as it is unknown what they persecute,
rightly after recognition they are found most unjust and are spat upon with
their swords and gallows and lions. There is no honor of an unjust law. But as
I suppose, there is doubt concerning the injustice of certain laws, since
every day by new decrees and constitutions you temper their hardnesses and
wickednesses.

** Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7
:END:

Whence then, you say, has so much of rumor been allowed concerning you, whose
testimony may perhaps have sufficed the founders of the laws? Who, I ask, is
the sponsor either to them then or from then on to you concerning the faith?
It is rumor. Is this not, Rumor an evil, than which no other is swifter? Why
an evil, if it were always true? Not by a lie, and most of all not even then,
when it reports true things, does it cease from the lust of lying, so as not
to weave false things into true, adding, taking away, confusing by variety.
What? that this is its condition, that it perseveres only in what it lies. For
it lives only so long as it proves nothing; since once approved it falls and,
as if having performed the office of announcing, it departs; from then on the
thing is held, the thing is named, (??)or does anyone say for example: They
say this was done at Rome, or: The rumor is that that man has obtained a
province, but: That man has obtained a province, and: This was done at Rome.
No one names rumor unless he is uncertain, because no one is sure by rumor but
by conscience; no one believes rumor unless a fool, because a wise man does
not believe the uncertain. However great the ambition with which rumor has
been spread, it must at some time have sprung from one mouth, from then it
creeps into the offshoots, as it were, of tongues and ears and obscures the
moderate vice of the origins of the report, so that no one thinks back whether
that first mouth sowed lies, which often happens either by the talent of
rivalry, or by the judgment of suspicion, or even by a new pleasure of lying.
But it is well that time reveals all things, by the witness of your sentences
and proverbs and of nature itself, which has been so ordered that nothing lies
hidden, even what rumor has not betrayed. See what sort of prodigy you have
suborned against us, because what it once reported and for so much time has
corroborated toward belief it has not been able to prove even until now. Under
prince Augustus this name arose, under Tiberius its discipline shone, under
Nero condemnation grew strong, so that from here you may weigh from the person
of the persecutor: if that prince was pious, the Christians are impious; if
just, if chaste, the Christians are unjust and incestuous; if he was not a
public enemy, we are public enemies; what we are the condemner himself has
shown, surely punishing things rival to himself. And yet when all things were
erased this Neronian institution alone remained, just finally as unlike its
author. Therefore our age has not yet two hundred and fifty years. So many
wicked men meanwhile, so many crosses that have attained divinity, so many
infancies slaughtered, so many breads bloodied, so many overthrows of lamps,
so many wanderings of marriages, and still rumor alone decides against
Christians. It has indeed a great foundation from the vice of human talent: it
lies more happily in bitter and atrocious things. For the more you are prone
to malice, the more you are ready for belief of evil; in short false evil is
more easily believed than true good. If however iniquity had left any place of
prudence among you, justice would surely demand, for exploring the faith of
rumor, that you look from whom rumor could have been given to the crowd, and
so to the whole world. For not from the Christians themselves, I suppose,
since even from the form and law of all mysteries the faith of silence is
owed. How much more of such things which, if betrayed, would not meanwhile
avoid punishment by present punishment? If therefore they themselves are not
their own betrayers, it follows that outsiders are. I ask you, from where is
knowledge for outsiders, when even just and lawful mysteries beware every
outside witness, unless unlawful ones scorn it less? Yet it suits outsiders
more both to be ignorant and to invent. The curiosity of household people has
stolen through chinks and caverns. What? when household people will betray
those things to you? We are all betrayed by none more, how much more if the
atrocity is so great that the justice of indignation breaks every faith of
familiarity? The mind could not contain what it shuddered at, the eye what it
feared. This too is a wonder, if even he who burst forth to report with so
great a right of impatience did not also desire to prove, and he who heard did
not also take care to see; since there is equal fruit both of the informer, if
he proves what he reported, and of the hearer, if he believes for himself also
what he hears. Then, you say, it was first reported and exhibited, heard and
inspected, and from then committed to rumor. This indeed oversteps all wonder,
if what is always committed was once caught; unless we have already ceased to
practice such things. Yet we are still called the same, and we are assigned to
the same, and from day to day we overflow; the more we are, the more hateful
to more. Hatred grows more as the matter grows: as the multitude of the
accused advances, why then does the multitude of reporters not advance? As far
as I know, even our manner of life has become better known, you know even the
days of our meetings, and so we are besieged and overwhelmed and detained in
the very secret congregations. Yet who ever came upon a half-eaten corpse? who
caught the traces of teeth in bloodied bread? who, when darkness was broken in
upon by a sudden light, recognized any unclean, not to say incestuous, signs?
If we obtain by a reward that we not be dragged out such into public, why are
we also overwhelmed? We can even not be dragged out at all. For who either
sells or buys the betrayal of some crime without the crime itself? But why
should I detract from outside spies and informers, you who object such things
as are not published by ourselves with the greatest outcry, either heard at
once, if they are first shown, or found afterward, if they are meanwhile
hidden? For without doubt it is the custom for those who wish to be initiated
first to go to the master of the rites or the father. Then he will say: An
infant is necessary for you, who is pledged for this, to be sacrificed, and
some bread, to be broken in blood; besides, candelabra, which dogs tied to
them may overturn, and morsels, which may overturn the same dogs: but a mother
or a sister is also necessary for you. What if there are none? I suppose you
will not be able to be a lawful Christian. These things, I ask you, do they
endure to be betrayed when announced by others? The diverse work is not for
them to know. First the business of deceit is perpetrated: to the ignorant
both feasts and nuptials are subjected; for they had never before heard
anything of Christian mysteries.

Yet afterward they must know, even to be served beforehand to others whom they
bring in. For the rest how vain it is that the profane know what the priest
does not know? They are silent therefore and take it as received, and they
burst out with none of the tragedy of Thyestes or Oedipus, and they do not
drag it forth to the people. They snatch nothing of faith from ministers and
masters and from those already taught who have the stronger bites. If nothing
of the sort is proved, I know not what great thing ought to be estimated,
whose compensation consists in the endurance of such atrocities. Miserable and
pitiable nations, behold we set before you the pledge of our discipline. It
pledges eternal life to its followers and keepers, on the contrary it
threatens the profane and rivals with eternal punishment by eternal fire; for
either cause the resurrection of the dead is preached. We shall see concerning
the faith of these things, while they are laid out in their own place;
meanwhile believe, just as we do. For I want to know, if you are prepared to
approach through such crimes, just as we are. Come, whoever you are, plunge
the iron into an infant; or if it is another's office, then only watch the
soul dying before it has lived, at any rate catch the raw blood in which you
may sate your bread, feed gladly; meanwhile recline, number the places where a
mother or a sister has pressed the couch; note carefully, so that, when the
darkness has rushed in, testing of course the diligence of each, you may not
err by rushing upon a stranger: you will have made a piacular crime unless it
is incest. When you have finished these things, you will live into the age. I
desire you to answer, if you make eternity of so much worth? Nay, for this
reason you do not even believe; even if you have believed, I deny that you are
willing; even if you were willing, I deny that you are able. But why should
others be able, if you are not able? why should you not be able, if others are
able? Impunity, eternity, at how much do you want them to stand? Or do these
seem to us to be redeemed in every way? or are there other orders of teeth of
Christians, and other caves of throats, and other sinews for the lust of
incest? I do not suppose. For it is enough for us to be discerned from your
position by truth alone.

** Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8
:END:

Plainly, we are called a third race. Some Cynopaenes or Sciapodes? or some
Antipodes from underground? If there is at least some account of this among
you, I would have you set forth the first and second race, so that thus it may
stand concerning the third. Psammetichus indeed thought he had explored for
himself by talent concerning the first-born. For he is said to have handed
over infants fresh from birth to be reared apart from the commerce of men to a
nurse whom he had himself for that reason deprived of a tongue, so that,
complete exiles of the human voice, they might not form speech by hearing, but
by putting forth their own they might designate that first nation whose sound
nature had dictated. The first voice was reported BEKKOS; its interpretation
is the name of bread among the Phrygians; the Phrygians are held from then as
the first race. But this will be one of the vanities of your fables not idly
to be retraced by us, by which we desire to show your faith given to vanities
rather than to truths. Or is it at all credible that, with such a member cut
away, the organ of the soul itself laid waste and surely cut to the root, the
throat castrated which even from outside is dangerously wounded, from then
corruption flowing back into the vitals, finally with nourishments ceasing for
some time, life lasted for that nurse? Come now, let her have persevered her
life by the medicines of Philomela, whom the more prudent also interpret as
mute not by the cutting of the tongue but from the blush of shame. If
therefore she lived, she could babble something blunt. And an inarticulate
sound and a ringing without the modulation of the little lips can be forced
from an opened mouth, the tongue stunned, from the throat alone. That perhaps
the infants then, because it was unique, more easily invented a little more
modulated, as being somewhat tongued, and struck upon a find of some
interpretation. Let the Phrygians now be first, yet Christians are not third.

For how many other series of nations after the Phrygians? But think back, lest
those whom you call a third race obtain the first place, since there is no
nation that is not Christian. Therefore whichever nation is first, it is
nonetheless Christian. Ridiculous madness, you call us newest and you name us
third. But we are assigned a third race from superstition, not from nation, so
that there are Romans, Jews, then Christians. But where are the Greeks? or if
they are counted in the superstitions of the Romans, since indeed Rome even
solicited the gods of Greece, where at least are the Egyptians, themselves
also, as I know, of a private and curious religion? Further if those of the
third place are so monstrous, of what sort are those to be held who precede in
the first and second?

** Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9
:END:

But why should I wonder at your vain things, when from a natural form malice
and folly have intervened, embodied and compacted under the same owner of
error? Surely, because I do not wonder, I must enumerate, so that you by
recognizing may wonder into how great a folly you fall, who want us to be the
causes of every public disaster or injury. If the Tiber has overflowed, if the
Nile has not overflowed, if the sky has stood, if the earth has moved, if
Libitina has laid waste, if famine has struck, at once the voice of all: The
desert of the Christians! As if those who have the true God have a moderate
thing or anything else to fear. I suppose, as despisers of your gods we
provoke these darts of theirs. As we set forth above, our age has not yet
three hundred years: how many disasters before that space fell upon the whole
world at single cities and provinces? how many wars foreign and intestine? how
many plagues, famines, fires, chasms and motions of the earth has the age
digested? Where then were the Christians, when the Roman state furnished so
many histories of its labors? Where then were the Christians, when Hiera,
Anaphe and Delos and Rhodes and Cea, islands, went to the bottom with many
thousands of men, or that which Plato records greater than Asia or Africa sunk
in the Atlantic sea? when fire poured over Volsinii from the sky, Pompeii from
its own mountain? when by an earthquake the Corinthian sea was snatched away?
when a cataclysm abolished the whole world? Where then, I do not say the
Christians despisers of the gods, but your gods themselves, whom places, towns
in which they were born, stayed, buried, even those which they founded, prove
later than that disaster? For they would not otherwise have survived to this
day, unless after the offspring of that disaster. If you do not care to reread
and roll back the testimonies of the times, otherwise reported to you, first
lest you pronounce your gods most unjust, who because of despisers also hurt

mark for us. If I ask this man who were the insinuators of the gods, he
designates either philosophers or peoples or poets. For he distinguished the
census of the gods by a threefold kind: one to be physical, which the
philosophers retrace, another mythical, which is rolled among the poets, a
third gentile, which the peoples have each adopted for themselves. Therefore
since the philosophers have fitted the physical by conjectures, the poets have
drawn the mythical from fables, the peoples have presumed the gentile of their
own accord, where is truth to be placed? In conjectures? But the conception is
uncertain. In fables? But the relation is foul. In adoptions? But the adoption
is promiscuous and municipal. In short among the philosophers uncertain
things, because various, among the poets all things unworthy, because base,
among the peoples all things promiscuous, because voluntary. Further divinity,
if you retrace the true, is of that definition, that it is neither gathered by
uncertain argumentations, nor contaminated by unworthy fables, nor judged by
promiscuous adoptions; for it ought to be held, as it is, certain, entire,
common, because of course of all. For the rest whom am I to believe a god?
Whom suspicion has estimated? Whom history has tossed? Whom a city has willed?

Why then do we not turn to an opinion somewhat more human, which may seem

things, the earth, upon which all things, and whatever of those conspires
among themselves toward human conveniences. Nor only by benefits does the
faith of divinity convene to the elements, but also from the diverse things
which are accustomed to fall as from their anger and offense, as thunderbolts,
as hails, as burnings, as pestilent breezes, likewise floods, likewise chasms
and motions of the earth, and the gods are rightly believed whose nature is to
be honored in prosperous things, to be feared in adverse, mistress of course
of helping and hurting. Further if they are so felt in the converse of
citizens, they do not likewise either return thanks or intend complaints to
the things themselves by which they are helped or hurt, but to those under
whose force and power the operation of things runs down. For in pleasures also
you do not assign a crown to the pipe or the cithara for a reward, but to the
artist who tempers the pipe and the cithara by the force of sweetness. Equally
when someone is badly in health, you do not remember thanks to the wools nor
to the antidotes or the plasters themselves, but to the physicians, by whose
work and prudence the remedies come forth. Likewise in adverse things those
who are wounded by iron do not accuse the sword itself or the lance, but the
enemy or the robber, and those whom roofs crush do not charge the tiles or the
rain-tiles, but old age, as also the shipwrecked do not impute to rocks and
waves, but to the storm. Rightly. For it is certain that whatever is done must
be ascribed not to that through which it is done, but to that by which it is
done, because he is the head of the deed who has also instituted both that it
be done and through what it be done (and there are in all things those three
titles: what is, through what it is, and from whom it is), because he is prior
who wills something to be done and by what it can be found. And so you rightly
act in the rest, considering the author, but in physical things against nature
your rule, by which in the rest you judge wisdom, by taking away the highest
degree of the author, and considering not from whom the things that are done
are done. So it happens to believe that the powers and judgments of the
elements are what are servitudes and offices. Do we not in that investigation

not decree so much honor to the fates, as to Larentina, as to those
surrendering Carthage to themselves. But those gods are not in the power of
conferring a kingdom. If Jupiter reigned in Crete and Saturn in Italy and Isis
in Egypt, men also reigned, for whom even many are handed down as having
worked. So he who serves both makes lords, and the surrendered of Admetus
increases Roman citizens by empire, while by deceiving with ambiguous lots he
destroyed Croesus his liberal worshipper. What was the true god fearing to
announce constantly that he must fall from the kingdom? Increased by the power
of reigning they could ever have guarded their own cities also! If they
availed to have furnished to the Romans, why did Minerva not defend Athens
from Xerxes? or why did Apollo not snatch Delphi from the hand of Pyrrhus?
They keep the city Rome who lost their own, if this Roman religiosity earned.
And yet was not superstition sought after the highest empire, things already
increased? Even if sacred things were introduced by Numa, yet not yet either
by images or by temples were your things frustrating the divine. A frugal
religion and a poor superstition; rash altars and sordid vessels, and a small
reek from them, and the god himself nowhere. Therefore not religious before
they were greater, because religious. Yet how can the highest empire seem to
have been sought for the Romans because of religion and the deepest care of
the gods, which was increased with the gods rather injured? For unless I am
deceived every kingdom or empire is sought by wars and is amplified by wars.
Further the gods of a city also are injured by the victors. For the same
overthrow both of walls and of temples, equal slaughters both of citizens and
of priests, the same plunderings of profane and of sacred. As many sacrileges
of the Romans as trophies, then as many triumphs over gods as over nations.
Captive images also remain, and surely if they feel their victors, they do not
love. But because they feel nothing, they are injured with impunity, and
because they are injured with impunity, they are adored in vain. Therefore
those whose height has grown adult through victories cannot seem to have grown
from the merits of religion, whether by growing they were injurers of
religion, or by injuring they grew. All nations have had a kingdom each in
their own times, as the Assyrians, as the Medes, as the Persians, as the
Egyptians; it is even still with certain, and yet those who lost did not
remain without religions and worship and gods to be propitiated, until at last
almost the whole domination yielded to the Romans. The lot of times has so
rolled kingdoms. Seek who ordered the turns of times. The same dispenses
kingdoms, and now with the Romans he has gathered that sum as money exacted
from many names into one chest. What he has established concerning it those
nearest him know.
