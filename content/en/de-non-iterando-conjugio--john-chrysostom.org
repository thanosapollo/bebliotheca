great disgust, again desire the same things, this is what most amazes me and
makes me at a loss, and to seek the cause through which the things which they
formerly thought to be things to be fled, when they were in them, these now,
when they have been delivered from them, they pursue again as desirable. And
turning a thousand reasonings up and down with myself I was scarcely at some
time, as I persuade myself, finding the cause of the matter; or rather it is
not some one, nor two only, but even more than these. For some, having by the
long time given the former things over to forgetfulness and remembering only
the things in hand, come to marriage as to a release from the evils of
widowhood, but they find other things far more grievous in it, so as again to
let out the same voices which they did also before. But others again, gaping
toward the affairs of the world, and hanging from the glory of the present
life, and thinking widowhood to be a thing of reproach, choose the miseries in
marriage for the sake of the empty glory and the superfluous vanity. And there
are some who, by none of these, but being mastered by incontinence alone,
return again to the former things and try to hide the true cause with the
excuses that have been said.

Now to accuse them and to condemn them for this marriage I myself neither
dare, nor do I advise another, since not even to the blessed Paul does this
seem good, or rather not to the Holy Spirit. For having said: "A wife is bound
by the law for as long a time as her husband lives; but if her husband has
fallen asleep, she is free to be married to whom she wishes, only in the
Lord," and having conceded to the widow, if she should wish, to be married
again, and having said: "But she is more blessed if she remains so," lest
anyone think the injunction to be human, he added: "And I think that I also
have the Spirit of God," making clear that he wrote these things by the
Spirit. Let no one then suppose that I say the things which I will now say as
accusing or blaming those who marry. For it would be of the last madness and
insanity for us, filled with a thousand evils, to condemn unsparingly, from
the opposite side, those whom that blessed man did not punish, but spared. For
if we are commanded not even to judge, lest we be judged with the same
measure, nor to become severe judges of the things others sin, but forbearing
and gentle, whenever you are at leisure with a matter and condemn others, you
deprive yourself of all pardon, preparing the Judge through the things toward
the neighbor to become more severe toward yourself. I have not then come
to this now as accusing or attacking; for that which is able to take place in
the Lord has been released from all accusation. "For only," he says, "in the
Lord." But just as when we speak concerning virginity, we do not raise that up
by dishonoring marriage; so when we discourse concerning widowhood, we do not,
setting second marriage among the things forbidden, exhort to be content with
the first, but we confess that this second also is according to law, yet that
the one is far better than the second.

Let no one then think that the superiority by comparison is a vice of the
thing that has been made less by it.

For we do not make this comparison as thrusting the matter into the rank of
the wicked; but leaving it to be among the things permitted and in one's
power, thus we prefer and admire what is far better than it. Why then? Because
it is not equal for the same woman to be wife of one husband and of two. For
she who was content with the first has shown that she would not even have
chosen this at the beginning, if she had known the experience of the matter
well; but she who brings in a second bridegroom to the bed of the former has
brought forth no small proof of much love of the world and of sympathy toward
earthly things. And that one, while her husband was living, was not fluttered
toward any other; but this one, even if she did not sin toward others while he
was living, yet rather admired many others than him. But that we may not be
guessing at the life that has passed, let us compare the matter itself. For
just as virginity is better than marriage, so this marriage is better than
that. For the widow, having been made less than virginity from the beginning
only, at the end is equaled to it again and joined with it; but this marriage
stands apart from it on both sides. And besides these things, she who bears
widowhood easily often practices continence even while her husband is living;
but she who has the matter as burdensome is prepared to keep company not with
two and three only, but with far more husbands, and scarcely stands off when
old age has come on. As therefore that marriage is a sign of much dignity and
chastity, so this I would not say of wantonness, God forbid, but of a weak
soul and most fleshly and bound to the earth, and able never to imagine
anything great and high. But if someone should say that the good is the same,
whether it happen once, or twice, or many times, for it will likewise be good,
and he who uses it often would more justly be praised; so that if marriage
also is a good, he who has used it continually is more admirable and more
acceptable than he who uses it seldom: we will say that this might be able to
trick the more simple, but for those who wish to attend, the sophism will be
easy to catch. For marriage is not called marriage because of the mixing;
since in that way even fornication would be marriage; but because the woman
who is married is content with one husband, and in this the free and chaste
woman differs from the harlot. For if she should be content with one husband
throughout, the matter might reasonably be called marriage; but if instead of
one she brings many bridegrooms into the house, I do not dare to call the
matter fornication, but I would say that she falls short by a great measure of
her who has not known another husband except one. For that one heard the Lord
saying: "For this cause a man shall leave his father and his mother, and shall
be joined to his wife, and the two shall be one flesh," and she clung as to
flesh truly her own and did not forget the head once given to her; but this
one has had neither the first nor the second in the rank of her own flesh; for
the first has been cast out by the second, and the second by that one; and she
could not remember the former well, attending to the other after him, nor will
she look upon this one with the fitting affection, her mind being split toward
the one who has gone. So that it results for each remaining, both this one and
that one, to have been cast out from the honor and the love belonging to a
husband from a wife.

And what soul at all does she think the second bridegroom has, being led into
the chamber of the former and going up onto his bed, and seeing that man's
wife laughing and being playful at these things? Very much so; for he will not
come to her with much love; for even if he is harsher than all men,


he will not be so savage as to suffer nothing human, even if she surround both
herself and the house with a thousand adornments. For the mourning that has
already overtaken the house does not allow the gladness to become pure; but
just as with walls, when some part happens to have been badly burned, then
being gently whitewashed, the intense and deep blackness spoils the whiteness
of the surface and is an unpleasant sight; so also here, even if she invent
many splendid things, in the midst of them the gloomy things show through, and
there is in them some joyless mixture. For servants and maids and farmers and
sojourners and neighbors and kinsfolk of the one who went before are downcast
at what has happened and groan. And if orphans also happen to have been left,
being quite young, they kindle in the mother a violent hatred from those able
to understand the things that are happening; but if they happen to be of age,
those more than all scatter disgust. Which things therefore the laws also,
having seen all, both comforting those who are pained at these things, and
making defense for themselves, that they did not appoint this marriage
according to their judgment nor as a primary thing, but fearing lest some
worse evil come about, declined all the bright things of second marriage; and
neither flute, nor clappings, nor wedding-song, nor dances, nor bridal crowns,
nor anything else of such things adorn that evening, but having stripped it of
all, thus they lead the man uncrowned to the widow woman, all but crying out
through these things that they do all things worthy of pardon, but not of
praises and clappings and crowns. How then did Paul prevent being a widow,
someone says, even the young women who wish it, writing thus: "But refuse
younger widows"? Paul did not prevent those who wish to remain widows, but
those women even compelled Paul, though he did not wish it, to appoint this
law for them; for if you wish to learn Paul's will, hear what he says: "But I
wish all men to be as also myself," in continence. So that that blessed man
would not have fought with himself nor have fallen into so great a
contradiction, nor would he who wished all men to be in continence have
prevented those who wish to remain widows. How then does he say: "But refuse
younger widows"? But tell, for whose sake and why? For he has not said it
simply thus, but has also added the cause, saying: "For when they have grown
wanton against Christ, they wish to marry." Do you see that it is not those
who wish to remain widows, but those who choose to marry after being widowed,
that he prevents from being enrolled in that holy choir? and doing it very
wisely. For if you are going, he says, to keep company with second marriages,
do not even promise widowhood; for to act faithlessly after the promise is far
worse than not to have promised at all.

Just as therefore he permitted continual intercourse not as legislating, but
as pardoning them: "For this I say," he says, "by way of pardon, not by way of
command, because of your incontinence." So also here, because of another
greater evil, he appointed second marriage, showing that this too is of
pardon, condescending to the weakness of the many. And I mean weakness not of
power, but of choice. For just as the virgin, having been corrupted after the
promise of virginity, has dared something worse than adultery, so also the
widow, having once promised, then having trampled the covenants toward God,
will fall under the same sin and will be liable to the same punishment; and if
one must say something even astonishing, perhaps even much greater; for it is
not equal, which I also said at the beginning, for both the inexperienced and
the experienced to fall again into the same temptations. And not here only,
but having said before, and saying again: "I wish younger women to marry, to
bear children, to rule the house," he adds the cause for which he wishes these
things. And what is it?


"To give no occasion to the adversary for the sake of reviling." For since it
was likely that many of the widows then used the life after these things more
rashly and more stubbornly, as having been delivered from some necessity and
mastery, the yoking toward the husband, so as even to draw upon themselves an
evil reputation through their boldness, leading them away from this
destructive freedom, he brings them again to the former yoke. For if a widow,
he says, is going to fornicate in secret and shame herself, it is far better
to marry, and "to give no occasion to the adversary for the sake of reviling."
So that they might not furnish the occasions of reviling, nor live an
insolent and fornicating life, he appointed marriage. Hear then also how many
things he accuses them of. For they ought to have been turned to prayers the
whole time, and to supplications. "But they," he says, "are idle also, but
also tattlers and busybodies, speaking the things they ought not." But he
himself does not wish it so, but that she be nailed throughout to the
spiritual things; "For she who lives in luxury," he says, "is dead while
living." Since even the virgin he does not wish to define this good by the
purity of the body, but to spend all her leisure upon the worship of God; "For
this," he says, "I say to you, not that I may cast a noose upon you, but
toward what is seemly and waiting upon the Lord without distraction." For he
does not wish her to be divided, but to be wholly of the spiritual things and
of the things in the heavens and to care for the things of the Lord. So also
he exhorts the widow to live, saying: "But she who is truly a widow and left
alone has hoped in God and continues in the supplications and the prayers
night and day." Whenever then the leisure which they ought to spend in the
evangelical matters they spend not only on superfluous and useless things, but
even on the things greatly harmful, throughout their whole life, reasonably he
leads them to marriage at last. Just as therefore God also gave the Sabbath to
the Jews not that they might be idle simply, but that they might keep from
wicked deeds; so also the widow and the virgin choose this life not that they
may simply not keep company with a husband, but that they may care for the
things of the Lord, that they may wait wholly upon the service of God. Yes,
someone says; but it will be an unbearable evil that a woman, being
inexperienced in affairs, should be forced to endure the things of men. For
she herself will not be able to handle them as he did, and from these things
she will gain only afflictions and the losing of everything. Is it then that
all who have not kept company with second marriages have lost all that was
theirs and have fallen from all things, and is it not possible to see a widow
woman presiding over affairs? These things are a pretext and an excuse and
coverings of their own weakness.

For many have both presided over houses more nobly than the men, and have
brought up orphan children, and of the properties in hand some have increased
them, and some have not diminished them. For God from the beginning did not
commit the whole to the men, nor did he leave the affairs in life to hang upon
them alone in all things; for then the woman would have been easy to despise,
contributing nothing to our life. Which thing therefore God knowing assigned
her a portion not less; and making this clear he said from above: "Let us make
for him a helper." For lest by having been made first, and by the woman having
been formed through him, the man should have a high mind against her, by this
word he brought down his vanity, showing that the affairs of the world need
the woman no less than the man. What then are these things, and in what does
she take part with us toward the constitution of life? For since the private
things no less than the public hold together the present state, having divided
them, he entrusted all the things in the marketplace to the men, but the
things according to the house to the women; and if they exchange the rank,
everything is corrupted and destroyed; so each in his own is far more useful
than the other. Therefore if the things according to the house


depend upon the woman's skill, and the woman is so much better than the man in
this as craftsmen are than the unskilled in the things in which they are
craftsmen, why do we fear this fear superfluously? For to bring in revenue
from outside and to gather is of men only, and it is not lawful for women to
make gain; but to keep and guard the things gathered is of this one only. So
that, even if acquiring seems to be more than guarding, yet without this that
also becomes useless and superfluous; and often even with this present that
has not only not helped at all, but has even destroyed everything. For since
it is a difficult thing for a man making gain from outside to furnish just
gains, for for the most part these traffic in the misfortunes of others, they
have often by the wife's skill and household-management ruined the things
brought unjustly and with violence into her hands. So that, even if acquiring
is greater than guarding, in another way this is shown to be less, whenever it
not only contributes nothing toward the addition of what there is, but even
destroys the things stored up. Why then does the widow fear that in the
absence of the husband she may dispose the things of the house worse, of which
even while he was living she herself had the care? But she will handle it more
easily, someone says, through fear of him, no one opposing, nor furnishing
difficulty. For servants and stewards and overseers all cower, and obey with
much compliance, and there is no one who speaks against; but when the one who
makes fear has gone, all set upon the widow, they do evil, they grow bold,
they confuse and tear everything apart; and if she comes on and defends
herself, twisting, whipping, throwing into prison, there are condemnations,
revilings, accusations from the many. But if she trample the covenants toward
the one who has gone and forget his love, and the evening on which he was
first joined to her, and the clapping, and the wedding-song, and the bridal
torches, and the first embraces, and the tables, and the salt of which he
partook with her through all the time, and of words which a wife is likely to
enjoy from a husband; if she throw all these away suddenly as not even having
happened, having thrown open the doors of the house to another, and drag him
to that man's bed which has known all the former things, if she does these
things, is there no one who blames or accuses? no one who will hate and call
her unaffectionate and faithless and without treaty and all such things? For
do not, because the blessed Paul conceded the matter, already think it also to
be worthy of praises, and to have been released from condemnation from the
many. For it is outside of punishment and vengeance, but it would not be able
to share in praises and encomia. For even for someone to be prone and lustful,
and to abstain from the wife neither in the time of the fast nor in any other,
this too is far and distant from punishment, yet not near to praises; for this
very condescension so great is nothing other than a sign of weakness and much
carelessness. So that if you have feared lest you receive a reputation of
boldness through the rebuke of the servants, before this you ought to fear
lest you rub on so great a reputation of lust and prodigality and
faithlessness. And besides these things it will be possible for the widow to
pursue the matter better, so that both the many affairs may be in safety for
her, and she may not only not be blamed but even be praised by all, and before
this may obtain the goods from God. For if she is willing to deposit the money
in heaven and bury it in that inviolable place, she will not only not be
diminished, but it will also become far more. For such is this seed. But if
she is too weak to come as far as that legislation and does not wish to
transfer everything at once, let her consider that again, that even having
taken a husband she will not altogether receive such a one as also to add to
what there is; if

then he also is such, let her not think this only, the addition of the money,
but that she will be led on to offend in many things both God and men. For if
he is of the powerful and having much strength, there are times when he will
force her against her judgment both to do and to suffer many things, and what
she feared in widowhood, this she will now undergo with greater necessity; and
not this only but that it is likely she will also receive the change very
swiftly. For being a widow, even if she diminish something of such things, yet
she will have the things remaining with much safety; but having been joined to
a powerful man who also manages the affairs of the city, or who also pursues
some other care, she will often throw away everything at once; for it is
necessary that those who dwell with them also share in the misfortunes of the
men. And if nothing of such a kind should happen, what is the gain, tell me,
in choosing slavery instead of freedom? and what is the profit of the much
money, when she is not able to use it toward what she wishes? Is it not far
better to have a little with authority, than the things of the inhabited world
with herself also, together with those, being subject to another? For the
cares, and the insults, and the revilings, and the jealousies, and the
suspicions in vain, and the birth-pains, and all the other things I pass over
now. For he who discourses with the virgin will reasonably discourse also
concerning these things, as she is inexperienced and ignorant of those
matters; but to a widow someone will even be a nuisance saying these things.
For what she has learned more accurately through the things themselves, it is
superfluous to try to teach her by the word. But this much it is good to add,
that the virgin, having been married, will keep company with more confidence
and freedom than she who marries after widowhood. For this one, even if he
cherishes her as a wife, yet he has not taken her as a virgin; and that the
loves of those are far more violent and mad than of that, is clear to
everyone, I suppose; but that one, as being experienced of a husband, he will
neither greet nor love with all his heart. For we have all been born, as I
said, all men, whether by jealousy, or by vainglory, or I do not know from
where else, to love especially those things of which we have both the power
and the enjoyment not after others, but we ourselves have been established
first and alone as masters. This too one might see happening to us in the
case of garments; for we are not disposed in the same way toward the things
used by others and the things used by no one. This also in the case of a house
and of vessels; for we do not love a house given to us by another in the same
way as that which we ourselves have built; and of the vessels, those newly
made and having received first

use among us we keep in much sparing and zeal, but those that have come to us
from others we do not very much accept, but we turn away from them so, as even
often to take them apart. And if this passion happens to us in the case of a
house and garments and vessels, consider in the case of the wife, than whom
nothing is more precious to men, how it is likely to fall with violence. For
of those we would even give a share to those who wish, but of this it is not
lawful for us, but we will stand off from the soul first, rather than endure
to suffer this. The virgin therefore, as I said, as being untouched and his
own and having become no one else's, he accepts with all eagerness; but her
who has formerly been joined to another he will not look upon with the same
love and goodwill. Do not tell me the things that happen rarely and scarcely
once, but the things continually shown in the facts. Not through these things
only will that one share in more confidence, but also through many others. For
this one could also easily reproach her as being despised by her, and bring
forward as a proof of the slight the faithlessness that has happened to her
toward the former, and shut her mouth both concerning the things that have
happened and concerning the things that perhaps will not even happen. For the
one who has gone and been despised will persuade the one living to suspect
these things concerning himself, even if they do not happen. And not that one
only, bringing these things forward to her, will be continually burdensome,


but also servants and maids, even if not openly, at least secretly murmuring,
strike her with a thousand jeers. And if it also happen that children of the
dead man are left young, how will she bring them up, and how will she preside?
and than what orphans will those live more grievously, seeing another having
all the things of the father, both servants and house and fields and the head
of all, the wife? how will they be able to be disposed as toward a mother? and
how she as toward children whom she is forced to be ashamed of and to blush
at, and to whom she is not able to assign all the maternal affection, her mind
being split toward the children from him? What then, if she is very much a
girl, someone says, and having enjoyed the husband a short time? For these
things have been said by me toward the girls, not toward those who have grown
old; for toward those who do this I will not even discourse; for if the long
time and the age and all the other things have not persuaded them to keep from
second marriage, very much will not even the word from us persuade; but all my
word is toward these. What then, if she is a girl, someone says, and having
dwelt with the former only a year, is married to a second again? for whose
sake will you prefer her to her who has spent the twentieth year and the
thirtieth in marriage? Not I, but the blessed Paul; for he says: "But she is
more blessed if she remains so." For what if that one also dwelt with a
husband a long time, yet with one and the same and only and whom she was
allotted from the beginning? but this one has given herself to two, and this
in a short time. But not willingly, she says; for if the first were living,
she would not have loved another upon him; but since he went away before the
season, she has been joined to a second again by necessity. Of what necessity?
For I see another necessity greater than this which you speak of, sufficient
to hold her to the dead man, the having so bitterly enjoyed the things of the
world.

For she who has been together with them a long time and has been well filled,
will lay hold again as one who will obtain the same equal things; but she who
has been tried with such hard beginnings, with what choice and hope will she
come to the trial of the hard things? For not even if someone wishing to
trade, before he has gained, together with going out of the harbor, should
undergo shipwreck, will easily lay hold of the business thereafter. Nor is it
reasonable that this woman, having expected many pleasant things, but before
taking good experience of them, having seen so great mourning, should love the
things of the world, unless she happen to be very incontinent; or rather, even
if she were very much disposed toward them, and had them with great passion,
the unpleasantness of the beginning is sufficient to quench all her desire.
For then we are accustomed to remain especially with the things, when we have
been tried with good beginnings; but when from the beginning and from the
very, as one might say, starting-line we perceive hard and difficult things,
we quickly draw back, the choice being quenched in us. So that those who have
undergone untimely widowhoods, these would reasonably keep from second
marriage, for the sake of not suffering the same things again. For she who
remains in widowhood will have the safety and will not suspect another such
mourning again; but she who has kept company with second marriage will be
forced to expect this terrible thing also. And otherwise, even if widowhood is
one thing, yet not all will receive the same rewards of this, but some more,
and some less.

For those who have taken up the yoke in youth will enjoy both more honor and
the gift, but those toward old age itself not in the same way as those. Why
then? Because the one, there being many hindrances, has endured all things
through the fear of God, but this one has not undergone even a short sweat nor
toil; for how, there being no matter that furnishes force? As therefore she
who has given herself to a second is less than her who has the one husband, so
she who remains in widowhood in youth itself will be able by a great measure
to overshoot her who


in old age has cast away the husband; and yet for both the husband has been
one, but nevertheless this one has run the course of purity, while that one
has been left behind by many stadia. Do not then see the toil only, but also
the reward. For the many of the achievements appear so difficult to us,
because continually turning their labor and their sweats we do not take into
mind the rewards stored up for them. But we must not do thus, but reckon all
together, with the toils also the recompenses, and thus they will appear easy
to us, as indeed they also are easy. For the champion also, not the wounds
only, nor the falls, nor the death, but also the trophies and the victories
and all the other honors having reckoned, thus comes to the championship; and
the farmer too, not the plowing only, nor the toil in digging, but also the
threshing-floor and the wine-vats having set before his eyes, thus lays hold
of the works.

So let us also by the good hopes lighten the labor of widowhood, and far more
we than those; for for those many things often of the things not lying upon
them have cut off the expectation, but there is no one who puts our hopes to
shame, unless we ourselves wish. Let us not then wish it, but having
considered that the widow falls short of the virgin by not much, and there is
where she has even overshot her, whenever the virgin is entangled in affairs;
"But the widow," according to Paul, "left alone and hoping in God continues in
the supplications and the prayers," and keeps from the things of life, let us
take up this contest, that we may obtain the crowns upon it. These things have
been said by us not for the sake of necessity, nor, as I said, condemning
those who do not wish to remain widows, have we come to this exhortation, but
urging and calling, not to be bound to the earth so far, but once having been
loosed to remain free, and to seek heaven, and to display the citizenship
there, and having been betrothed to Christ so to do all things as befits those
who have such a bridegroom.
