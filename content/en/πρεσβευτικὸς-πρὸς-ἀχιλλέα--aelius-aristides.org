Best of the Achaeans, Achilles, it is in no way unreasonable that you are
angry and harsh over the things by which you were insulted; but not so far as
this. I, however, if Agamemnon still even now held the same opinion, or if he
were denying what he did wrong, I would not have made this request, but I
would have let you do as some day seems good to you; but since for this very
thing he both makes entreaties and offers gifts, giving some and promising
others, what could one find fault with in a man who confesses? But just as he
concedes to you to speak justly, so must you be persuaded by one who entreats
you. Otherwise, how is it not absurd, first, to hold the same opinion as
before toward one who no longer holds the same; then, consider lest you
receive from us the justice for what you blame in him. For if you are angry
with them all, say, by Zeus, on what account; but if you acquit the others of
blame, the danger is common to all. How then are you just to look on? So that,
by Zeus, he may be hated still more by all on account of these things? But I
fear most of all that those who would hate him may not survive; so thickly do
they fall. And besides, now, in the name of the gods, how else do you think
they hold their opinion? Yet I fear that while you are in the ascendant the
contrary may come about from what you wish, and that, abandoning him, they
then blame you. For do not think that the one who first began the anger is
culpable, but that the one who not even

later willing to stop, seems to have deliberated moderately. Apart from these
things, it is not more yours, dear Achilles, to seek how someone will justly
be hated than how the common cause will be saved. For if one must be angry
forever, I would say one must be so with the barbarians and with those who are
enemies by nature. For against them our grievances are far greater and concern
greater things. How then is it just to hate the one who grieved you on a
single occasion, but to look on the enemies, enemies from the time they were
born, not merely as innocent, but as winning so far over your companions and
those with whom you were first seen by them? Since now, if you have decided to
destroy the camp utterly, why do you not openly go over to the barbarians, and
let those from the city drive in upon us, while you yourself, attacking from
Sigeum, make yourself the middle between yourself and them? Because, in the
name of Zeus, these things are not bearable nor holy? To the same
considerations then belongs this present aid. For they are destroyed no less
while you keep quiet, and to the posture there is added indifference, from
seeming to watch what happens. So the reproach becomes double instead of
single: for you seem both to hate your own people and to fear the battle. Not
you; but choose from the beginning one of the two: if the claims of anger win,
use your whole wrath; but if you know how to calculate, do not be divided in
your judgment, nor hold back from them as from friends while advancing as
against enemies, but know that you are doing so much worse now than if you
were marching against us with the barbarians, by as much as treachery is
harder than open enmity.

Yet you: if ambassadors were to come now from Priam about an alliance, you
would not receive them in your tent; but what those men would pray for, that
you yourself do, and destroying the cause with your anger you sit idle; then
again, if not with iron

or with fire you do this, consider it something mild. And you would not grant
equal standing to them and to us, but you stand equally apart from both;
rather, you aid neither, but in judgment you lean toward them: so much more do
they have than we. And toward the anger itself you bore yourself so like a
citizen that you did not think even Agamemnon himself had to die; a great
proof of this: you did not kill him, though you were able. But now you think
not even one of the others must be left; and yet it was far better to save him
too, undeserving, along with the others, than to look on while all are utterly
destroyed because of him, and so to receive from him the justice that is
coming to be. Tell me, then, what you would pray for most of all? Is it not
that he be ill-thought-of and ill-spoken-of and held in hatred in the camp?
This then he will have from both sides, both from those who have perished so
far and from those who will be saved hereafter; for he will seem to have been
the cause of this for those men, having estranged you from the alliance by his
insolence, while you will seem to have saved these alone of men by your aid,
so that of his part no one of the Achaeans survives, while of yours no one has
been lost. Consider then also the cause of the quarrel itself, how it arose
for you against him from no other matter, but on behalf of the Achaeans, that
they might be saved. For it was for this that you called the assembly, seeing
the multitude perishing by the plague, and you urged Calchas to speak boldly
if he knew anything. And when he came forward and said that he feared lest, by
provoking someone stronger than himself, he might suffer some evil, you swore
an oath that you surely would not look on at this. From these things the one

declared the guilty one, and the other was angry at both; but you did not
withdraw until what was needful had been done.

Is it not absurd, then, that you should destroy by the very enmity those for
whose sake you took up the enmity then, that they be saved, and should demand
that the calamities from fortune be set right, while yourself deliberately
doing the things from which we are being destroyed, and, concerning the very
things for which you then prayed to the gods, be unwilling now to commune with
yourself; and for the barbarian priest to demand that the god be respected,
but for the multitude of your own kindred to be unwilling to be ashamed before
the gods who are common, nor before the holy rites nor the libations; and then
to give up your own favorite, that the cause of the Achaeans be saved, but
now, in place of her, again to wipe out the Achaeans to a man? And indeed if
there were a choice between two, and let me have frankness, by Zeus, for it is
just, especially before a man who honors the truth: whether this girl must be
kept safe, or the camp and the cause of the Achaeans? You would neither have
asked this of Zeus your ancestor, nor of any other of the gods, that she be
left to you instead of all things; what then? If it is possible both to have
her back and to save the Achaeans, will you choose to be alienated from her
and to have lost these? And how is it not absurd to know how to exchange
misfortune for misfortune, when it is possible to have both without loss, and
then to accept being deprived of both? For it was not the Atreidae alone who
loved their own wives. But who could say such things? And yet even in this
very thing the nature of the war is worthy of wonder. For if each of us had
had this at the very beginning, that Menelaus was now gathering an army for
the sake of his own wife, how would it have been reasonable for me to leave my
own wife and, in addition to my wife, children and parents in old age and
possessions and servants, to sail outside of Hellas, and besides

these things still to run risks on campaign with life at stake, which for all
men has happened to be the most precious of all possessions and by which the
best of the Greeks is defined first: if each had used these calculations,
surely the army would never have been gathered at all. But as it is, I suppose
these two were the things that moved us: that we were long since bound
beforehand by the oaths, through the foresight, I suppose, of some god, and
that we all seemed to be insulted in common by the aliens and that we ought
not permit it. One might perhaps add a third, that one ought to obey the
leaders.

But let us not examine now for what reason we took up the campaign from the
beginning, but consider that it is not the same thing, then to make all other
things second, and now to desert before accomplishing that for which we came.
For it is not consistent with the pretext of the campaign, but appears
contrary to the campaign itself; and then it is not the same thing either; how
so, you shall hear kindly. For I speak not that I may refute you, but that I
may console you: for if you were not getting back the woman, you would
naturally have retired to this; but now he gives you not only her, but seven
in addition to her; for I pass over others, so many in number. And yet if the
Trojans had been willing to give back Helen and all the things they carried
off with her, there would have been no quarrel between us and them; and if
besides they added from their own means things from home, not one would have
uttered a word that one must not accept. What is the great proof of this? We
ourselves challenged them to these things right at the beginning, but neither
did any of them wish to do these things, and the one of which you were robbed
returns to you with many things. And if again he must further promise a
penalty for the outrage, he has already paid a very great one. Besides the so
many thousands of soldiers he has lost, what sort of penalty, from fear and
from being humbled, do you think he gives you?

For now nothing else at all has been laid aside except the whole posture: he
has become a suppliant instead of a king and a slave instead of a free man,
and, if one must speak more truthfully, one trusting in pity, if he bears what
is strongest from fortune.

And indeed if we must remember these things too, consider this: it is among
the absurdities that, when the army was then being gathered, you had so eager
a disposition to take part in the affairs that, as soon as you saw the arms,
you rushed at them and could not restrain yourself, and that too being a boy
and hidden among the girls, but now that you are of such an age and have a boy
beautiful enough to share your bed and serve as a soldier, and have taken the
first glory among all, you are unwilling to use your own arms, but seek
pretexts through which you will give up everything, and then you left the girl
easily, though what would more likely be dearer to you? and her, whom you won
as a wife of equal honor to yourself, now, for the sake of another girl, to
judge the opposite about the whole matter. Consider whether these things would
be worthy of praise even for Deidamia, much less for a man of whom it is noble
even to be spoken. And you say you heard of the two fates from your mother,
who best knew about them. I, however, am in doubt whether you say these things
in earnest or teasing us and angry with some love of victory; for these words
are not yours; I infer this from your many deeds. Nevertheless, even if you
have been most in earnest, it is possible for you, even with these things so,
to deliberate what is needful: for I would say, at least, that you should make
your mother agree with these words, since your father and your teacher tried
to educate you in this way; and then here is the most terrible thing of all,
if by their very words and by your hearing them you stood so firmly as to
choose glory and the noble before safety and old age; but since

you have agreed with her about the better fate, you will become another man
again from the beginning, and then you thought the camp worth more than your
own soul, but now not worthy even of the girl, or rather of one of the things
that happened about the girl. Thus you would seem to hold the girl dearer than
both, than yourself and than the Achaeans. For how not also than yourself, if
being saved is better than being glorious, and the girl better than glory? And
thus you would seem both to have made your mother a liar and to convict
yourself of not knowing how to calculate. For to choose at the very beginning
to sail home and live in quiet was a judgment of opinion; but to have chosen
the dangers on campaign and then to change about concerning the same things is
not to know the same things; which least of all becomes you, of all men, as
much as anyone you cultivate virtue and truth-speaking. For surely you will
not accuse those who say one thing and know another, and praise those who
deliberate now one way now another about the same things and are not the same
men? Consider also that in the camps, until the generals form them up, each
man is, so to speak, his own autocrat, but once he has come into rank, he must
preserve it, or be considered worthless; but you will seem not to be
deliberating about what is your choice, but to be withdrawing from what you
yourself preferred and simply abandoning your rank; at which, I think, even in
a dream you would justly be ashamed. You were judging these things just and
noble: that a long life is not a thing to choose for a man, unless there be
added the doing of what is needful. But since the heralds went away leading
the girl, and for that reason the things of nature stand otherwise. You will
not stop contending for the two worst reproaches, cowardice and faithlessness,
which not one of your enemies ever cast at you.

And yet who would you be, in the name of the gods, if Agamemnon had laid these
things upon you?

I, however, think that the end of all things must be left to the god, just as
you see all of us deliberating about ourselves. For this one thing alone needs
no subtlety, but to all it is clear that peace is a more profitable prize than
war, and that it is not possible for men fighting and risking to pass their
lifetime without bloodshed. But we do not transgress our faith for the sake of
this; but we leave to the god the things of which he is lord, while of the
things that come into calculation and deliberation, we do not anywhere honor
for ourselves the worse part of a man. In the name of Zeus, but the gifts are
not worthy of you, nor will he startle you with his promises, not even if he
should pass the sand and the dust in quantity. Excellent, brave man, and I
recognize these words already. I, however, do not counsel you to aid the
Achaeans for the sake of the gifts; whence? May I never see your possessions
so cheaply; but for the sake of justice itself and of its being fitting to act
so. But whether you receive the gifts as some token of friendship or let them
be and refuse them, I shall not contend; and to deliberate at leisure about
this marriage, for not even this would hurry you. For I should be a child if I
counseled this, to think that he is not ungentlemanly in the giving and the
promises, but that the things that bring your departure are other for
yourself. What are these? The things you yourself counted off a little before,
things far nobler than money. For how is it not absurd and beyond all things,
if formerly, leading these Achaeans, you captured twelve cities by sea and
eleven on land, reckoning nothing of your own, neither toil, nor danger, nor
preoccupation, nor the soul you say is dearest to yourself if you are to give
it up, and showed yourself master of so many resources and such felicity; but
now not even toward their very

you win the girl, that we may let the rest go? From the things by which you do
us good. So at least you took Lyrnessus among the multitude of the other

Then it is not likely that the army is an appendage of the girl, but that the
girl is of the camp and the other affairs. But most absurd of all, if Chryses,
getting back his daughter, was not led on further to pray against us as
enemies, but stopped his anger, a man a barbarian and by nature an enemy,
though he too had shared in the danger and in her safety, while you, getting
back the woman herself, and receiving beside her so many women, and besides
the women money, cities, land, kindred, things one ought not to wonder at,
though you have no ground of complaint, will you remain forever in your
exasperation? And he prayed together with the god to stop the plague among us,
and you will not be willing to stop the hardships of the war? But the priest
showed his power to the Achaeans on both sides, both from his cursing, by
which he destroyed many, and from his prayer, by which he saved all; and will
you demand to imitate the priest from the one side only, and that from the
perishing? But it was far better to show your vigor by saving all, than, after
doing the one thing, to flee from the rest, and you would think it terrible to
betray the camp in order to have back your own, but saving the camp along with
having back your own will not satisfy you; but Hector comes on over everything
with fire and iron, and it is a small thing for him to save Troy, and you will
stay in your tent singing to the lyre? Will you stay singing the deeds of
others? But it is better to leave your own to others to sing, for some good
end. But the wrath, in the name of Zeus, that you cherish, will you hymn this
like a god? I fear you will sing funeral dirges for the Achaeans, so untimely
for the whole time

is content to live and for this reason is out of range of the missiles,

but you, dear sir, do not jest, nor requite a baseness that in no way becomes
you; for you will persuade neither us nor, I think, yourself. But, having
launched your ships, will you sail away and be gone? Then will you not be
ashamed, the Nereids, your kinsfolk by your mother, to be sailing the sea so

but now dishonored by all. And from which kinsmen are these things worthy of
you, whether of those of the sea or those on land, who, coming here once, did
not sail away before they had stormed the city together with the warlord? And
what name, in the name of the gods, will you give to the sacrifice you say you
will sacrifice tomorrow? Thank-offerings? Or what do you call it? Then are you
not ashamed, making the gods themselves witnesses of such wickedness,
sacrificing and pouring libations and singing paeans over the calamities of
the Greeks, saying that you are quit of them all, while you gaze on the plain,
full of the dead ranged beneath you, for some day, I know not which, onward?
And what will your sailing's posture be? Or what the report of those who see
or hear? 'Achilles sails home'? Bringing what spoils, or leading what first-
fruits, or what will you crown at home, of private or of public things? And
fair it will be now to put in at Scyros. And what will you say to your own
boy? In what will you seem better than your wife, having said it? And why do I
speak of Scyros or of a wife?

But what will the Trojans say? Will they not say that you fled fearing, seeing
no safety if you remain? Worthily, is it not? The old age of Priam and Nestor
compounded of such disgrace.

But you will not do these things; you will remain until Hector has come to
your tents and ships, killing all in turn, and then you will show them who you
are? If so much is left over to you, how is it not disgraceful to let that
occasion pass by and not to use your abundance for the salvation of all, but
only when no one is left? But if it is already becoming laborious, one must
aid while it is possible? I, however, distrust even that, that you alone are
not only sufficient against all the barbarians but still superior. I think,
however, that in every way the occasion makes a difference, not only in this,
that with spectators and witnesses present it is better to display this
surpassing quality, but also in that: at that time you would seem to be aiding
yourself and defending under compulsion, but now to be going out for those who
called you and to be aiding them; so that you will gain no small thing in
gratitude. In general, one cannot become a treasurer of the war for whatever
portion one wishes. But many of the things you wish may befall together with
what you would not accept. Some even of those very men so dear to you will
fall. And I pass over the others; but we, these men whom you greeted as
dearest, and whom you raised up when you saw them, and for whom you set the
bowl and called for stronger drink, what hinders that we also lie tomorrow at
that time, mingled together with the rest, and that, from those with whom you
now pour libations, the earth flow with blood, and bowls be set up in Hector's
honor as victory-offerings, and paeans be sung while you live, O Zeus? And
what if, along with the tents being taken and the ships being burned and this
much-longed-for

girl they slaughter, or even taking her captive lead her away again to
themselves? Or is this now the sum of all things? I would gladly learn from
you: if Agamemnon then, taking her, had killed her, or even while she stayed
with you, what he ought to do now, or how much blood-money he must lay down to
win goodwill, if indeed, now restoring her alive and besides swearing what you
would most wish, he will not stop; but you will come to such a height of anger
that, unless you receive your penalty from all, by nations and by cities and
by each body, you will reckon life unlivable for yourself. Take care lest you
come to hate the girl herself, when because of her the camp has been utterly
destroyed.

I wish to tell you also a saying of your teacher Chiron, which he once uttered
on Pelion to one of the men from Aetolia. For he said that of anger, nothing
would become more beautiful for a man than the moderate, and nothing more
disgraceful than the excessive; anger, having grieved its enemies in return,
is released, but it destroys the man who has it always and continually. But
may you neither work anything further of this nor yourself suffer it. And
reflect that nature has set bounds, as it were, for all things, and nothing
among human things is immortal, neither war, nor peace, nor favor, nor anger,
nor anything else of all; or has a dissolution been found for the friendship
you have with the king, but will none again appear for the anger? And indeed
it is far better to demand that friendships be immortal than, when those
things are coming under the fortune of enmity, to be unwilling to provide
dissolutions; for these two goddesses, Nemesis and Justice, walk round all the
affairs of men, not allowing them to think greater than nature, but easily
making the great small, if one makes no account of them. And of this you have
the example near at hand: one who then thought more highly than you, of what

does he not think no less now? But you must be led: in word to hear these
things from us, but in truth and by what is happening to have Hector as your
ambassador and to converse with you; as for the rest of his outrages and
taunts, what would one say? But you he threatens to seize while you sleep. In
any case he does not consider his own ancestor Dardanus at all inferior to
your Aeacus, you hiding behind your wrath and he himself in truth fearing. How
are these things bearable for me to hear? Who will take vengeance for you for
these things? Will he not rise up, in the name of the gods? Will you not be
indignant that he has not set a penalty long since, in proportion as time has
advanced? And if one must even be angry for the girl's sake, will you turn
your wrath upon the Trojans, the causes of all these things? For if they had
stirred up nothing at the beginning, what of all things greater or less would
have happened here that was grievous? And reflect that Peleus stands between
Aeacus and you, that Aeacus on whom the affairs of the Greeks once came. And
you have this too in the fairest way: imitating your ancestor's judgment you
surpass it in the deed. For he benefited by praying, but you, aiding in deed,
will outdo the prayers of all. And reckon this further besides: we shall go
out at once from the tent, with what face, by Zeus, if these things do not
meet your approval? What words shall we speak when we have gone out, or what
answers shall we give? All are watching, surely, whether they will hear
gladly, running together, each from his place. What must we say to them?
Answer: pour a libation with us to Zeus the Savior, that this may not be the
end of things as they are, but, ashamed before the common need and before us
the ambassadors and the occasion of the embassy, this night, or even these
stars and the gods and heroes common to the Greeks, do you strip off your
anger like a sickness and appear to the barbarians together with the sun.
