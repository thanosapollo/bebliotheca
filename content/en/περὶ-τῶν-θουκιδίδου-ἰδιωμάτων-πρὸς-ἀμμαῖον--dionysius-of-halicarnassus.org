* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I for my part supposed that I had sufficiently exhibited the style of
Thucydides, having gone through the greatest and most essential of the
peculiarities belonging to him, in which indeed he seemed to me especially to
differ from the orators and historians before him: first in the notes on the
ancient orators composed to your name, and a little while ago in the treatise
composed about Thucydides himself, addressed to Aelius Tubero, in which I have
gone through everything that required discussion, together with the proper
proofs, according to my ability. But since you suppose that the writings have
been worked out less exactly, because I first set out all the features that
belong to the style and then furnish the proofs concerning them, and you think
that the exhibition of the peculiarities of the style will be more exact if
beside each one of the propositions I set the words of the historian, which is
what those who compose the arts and the introductions of speeches do, having
chosen to leave nothing wanting I have done this too, taking the didactic form
instead of the demonstrative.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

And so that the argument may be easy for you to follow, having first set out
word for word whatever I happen to have said before about the historian, I
will take up each of the propositions cursorily and furnish the proofs,
just as you asked. What is about to be shown follows upon what was written
about Herodotus. For Thucydides, coming after this man and the others of whom
I made mention earlier, and having observed what virtues each of them
possessed, having worked out a certain peculiar kind of style, neither wholly
prose nor completely metrical, but something common and mixed
from both, was eager to introduce it into the historical work: in the choice
of words often taking over figurative and rare and archaic and
foreign diction instead of that common and customary among the men of his own
time; and in the figures, in which he most wished to differ from those before
him, having brought in the greatest labor, now making a phrase out of a noun,
now contracting the phrase into a noun; and now expressing the verbal element
as a noun, and again making the noun a verb; and even reversing the uses of
these themselves, so that the proper noun becomes appellative, and the
appellative is spoken as a proper noun, and the passive verbs become active,
and the active passive; and changing the natures of plurals and singulars and
predicating these of one another, joining feminines to masculines and
masculines to feminines and neuters to some of these, from which the natural
sequence is led astray; and turning the cases of the nominal or participial
forms now toward the thing signified away from the signifier, now toward the
signifier away from the thing signified; and in the connective and
prepositional particles, and still more in those that articulate the forces of
the nouns, using license in the manner of a poet. And one would find very many
of the figures in him altered by turns of person and exchanges of tenses and
transfers of figurative expressions, and taking on the appearance of
solecisms; and as many things as become things instead of bodies or bodies
instead of things, and those arguments and thoughts in which the intervening
insertions, becoming many, fetch the sequence from far off, and the crooked
and intricate and hard to disentangle and the others akin to these. And one
would find not a few of the theatrical figures lying in him as well, I mean
the equal clauses and the like-sounding clauses and the word-plays and the
antitheses, in which Gorgias of Leontini and the circle of Polus and Licymnius
and many others of those who flourished in his time were excessive. But most
manifest and most characteristic of him are both the attempt to signify the
most things through the fewest words and to compose many thoughts into one,
and the leaving the hearer still expecting to hear something, by which the
brevity becomes unclear. And to speak in summary, there are four instruments,
as it were, of the diction of Thucydides: the poetic quality of the words, the
variety of the figures, the roughness of the composition, the speed of the
signification; and its colors are the compact and the bitter and the dense and
the austere and the weighty and the terrible and fearful, and above all these
of his the passionate. Such then is Thucydides according to the character of
his diction, in which he differed from the others.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Rare, then, and archaic and hard for most to guess are "the unmixed"
and "the calculation" and "the lookout" and "the armistice" and the things
like these. Poetic are "the hindrance" and "the embassy" and "the outcry" and
"the vexation" and "the claim of right" and the like.

And the novelty and versatility in the figures, and the departure from
customary use, in which we think he especially differs from the others,
becomes apparent in these operations:

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

When he expresses one word, whether a noun or a verb, in several nouns or
verbs, paraphrasing the same thought, he makes diction of this kind: /For
Themistocles was a man who most firmly displayed strength of nature, and was
in a remarkable degree more worthy than another of admiration for this./ And
indeed in the Funeral Oration he has written: /nor again by poverty, having
something good to do for the city, is he prevented by obscurity of rank/; for
in these also the conciseness makes the speech of this kind, as he has set it
in the case of the Lacedaemonian Brasidas, when fighting about Pylos he fell
wounded from the ship: /and when he had fallen/, he says, /into the outrigger
the shield slipped off./ For he wishes to make clear: /and when he had fallen
outside the ship onto the projecting parts of the oar-gear/ into the sea.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

And in the places where he figures the verbal parts of the diction as nouns,
he makes phrasing of this kind. The Corinthian in the first book says this to
the Athenians: /These just claims then we have toward you, and this
exhortation and this claim of gratitude of such a kind./ For "to exhort" and
"to claim," being verbs, have become nouns, "exhortation" and "claim." Of this
kind also are both the "not-walling-off" of Plemmyrium in the seventh book and
the "lamentation" which he has set in the first book in a public speech. For
"to wall off" and "to lament," being verbal, he has figured as nouns,
"walling-off" and "lamentation."

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And when, reversing the nature of each of these, he makes the nouns into
verbs, he expresses the diction in this way, as in the first book he writes
about the cause of the war: /the truest cause, though most unapparent in
speech, I think the Athenians becoming great forced them into warring./ For he
wishes to make clear that the Athenians, becoming great, furnished a necessity
of the war; but instead of "necessity" and "war," which are nouns, he has made
verbal forms, both "to force" and "to war."

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

And when he exchanges the forms of the verbs, the passive and the active, he
figures the speech thus: /for neither does that hinder the treaties, nor
this/; for the verb "hinders," being active, has been taken instead of "is
hindered," which is passive; and what was signified by the diction was of this
kind: /for neither is that hindered by the treaties, nor this./ And further
the things said in the preface: /commerce not existing, nor mixing fearlessly
with one another/; for in these also "mixing," being an active verb, occupies
the place of "being mixed," which is passive.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And when he takes the passive instead of the active, he figures it in this
way: /and as many of us as have already been exchanged with Athenians/; for he
wishes to make clear: /and as many of us as have already exchanged with
Athenians/, but he has taken "have been exchanged," which is passive, instead
of the active "exchanged." And what follows upon this: /and those settled more
in the interior/; for instead of the active verb "having settled" he has taken
the passive "settled."

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

And in the differences of singulars and plurals, when he exchanges the order
of each of these, he expresses singulars instead of plurals thus: /and if it
has occurred to anyone that the Syracusan is, and he himself is not, an enemy
to the Athenian/; for he wishes to speak of the Syracusans and the Athenians,
but he has made each of the names singular. And in the places where he says:
/and we shall have the enemy more terrible, his retreat not being easy for him
again/; for he has figured the enemies in the singular, not in the plural. And
instead of the singular he takes the plural, altering the customary phrasing
in this way; and the passage lies in the preface of the Funeral Oration: /for
until this point the praises spoken about others are tolerable, as far as each
himself also thinks himself sufficient to do something of what he heard/; for
"each" and "heard" are singular, but the things that follow upon these have
been expressed in the plural: /but already envying the one who exceeds him
they also disbelieve/, which are naturally said not of one but of many.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

And the transpositions of masculines and feminines and neuters that have
departed from the customary figures are of this kind, when he calls
"disturbance" "a tumult," expressing the feminine as masculine, and
"annoyance" "a crowd," and says of "wish" and "power" "the wishing" and "the
being able"; as he has set it in the case of the Athenians, when they were
sending the army to Sicily: /the Athenians were not deprived of their wishing
by the crowdedness of the preparation,/ and in the places where he has spoken
about the Thessalians: /so that if the Thessalians used not lordship rather
than equal law, the native thing/; for here too he has made the feminine into
a neuter; and what was signified by the diction was of this kind: /so that if
the Thessalians used not lordship rather than equal law, the native custom./

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

And in the places where he alters from the customary the cases of the nouns
and of the appellatives and of the participles and of the articles joined to
these, he figures the phrasing thus: /for the cities, having taken moderation
and license of what was being done, went toward outright freedom, of the
hollow good-order from the Athenians not having preferred./ For those who
figure the phrasing in accordance with common custom would have joined the
feminine particle to the feminine gender of the appellative, and would have
set the accusative case of the participle instead of the genitive, in this
way: /for the cities, having taken moderation and license of what was being
done, went toward outright freedom, not having preferred the hollow
good-order from the Athenians./ But those who join masculines to feminines,
as this man has done, and take genitives instead of accusative cases, would
be said by us to solecize. And further such things as: /and not being struck
with panic at their number/; for it was not fitting that the diction be
figured in the dative case, but in the accusative: /and not being struck with
panic at the number of the enemy./ For one would not say /to fear at the
anger from the gods/, but /the anger of the gods./

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

And the phrasing that has departed from consistency in the tenses of the verbs
is of this kind: /and yet if we should be willing to take risks with ease
rather than with practice of toils, and not with laws the more but with habits
of courage, it results for us both not to grow weary beforehand at the coming
pains and, when we have come to them, not to appear less daring than those who
are always laboring/; for here "we should be willing" is a verb indicative of
future time, and "it results" of the present. And it would have been
consistent if he had joined to "we should be willing" the form "it will
result." /For the difficulty of entering the place I think ours, which while
we remain becomes an ally; but for those who have withdrawn, though it is
hard, it will be easy./ For "becomes" is of the present, and "will be" is
indicative of future time. And there has also come about an inconsistent
figure in the cases: for he has expressed both the participial noun
"remaining" and the pronoun "of us" in the genitive case, but "for those who
have withdrawn" in the dative; and it would have been more proper that this
too be expressed in the same case.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

And when he makes the turn toward the signifier from the thing signified, or
toward the thing signified from the signifier, he figures the speech thus:
/the people of the Syracusans were in much strife toward one another/; for
having added a singular noun, "the people," he turned the speech from this
toward the thing signified, which is plural, the Syracusans. And again: /for
the Leontines, when the Athenians had left Sicily after the agreement, both
enrolled many citizens and the people intended to redistribute the land/; for
from the plural noun "Leontines" he turned the speech toward the singular
noun, "the people." [lost]

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

And things become persons in him, just as has happened in the public
speech of the Corinthians toward the Lacedaemonians. For the Corinthian,
claiming that those who stand at the head of the Peloponnese should guard its
dignity toward the cities outside, such as they received it from their
fathers, says this: /deliberate well about these things, and try not to lead
the Peloponnese in a lesser degree/ than /your fathers handed it down to you/;
for he has now set "to lead" of bringing the Peloponnese forward outside,
being leaders of it; and this could not happen to the land, but it could to
the reputation and to the affairs that exist about it, and this is what he
wishes to make clear.

And things of this kind are made by him into things instead of bodies.
And it is said by the envoy of the Corinthians toward the Lacedaemonians,
comparing the things of the Athenians and of the Lacedaemonians: /they are
innovators and sharp to conceive and to accomplish in deed whatever they
decide; but you to preserve what exists and to decide nothing extra and in
deed not even to arrive at what is necessary/; up to this point, then, the
figure of the diction preserves the sequence, as being set of both as persons.
Then the speech turns in one of the two parts, and instead of bodies a
thing comes about concerning the Lacedaemonians, when he says: /and
again they are daring beyond their power and risk-takers beyond their judgment
and in dangers of good hope; but your part is to act things falling short of
your power and not to trust even the sure things of your judgment/; for "your
part" has been taken instead of "you," being a thing instead of a body.

And in the arguments and thoughts the intervening insertions, becoming many
and scarcely to the end

arriving, through which the diction becomes hard to follow, are very many
throughout the whole history; but two taken from the preface alone will
suffice, both the one showing the weakness of the ancient affairs of Greece
and giving the causes: /for commerce not existing, nor mixing fearlessly with
one another either by land or through the sea, and each inhabiting their own
only so far as to live, and not having a surplus of money, nor planting the
land, it being unclear when someone would come against them and, they being at
the same time unwalled, another would take it away; and thinking that they
would prevail over the necessary food everywhere alike, they would not with
difficulty move/ (for if he had added "they would not with difficulty move" to
the first period and had figured it thus: /for commerce not existing, nor
mixing fearlessly with one another either by land or by sea, and each
inhabiting their own only so far as to live, they would not with difficulty
move,/ he would have made the thought clearer; but by the insertion of the
things in between, which are many, he has made it unclear and hard to follow),
and this one about the expedition of Eurystheus against Attica: /Eurystheus
having died in Attica at the hands of the Heraclidae, and Atreus being a
brother of his mother, and Eurystheus having entrusted, when he was
campaigning, both Mycenae and the rule to Atreus according to kinship; and
that he happened to be in exile from his father because of the death of
Chrysippus; and when Eurystheus did not return, the Mycenaeans also being
willing through fear of the Heraclidae, and he at the same time seeming to be
capable and having courted the multitude of the Mycenaeans and of as many as
Eurystheus ruled, Atreus took over the kingship./

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

And in the places where the construction of the arguments becomes crooked and
intricate and hard to disentangle, it is of this kind in him; and the passage
lies in the Funeral Oration: /and having taken the punishment of their
opponents as more to be desired than themselves, and having judged this at the
same time the fairest of dangers, they wished to punish the one and to aim at
the other, committing to hope the unseen of success, but in deed thinking it
right to trust themselves about what was already seen; and having thought it
better to suffer in defending themselves than to save themselves by giving in,
they fled the shame of the word, and they endured the deed with the body, and
through the briefest moment of chance, at the height at once of glory rather
than of fear, they were released./ Such also are the things said about
Themistocles by the historian in the first book: /For Themistocles was a man
who most firmly displayed strength of nature, and was in a remarkable degree
more worthy than another of admiration for this. For by native intelligence,
and having learned beforehand into it nothing and having learned afterward
nothing, he was both the strongest judge of the things of the moment by the
briefest deliberation and the best conjecturer of the things to come for the
farthest stretch of what would happen; and what he had in hand he was able
also to expound; and of what he was inexperienced he was not without the power
to judge sufficiently; and he foresaw the better or the worse while it was
still in the unseen. And to speak the whole, by power of nature and by brevity
of study this man became the strongest at improvising what was needed./

* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

And the boyish figures of the antitheses and the like-sounding clauses and the
equal clauses, in which the circle of Gorgias were especially excessive, least
belonging to this style, which has a severe manner and stands farthest from
the elegant, are of this kind in the historian: /for it is apparent that what
is now called Hellas was not long ago securely inhabited./ And still further:
/they are daring beyond their power and risk-takers beyond their judgment; but
your part is to act things falling short of your power and not to trust even
the sure things of your judgment, and never to think that you will be released
from the dangers/; and in the places where he goes through the calamities that
overtook Greece because of the factions, writing such things: /for reckless
daring was counted courage loyal to friends; and provident delay specious
cowardice, and the moderate a pretext of the unmanly, and the intelligence
that went toward everything idle toward all./ One would find many such things
said throughout his whole history, but these too are enough to have been said
for a sample.

You have, dear Ammaeus, the observations examined according to each of them
from the common treatise, as you sought.
