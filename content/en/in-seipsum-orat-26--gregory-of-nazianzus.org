ORATION XXVI.
To himself, on returning from the country after the affairs concerning
Maximus.

A. I longed for you, children, and I longed in return with equal
measures. For I am persuaded, if I must add also faith to my word: Yea,
by your boasting, brethren, which I have in Christ Jesus our Lord. For
the Holy Spirit has made this oath for me, by which we have been stirred
toward you, that we may furnish to the Lord a people peculiarly his own.
See how great a faith; I am confident of my own, and I contend for what
is on your behalf. And nothing wonderful. For of those whose Spirit is
common, the passion also is common; and to those to whom suffering is
equal, believing also is equal. For what one has not suffered, neither
would he believe for another; but he who has suffered is readier for
sympathy, an unseen witness of an unseen passion, a fitting mirror of an
alien form. For this reason I could not endure to be away from you
longer, though I greatly dislike and am burdened by the things here: not
those only which cities bear (mobs, tumults, market-places, theaters,
excesses, insolences, leading and being led, fining and being fined,
mourning and being mourned, weeping, rejoicing, marrying, burying, being
praised, being slandered, the matter of wickedness, the surging of the
world, the whirlings-about of changes, as in a Euripus and in spirits),
but also by these now more reverend and more precious things, I mean
those about this bema =35.1229= and the holy table; of which we,
thinking to have the power, and being of those who draw near to God, I
fear lest we draw near ill, and, like straw to fire, not bearing the
fire.

B. However, I have returned to you, having run away by force, but not
running back by force, and indeed most eagerly, and on feet of their own
accord, the Word, the Spirit so leading, like some stream, forcing
against the upward and hastening to the slope. Truly one day is a whole
life of a man to those who grow weary with longing. For Jacob's case
seems to me to be otherwise; who serving fourteen years the Syrian Laban
for the two maidens did not grow weary; for all his days were, he says,
as one day, because he loved them. Perhaps because the longed-for was in
his sight, or because to toil for love is light, even if the delay has
what is painful. Thus the ready is into power, the idle into desire, as
someone of those before us said. For my part, when I was present I
perceived the passion very little; but when I was separated I knew
desire, the sweet tyrant; and nothing new. For if a herdsman grieves so
much over a calf departed from the herd, and a shepherd over sheep
lacking by ten, and a bird over a brood which it left for a little
while, so that they, taking the pipes and going up to some lookout, fill
the reeds with their dejection, and call back the straying as rational;
and if they obey, they rejoice more than in all the other things
together, for which they did not care; and the blackbird flies down upon
its nest, and hovers over its nestlings, and warms them with its wings;
how great a thing would rational flocks be to a skillful shepherd, over
which he has hazarded something beforehand, since he adds even this to
his love?

C. For I fear the heavy wolves, lest, watching for our darkness, they
tear the flock with words rapacious and violent; =35.1232= since they
watch for the unseasonable moments, not having power openly. But I fear
the robbers and thieves, lest, climbing over by the court-yard, they
either plunder with shamelessness, or steal by deceit, so as to
sacrifice, and kill, and destroy, seizing seizures, devouring souls, as
one of the prophets said. But I fear the window, lest someone, finding
one of our yesterdays and recent ones unlocked, then entering as if his
own, should plot as an alien. For many and varied are the devices of him
who works such things; and no one is so much the architect of any of the
varied things as the adversary is a sophist of wickedness. But I fear
now also dogs, forcing themselves to be shepherds, and, the paradox,
bringing nothing into the pastoral office, or only the cutting of the
locks which they have practiced badly; who remained neither dogs nor
became shepherds, except to tear, and scatter, and undo another's
labor. Since destroying is always easier than preserving; and a man is
begotten with labor, says Job, and a ship is built, and a house is
established; but to kill, or break up, or burn, is for anyone who
wishes. So let not even now those who set the dogs upon the flock think
greatly of themselves, who could not name one sheep that they brought
in, or saved; for they did not learn to do good, having practiced
wickedness. But if they trouble the flock, this also a small storm does,
this also a slight disease, this also one beast falling upon it all at
once. Let then those who boast in their own shame stand still; and
ceasing from wickedness, if they can, let them worship, and fall down,
and weep before the Lord who made them, and be mingled with the flock,
as many as are not wholly incurable.

D. These things I, the timid shepherd and one who looks around, and
therefore reproaching security as negligence. For I am not one of those
shepherds who eat the milk, and clothe themselves with the wool, and
slaughter the fat, and work it with labor, or sell it off and say:
Blessed be the Lord, and we have become rich; shepherding themselves,
not the sheep, if you remember any of the prophetic voices, by which
those drive out the evil shepherds; but rather of those who can say with
Paul: Who is weak, and I am not =35.1233= weak? who is scandalized, and
I do not burn, or care? For I seek not yours, but you. And: I was made,
scorched with the heat of the day, and pressed by the frost of the
night; the voice of a patriarch shepherd, of the one whose flocks were
marked, and who studied to wash them at the troughs there. Thus then,
and for these reasons I stood beside you, and so things stand. But since
I have stood beside you, let us give account to one another of what we
have achieved in the meantime. Since it is good to think that we are
called to give account not only of word and deed, but also of every
season, and of the very hour of it, the momentary and most subtle. Do
you report to me your own labor; and I will set forth in the middle
what, coming together with myself in quiet, I philosophized.

E. What contemplation of the lofty things have you kept, whether
received from me, or brought in from among yourselves, whether
concerning theology, or concerning the other doctrines, which I set
before you many and often? For I seek not only the loan, but also the
interest; not only the talent, but also the labor; lest anyone, hiding
and burying the deposit, should even slander the depositor, as harsh and
desiring the things of others. And what action of the praised things, or
even of what the left hand does not know, have you borne fruit in, or so
that your light shines before men; so that the tree may be shown from
the fruit, and the teacher be known through the disciples, and someone
of those watching our things (and they are many, some with approval, and
some also curiously) may say that truly God is among you, not only being
proclaimed soundly, but also being worshiped? For as there is no
approved action without faith (since many practice the good even for the
sake of glory, and having such a nature), so also faith without works is
dead. And let no one deceive you with empty words, of those who readily
concede all things, except one, to be impious in the doctrines, and who
set a bad wage for a bad thing. Show then from the works the faith, the
fertility of your soil, if we did not sow in vain, if there is in you
any handful of strength to make flour, and worthy of storehouses, that
we may farm you more eagerly. Who bears fruit a hundredfold, who
sixtyfold, who last even thirtyfold? Or who conversely, advancing from
thirty to sixty (for we have this order also in the Gospels), finished
at the hundred? So that, advancing, like Isaac, he may become great,
going from strength to strength, and singing the songs of the Steps, and
setting ascents in his heart? =35.1236=

F. I seek the fruit abounding to your account. For the gain is yours,
not mine; and if also ours, because yours, the benefit turning back from
you to us, as in the rays of reflections. Whether you have nourished the
poor, lodged strangers, washed the feet of the saints, delighting the
belly that is being brought to nought (for let it be granted), and have
also delighted through the commandments; as there is no delight better
than this, nor more lasting for those willing to delight. Whether you
have refreshed with power some of those serving the altar, and being
honorably poor (for grant me to say even this), so that they may attend
the altar more without distraction, and, partaking of your things,
contribute in return their own? As it is shameful both for us to ask
these things, and for you not to provide them. But I did not admonish
these things that it might thus be in me (for it is better for me to die
rather, than that my boast should be emptied, and the Gospel become
without wages to me who have reaped fruit here. For to evangelize is of
necessity; but the disinterestedness, that it is without charge); but
that you may learn to do good to Christ, by doing good to someone even
of the least. Since, as all things that are mine, except sin, came to be
through me, so He also takes upon Himself the least things of mine;
whether you share shelter, or a garment, or visit one in prison, or see
one ailing; and the very least, even if you cool the tongue of one who
is suffering with only a cup of cold water, as the rich man toiling in
the flame desired the poor Lazarus, having had some of the luxury here,
and of overlooking Lazarus hungering and covered with sores, to need
Lazarus there and not to obtain him.

G. What then we require of you, these are; and I know that you will not
be ashamed, either being called to account by us, or by the last day,
into which all our things are gathered, according =35.1237= to the word
spoken: And I come to gather together your counsels and your works; and,
Behold a man, and his work, and his reward with him. And these are our
things, and what we bring you from the desert. Since Elijah also
delightfully philosophized on Carmel, and John in the desert, and Jesus
himself, assigning for the most part his works to the crowds, and his
prayers to leisure and to deserts. Legislating what? As I think, that
one should do something and also keep quiet, so as to hold intercourse
with God more purely, and draw back the mind a little from the things
that lead astray. For he did not himself need retirement (for he had not
into what he might be drawn in, being God, and filling all things), but
that we might learn both the season of action, and higher occupations.
What then are the things of my desert? For I wish, as a good merchant
and gaining from everywhere, to bring you something from here also worth
carrying.

H. I was walking thus alone, the day already declining. And the walk was
along the shore; for I am somehow accustomed always to dissolve my
labors with such relaxations; since not even a bowstring bears being
always drawn taut, but needs a little loosening of its sinews, if it is
to be stretched again, and not to become useless to the archer, nor
unprofitable in the season of use. I was walking then, and my feet were
borne along, and my sight held the sea. And it was a sight not pleasant,
though in other ways most sweet, when it grows purple with calm, and
plays upon the shores something sweet and gentle. But what then (for I
gladly utter also the words of Scripture)? A great wind blowing, it was
stirred up and heaved; and of the waves, as is wont to happen in such
motions, those rising from afar, and cresting little by little, then
diminishing, broke at the shores; and those falling upon the neighboring
rocks and being dashed back, were scattered into frothy and bare spray.
There pebbles, and seaweeds, and trumpet-shells, and the lightest of the
shellfish were pushed out and spat up; and some were even snatched back
again, the wave withdrawing. And the rocks were unmoved and unshaken,
none the less as though nothing disturbed them, except so far as being
struck by the waves.

I. From this I know I was benefited somewhat toward philosophy, and
(such as I am, straining everything toward myself, and especially if I
chance to have been made dizzy by something of the things happening
=35.1240=, which I have also now suffered) I received the sight not
incidentally; and the spectacle becomes for me a lesson. For was it not,
I said, the sea our life and the things of men (for much in it too the
briny and unstable), and the winds the temptations that fall upon us,
and as many things as are unexpected? Which seems to me the most
wonderful David also, having perceived, to say: Save me, O Lord, for the
waters are come in even to my soul; and, Rescue me out of the depths of
the waters; I came into the depths of the sea, and a storm overwhelmed
me. And of the tempted, some seemed to me to be swept away like the
lightest and breathless things, and not to hold out even a little
against the insults; for they have not in themselves firmness, and the
weight of a prudent reasoning, and one resisting the things that fall
upon them; and others to be rock, worthy of that rock on which we have
stood, and which we worship, as many as using philosophical reason, and
having climbed above the lowliness of the many, bear all things
unshaken and unagitated, and either laugh at, or pity those who are
shaken (the one from philosophy, the other from philanthropy); while
they themselves take shame in this: to despise the terrible things when
absent, or rather not even to think them terrible, but to be conquered
when present, and that, of what sort? of things that pass by, as it were
standing still; and to philosophize outside the season, but to appear
unphilosophical in the needs; as if one should think himself best of the
athletes, without even going down to the stadium; or a pilot of the
approved, boasting in his art in calms, but letting go the rudders in
storms.

J. And since I once came to stand in these discourses, I came also to
another image, happening very much to the present things. Perhaps you
will think me an old man and a teller of myths, if I make this known to
you also; but it must be made known, since I know Scripture too often
uses such things for a clearer narrative. There is a certain plant,
according to myth, which flourishes in being cut, and fights against the
iron; and, if we must speak paradoxically of a paradoxical thing, it
lives by death, and grows by cutting, and increases by being =35.1241=
consumed. Such then is the myth, and the self-will of the fabrication;
but it seems to me that the philosopher is clearly some such thing. He
flourishes in the passions, and makes the painful things the material of
virtue, and is adorned by opposites; neither lifted up by the right arms
of righteousness, nor bent by the left; but remaining ever the same, not
in the same things, or even being found more approved, like gold in a
furnace. But let us consider thus. Is he well-born? He will
counter-display the goodness of his blood, so as to flourish from both
sides, both being traced in genealogy and being seen. Of low birth as to
the statue and the clay (if clay differs at all greatly from clay)? He
will pay back in return the nobility that is understood, and that which
each fashions himself toward the worse or the better; and the other he
will strike out, such as is sown or written, as of no worth and
counterfeit. For there is a certain threefold race: one wrought from
above, in which we are all equally well-born, since we have become
according to the image of God; one coming from flesh, I know not if
anyone is well-born, this being established in corruption; and one known
from wickedness or virtue, of which we partake more or less, as much as,
I suppose, we keep or corrupt the image. This nobility will love he who
is truly wise and a philosopher. For the fourth race, that in writings
and commands, I shall then deem worthy of discourse, when I also accept
the beauty in colors, and am ashamed to have a monkey commanded to be a
lion.

K. Is he young? He will play the man against the passions, and will
enjoy this of his youth, not to suffer the things of the young, but to
show elderly wisdom in the body in its prime; and will rejoice in the
victory more than those crowned in Olympia; for he conquers in the
common theater, the inhabited world, and a victory unpurchased. Is he
inclined to old age? But will he not grow old also in soul? He will
receive the dissolution as a term of necessary freedom; he will gladly
pass over to the things hereafter, where there is neither child nor
elder, but all perfect in spiritual stature. Has he drawn beauty as his
lot? Beauty will vie with beauty, that of the soul with that of the
body. Has the flower passed by unassailed? He inclines toward himself,
and does not even know he is seen. Ill-favored to =35.1244= look upon?
But lovely in what is hidden, like a rose in its calyx, blooming in what
is not yet blooming and without scent, the most fragrant. Beautiful
beyond the sons of men? He gives not even the time for the outward to be
looked at, turning the beholder to the inner man. Is he in good health?
He will use his health for the best; he will admonish, chastise, speak
with boldness, keep vigil, sleep on the ground, fast, empty the matter,
contemplate the things on earth and in heaven, with all earnestness
study death. Is he infirm? He will fight; and if he is defeated, he will
conquer by taking not to fight again. Is he rich? He will philosophize
in growing poor, and share with the needy of his possessions, as steward
of the things of others; that the other may fare well by the sharing,
and he himself be gathered to God, having nothing except the cross and
the body. Is he poor? He will grow rich toward God, and in laughing to
scorn those who have, as ever acquiring and ever poor, needing the more,
and drinking that they may thirst more.

L. Does he hunger? He will be fed with the birds, whose life is unsown
and untilled; he will live with Elijah by Zarephath; the cruse of oil
shall not fail, and the jar of meal shall not diminish; the one shall
ever well up, and the other shall yield richly, that the hospitable
widow may be honored, and feed the one who feeds her. Will he thirst?
Wells and rivers are his drink, a drink that does not intoxicate, nor is
measured; if all fail through drought, he shall chance to be watered by
a winter-torrent. Will he shiver with cold? This also Paul, but for how
long? There is a garment even of rock; let Job persuade you, saying:
Having no shelter, they wrapped themselves in the rock. Consider for me
also the things that remain. Will he be reviled? He will conquer by not
reviling in return. Will he be persecuted? He will endure. Blasphemed?
He will entreat. Slandered? He will pray. Will he be struck on the right
cheek? He will offer also the other; if he had a third, he would offer
even this, that he may the more teach the striker long-suffering,
teaching by deed what he could not by word. Will he be reproached?
Christ also was this; he will be honored by the communion of the
passion. And if he hear Samaritan, and if he be accused of being mad,
with God he will accept all things. Many things will remain, even if he
suffer many: vinegar, gall, a crown of thorns, a reed scepter, a scarlet
cloak, a cross, nails, robbers crucified with him, passers-by =35.1245=
insulting. For one must have the more God, in bearing the more
dishonored.

M. Nothing more unconquerable than philosophy, nothing harder to take.
Everything will yield sooner than the philosopher. The wild ass of the
wilderness, says Job, free and unenslaved, laughing to scorn the crowded
city, not hearing the demand of the tax-collector. He is as a unicorn, a
self-governed animal. Will he consent to serve you? Will you bind him to
the manger? Will he be led under the yoke? When he has been shut out of
all the things on earth, wings have been fashioned for him as of an
eagle; he will return to the house of the one who stands over him, he
will lift himself up toward God. Let me say a summary: These two are
hard to master, God and angel; and the third is the philosopher,
bodiless in matter, in a body uncircumscribable, on earth heavenly, in
passions passionless, overcome by all things except purpose, conquering
by being conquered those who think they master. And since the discourse
has drawn for us the philosopher, from where I began, come, let us
consider by him our own things (for I think that I too have the Spirit
of God, and am vulnerable and capturable to any of these); so that if
the haters and warring ones find him conquered, may it at least be
forgiven to the attempt, if not to the choice; but if they find him
better and higher than those warring, either may they be delivered from
wickedness, or may they invent a newer path of injustice, as despising
the present one, and not, besides wickedness, be reproached also with
folly, as striving in vain, and not even knowing how to do the injustice
they strive for.

N. For what indeed will they grieve him, coming against everything? Let
us see how much a man may be wronged by men. Will they call him
uneducated? One wisdom I know, to fear God (for the beginning of wisdom
is the fear of the Lord; and the end of discourse: Hear all, fear God.
These things Solomon the wisest). Let them then show one without fear,
and let them conquer; but of the other wisdom, the one part I have
passed by, and the other I pray and hope to receive, trusting in the
Spirit. Will they indict poverty, my possession? For would that I might
strip off even these rags, that naked I might run through the thorns of
life; would that I might cast off also this heavy =35.1248= tunic as
quickly as may be, that I might go more lightly. Will they call him an
exile from his fatherland? How small do they think of us, truly wanton
men and haters of strangers! For I have, O men, a fatherland drawn round
about, in which every land is a fatherland, and none is foreign. But are
not you a stranger and sojourner? I do not praise your dwelling, if you
have it thus, lest you fall away from the true fatherland, into which we
must deposit the citizenship. But will you not reproach us also with old
age and sickness? Is not all this of matter and of nature, that you may
know something of my hidden things; something even reason has spent,
that I may boast a little. Nor are you, robust and flesh-fed, a pleasant
spectacle to me. Would that something of hoary age and paleness might
flourish also on you, that you might at least be believed to be prudent
and a philosopher.

O. What still? Will they cast him down from thrones? From what ones,
pray, which I mounted gladly, either now or before? And I count happy
those who mount them? But do you make these pleasant to me, mounting
them thus unworthily? Did not the things that happened before show my
judgment to you? Or were even those a crushing of some sort, and a
testing of desire? The practiced would of all things about others
suspect some, and speak their own. What then the breaking? What the
curses which we have publicly made against ourselves? What the tears,
and becoming to you almost pitiable, and hated because of the
opposition? Will they deprive him of the seat of honor? Which when, and
who of those of sound mind ever admired? But now even to flee is, as at
least it seems to me, the first mark of good sense; because of which all
our things are shaken and moved; because of which the ends of the
inhabited world are under suspicion, and in a war that is mute and has
not even a name; because of which we are in danger of being men, though
we have become of God, and of losing the great and new name. Would only
that there were neither seat of honor, nor preference of place, nor
tyrannical privilege! That we might be known from virtue alone. But now
this right and this left, and the middle, and the higher, and the lower,
and the walking before or walking with, has made many of our breakings
in vain, and has driven many into the pit, and led away into the country
of the kids; not of the lower only, but now also of the shepherds, who
being teachers of Israel ignored these things.

P. Will they shut him from altars? But I know another altar, of which
the things now seen are types; upon which no chisel has mounted, nor
hand, nor was iron heard, or anything of craftsmen and =35.1249= varied
arts, but the whole work of the mind, and the ascent is by
contemplation. To this I will present myself, to this I will sacrifice
acceptable things, a sacrifice, and an offering, and burnt-offerings,
better than those now brought, as much as truth is better than shadow;
concerning which the great David seems to me also to philosophize,
saying: And I will go in to the altar of God, to God who makes glad my
spiritual youth. From this altar will not lead me away anyone who
wishes. Will they banish him from the city? But will they not also
banish him from the one laid above? Let those who hate us, and have
truly made war, accomplish this; but so long as they cannot do this,
they cast drops, or strike with breezes, or play with dreams; thus I see
their war. Will they take away his money? Of what? If of what is mine,
let them clip also the wings, with which I am not furnished; but if of
the Church's, this is that for which is all the war; for which the thief
envies the money-box, and betrays God for thirty pieces of silver, the
most terrible thing. For of so much is worthy not the betrayed, but the
betrayer.

Q. Will they shut him out of houses? Will they cut off luxuries? Will
they estrange friends? For very much, as you see, have we declined of
most things, though they were inviting (for we will not be unthankful);
but if we declined, it was in sparing rather than in rejecting. And the
cause: a certain house has given us rest, pious and godly, and such as
the Shunammite's for Elisha, in body kinsmen, in spirit kinsmen, in all
things honorable; at whose house also this people has settled, still
stealing the persecuted piety, not without fear, nor without danger. May
the Lord measure back to him in the day of recompense! And if it is
luxury that we pursue, let those who hate us make their luxury against
us; for I will curse myself with nothing greater. And of friends, some,
I know well, will flee from us even when not suffering ill (for it has
made them fear to be wronged together, and to ache together); but of the
others we have already been exercised, and, being looked down upon, to
bear the contempt. For of friends and neighbors, some came near openly
from opposite and stood; and others, most humane! stood from afar, and
in this night all were scandalized. Peter all but denied me; and perhaps
he does not even weep bitterly, to heal his sin. =35.1252=

R. I alone daring, and full of boldness, as it seems; alone hopeful in
the fearsome things, alone enduring, and set forth publicly, and
despised in private, and made known to East and West by being warred
upon. O the desperation! If a camp be arrayed against me, my heart will
not fear; if war rise up against me, in this I hope. So far am I from
thinking anything of the present things terrible, that, leaving my own
case, I mourn those who caused grief. Members of Christ once, members
precious to me, even if now corrupted, members of this flock, which you
betrayed almost before it was gathered. How have you been torn in pieces
and torn in pieces, like calves loosed from their bonds? How have you
raised altar against altar? How have you suddenly become a desolation?
How have you yourselves been put to death by the cutting, and made us to
ache? How have you used the simplicity of a shepherd for the dissolution
of the flock? For I do not blame those for inexperience, but I will
accuse you of wickedness. Who will help thee in thy corruption, Israel?
What medicine shall I find that heals the whole wound? What bandage? How
shall I join the things sundered? With what tears, what words, what
prayers shall I treat the fracture? Or perhaps in this manner:

S. O holy Trinity, and worshiped, and perfect, and rightly gathered
together and reverenced by us, thine is the work, thine the success. May
thou restore these again to us, having stood apart so much as to be
taught by the separation toward concord; and to us for the labors here
may thou give the heavenly things and the unwavering. And of these the
first and greatest is, to be illumined by thee more perfectly and more
purely: how the same is apprehended both as Monad and found as Trinity;
how the unbegotten, and the begotten, and the proceeding, one nature,
three properties, One God, who is over all, and through all, and in all;
neither set over, nor transferred, nor diminished, nor divided; now
apprehended, now sought, and at some time perhaps to be apprehended as
far as thou art, by those who have sought well here through life and
contemplation; to whom all glory, honor, might unto the ages. Amen.
