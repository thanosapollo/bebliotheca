* On that saying, The Son can do nothing
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of Amphilochius, bishop of Iconium <from the homily on "The Son can do
nothing of himself">
... How do you understand "The Son can do nothing of himself"? For you
take "he cannot" in two ways: both according to common usage and according
to the divine Scripture. For "he cannot" is said both of things that are
impossible and of things that are not admissible. But not being able is one
thing, and not being admissible is another. Concerning things that are
impossible Paul speaks thus: "It is impossible that the blood of bulls and
goats should take away sins"; for the blood of an irrational creature does
not cleanse the passions of a rational soul. And concerning things that are
not admissible, the apostles in the Acts: "We cannot," they say, "but speak
the things which we have seen and heard." And yet they were able not to
speak, but they could not bear to keep silence, lest they should seem to
deny the grace of the one who works wonders. Tell us, then, here, in which
sense you take "The Son can do nothing of himself": by condemning him of
inability, or by taking away the motions of his self-determining will,
because whatever he wills, he can do. But what do you say? He does not act
of himself, but, fulfilling a command of God, he works those things toward
which the nod of the one who commanded looks? And how is this not the last
degree of folly to say? For if he does nothing at all of himself, then
neither did he fulfill the whole economy by will, nor did he take up our
flesh by compassion toward us, nor did he of free friendship accept the
death on our behalf, but he endured all things by force, driven on by
necessity. But if it is rash and blasphemous and beyond all impiety to say
this, tell us according to what account you take "The Son cannot do
anything of himself." For that you say, even if you do not wish it, that he
is able to do whatever he wills, driven by the truth, is clear. But why do
I weave superfluous words? For the continuation of the saying solves the
question. For he did not say that "He can do nothing of himself, unless the
Father command something," but "unless he see the Father doing"; and if he
see him doing something, these things the Son likewise does. Therefore he
does not do what the Father commands as one fulfilling a ministry, but as
like, as equal in power, as being of the same divine nature: whatever he
sees the Father doing, these things the Son likewise does. After these
things, it says, there was a feast of the Jews, and Jesus went up to
Jerusalem. And lest they ascribe the whole to the waters, the waters did
not heal before an angel, standing over them, troubled them, so that they
might learn that neither do the waters of baptism by themselves benefit the
soul until the Angel of Great Counsel, having put on a human body, come to
the baptism. Since, then, there were many sick, there also lay a paralytic
man who had been in his infirmity thirty-eight years, whom Jesus, seeing,
asks: "Do you wish to become whole?" See his lack of arrogance: he did not
say, "Do you wish that I heal you?" for he did not wish to speak
boastfully, nor to proclaim himself before the miracles. But he says: "I
wish, but I have no man"; for where there is no friend, there is no man
anywhere. For this reason I also ask, not that I may learn (for who among
the sick does not wish to be made whole?), but that you may show the
cruelty of your good citizens, that they did not even give you a hand to
throw you into the spring, which even an enemy, if asked, would have
granted. Since, then, until now you have had no man to put you into the
pool, behold, he who became man for your sake heals your affliction even
without waters: "Rise, take up your bed, and go." And he does not simply
command him to carry the bed; for what he did in the case of the loaves,
this he does here also. For just as there he commands the remnants of the
loaves to be carried, so here too, in order to make the miracle plain, and
to free the healing from Jewish suspicion, and to show that he is no
deceiver, but a savior and the common physician of nature, he commands him
to carry the bed. The Jews therefore said: "It is the Sabbath; it is not
lawful for you to take up your pallet." But he answers: "He who made me
whole, that one said to me, Take up your pallet and walk." They therefore
said to him: "And who is he?" But the one who had been healed did not know.
When, then, they learned that it was Jesus who had made him whole, they
persecuted him in order to kill him. What then does Christ do? He brings
forward the Father, saying: "My Father works until now, and I work." For
just as, he says, my Father is not subject to bounds, does not serve
legislations, nor waits for commands, but by his own will does whatever he
wishes, so I too work all things by sovereign authority equally with the
Father. But for this they sought all the more to kill him, because he not
only broke the Sabbath, but also called God his own Father, making himself
equal with God. But while they were raging and going mad, what does Christ
do? "Amen I say to you, the Son can do nothing of himself, unless he see
the Father doing something; for whatever that one does, these things the
Son likewise does." Not because he creates some things, while I work others
by imitation. For if this were so, there would have to be two heavens, two
suns, two seas, two races of men, and a double cosmos, one of the Father,
and one of mine. For it would be necessary, as with painters the lines and
with teachers the letters are prescribed, that the Father's creation should
have been prescribed, in imitation of which I worked. If, then, it is not
so, Master, why do you say, "The Son can do nothing of himself, unless he
see the Father doing something"? In order that I may slacken the madness of
the Jews who think that I legislate against the Father. For since they
accuse me concerning the breaking of the Sabbath, I said, "My Father works
until now, and I work," wishing to show the equal honor from both sides.
But those who are fools in all things, thinking that I was fortifying works
against works and introducing a will against a will, were moved to envy.
Hence, correcting their mind, I was saying, "The Son can do nothing of
himself, unless he see the Father doing something," instead of: Why do you
untimely examiners of the Sabbath rage at me, because I break the Sabbath?
But I do not take a contrary course against the Father, for it is not that
he wishes to establish the Sabbath and I to break it. I do not fight
against him, I do not rebel against his will, it is not that he wills one
thing and I wish other things: what he wills I will, what he wishes I wish,
what he does I do. For thus I do nothing apart from him, I will nothing,
because, setting the statement into impossibility, I say: "Amen I say, the
Son can do nothing of himself"; for I do not have a will separated and cut
off from the Father. For since the nature is one, and the will is one, the
result must necessarily be one. The Son therefore can do nothing of
himself, for he cannot do anything without the Father, having him wholly in
himself; for "I am in the Father and the Father in me." For if the works of
soul and body are indistinguishable because of the union, although the
nature is divided, the body being one thing, mortal, and the immortal soul
another, how can the works of me and of the Father be distinguished? Where
counsel and word and knowledge and wisdom and nature and godhead are one,
the Son therefore can do nothing of himself, because he is not separated
from the Father, unless he see the Father doing something, that is, unless
he see the Father willing. For having, as of one and the same nature, a
concurrent will, I bring the things that come to be to completion. For if I
were fulfilling a bodily working, the construction of what comes to be
would necessarily be divided. But since will is sufficient for the
production of things that are not, therefore the creation of all things
also knows the Father and the Son as creator, the Spirit not being thrust
out. For whatever that one, the Father, does, these things the Son likewise
does: these things, and not things of such a kind; for I do not do other
things by imitation, but I work the same things which the Father also
works. Hence, assigning even my words to him, I was saying: "The words
which I speak to you I do not speak of myself; but the Father who remains
in me does the works." And I assigned the works and the words to him, not
that I might show that I fulfill a ministry, but that I might show that I
am so united to the Father by nature that I have neither word nor work
without him. Hence he added, saying: "Believe me that I am in the Father
and the Father in me; but if not, believe even because of the works." Just
as, then, there, while working, I was saying that "The Father who remains
in me does the works," so here also I say that "The works which that one
does, these the Son likewise does": these, and not things of such a kind.
Does then "The Son can do nothing of himself" <nothing> take away my
freedom? Understand: for as many things as I will to do I am able also of
myself, but I do not will it; for if the Father loves the Son and shows him
all things that he himself does, how should I escape the notice of him who
does all things through me? And he does through me not as through a
subordinate; for I do not serve as underling but I work together, not
fulfilling a ministry but working as one who has sovereignty. For just as
the Father raises the dead and gives life, having power concurrent with the
will, so I do not raise the dead as fulfilling a ministry, but by power,
sovereignty, authority, will, because those whom I raise I also judge. For
the Father judges no one; but he does not judge because of inability, but
because he has given all judgment to the Son; for through him by whom he
worked, through him he also judges. And he does all things for this reason,
that all may honor the Son even as they honor the Father. For then he
especially perceives honor, when he sees the Son who is from him honored in
a manner befitting God. He who does not honor the Son does not honor the
Father who sent him, for he does not receive honor upon the dishonor of the
Son. For he sent me on account of the flesh, not that I might be insulted,
but that I might be glorified. Hence, since you were disposed ungratefully
toward me, for this reason even through the very flesh that was sent I was
saying to him: "Father, glorify your Son." You glorify the one whom these
insult; you also glorify the flesh set aside by them, that they may learn
that you will also, as Father, glorify without distinction the man from
Mary because of his communion with your only-begotten Son, giving now to
the temple assumed by me that glory which I myself had from nature with you
before the ages ...
