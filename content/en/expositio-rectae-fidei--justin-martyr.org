What I am saying would become clearer. One who inquires concerning the
existence of Adam, how he was brought into being, will find that he is not
begotten, for he is not from some other man, but was formed by the divine
hand. But the forming shows the manner of existence; for it signifies how he
came to be. Likewise again the manner of existence characterizes the forming;
for it shows in the same way that he existed having been formed. But if you
should seek his essence, according to which he is joined in communion with
those from him, you will find that the underlying subject is man. As therefore
the fashioning shows the manner of existence, and the manner of existence
characterizes the forming, and the account of the essence shows that the
underlying subject is man, so we shall find it in the case of God and Father.
For if you should seek the manner of his existence, seeing that he has been
begotten from no other, you will call him unbegotten; but if you should
examine the appellation unbegotten, you will find it interpreting the manner
of existence. And if you should also wish to know the essence itself,
according to which he is joined in communion with Son and Spirit, you will
indicate it by the name God. So that the unbegotten and the manner of
existence are indicative of one another, while God is declarative of the
essence. For as Adam, although he did not admit generation, is joined in
communion with those begotten from him according to the sameness of essence,
so no argument will be able to tear apart the common essence of the Father
toward the Son and the Spirit because of the unbegotten. So that the
unbegotten and the begotten and the proceeding are not declarative of essence,
but are significant of the hypostases; for they are sufficient for us to
distinguish the persons and to show the hypostasis of Father and Son and Holy
Spirit in its particularity. For just as, when unbegotten is spoken, a seal as
it were at once marks off for us the hypostasis of the Father, and again as
some sign, when we hear the appellation of the begotten, we receive the
thought of the Son, and again through the signification of the proceeding we
are instructed in the proper person of the Spirit. And these things suffice
for proof that the unbegotten and begotten and proceeding do not show the
essence itself, but are definitive of the hypostases, besides also indicating
the manner of existence.

It would remain to show concerning the essence how it is one of Father and Son
and Holy Spirit. We see then in common custom that the things begotten are of
the same essence as those who beget. Or rather we must begin from above, so
that some inquiry falling into the midst may not interrupt the continuity of
the argument. And first let us divide the things that are. For we shall find
that all things are divided into created and uncreated; for if anything is
among the things that are, it is either an uncreated nature or a created one.
But the uncreated is also lordly and free from every necessity, while the
created is servile and follows lordly laws; and the

former both does and is able to do, according to authority, whatever it may
will, while the latter both is able and fulfills only the ministry which it
has received from the Godhead. The division being thus, we must set forth the
divine voices in the midst and consider with accuracy to which they teach us
to rank the Son and the Spirit; for toward the nurslings of the Church we
must not direct the divine things by human reasonings, but make the
exposition of the words according to the will of the teaching of the Spirit.
And first let David teach us. For this man, having composed a hymn to God
from the whole of creation, then having treated of all the things in heaven,
and having enumerated all the powers in it, and likewise having made the
narration concerning earth and all the things in it, does not include the Son
and the Spirit in this doxology, as being clearly yoked together with the
divine nature; for he would not, if he knew them to be of created essence,
have left these alone unnamed, of whom first and most of all the other powers
he would reasonably have made mention. And likewise also the blessed Paul,
being possessed by a divine fire and showing the burning of his love toward
God and bearing witness to the firmness of his affection, speaks thus: "For I
am persuaded that neither life nor world nor death, neither angels nor powers
nor principalities, neither things present nor things to come, neither height
nor depth nor any other creation will be able to separate us from the love of
God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord." For likewise he himself also, having
enumerated both world and life and death, angels and powers and
principalities, and things present and things to come, height and depth,
since he found nothing left remaining to the created nature, and still
holding to the impulse of crying out and bearing witness, filled up the word
by adding something hyperbolic, bringing on another creation. Would he then,
having indicated even by the hyperbole of what was said his immovable longing
toward God, if he knew the Son and the Spirit to be of created essence, not
have made narration of them together with the others? But that the Son and
the Spirit have not been yoked with created nature, one must know from these
and such things; and it was possible to set forth also some other such
testimonies in great multitude, but since the word is toward sons of the
Church, and our aim is to speak through brief things, I think even the things
that have been said suffice.

It would remain to show that the Son and the Spirit have been ranked with the
divine nature. And first I will mention the most timely. Our Lord Jesus Christ
then, after the resurrection from the dead, being about to make the ascent
into the heavens, taught the apostles the discipling of the nations and the
teaching of baptism: "Go, make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in
the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."

And writing to the Corinthians, the blessed Paul, at the end of the epistle,
setting as it were some seal upon the teaching, adds: "The grace of our Lord
Jesus Christ and the love of God and Father and the communion of the Holy
Spirit be with you all." And again to the Ephesians he speaks thus: "Christ
Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone, in whom the whole building, being
fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord, in whom you also are
being built together into a dwelling-place of God in the Spirit." You see
how, teaching the building that is in Christ, through which we become a
temple of the Lord, according to "I will dwell in them and walk among them
and I will be their God," he introduces the three persons to us conjointly.
For through such teaching he instructed that Christ and God and Spirit, the
one Godhead, dwell in us according to energy, in those who are counted worthy
of grace. And this is clear from what he also says in another place: "For
this cause I bow my knees to the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, from whom
every family in heaven and on earth is named, that he may give you according
to the riches of his glory to be strengthened with power through his Spirit
in the inner man, that Christ may dwell." For see, again making mention of a
divine indwelling he is shown encompassing Father and Son and Holy Spirit.
And everywhere in the teaching he appears ranking the three persons together.
For writing to the Corinthians in a second epistle he speaks thus: "Now he
who establishes us with you in Christ and has anointed us is God, and has
sealed us and given the earnest of the Spirit in our hearts"; clearly here
too having yoked both Father (and God) and Christ the Son and Holy Spirit in
the teaching. And again to the Galatians: "But because you are sons, God sent
forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying Abba, Father"; likewise
again delivering to us conjointly the thought concerning Father and Son and
Spirit. And see how he sets the marks of the extreme conjunction. For he did
not simply say: "God sent forth the Spirit," but: "of his Son"; now joining
it to the Son, and now assigning it to the Father in the words: "Now you did
not receive the spirit of the world, but the Spirit that is from God and
Father"; and again calling it the Spirit of his Son, the Spirit of truth,
since he himself is the truth, and again teaching that it is of the Father:
"for it proceeds from the Father." And with the divine scripture simply
confirming the thought for us through all things, you possess an inseparable
conception concerning Father and Son and Holy Spirit.

But neither has the divine oracle taught us that the energy of Son and Spirit
in the creation of the whole is separated from the Father. And of this let
David be your teacher, speaking somehow thus: "And you, Lord, in the beginning
laid the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are works of your hands";
having

included through the signification of Lord both the Son and the Spirit, and
no less, because of the ungrateful, having used also the division according
to person in the words: "By the word of the Lord the heavens were made firm,
and by the spirit of his mouth all their power." But neither have we learned
from the divine scripture that the Son and the Spirit are lessened from the
authority of the Father; and how, hear the letter: "But our God," it says,
"is in heaven and on earth; he has done all things whatsoever he willed."
This David says concerning the Father. The Son, showing this authority in the
case of the leper: "I will," he says, "be cleansed!" The blessed Paul,
bearing witness of this also to the Holy Spirit, writes such things: "But all
these things the one and the same Spirit works, distributing to each one
privately as he wills." If then in the discipling of the world, and in the
teaching of baptism, and further also in the account of creation and in the
power of authority, the one name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit has been
delivered to us conjointly, what argument will take away the Son and the
Spirit from being of the divine and blessed essence?

And let no one attack us, as having promised some things and gone through
others, if, having announced that we would show the sameness of essence, we
have furnished the proofs that the Son and the Spirit have been ranked with
the Father. For the account of the ranking presents nothing other than the
sameness of essence of Father and Son and Holy Spirit, and let the one who
contradicts take up the division for me and consider it with accuracy; for he
will find there the account of the essence fulfilled in the order of the
conjunction. From above then the things that are had been divided by us into
two, into uncreated and created nature; and of the uncreated the tokens

had been confessed by us, that it is lordly and free from every necessity, and
further both does and is able to do, according to authority, whatever it may
will, and of the created, that it is servile by antithesis and follows lordly
laws, and further both is able and fulfills only the ministry which it has
received from the Godhead. The division being thus, confirming that there is
nothing in between Godhead and creation, everything that has been changed from
creation has clearly not been changed from the divine. If then through many
things it was shown by us that the Son and the Spirit differ from creation,
since they have not been numbered with any created thing, but have been yoked
with the Father everywhere, how is it not of extreme mindlessness not to think
them of the uncreated essence? For of the two the one will necessarily fall
away; for one must either, showing them to be of the created, mark them off
from the uncreated, or, showing them of the uncreated, necessarily separate
them from the created. But they have appeared both separated from the created
and yoked with the uncreated; and it was confessed that there is nothing in
between these. It would remain that they share the essence with that with
which they were also yoked everywhere. For if, as has been said (for it is
good to take it up again toward a more complete proof), both in the

discipling of the world in Christ, and in the teaching of baptism, and
further also in the divine teaching and in the creation of the whole, and not
only so but also in the sovereignty according to authority, like and the same
things have been delivered concerning Father and Son and Holy Spirit, who is
so clumsy as to dispute concerning their communion with one another according
to essence? It is fitting then to confess one God, known in Father and Son
and Holy Spirit, as Father and Son and Holy Spirit knowing the hypostases of
the one Godhead, and as God understanding the common according to essence of
the hypostases. For a monad is thought in a triad, and a triad is known in a
monad.

And how this is, I would neither wish to keep asking another, nor would I
myself be able to persuade myself to dare the words concerning the ineffable
things with a clay tongue and a defiling bit of flesh. For even if a pure mind
is seated in us, through which we often grasp by reason the things above us,
yet being weighed down by the yoked flesh it is too weak for the clear
apprehension of the greater things, since the earthly tent burdens a mind of
many cares. In no way then would it be possible for us who are humans to
arrive at that first and blessed essence. And why do I speak of the divine
essence! But not even of the things mystically accomplished concerning it. For
nothing of the divine things is clear to humans, as some wise man of the
Greeks uttered; and I receive what was said as true. For when I hear Paul, the
vessel of election, who has entered the third heaven, who has heard
unspeakable words which it is not lawful to speak out with human tongues, who
has in himself the speaker, the supplier of the words, bearing witness in
himself to a partial knowledge and saying: "Now I know in part, but then I
shall know fully even as I have also been fully known," and again: "For we
know in part and we prophesy in part"; how shall I trust that there is perfect
knowledge of the divine things for those who are humans? For if for those who
have arrived at the measure of Paul some dim and partial knowledge has come to
be (for to see through a mirror and in a riddle hints at the dim), who is so
bold toward madness as to bear witness to himself of a perfect knowledge of
the divine things? And immediately we too, knowing the impassable character of
the ineffable things, utter the things of David toward the God of the whole:
"Your knowledge was made wonderful from me; it was strengthened, I shall not
be able toward it." But these things it is holy both to say and more holy to
think; for to yield to the divine things would be of a pious and temperate
man.

Therefore as much of the divine things as is toward a secure inquiry, and as
much as is toward pious worship, we think in a God-befitting way. For it is
not, since the divine is wholly incomprehensible, for this reason somehow
altogether fitting not to seek concerning it at all, but to spend the time of
life in ease; but according to the measure of knowledge portioned to each by
the Lord to make the examination laboriously, being accurately persuaded that
it is incomprehensible,

and joining ourselves to him through contemplation as far as we have room.
Thus then we think that the Son has been begotten from the Father, as light
that has shone forth from light. For the image is sufficient to present both
the co-eternity and the sameness of essence and the impassibility of the
generation. For if he was shone forth, he subsisted timelessly together with
the one who shone. For by what interval of time would a shining-forth of
light be cut? And if light from light, it would show the sameness with that
from which it has also been begotten. And if again that which has been
begotten is also light, the generation too would be impassible. For the
shining-forth does not come to be according to a cutting or a flowing or a
standing-apart of the light, but comes forth impassibly from the essence
itself. And let us hold the same knowledge also concerning the Holy Spirit,
that, as the Son is from the Father, so also the Spirit; except that it will
differ by the manner of existence. For the one, light from light, shone forth
begottenly, while the other, itself also light from light, yet not begottenly
but proceeding, came forth; thus co-eternal with the Father, thus the same in
essence, thus having proceeded from there impassibly. Thus in the triad we
think the monad, and in the monad we know the triad. Having had room for
these things and having received this measure from the Lord of knowledge, we
set forth what has been apprehended to the sons of the Church, exhorting them
to think thus, until they receive a more perfect shining-forth of knowledge,
since indeed it is temperate to attend with care to the things set forth by
us. For we have not imagined some clever or overblown thing or one having a
demonstration of boastfulness, but having collected as far as we were able
what is rather pious and fitting to the true knowledge we have set forth the
knowledge of the one Godhead in three perfect hypostases. And glorifying thus
concerning the Holy Trinity let us approach by the word to the grace of the
Word from the economy. For ineffable also is the account of the economy; but
this too we must examine again according to our power.

When the Word understood that the refashioning of his own formed creatures
needed to come to be, and that the debt of the punishment of Adam, which he
owed having transgressed, needed to be paid, then indeed, not departing from
the heavens, he came down to us; for the descent was not of a body, but a will
of divine energy. And using a virgin in the midst, descended from a Davidic
race because of the promises toward him, for the need of the economy, and
entering her womb as some divine seed, he forms a temple for himself, the
perfect man, taking a certain part of her nature and substantiating it into
the forming of the temple. And having put this on according to an extreme
union, coming forth God together and man, he thus fulfilled the economy
concerning us. Since Adam, having sinned, subjected the race to death and made
the whole nature liable to the debt, the Son, being God and man, calls back
the transgression of Adam. And as man he lives blamelessly and accepts a
voluntary death, through the extreme citizenship

wiping out the transgression, and through the death not owed making void the
one that was owed; and as God he both raises what was dissolved and utterly
destroys death itself. One then is the Son, both the one dissolved and the
one who raised what was dissolved; for as man he was dissolved, and as God he
raised. When therefore you hear the contrary voices concerning the one Son,
distribute the things said correspondingly to the natures, assigning if
something is great and divine to the divine nature, and reckoning if
something is small and human to the human nature. For thus you will both
escape the discord of the voices, each nature receiving the things that
belong to it, and you will confess the one Son both before all ages and
recent according to the divine scriptures.

And let no one ask me the manner of the union. For I shall not be ashamed,
confessing the ignorance; on the contrary I shall even rather boast, believing
the ineffable things and being an initiate of these things of which both word
and mind are too weak for the apprehension. So let him not hope to learn
anything clear concerning this either from me or from another. But if you wish
to know as much as our mind had room for concerning this, there is no envy
toward children of the Church, yet having marked so much upon the things said,
that those who attempt the exposition have need of the inclination and
cooperation from above, I go on to the apprehension according to power. Some
then, having thought the union as of soul toward body, have thus given it out;
and the example is even fitting, if not in all respects, at least in some. For
as the man is one, yet has in himself two different natures, and according to
one he reasons, and according to another he works what was reasoned (for
having reasoned with an intelligent soul, if it so happen, the construction of
the ship, he brings what was thought to completion with hands), so the Son,
being one and two natures, according to one worked the divine signs, and
according to the other accepted the lowly things. For as from the Father and
God, he works the wonders, and as from a virgin and man, he endured the cross
and the passion and the like things naturally, willing it. If someone should
accept the things of the example as far as this, the image is well; but if he
should compare the whole with the whole example, the things of the difference
will appear. For the man, even if he shows in himself double natures, is not
two natures, but from the two; for as the body is composed of fire and air,
water and earth, you would no longer say that the body is fire nor air or some
of the others (for it is not the same as the things from which it is, since
the account also of what is composed and of the things put together is
different), so the man, even if he is from soul and body, is other besides the
things from which he is. And here you might learn more clearly what I say. We
build the house from different materials; but no one would say that the house
is the same as the materials from which it is. For the stones and the timbers
and the rest are not simply the house; for if this were so, even before the
building one would justly

call the materials themselves, being divided, a house. But such a fitting of
the materials toward one another is accustomed to complete the house for us.
Indeed when the house has been dissolved the materials remain similarly,
holding their own account, but we say that the house has perished, which such
a yoking of the materials completed. So in the case of the man. Even if he
exists from soul and body, he would not be the same as the things from which
he is, but other, so that the man is a third other thing completed from the
very conjunction of soul toward body. And this is clear from the fact that,
when the separation of these has come to be, the body remains thus, saving
its own account (for it is extended in three ways, even if it is dead), and
the soul again similarly happens to be rational, even if it is separated; but
the man has perished, whom the yoking of both completed. But Christ was not
completed as Christ from Godhead and manhood, being another besides the two,
but he happens to be both, God and man, being thought God by the energy of
the marvels, and being shown man by the like-suffering of the nature.
Besides, the soul also, being preoccupied by many of the passions of the
body, both often suffers beforehand the body, and suffers with it
continually, appearing often in agony at the cutting of the body and being
altered before the passion of the body and after the cutting no less
receiving the things of the pain passibly. Which in the case of the Godhead
of Christ none of the pious would dare to say or to accept. So that the
example of the man is to be accepted in some respect, and in the rest to be
fled.

But we both long ago confessed beforehand that we do not know the clear
apprehension of this truth, and now no less we acknowledge, confessing the
knowledge according to the measure given to us, both as far as we hasten to
raise the things of the example to a more pious clear account, and we wish to
liken the union not to some small image and one of the cheap things, but to a
great one and one fitting to the generation from the Father. Since therefore
the Word has come into the world as light, having shone forth from the
uncaused light, I wish the example of the union also to be light. Let the Word
then be thought to be the primordial light, which by a first voice of God has
been generated through the Word himself, and the human body the solar body, to
which according to an ineffable account the Word was united. And do not think
for me that the sun has become another light besides the former; for it is not
as if the first light was lacking toward the illumination of the whole that
the sun has come to be, filling up what was lacking; for the craftsman is not
such as neither to have foreseen the perfect nor to have crafted the beam
toward the need provided from it. One then is the primordial light, and the
solar body was prepared for this, upon which the light, gathered together, is
carried, having been poured out everywhere at the beginning. This also
fulfills blamelessly for us the course of the hours of the day through the
body. For if

it had not been bound to the body, but had been poured out whole in the whole
air, it would not have defined for us even the measures of the day, nor would
it have presented an ordered motion and the one who made. But if you say to
me that even before the generation of the sun day and night came to be, first
you will confess similarly that the nature of the light sufficed to
illuminate all things, and consequently then, having sought the cause of the
generation of the sun, you will find it being no other than the one just now
said by us. For of old the light, poured out whole through the whole air,
neither ran through an even motion nor defined the measures of the day, but
being contracted it gave passage to the night. So that the light is one
thing, and the solar body that received this is another. The light then being
thus toward the solar body, consider the rest of you with accuracy the
account. For as after the union of the first-born light toward the solar body
no one would divide them from one another, nor would he call the one sun
particularly, and the other again light separately, but one sun the light is
called together with the body, so in the case of the true light and the holy
body no one would say after the union that the one is separately Son, the
divine Word, and the other again Son, the man, but he will think both one and
the same, as one light and one sun both the received light and the receiving
body. Again as there is one light and one sun, but two natures, the one of
light, the other of solar body, so here too one Son and Lord and Christ and
Only-begotten, but two natures, the one above us, the other ours. And again
as in the case of the light no one would separate the energy from its
receptive body, but dividing by the account he would know the nature whose
own the energy is, so in the case of the one only-begotten Son of God no one
would separate every energy from the one sonship, but he would know by the
account the nature whose own the thing that comes to be is. Thus we, having
made the example of the divine union so that we might take refuge in a more
pious thought, even if we have not arrived at the truth itself once for all,
at least having collected an image sufficient for us toward a pious inquiry.
If any of these things should seem to you to be somehow nearer the truth,
hymn the one who has given the measure of knowledge; and if you have learned
something more pious from another, hymn him again, the guardian; for he
himself, being of old, has worked into another. Having then sufficiently
according to power set forth the right confession, having said farewell to
the sons of the Church, and having confessed thanks to the one who has given
the supply of the word, having laid the word to rest in the manner of life
according to quiet, we shall live for the rest.

But I see some calling the word back again and pointing out a stadium of
another inquiry and forcing us to run again, perhaps somewhere even testing
whether it has not given out in the former double-courses. And the word
imitates a spring, which being drawn more continually pours forth the streams
more clearly. Whole

it has bent toward the course and has all but already peered out of the doors
and awaits the signal of the starter; and for you, wishing to indicate the
inquiry, it is ready to urge on and to throw down the unbelief and to shut in
the God-fighting tongues and to distinguish what one must seek concerning the
divine things, and what believe, and to be proclaimed victor by the law of
the course. How then, he says, is the Word everywhere according to essence
and how in his own temple? For if as in all things, there too, the temple
will have nothing more than all things. And where shall we put "In whom
dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily"? But if one should grant that
there is more in the temple, he is not present to all things according to
essence, which is proper to God.

A clear proof of unbelief is to say how in the case of God. For how is he
creator of heaven, how of earth and sea, of air and plants and of all the
animals and of you yourself, who seek all things concerning God with accuracy?
But you will altogether say that by an abundance of power he brought all
things forth. Was then the power of God present to the things that came to be
accidentally or according to essence? If then accidentally, as to things that
are, even before they came to be, since the accidental is not naturally by
itself, but exists in some things already underlying. But if this is
ridiculous, it remains that the power is present to all according to essence.
Did then, since the power was present according to essence to all the things
that came to be, the one called temple have nothing more than they? This
argument is without resource, and that one is without resource, and faith is
the solution of both. You saw how the word, having rushed, threw down the
unbelief; see the God-fighting tongues being shut in.

For tell us, you who pretend to honor Christianity, you who both seek and put
forward such things toward a destruction of the two natures, you who busy
yourselves with the things of mixture and confusion and of the change from
body into Godhead and such further-inquiries, you who sometimes say that the
Word has become flesh, and sometimes that the flesh has been substantiated
into Word, and through such perversions of your mind are shown to think not
even anything; tell us then, how the Word, having become flesh, did not leave
the heavens. You will altogether say that remaining God he thus became. Tell
us then again, how remaining he became. For if he remained what he was, how
has he become what he was not? And if he has become what he was not, how did
he remain what he was? You are at a loss for the solution; be at a loss also
for the manner of the union. But you believe that remaining he became; believe
also that the Word is present everywhere according to essence and exists in
his own temple according to an exceptional account. Let us ask again, how
after the union the body was deified. Did it fall over into an essence of
Godhead, or did the body remain a body of man, yet remain incorruptible and
immortal through the union of the Word? But if this, the body remains a body,
if indeed God is not a body, and it has partaken of divine dignity, not of
nature, by the good-pleasure of the Word. But if the Word through the union

changed the body into his own essence, let us ask again, how the body was
changed into the essence of the Word. Did it, having been changed into the
essence of the Word, furnish an addition to the essence? Then it would have
been incomplete before this, if indeed it received an addition. But it took
on nothing from this. Then that which was changed would be nothing. And how
did the nothing fall over into a divine essence? But, he says, the Word did
not resolve the body into his own essence, but he remade this into a divine
one. Let them then answer us again, a divine as another besides his own, or
the same as his own? And if the same as his own, let us proclaim two divine
essences of the Word, one

You grow dizzy at the difficulties, and perhaps somewhere you even fear lest
somewhere some of the things said may loosen the account of our faith. But
when I, seeking, am at a loss, then I will cry out the wonder of the mystery
of the Christians, that our things are above mind, above word, above
apprehension. And when also for you, seeking such things, some difficulty
should come upon you, bring to the things sought a ready solution, the faith,
reckoning that wherever God is, even if some of the things said is unknown,
either through excellence of nature or through a manner of economy, no harm
from this comes to be for those who do not know. And how do you not fear the
daring at all, attempting to direct the divine things? Or have you not heard
the divine words, which, restraining those among us from such attempts, set
before us the image of the clay and the potter, teaching us by this that we
must not become busybodies or examiners of the divine things, but yield to the
divine will, just as the clay to the potter? Be reverent then at last and put
your reasonings to sleep with a mind exceedingly reasonable, and let faith
alone solve the further-inquiries in you, and tremble at the divine words
according to what is written, in order that, having become worthy of the
divine rest, you may hear from the God of the whole those blessed words, when
he says: "Upon whom shall I look, but upon the humble and quiet and the one
who trembles at my words?" These things then having shut in the God-fighting
tongues, the word, being urgent toward the distinction, runs. And you, the
sons of the Church, who make the inquiry piously and bring the questions of
the things further-inquired not testing but if possible to learn, prepare your
mind for me thus. Many-branched is the form of the divine teaching, but it is
gathered as in a summary both into learning and keeping of commandments, and
into the divine knowledge and worship. The lovers of piety then will attempt
to be ignorant in no respect of the keeping and learning of the commandments,
and before these indeed the divine worship.

The knowledge of the divine things they will indeed wish to track according
to power; but being too weak let them worship as unreachable, in order that
the things of our faith may not be emptied. And we, knowing by this what
things one must seek, and what believe, having then embarked the word upon
the contests of the course, let us be eager to show it victor. Embark then at
last upon the contests, O word.

We confess that we do not know the clear knowledge of the truth, since indeed
a great part somewhere of the victory. And as much as is attainable for the
nature of man, having examined this piously I will solve the thing sought for
you. For how, he says, is the Word according to essence both in his own temple
and likewise in all the things that are, and what more will the temple have
than all things? Let us hear then at last the word saying: He who is in the
bosom of the Father is present according to essence also to all things
undividedly. And we do not say that he is in the Father thus as to be in the
rest, not because the essence when it comes to be in the others is contracted,
but because of the measure of those who receive, being too weak for the divine
admission. Thus saying that he is inseparable in his own temple and confessing
that as it were the fullness of the Godhead dwells, we also say that he is
present to all according to essence, and not similarly; for the body does not
receive, being filthy, rays of Godhead. And learn what was said by an example;
for I would not shrink from the greatness of the thing sought, conversing
piously with the sons of the Church. The sun is set forth common to us all
each day, and it does not strike upon one less, upon another more, but it
sends forth its own energy common to all equally. But if someone should have
strengthened sights, he receives the more of its ray, not at all because of
the sun as having been spread out more into him than the rest, but because of
the own power of the sight; but the one whose sights are weak will not be able
to look even at the radiance of the light itself, because of the weakness of
the eyes. Thus think for me that the sun of righteousness is present to all
equally according to essence as being God, but that we all, as weak and
blear-eyed eyes with the filth of sins, are too weak for the admission of the
light, while his own temple, as a most pure eye, even has room for the
brightness of the whole light, as having been formed from Holy Spirit, and
having been separated from sin once for all. For as the sun, striking upon all
similarly according to energy, is not similarly contained by all, so the Word,
being present to all according to essence, is not present similarly to the
others and to his own temple.

You saw how the word, having contended the course, was shown victor. Let it
wear the crown at last and go in procession and be adorned with the crowns of
victory and triumph over the defeat of the adversaries! And we, as Christ
leads forth, let us

sing the victory-song, crying: "The good contest, O word, you have contended,
you have finished the course, you have kept the faith, there is laid up for
you henceforth the crown of righteousness." Or rather let us hymn the one who
supplied the victory, the most divine Word, the true light, which lights
every man coming into the world, the one through whom are all things, the one
in whom we live and move and are, the one through whom we dissolve such turns
of the words, the guardian, the president, the benefactor, to whom we also
sacrifice continually a sacrifice of praise and pour as to God sincere
prayers and sacrifice a sweet savor of deeds, turning him about in ourselves,
breathing him, reckoning him, attending upon him, hymning him in all things,
the blessed hope and supplier of the highest kingdom.
