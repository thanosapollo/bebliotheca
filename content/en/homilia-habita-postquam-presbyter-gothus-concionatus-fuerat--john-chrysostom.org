Homily 8.
=63.499=
A homily of the same, delivered in the church of Paul, Goths having read, and
a Gothic presbyter having preached first.

1. I would have wished that Greeks were present today, so as to hear what was
read and to learn how great is the strength of the Crucified, how great the
power of the cross, how great =63.500= the nobility of the Church, how great
the vigor of the faith, how great the shame of error, how great the ridicule
of the demons. For the things of the philosophers have been dissolved even
among those of the same tongue, but ours have great power even among those of
another language; and the former were torn apart more easily than a spider's
web, but the latter have been fixed more firmly than adamant. =63.501= Where
are the things of Plato and Pythagoras and of those in Athens? They have been
quenched. Where are those of the fishermen and tentmakers? Not in Judea only,
but even in the tongue of the barbarians, as you heard today, they shine more
brightly than the sun; and Scythians and Thracians and Sauromatians and Moors
and Indians, and those settled at the very extremities of the inhabited world,
each having translated into his own tongue, philosophize these things that
were said; things which those among the Greeks who drag out the beard, and
scare those they meet in the marketplace with the staff, and shake the curls
from the head, displaying faces more of lions than of men, never even imagined
in a dream.
But ours are not such, nor is philosophy in the outward form, but in sobriety.
For the harlot, inasmuch as she has no natural beauty, by paints and outlines
and white garments and other such devices lays upon herself a manufactured
bloom, shadowing the ugliness that is from nature; but the maiden who is
beautiful by nature and graceful and well-born lets the gift of nature contend
unadorned, needing none of these aids, but thrusting them away, so as not to
shadow the natural bloom with these curtains. This one may see both in the
Church and among those outside. For they, having no natural beauty, nor being
able to be adorned with piety, magnify their own affairs with fluency and
words turned on the lathe, and combinations of diction, and hair and
well-arranged dress and certain other things; but those among us are not so,
but having thrust all these away, and having cast off the outward show, they
display the natural beauty, not sharpening the tongue, nor chasing applause,
but philosophizing in the power of thoughts, and by display of works and
exactness of life proclaiming through all things the grace of God that dwells
in them. For this reason they have netted not the inhabited world only, but
also the uninhabited; not the land only, but also the sea; not the cities
only, but also the mountains and the hills and the glens; not Greece only, but
also the barbarian world; not those in office only, but also those in extreme
poverty; not men only, but also women; not the aged only, but also the young.
And they did not stop at these, but went even further, and not content with
the world we inhabit, they went out to Ocean itself, and took the barbarian
lands and the British islands within their own nets; and wherever you arrive
henceforth, you will see the names of the fishermen carried about in the
mouths of all, not through the power of the fishermen, but through the
strength of the Crucified, which makes a way for them everywhere, and shows
the unlearned wiser than the philosophers, and those who are unlettered and
more voiceless than fish more vigorous than orators


and chroniclers and sophists. Let no one therefore count it a shame of the
Church that we have prepared barbarians to stand up in the midst and speak;
for this is the Church's ornament, this her adornment, this a proof of the
power in the faith. This the prophet also, proclaiming beforehand from of old,
said: "There are no speeches nor words, whose voices are not heard; their
sound has gone out into all the earth, and their words to the ends of the
inhabited world." This another also =63.502= intimating again, hinted in other
words, saying: "Wolves and lambs shall graze together, and the leopard shall
rest with the kid, and the lion shall eat straw like the ox"; not telling of
lions and lambs and leopards and kids, but proclaiming beforehand to us and
showing that the beastliness of men, mixed with the philosophy of the
preaching, will come to such gentleness as to flock together with the gentle
and most meek of men. And this you have seen today, the most barbarian of all
men standing with the sheep of the Church, the pasture being common and the
fold one, and one table set before all.

2. Let the Jews be ashamed, who read the letters and are ignorant of the
meanings; let the Greeks hide their faces, seeing the truth shining brighter
than a ray, they who sit beside stones and pursue the darkness; let the Church
be adorned, which shines and flies through all things. For as the sun is
common and the earth common and the sea and the air, so much more has the word
of the preaching become common; therefore Paul also said: "That I may have
some fruit among you also, even as among the rest of the nations. To Greeks
and to barbarians, to wise and to foolish I am a debtor; so, as much as in me
is, I am ready to preach the gospel to you also who are in Rome." And why do
you wonder if in the New, when even in the Old this same thing happens? For
the first ancestor both of the Church and of the Synagogue, of those according
to the flesh, of us according to the spirit, was a barbarian and was led from
the midst of Persia, I mean the patriarch Abraham, and he had neither heard
letters, nor shared in prophecy, having no teacher, receiving no history; for
Moses had not yet come into being; learning nothing of those before him,
taught nothing of what would be after him, but born and raised in the midst of
the land of the Persians, he philosophized so suddenly that he even
anticipated many commandments of the New, and displayed them through his
works. For being commanded to go out from his country, and to leave house and
friends and kinsmen, and to come to a foreign land, he suffered nothing human,
nor was he held by affection, nor did he reason with himself and say: "Shall I
leave the things that are clear and evident, and go away to the things that
are unseen and uncertain?" but having faith as a guide, and having acquired
the promise of God in place of a staff, he let go what was in his hands, and
took up what was in hopes; for in this too he was an ancestor of the Church.
For we too are commanded to despise the affairs of this life and the things
that appear, and to stretch our hope toward the things that are unseen, and to
hold fast faith, the anchor of our salvation, and to seek those things;
therefore Paul also said: "For we were saved in hope; but hope that is seen is
not hope"; and again, "For the momentary lightness of our affliction works for
us an eternal weight of glory from excess to excess, while we look not at the
things that are seen, but at the things that are not seen."
And when this blessed =63.503= Abraham had gone out from his country, and had
pitched a hut in a foreign land, he again displayed apostolic commands through
his works. For when he had set up that brilliant trophy, and had won the
unexpected victory, and had prevailed over the barbarians, not by strength of
body, but by power of faith, and was bidden to take a reward of those labors
and of the hardship from those


who had been saved; for when the barbarian said to him, "Take you the horse,
and give us the men," what does he say? "I will stretch out my hand to God the
Most High, if I take from you from a thread even to a shoe-latchet." Did you
see how he fulfilled that evangelical command which says: "Freely you
received, freely give"? And Moses too was both reared and grew in a barbarian
house; yet he was in no way harmed from this, but he too philosophized no less
than the patriarch, mocking the Sybaritic table, and becoming higher than
their luxury, and dishonoring wealth and kingship and the scepters of Egypt,
and going over of his own accord to the clay and the brickmaking. Therefore
Paul also, marveling at him, said: "Accounting the reproach of Christ greater
wealth than the treasures of Egypt." Then also showing the teacher of these
goods, he added, saying: "For he endured as seeing the Invisible"; which was
of the greatest faith. Let us not therefore think it a shame that barbarians
are in the Church, but even a great ornament; for our Lord Jesus Christ
himself also, when he came into the inhabited world, called barbarians first.
For when he was born and was laid in the manger, Magi also came from Persia
and worshipped him; O new and unexpected things! When a king is about to enter
a city, curtains and lamps, and those in office and those in power, all meet
him with a bright appearance, and flutes and pipes and lyres, and every kind
of music, and splendid garments and crowns, and much magnificence; but when
the King of the heavens is about to enter the inhabited world, nothing of this
sort, but all the contrary: a hut and a tent and a manger, a mother cheap in
what appeared, and much beggary and an intensification of poverty. And yet, if
he had wished, he could have come shaking the heaven, rocking the earth,
sending forth lightnings, and why do I say these things? if he had shown the
Godhead naked only, how much greater than this splendor and brightness would
that not have been? But he did not do so; for he wished not to destroy, but to
save, not to astound, but to correct, and from the very preludes to trample
down human pride and to bring down conceit. For this reason he does not become
man only, but also a poor man, and he chooses such a mother, and a cheap
lodging, embracing from the very preludes and from the very birth-pangs the
last bound of poverty. For tell me, what woman, even if she be poorer than all
men, even if she be a beggar herself, does not have a bed on which to lay the
child that has been born? But he was not even laid on a bed, but in a manger
simply and as it chanced, and in a hut and not in an inn. Such were the
preludes of his entrance, so splendid and magnificent; and when you hear
"entrance," do not think a change of place, but the condescension of the
economy.

3. For when Paul says, "And when he again brings the firstborn into the
inhabited world," he announces the economy to us; for he who is present
everywhere, and fills all things, where would he enter? but he called the
manifestation through the economy thus. Entering then into the inhabited
world, he entered with a humble appearance, trampling down pride, checking
conceit, persuading all to be moderate, not to dishonor poverty nor to make
beggary cheap, not to gape after wealth, not to think human show a great
thing, but weaker than a shadow, and cheaper than leaves, [and] more deceitful
than dreams.
Entering then, he called barbarians, and barbarians not simply barbarians, but
Magi too, the intensified form of impiety. For since all the affairs of the
inhabited world were in a bad state, and impiety prevailed everywhere, and the
smell of fat and smoke, and neither did the law have force, nor did prophets
correct, nor did exhortations help, nor


wonders, nor chastisement, nor punishments, and the earth was defiled with
human blood, and nature itself was unknown; "For they sacrificed their sons
and their daughters to the demons"; altars were dug down, prophets were
killed, and the temple of piety became a ruin and a lodging of idols, and the
people that had been brought forward into the midst so as to instruct others
became a matter of blasphemy for others; "For my name," he says, "is
blasphemed among the nations because of you"; and sacrifices were convicted,
and feasts and new moons and sabbaths, and the remaining form of the worship
had been cast out, virtue being neglected, and a voice from above of God
accusing, and voices from below of the prophets lamenting, and voices from
every side, of affairs in a bad state, and great was the power of vice, and
there was a dense cloud and a deep night, and the things of wickedness in
openness, and the things of virtue out of the way, and the storm was raised on
high, and the waves successive, and shipwrecks frequent and continual, and all
were underwater, and not even a helmsman's voice was heard, nor did the skill
of sailors prevail, but the wicked spirits had the mastery, and there was no
hope of salvation, nor did anyone dare to call upon God; for how could they,
when the prophets were accusing, and the angels set over each nation were
hiding their faces? the time was now calling the Maker of all, the very
Fashioner who also from the beginning made our race.
Since then the disease was most grievous, and this was born especially from
conceit and arrogance, and the beginning and the middle and the ends of
affairs were being corrupted; he sprang from the paternal thrones, and came to
the earth. And when you hear "he sprang" again, do not think a change of
place, do not think an emptying of the heavens; for even while he was in the
virgin womb, he was with the Father; but the how do not seek, nor demand an
accounting; for when God works, there is need of faith only, and assent, and
confession. Coming then and seeing the sick man lying on a bed and given up
(and when I say "sick man," I mean the human race, not on such a bed, but on
the bed of wickedness), abandoned by physicians, besieged by passions,
mastered by the disease, every kind of infirmity having fallen upon human
nature, and man himself having now given himself up, and the physician given
for the wounds becoming a bitter accuser, I mean the law, and increasing the
charges, and making man =63.505= liable to a greater judgment, and the tyranny
of the infirmity, and the excess of the evils, seeking no other, but him who
fashioned us from the beginning; see from the preludes what he does: he puts
on our nature, the one that had grown weak, the one that had been defeated, so
as to fight and wrestle again through it; and from the very gateways at once
and from the preludes he pulls up by the root the nature of conceit; for this
has been the cause of all evils. This at any rate made the devil, who was not
a devil, to be a devil; and Paul shows it, saying: "Not a novice, lest being
puffed up he fall into the condemnation of the devil." This cast Adam out of
paradise; for when he heard, "You shall be as gods," deceived by this hope, he
touched the tree, and trampled the law, and transgressed the commandment. For
this reason God also prepared a medicine suited to the wound. For as in bodies
the contraries are healing of the contraries, as the sons of physicians also
say, and we warm what has been chilled, and we moisten what has been dried; so
too God did in the passions of the soul.
For since conceit had lifted it up and made it to inflame, he applied a
medicine that settles its swelling and reduces the tumor. And what medicine?
Walling death against it. For this reason he not only made the body mortal,
but also prepared it to rot and to be defiled, and to gush with


worms, and to dissolve into ichor and to be filled with stench, laying down in
advance the foundations of humility, and never even letting the one who is
extremely conceited think great things. For what is more stinking than a human
body? and what cheaper than one that has died?

4. But do not be gloomy, beloved, but consider here too God's care. For
neither does the one who has died perceive what happens, so as to be in pain,
and the one who lives is trained in another's body to philosophize. For even
if he lift the eyebrow ten thousand times and boast greatly, when he has gone
to a tomb, and perceives the stench falling upon him, and is reminded of our
cheapness, he is checked and collapses, and comes down to Hades itself. And
that you may learn that this has come about for this reason, not because he
dishonors our nature, but because he bridles the conceit of the mind, and
keeps man in continual humility, since from the beginning his sin came about
from this; hear what the prophet Isaiah says: "How has the morning star fallen
from heaven, that rose at dawn"; not speaking of the morning star; for the
morning star never fell, but has been fixed, founded once in heaven, and keeps
its own course. What then is it that the prophet says, "How has the morning
star fallen from heaven, that rose at dawn?" He speaks of a certain barbarian
king; and he calls him morning star because of the magnificence, because of
the diadem, because of the purple, because of the brightness of the
bodyguards, because of the gleam from the arms, because of the shining gold,
because of the spears sharpened and glittering, because of all the rest of the
show, because he leads and carries off all things. For that he does not speak
of the morning star, but has used the name metaphorically, saying, "How has
the morning star fallen from heaven, that rose at dawn?" going on he says,
"But you said in your heart, I will ascend into heaven, I will set my throne
above the stars of heaven, and I will =63.506= be like the Most High." And
that a star has neither thought nor mind nor words is surely clear to
everyone; but he speaks of a certain blasphemous and arrogant and boastful
king, who boasts such things. And who is the king? It is possible for us too
to say, but so that you may not learn everything from us, but that you
yourselves also, taking thought and watching, may search the treasures of the
divine Scripture, go over the passage of the prophet, and you will know both
who the king is, and of what nation, and when he was; "For give," he says,
"occasion to a wise man, and he will be wiser."
Since then this king, being splendid and magnificent, and having a great
abundance of wealth, and having gone over the whole inhabited world, and
having stretched out his own kingdom, was suddenly snatched away and died; the
prophet, knowing the change of men, said, "How has the morning star fallen
from heaven, that rose at dawn, who shakes kings, who has made the whole
inhabited world a desert?" and addressing him and mocking him, he says: "But
you said, I will ascend into heaven, I will set my throne above the stars of
heaven, and I will be like the Most High; but now you shall go down to Hades,
they shall spread decay under you; and your covering is a worm." Do you see
how, as I said, he prepared our body to have such an end, that it might be a
medicine for conceit? Seeing him then conceited, he trains him from the things
after death, reminding him of the worms, of the stench, of the ichor, so that
having considered that, he may slacken from conceit, not looking at the
present things and being puffed up, but to what end he will come, what change
he will receive. For this reason we have been allotted a passible body, and
after death so stinking. For if, nature being such, many of the foolish have
run aground on this conceit, so as to imagine equality with God and to look at
heaven; if they were not held by this bridle, into what lawlessness would they
not have burst? Not this


king only, but another also is found imagining this very thing; whom at any
rate the prophet Ezekiel, another prophet, mocking, said: "But you said, I am
God and not a man, dwelling in the heart of the earth; in the hand of those
who slay you will you say that I am God and not a man?" Do you see that this
one also hinted that death came in for this reason, that it might check
conceit, that it might pull up by the root that wicked thought which the devil
sowed from the beginning in the first-formed man? For idolatry also came about
from this, the crown of the evils of men, when the many are lifted into
conceit and desire equality with God.
All the gods among the Greeks, at any rate, were fashioned by men; which
things, that they might not happen, God walled death against us, convicting
the weakness of nature. For this reason the only-begotten Son of God, checking
conceit, prepared such preludes of the entrance to be undergone, a hut and
poverty and a poor mother, persuading all through the works to trample down
human pride; and when he came, he calls barbarians first. For what reason and
why? Since the race of men was in a bad state (for I will take up the same
word again), and all had given up hope of salvation, he first transfers the
citadel of the devil and his strongest weapons, so that none of the =63.507=
others may despair. And otherwise this also happens for the shame of the Jews,
that they were in revolt [hesitated] to approach, but these even made a long
journey abroad, so as to see him and worship, and going about they say: "Where
is he who has been born king of the Jews?"

5. O marvelous and unexpected things: by a barbarian voice first the
only-begotten Son of God is preached in Judea, where there were prophets and
patriarchs and righteous men and law and ark and covenant and temple and
sacrifices and services; and those who were reared in these things are not
even trained when they have learned from barbarians; but those who had never
heard anything of this kind become teachers of those who had practiced these
things. For this reason the prophet David, mocking them, said: "Why did the
nations rage, and the peoples imagine vain things?" For how did they not
imagine vain things, not receiving the one who had been prophesied? But not so
the Magi, but leaving country and house and friends and kinsmen, they even
made a long journey abroad, and threw themselves into danger. For coming into
a city under a king they asked where the king had been born; not suspecting
the wrath of the one then reigning, nor the uprising of the people, nor the
plots of the city. For when they had received the preaching, they become at
once both philosophers and living martyrs, daring death, despising dangers,
overlooking the present life, proclaiming with boldness what they had learned,
announcing in the midst of a people and in the midst of a city the one who had
been born.
For this reason the star is also hidden, so that having lost the one who was
guiding them they might be forced to learn from Jews, or rather in the
learning to teach those men; for they seek the place only, but that he had
been born they knew beforehand. But see the wisdom of God, how he called them.
He did not send a prophet, for they would not have endured it; nor an apostle,
for they would not have attended; nor scriptures, for they did not know them;
but from their own and familiar affairs he draws them up out of error. For
since they were Magi, and their art was about the stars, a star appears to
them, drawing them from beyond the borders, a star not one of those that are
seen, but a certain divine and invisible power shaped into the appearance of a
star; and a star draws them, that he may thereafter free them from being
occupied about the stars, and may dissolve the tyranny of astronomy. So Paul
also did, imitating his own Master; "Imitators of me


become," he says, "as I also of Christ." For just as his Master, calling Magi,
sent the appearance of a star, so that recognizing what was customary they
might easily obey, and coming might see the Master of the stars, and being
freed from that slavery, might come over to this mastery; so he too, being
about to pull down circumcision, sent Timothy with circumcision. For since
those who were about to receive him were Jews, and he was the son of a
faithful Jewish woman, but of a Greek father, and for this reason
uncircumcised, he put circumcision around him, working the things of the
fishermen; for they too do not let the hook go naked into the sea, but putting
flesh around it. Let the synagogue then be a sea, and the Jews fish, and the
hook Timothy, and the circumcision the flesh put around the hook, and the
fisherman Paul; he therefore casts the hook into the sea, but not naked,
Timothy, with circumcision, but =63.508= not uncircumcised; for if he had sent
him uncircumcised, the fish would have fled, seeing the hook naked; for this
reason he wraps it with the circumcision, so that recognizing what is
customary they may run to the hunt, and he may not scare the catch when they
have run to the hunt; that they may be drawn by circumcision, but held by the
word of teaching; which indeed also happened. For having entered and having
circumcision, he dissolved circumcision.
For such are the things of the economy: he condescends first, and then draws
up. So Christ also did, calling barbarians. For just as, calling men, he put
around himself a man [of a man?] both appearance and nature; so also, calling
Magi, he shaped the invisible power into the appearance of a star. For that
that star was not a star, one of the many, learn from its course. For it did
not run from east to west, but from north to south; for so Palestine lies in
relation to Persia; and it appears not by night, but by day, which is not of a
star; for this reason it both appears and is hidden, which is not of a star
either; and it is hidden when they entered Jerusalem, and it appears when they
were freed from the king. From which it is clear that it was a certain
rational power managing all things toward what had been commanded. And this is
clear not from this only, but also because, having guided them to the manger,
it is not shown to them from above, but having come down below it stood where
the child was, above over his head so to speak; for if it had been a star, it
would not have shown such a place, narrow and bounded. For you know clearly, I
suppose, that because of the excess of height a star could not even mark the
place of a city, nor is the straight line dropped vertically from it easy to
distinguish because of the excess of the heights. But that one showed even a
small spot and a manger, having come near the head, and having shown it at
once sprang away; which was not of a star.

7. And if the Magi say, "We have seen his star," speaking for the time from
their own art, yet later, having been guided, they were freed. For that as
soon as they saw the star they were also freed from the error is clear from
their worshipping; for if they had thought it one of the many, they would not
have worshipped. For what did they expect from a child, being both strangers
and newcomers and barbarians? and from where did they know that he would live
and would render them the returns, they being at so great a distance? and how
did they not prefer [foresee?] the present danger? and how did they not
consider that they might even be snatched away beforehand from the present
life, the one then reigning killing them? and what symbol of kingship did they
even see? what gateways? what bodyguards? what shield-bearers? what
attendance? what golden shields? what chariots? what roof? what courts? what
herd of eunuchs? Nothing of these, but all the contrary, a manger and a hut
and a lodging and a poor mother and intensified poverty and swaddling-clothes.
To whom then did they offer the gold and the frankincense and the myrrh? is it
not quite clear that by faith.


Already they were winged? And great is the difference of the gifts, [and]
holding the rank of a proclamation. For the word is not to be rejected, even
if it has been said by many, that they confessed God through the frankincense,
and a king through the gold, and the one who suffers through the myrrh,
indicating the tomb; so at any rate he was also buried; and these things they
proclaimed beforehand through what they did. What happened then was not the
power of astronomy, but of the economy of God; for he who =63.509= came to
teach virtue was not causing astronomy to be introduced, but even strongly
dissolves it, determining that both the good things and the things that are
not good are in willing and in not willing. For this reason he also threatened
Gehenna, and prepared a kingdom; and he would not have made ready crowns for
those bound by necessity, nor have threatened punishment; for both of these
belong to the things that come about in the will; he would not have laid down
laws, he would not have given exhortations, he would not have done so many
things, if the race of men were bound by necessity; but since we are free and
masters of choice, becoming base from slackness, good and reasonable from
zeal, he came preparing the saving medicines that lead to this also,
correcting the many by the fear of Gehenna and by the promise of the kingdom,
and training them through the laws to philosophize.
For that neither is there a nativity, nor a course of stars administering
human affairs, is clear even apart from the Scriptures from the facts
themselves. For if human affairs are under a nativity, why do you scourge the
servant who has stolen? why do you drag the wife who has been adulterous into
the courts? why are you ashamed when you do wicked things? for if the sin is
of necessity, why do you not even bear the words when you are reproached, but
if someone calls you an adulterer, or a fornicator, or a murderer, you call
the thing an insult? For if the sins are not of your will, neither is what
happens an accusation, nor is what is said an insult. But as it is, both by
the things for which you judge others, and by the things for which you do not
pardon those who sin, and by the things for which you yourself are ashamed
when you act and are eager to escape notice, and think those who bring these
things against you insolent, [and] through all things you confess that our
affairs are not bound by necessity, but have been honored with freedom; those
held by necessity, at any rate, we know how to pardon. If then someone
possessed by a demon strike us, and tear the tunic, or inflict blows, not only
do we not punish him, nor drag him into the courts, but we even pity him and
pardon him. Why then? Because not the freedom of choice, but the necessity of
the demon has done these things. So that if the other sins also were from the
necessity according to fate, we would have pardoned; but since we know that we
are not of necessity, for this reason we do not pardon, neither masters
servants, nor husbands wives, nor wives husbands, nor fathers children, nor
teachers pupils, nor rulers subjects, nor lawgivers those under the laws, but
we become bitter examiners and punishers of what has been dared, and we take
to the courts, and we inflict blows, and we use rebukes, and we do all things

for if it was fated for him to become base, what need of the helps? It is
quite clear then that he is not bound by necessity, but the whole lies in the
choice. Therefore we also apply helps, and lead away from slackness, and guide
toward virtue. And why do we sail the sea, till the land, and build cities,
and make much zeal when we are sick, and spend money, and call physicians, and
prepare medicines, and restrain the belly, and chastise desire? For if, as you
say, it has been fated to live and to die, superfluous are the expenditures of
money, superfluous the visits of physicians, superfluous the exactness of the
philosophy of the sick. But now


from what all do, they make clear that none of these things is superfluous;
but the things of fate are gone, and our affairs are led by no necessity, but
all have been honored with freedom of choice. And what need of toils and
sweats on behalf of virtue? for if it has been fated to become good, such a
one will be good even sleeping and snoring; or rather it is not even possible
to call such a one good, who becomes this under necessity. What need of so
many toils and sweats? for if it was fated to become evil, even if one suffer
ten thousand hardships, he will be evil; or rather it will not even be
possible to call such a one evil, who is pushed by necessity. For just as the
one possessed by a demon, even if he revile (for I use the same example
again), we will not address him as insolent, nor do we reckon the reviling an
insult, but as belonging to the necessity of the demon; so also the evil man,
if he is pushed by fate and necessity, let us not call him evil, just as
neither the good man good. For if we do this, all will be confusion and
turmoil and a twisting of affairs, and neither will virtue be anything, nor
vice; not arts, not laws, nor anything else of such things. Do you see where
the devil's bait tends, and what he wished to work upon the inhabited world,
and to what end it meets?
Knowing all these things then, and more than these (for it was possible to say
even more than these, but for the more intelligent these too suffice), let us
flee vice, let us choose virtue, so that first of all we may have God
gracious, and then also be delivered from Gehenna, and enjoy the eternal
goods; which may it be that we all obtain, by the grace and love of mankind of
our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom glory to the Father together
with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
