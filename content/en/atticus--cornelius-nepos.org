* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Titus Pomponius Atticus, sprung from the farthest origin of the Roman stock,
held without break the equestrian rank received from his ancestors. He had a
careful father, and, as times then were, a rich one, and especially devoted to
letters. This man, as he himself loved letters, instructed his son in all the
learning with which boyhood ought to be furnished. In the boy, besides a mind
that took instruction readily, there was the greatest sweetness of mouth and
voice, so that he not only took in quickly what was taught, but also delivered
it with distinction. From this, in boyhood, he was counted distinguished among
his equals, and he shone out more brightly than his well-born schoolfellows
could bear with even mind. And so he stirred them all by his own zeal. In that
number were Lucius Torquatus, Gaius Marius the son, and Marcus Cicero, whom he
bound to himself by his companionship so that no one was dearer to them
through the whole of life.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

His father died early. He himself, as a very young man, because of his
connection with Publius Sulpicius, who was killed as tribune of the plebs, was
not free of that danger: for Anicia, Pomponius's cousin, had married Servius,
brother of Sulpicius. And so, after Sulpicius was killed, when he saw that the
state was thrown into confusion by Cinna's tumult and that he was given no
chance of living according to his rank without offending one side or the
other, the minds of the citizens being split, some favoring the Sullan party,
some the Cinnan, he judged it a fit time to follow his studies and went to
Athens. Yet even so he helped with his means the young Marius, who had been
judged a public enemy, and eased his flight with money. And lest that sojourn
abroad should bring some loss to his estate, he transferred a great part of
his fortune there as well. Here he lived so that he was deservedly dearest to
all the Athenians. For besides the favor which was already great in him as a
young man, he often relieved their public want with his own means. For when it
was necessary for them to take a public loan and they had no fair terms for
it, he always interposed himself, and in such a way that he never took
interest from them nor suffered them to remain in debt longer than had been
agreed. Both of these things were for their good: for he did not allow their
debt to grow old by indulgence, nor to grow by multiplying interest. He
increased this service by another kindness as well: for he gave grain to them
all, so that six modii of wheat were given to each man, a measure which at
Athens is called a medimnus.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

He conducted himself here so that he seemed a fellow to the lowest and a peer
to the foremost. From this it came about that they publicly paid him all the
honors they could and were eager to make him a citizen: a benefit he would not
use, because some interpret it that Roman citizenship is lost when another is
taken on. As long as he was present, he resisted having any statue set up for
him; in his absence he could not prevent it. And so they set up several, for
him and for Phidias, in the most sacred places: for in every management of
public business they had him especially as agent and adviser. Therefore that
was the first gift of fortune, that he was born in that city in which the seat
of the world's empire was, so that he had the same place as both fatherland
and home; this was the proof of his prudence, that when he had gone to that
state which in antiquity, culture, and learning surpassed all, he alone was
dearest to it.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

When Sulla, coming down from Asia, had arrived here, as long as he was there
he kept Pomponius with him, taken by the young man's culture and learning. For
he spoke Greek so that he seemed born at Athens; but there was such sweetness
in his Latin speech that it was clear there was in him a native charm, not a
borrowed one. He also recited poems both in Greek and in Latin so that nothing
could be added above it. By these things it came about that Sulla nowhere let
him leave his side and wished to take him away with him. When he tried to
persuade him, "Do not, I beg you," said Pomponius, "wish to lead me against
those with whom, that I might not bear arms against you, I left Italy." But
Sulla, having praised the young man's sense of duty, as he set out ordered
that all the gifts he had received at Athens be delivered to him.

Having stayed here several years, while he gave as much care to his estate as
a not careless head of a household ought, and devoted all remaining time
either to letters or to the public affairs of the Athenians, he nonetheless
performed civic services for his friends. For he went often to their
elections, and if any greater matter was afoot he was not absent. So to Cicero
in all his dangers he showed a singular loyalty: to him, fleeing from his
country, he gave two hundred and fifty thousand sesterces. When Roman affairs
had grown quiet he returned to Rome, as I think in the consulship of Lucius
Cotta and Lucius Torquatus. The whole Athenian people attended him as he left,
so that by their tears they showed the pain of the longing that would come.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

He had as uncle Quintus Caecilius, a Roman knight, an intimate of Lucius
Lucullus, a rich man of a most difficult nature: whose harshness he so
respected that he kept, without offense, to extreme old age, the goodwill of a
man whom no one could bear. By this deed he reaped the fruit of piety. For
Caecilius dying adopted him by will and made him heir to three-quarters: from
which inheritance he received about ten million sesterces. Atticus's sister
was married to Quintus Tullius Cicero, and Marcus Cicero had arranged that
marriage, with whom from their schooldays he lived in the closest union, even
much more intimately than with Quintus, so that it may be judged that likeness
of character has more force in friendship than kinship. He was also on most
intimate terms with Quintus Hortensius, who at that time held the first place
in eloquence, so that it could not be understood which of the two loved him
more, Cicero or Hortensius; and, what was most difficult, he brought it about
that, among men between whom there was rivalry for so great a distinction, no
detraction came between them, and he was the bond of such men.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

In public affairs he so conducted himself that he always both was, and was
thought to be, of the best party, and yet he did not commit himself to the
waves of civil strife, because he did not think those who had given themselves
to them were any more in their own power than those tossed on the sea. He did
not seek honors, though they lay open to him by favor or by rank, because they
could neither be sought in the manner of the ancestors nor taken while the
laws were kept amid such unbridled bribery of canvassing, nor administered to
the advantage of the republic without danger, the morals of the state being
corrupted. He never came to the public auction. He became neither surety nor
contractor for any public business. He accused no one, either in his own name
or by joining a prosecution; he never went to law about his own affair; he had
no lawsuit. The prefectures of many consuls and praetors, offered to him, he
accepted in such a way that he followed no one into a province, was content
with the honor, and looked down on the profit of the office: he would not even
go with Quintus Cicero into Asia, though he could have held the place of
legate with him. For he did not think it becoming, when he had been unwilling
to hold the praetorship, to be an attendant of a praetor. In this he served
not only his dignity but also his quiet, since he avoided even suspicions of
charges. From this it came about that his attentiveness was dearer to all,
when they saw it attributed to duty, not to fear or hope.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Caesar's civil war broke out. When he was about sixty years old, he used the
exemption of age and did not move anywhere from the city. Whatever his friends
who were setting out to Pompey needed, he gave all of it from his own estate;
he did not offend Pompey himself, who was connected with him. He had received
no distinction from him, as the rest had, who through him had taken either
honors or riches: of whom some followed the camp most unwillingly, some
remained at home with the greatest offense to him. But Atticus's quiet was so
welcome to Caesar that the victor, when he was demanding money from private
men by letters, not only was not troublesome to this man, but even granted him
his sister's son and Quintus Cicero from Pompey's camp. So by his old rule of
life he escaped new dangers.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

What follows came next. After Caesar was killed, when the republic seemed to
be in the hands of the Brutuses and Cassius, and the whole state had turned
itself to them, he was on such terms with Marcus Brutus that that young man
was intimate with no equal more than with this old man, and he had him not
only as chief of his counsel but also in his daily company. It was thought out
by some that a private treasury for Caesar's killers should be set up by the
Roman knights. They thought this could easily be done if the leading men of
that order had contributed money. And so Atticus was called on by Gaius
Flavius, an intimate of Brutus, to be willing to be the chief of that
business. But he, who thought duties should be rendered to friends without
party, and had always kept himself apart from such plans, answered: if Brutus
had wished to use anything of his means, he would use as much as they allowed;
he himself would neither speak with anyone about that matter nor join in. So
that mass of agreement was scattered by this one man's dissent. Not long
after, Antony began to be the stronger, so that Brutus and Cassius, dropping
care of the provinces which had been given them by the consul for form's sake,
despaired of their affairs and set out into exile. Atticus, who had been
unwilling to contribute money together with the rest while that party was
flourishing, when Brutus was cast down and withdrawing from Italy sent him a
hundred thousand sesterces as a gift. To the same man in Epirus, though
absent, he ordered three hundred thousand to be given; and he was no more a
flatterer of Antony in his power, nor did he abandon those who had lost hope.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

There followed the war waged at Mutina. In which if I should call him only
prudent, I would praise him less than I ought, since he was rather divine, if
a constant natural goodness, which is shaken by no chances and is not
lessened, ought to be called divination. Antony, judged an enemy, had
withdrawn from Italy: there was no hope of restoring him. Not only his
enemies, who were then most powerful and very many, but even those who were
selling themselves to his adversaries and hoped they would gain some advantage
by injuring him, were pursuing Antony's intimates, wished to strip his wife
Fulvia of all she had, and were even preparing to destroy the children.
Atticus, though he enjoyed Cicero's closest friendship and was most friendly
to Brutus, not only granted them nothing toward harming Antony, but on the
contrary sheltered, as far as he could, Antony's friends fleeing from the
city, and helped them with what they needed. To Publius Volumnius indeed he
gave so much that more could not have come from a father. And to Fulvia
herself, when she was pulled this way and that by lawsuits and harassed by
great terrors, he performed his duty with such care that she met no bail-day
without Atticus, and Atticus was surety in all her affairs. Nay more, when she
had bought an estate in her prosperous time for payment on a set day, and
after the disaster could not raise a loan, he interposed himself and lent the
money without interest and without any bond, thinking it the greatest gain to
be known as mindful and grateful, and at the same time showing that he was
accustomed to be a friend not to fortune but to men. When he was doing these
things, no one could think he was doing them to suit the time: for it came
into no one's mind that Antony would become master of affairs. But his feeling
was blamed by some of the optimates, because he seemed to hate bad citizens
too little. He, however, of his own judgment, looked rather at what it was
right for him to do than at what others would praise.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Fortune suddenly turned. When Antony returned to Italy, there was no one who
had not thought Atticus in great danger because of his close friendship with
Cicero and Brutus. And so at the coming of the commanders he had withdrawn
from the forum, fearing proscription, and was hiding at the house of Publius
Volumnius, to whom, as we have shown, he had a little before brought help (so
great was the variety of fortune in those times that now these, now those were
at the highest either of eminence or of danger), and he had with him Quintus
Gellius Canus, of the same age and most like himself. This too is an example
of Atticus's goodness, that with a man whom he had known as a boy at school he
lived so closely that their friendship grew to the last of life. Antony,
although he was carried by such hatred against Cicero that he was an enemy not
only to him but also to all his friends and wished to proscribe them, with
many urging it, yet was mindful of Atticus's service, and when he had asked
where he was, wrote to him with his own hand that he should not fear and
should come to him at once: that he had taken him and, for his sake, Canus out
of the number of the proscribed. And lest any danger should befall him, since
this was happening by night, he sent him a guard. So Atticus, in the height of
fear, was a protection not only to himself but also to the man he held
dearest; for he did not ask help from anyone for his own safety alone, but for
their joint safety, so that it was clear he wished no fortune separate from
that man. But if a helmsman is raised with special praise who saves a ship out
of a storm and a rocky sea, why should not his prudence be thought singular,
who from so many and so heavy civil tempests came through to safety?

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

When he had emerged from these evils, he did nothing else than be a help to as
many as he could, with whatever means he could. When the crowd, for the
commanders' rewards, was hunting down the proscribed, no one came to Epirus
who lacked anything: to everyone power was given of remaining there for good;
nay even after the battle of Philippi and the death of Gaius Cassius and
Marcus Brutus he undertook to protect Lucius Julius Mocilla, a praetorian, and
his son, and Aulus Torquatus, and the rest struck by like fortune, and ordered
everything to be brought to them from Epirus to Samothrace. It is hard to
follow all things, and not necessary. This one thing we wish to be understood,
that his generosity was neither for the moment nor crafty. That can be judged
from the very facts and times, because he did not sell himself to the
prosperous, but always came to the aid of the afflicted: he indeed cultivated
Servilia, Brutus's mother, no less after his death than when she flourished.
Using generosity in this way he carried on no enmities, because he injured
no one, and if he had received any wrong he preferred to forget it rather
than avenge it. He also kept benefits received with undying memory; but
those he himself had bestowed he remembered only so long as he who had
received them was grateful. And so he brought it about that this seems truly
said: /Each man's own character shapes fortune for men./ Yet he did not shape
fortune before he shaped himself, who took care that he should not justly be
punished in any matter.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

By these things, then, he brought it about that Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa,
joined in closest friendship to the young Caesar, though because of his own
favor and Caesar's power he had the choice of any match, chose especially
affinity with this man and preferred the daughter of a Roman knight to
marriages with women of high birth. And the arranger of this marriage was (for
it is not to be hidden) Marcus Antonius, one of the triumvirs for settling the
republic. Though by his favor he could have increased his possessions, he was
so far from greed for money that he used that influence in no matter except in
begging off friends from dangers or inconveniences. This was very clear at the
very time of the proscription. For when the triumvirs, in the custom by which
things were then done, had sold the goods of Lucius Saufeius, a Roman knight
of his own age, who led by the study of philosophy had lived several years at
Athens and had valuable possessions in Italy, it was brought about by
Atticus's labor and industry that by the same message Saufeius was informed
that he had lost his inheritance and that he had recovered it. He likewise
rescued Lucius Julius Calidus, whom after the death of Lucretius and Catullus
I think I can truly maintain our age has produced as by far the most elegant
poet, and no less a good man and trained in the best arts, who after the
proscription of the knights, because of his great African possessions, had
been put, though absent, on the list of the proscribed by Publius Volumnius,
Antony's prefect of engineers. Whether this was more laborious or more
glorious for him at the time is hard to judge, because it was known that
absent friends were no less Atticus's care in their dangers than those
present.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

Nor was he held a less good head of a household than a citizen. For though he
was wealthy, no one was less a buyer than he, less a builder. And yet he lived
especially well and used all the best things. For he had the Tamphilian house
on the Quirinal hill, left him by inheritance from his uncle, whose
pleasantness consisted not in the building but in the wood: for the house
itself, put up in old times, had more taste than cost; in it he changed
nothing, except if he was forced by age. He used a household which, if it is
to be judged by usefulness, was the best, if by looks, scarcely middling. For
in it there were most lettered boys, excellent readers, and very many
copyists, so that there was not even any attendant who could not do both of
these things well; in the same way the other craftsmen whom household elegance
requires were first-rate. Yet he had none of these except born in the house
and trained in the house: which is a sign not only of self-restraint but also
of diligence. For not to desire intemperately what you may see in very many
ought to be counted the part of a self-restrained man, and to get things by
industry rather than by price is of no mean diligence. He was elegant, not
magnificent; splendid, not costly: and all his care aimed at neatness, not
abundance. His furniture was moderate, not much, so that it could not be
noticed either way. Nor will I pass over, though I think it will seem slight
to some, that though he was an especially refined Roman knight and invited men
of all ranks to his house not a little generously, he was accustomed to enter
as spent for expenses, from his day-book, no more than three thousand
sesterces evenly for each month. And this we declare not as heard but as
known: for often, because of our intimacy, we were present at his domestic
affairs.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

No one at his table heard any other entertainment than a reader, which we
indeed think most pleasant; nor was dinner ever taken at his house without
some reading, so that the guests were delighted no less in mind than in belly:
for he invited those whose manners did not differ from his own. When so great
an addition had been made to his money, he changed nothing of his daily way of
living, nothing of the habit of his life, and he used such moderation that
neither on the two million sesterces he had received from his father did he
conduct himself with too little splendor, nor on the ten million did he live
more lavishly than he had begun, and he stood on an equal height in either
fortune. He had no gardens, no costly suburban or seaside villa, nor in Italy,
except the Arretine and the Nomentan, a country estate, and all the return of
his money consisted in Epirote and city possessions. From which it can be
known that he was accustomed to measure the use of money not by size but by
reason.

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

He neither told a lie nor could bear one. And so his courtesy was not without
severity, nor his gravity without ease, so that it was hard to understand
whether friends more respected him or loved him. Whatever he was asked, he
promised scrupulously, because he thought it the part not of generous men but
of light ones to promise what they could not make good. In keeping what he had
once agreed to, he had such care that he seemed to be doing not a commissioned
affair but his own. He never grew weary of a business once taken up: for he
thought his own reputation was at stake in it, than which he held nothing
dearer. From this it came about that he managed all the affairs of the
Ciceros, of Marcus Cato, of Quintus Hortensius, of Aulus Torquatus, and of
many other Roman knights besides. From which it can be judged that it was not
by inactivity but by judgment that he had avoided the management of the
republic.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Of his humanity I can bring no greater testimony than that the same man as a
youth was most pleasant to the old Sulla, as an old man to the young Marcus
Brutus, and with his own equals, Quintus Hortensius and Marcus Cicero, lived
so that it is hard to judge to which age he was most suited. Yet Cicero loved
him especially, so that not even his brother Quintus was dearer or more
intimate to him. Evidence of this, besides those books in which he makes
mention of him, which have been published abroad, are sixteen volumes of
letters sent to Atticus from his consulship even to the last time: he who
reads them would not much miss a connected history of those times. For all
things about the pursuits of the leading men, the faults of the commanders,
the changes of the republic, have been written out so that nothing in them is
not apparent, and it can easily be judged that prudence is in a way
divination. For Cicero not only foretold as future those things which happened
while he was alive, but even those which now come about he sang as a seer.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

But of Atticus's piety why should I mention more? When I myself heard him
truly glorying, at the funeral of his mother, whom he buried at ninety years,
when he himself was sixty-seven, that he had never needed to be reconciled
with his mother, because they had never been at variance, and that he had
never been at odds with his sister, whom he had nearly as his equal in age.
Which is a sign either that no complaint ever came between them, or that he
was of such indulgence toward his own that he counted it a sin to be angry
with those he ought to love. Nor did he do this by nature only, though we all
obey her, but also by teaching: for he had so taken in the precepts of the
chief philosophers that he used them for living life, not for show.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

He was also the greatest imitator of the manners of the ancestors and a lover
of antiquity, which he had so carefully learned that he set it all out in that
volume in which he arranged the magistrates. For there is no law nor peace nor
war nor illustrious affair of the Roman people which is not noted in it at its
own time, and, what was most difficult, he wove in the origin of families so
that from it we can know the lines of famous men. He did this same thing
separately in other books, so that at Marcus Brutus's request he enumerated
the Junian family in order from the stock to this age, noting who was born
from whom and what honors he had taken and at what times: in like manner, at
the request of Claudius Marcellus, the Marcelli; of Cornelius Scipio and
Fabius Maximus, the Fabii and the Aemilii. Than these books nothing can be
sweeter to those who have some desire for knowledge of famous men. He touched
poetry too, we believe, that he might not be without its sweetness. For in
verse he set out those who by honor and the greatness of their deeds surpassed
the rest of the Roman people, so that under the images of each he described
their deeds and magistracies in no more than four or five verses: which it is
scarce to be believed, that such great things could be made clear so briefly.
There is also one book composed in Greek, on Cicero's consulship.

* Chapter 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

These things thus far were published by us while Atticus was alive. Now, since
fortune has willed that we be his survivors, we will follow the rest and, as
far as we can, we will teach readers by examples of facts, as we have
indicated above, that each man's own character for the most part wins him his
fortune. For this man, content with the equestrian order from which he had
sprung, came into affinity with the commander, son of the deified, when he had
already before gained his friendship by no other thing than the elegance of
his life, by which he had taken the other leading men of the state, equal in
rank, humbler in fortune. For such prosperity followed Caesar that fortune
granted him everything she had ever conferred on anyone before, and won for
him what no Roman citizen has yet been able to attain. Now a granddaughter was
born to Atticus from Agrippa, to whom he had given his daughter as a virgin.
This girl, when she was scarcely a year old, Caesar betrothed to Tiberius
Claudius Nero, son of Drusilla, his stepson: which connection ratified their
bond and made their intimacy more frequent.

* Chapter 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

Yet even before this betrothal, not only, when he was away from the city, did
he never send a letter to any of his own without writing to Atticus what he
was doing, especially what he was reading and in what places and how long he
meant to stay, but even when he was in the city and, because of his endless
occupations, enjoyed Atticus less often than he wished, scarcely any day
passed on which he did not write to him, now asking him something about
antiquity, now putting some poetic question to him, sometimes in jest drawing
out his more wordy letters. From this it happened that when the temple of
Jupiter Feretrius on the Capitol, established by Romulus, was falling in, its
roof gone from age and neglect, Caesar at Atticus's reminder had it restored.
Nor was he cultivated less by letters from Marcus Antonius when absent, so
much so that Antony, from the farthest lands, took care to inform Atticus
carefully what he was doing. What this is like, he will more easily judge who
can estimate how great a wisdom it is to keep the intercourse and goodwill of
those between whom there was not only rivalry in the greatest affairs, but
such detraction as had to fall between Caesar and Antony, when each wished to
be first not only of the city of Rome but of the whole world.

* Chapter 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

When in this way he had completed seventy-seven years and had grown, even to
extreme old age, no less in rank than in favor and fortune (for he obtained
many inheritances by no other thing than goodness) and had enjoyed such
prosperity of health that for thirty years he had not needed medicine, he met
with a disease which at first both he and the doctors made light of: for they
thought it was tenesmus, for which swift and easy remedies were proposed. When
he had spent three months in this without any pains except those he took from
the treatment, suddenly so great a force of the disease burst into the lowest
intestine that at the last stage fistulas of pus broke out through the loins.
And before this happened to him, after he felt the pains growing day by day
and fevers added, he ordered his son-in-law Agrippa to be summoned to him, and
with him Lucius Cornelius Balbus and Sextus Peducaeus. When he saw they had
come, leaning on his elbow he said: "How much care and diligence I have used
at this time in watching over my health, since I have you as witnesses, there
is no need to recall in more words. Since I have satisfied these, as I hope,
that I have left nothing undone that pertained to healing me, it remains that
I take counsel for myself. I did not wish you to be ignorant of this: for I am
resolved to cease feeding the disease. For in these days whatever food I have
taken, I have so prolonged life that I have increased the pains without hope
of health. Therefore I ask of you, first that you approve my plan, then that
you do not try to hinder me by vain dissuasion."

* Chapter 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

When he had delivered this speech with such constancy of voice and look that
he seemed to be moving not out of life but from house to house, though
Agrippa, weeping and kissing him, begged and implored him not to hasten for
himself also what nature would compel, and, since even then he could survive
for a time, to keep himself for himself and his own, he bore down his prayers
by his silent obstinacy. So when he had abstained from food for two days,
suddenly the fever left him and the disease began to be lighter. Yet he
carried out his purpose none the less, and so on the fifth day after he had
