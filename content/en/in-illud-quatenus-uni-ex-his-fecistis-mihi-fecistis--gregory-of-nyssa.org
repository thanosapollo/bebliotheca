* On that saying, Inasmuch as you did it to one of these, you did it to me

OF GREGORY, BISHOP OF NYSSA, ON THE SAYING, INASMUCH AS YOU DID IT TO ONE OF
THESE, YOU DID IT TO ME I am still at the spectacle of the fearful appearing
of the king, which the gospel sketches; still the soul has cowered toward the
fear of the things spoken, gazing as if in some manner it saw the heavenly
king himself, as the word says, seated fearfully upon the throne of glory; and
that magnificent throne, whatever the throne also is that contains in itself
the uncontainable; and those infinite myriads of angels standing in a circle
about the king; and the great and fearful king himself stooping down from the
unutterable glory upon human nature and gathering to himself every race of men
found from when men came to be even until that fearful manifestation, and
bringing the judgment upon each according to the worth of =9.112= what has
been lived, to those who have lived with a right purpose granting the things
on the right, as it has been said, and to the left-handed and rejected
assigning the sentence corresponding to what they have lived, saying besides
to each of the two, to the ones that sweet and good voice, Come, you blessed,
and to the others the shuddering and fearful threat, Depart, you cursed. The
soul having been so disposed toward the fear of the things that have been
read, as to seem to be at the very matters themselves and to perceive nothing
of the things present, the mind has no leisure to look at anything else of the
things set forth for examination and contemplation by the word; and yet these
things are not small nor worthy of little inquiry, to know how he comes who is
always present. For behold, he says, I am with you all the days; and if he is
believed to be with us now, how does he promise that he will come as one not
present? For if in him we live and move and are, as the apostle says, there is
no device that will locally separate those held in him from the one who has
laid hold of all things, so as either not to be present now to those contained
in him or to expect that he will sometime be present later in time. And what
also is the sitting of the bodiless and the circumscription of the
uncontainable in the throne, and all such things, as greater than the present
occasion, I will put off. But that we may not be thrust away into the rank of
the rejected, toward =9.113= this, as I am able, I will turn the word toward
the common profit. For exceedingly, brothers, exceedingly I have been
terror-struck toward the threat, and I do not deny the passion of my soul. And
I would wish that you too not be contemptuous of the fears; For blessed is he
who cowers at all things through reverence. But he who despises a matter will
be despised by it, says somewhere the word of wisdom. Therefore before the
hour of the evils let us take thought not to come into experience of the grim
things. And what is the deliverance from the fearful things? To choose this
way of life which the word has now pointed out to us, the one that is truly
new and living. And what is the way? I hungered, I thirsted, I was a stranger
and naked and sick and in prison; inasmuch as you did it to one of these, you
did it to me. And because of this he says, Come, you blessed of my Father.
What do we learn through these things? That blessing is the care of the
commandments, and curse is slackness toward the commandments. Let us love the
blessing and flee the curse. For it is in our power to choose or not to choose
either according to authority; for toward whatever we incline with eagerness,
in that we shall be. Therefore let us make our own the Lord of the blessing,
who accounts to himself the zeal concerning those in need; and especially now,
when in the present life the commandment has much matter, and many are those
in want of the necessities, and many also have their body itself in want,
consumed by the wicked sickness. Therefore in the zeal concerning these let us
accomplish the good promise for ourselves; and I speak clearly thus concerning
those mutilated by the hard disease; for by how much =9.114= the sickness is
greater upon these, by so much it is clear that the blessing is more for those
who have accomplished the toilsome part of the commandment. What then must one
do? Not to resist the ordinance of the spirit; and this is not to be alienated
from those who share the nature, nor to imitate those accused in the gospel, I
mean the priest and the Levite, who uncompassionately passed by that one in
need of mercy, whom the narrative says was made half-dead by the robbers. For
if those are liable who did not turn toward the wounds swelling on the naked
body, how are we unaccountable, imitating the liable? And yet what was there
so great to behold upon the one who had fallen among the robbers, as is the
spectacle upon those taken by the passion? You see a man being remolded
through the wicked sickness into the form of a four-footed creature, taking
wood under the palms instead of hoof and nails, laying a strange track upon
human paths; who from the track would recognize that a man marked such
impressions on the way? A man, the one upright in form, the one looking toward
heaven, the one having the hands from nature for the service of works, this
one is bent to the earth and becomes four-footed and little falls short of
being irrational; in a thick and hoarse panting being forcibly submerged from
within from the inward parts, or rather, if one must dare to say it, becoming
even more wretched than the irrational creatures; for these =9.115= have for
the most part preserved the character from birth until the end, and none of
them from some such misfortune has been moved over into some other character.
But this one, as if the nature had been changed, appears as something else and
not the accustomed animal; his hands take the use of the feet, the knees
become bases, and the bases according to nature and the ankles either flow
away entirely or are dragged along as it happens, hanging idly as some of the
outer trailing-pieces. Seeing the man then in such things, do you not
reverence the kinship? Do you not pity the one of the same race, but you
loathe the misfortune and hate the suppliant, fleeing the approach as the
assault of some wild beast? And yet one ought reasonably to reckon that an
angel touches you the man, and being bodiless and immaterial he did not loathe
the one mixed with flesh and blood. And why do I speak of angels? The Lord of
the angels himself, the king of the heavenly blessedness, became man for you
and put around himself this foul and filthy little flesh together with the
soul bound in it, that he might heal your passions through his own touch. But
you, being the same according to nature as the one who has fallen sick, flee
the one of the same kind. No, brother, let the wicked counsel not please you.
Know who you are, concerning whom you take counsel; that a man concerning men,
having acquired nothing peculiar in yourself beyond the common nature. Do not
prejudge the future; for in that you condemn the passion appearing for the
time in another's body, you bring forth an unbounded sentence against the
whole nature; and you too share in the nature similarly with all. Therefore
let the word be as concerning a common matter. Why then does no pity of the
things seen enter you? =9.116= You see men nomads, as it were the cattle,
scattered toward the need of food; rags stitched about, this is the clothing;
a staff in the hands, this the weapon, this the vehicle; not even itself held
by fingers, but bound to the palms by certain handles; a torn wallet, and a
morsel of bread, ruined by mildew and rot; the hearth, the house, the pallet,
the bed, the store-chamber, the table, the whole provision of life is the
wallet. Then do you not reckon who is the one in these things being? That a
man, the one who has come to be according to the image of God, the one
appointed to rule the earth, the one having the service of the irrational
creatures under his hand. This one has come forward to this misfortune and
change, so that what appears is ambiguous, bearing upon himself neither the
marks of a man purely nor the clear marks of any other of the animals; if you
liken him to a man, the human character denies the shapelessness; if you turn
the likeness toward the irrational creatures, neither do those admit the
resemblance of what appears. Such people only, looking toward themselves alone
and herding with one another because of the like manner of the passion;
becoming loathsome to all others, but by necessity not loathing one another;
for being driven out from everywhere they become a people of their own,
flowing together toward one another. Do you see the unlovely dancers, this
wailing and downcast chorus? How they parade with their own misfortunes? How
they make a theater of nature behaving unseemly in themselves, like certain
wonder-workers displaying the manifold sicknesses to those who come together?
Poets of wailing songs, inventors of grim narratives, songmakers =9.117= of
those wicked chants, tragedians of this strange and unhappy tragedy, not using
others' tragic pieces toward the passions, but filling the stage through their
own evils. What the figures are; what the narratives. And what do we hear them
saying? How they were thrust away by those who begat them for no injustice;
how they are driven from cities, and driven from common assemblies, and from
feasts and festivals, as if certain man-slayers or father-beaters condemned to
perpetual exile, or rather far more unfortunate than those too; if indeed it
is permitted to the man-slayers, having moved elsewhere, to live with men, but
these alone of all are barred from everywhere as certain declared common
enemies, not deemed worthy of one roof, nor of a common table, nor of the use
of vessels. And this is not yet terrible. But neither do springs gush for
these in common with the other men, nor are rivers trusted not to drag
anything of the pollution of the sickness. If a dog laps the water with the
blood-eating tongue, the water was not thought loathsome because of the beast;
if the sick man draws near to the water, at once even the water has been
proclaimed away because of the man. These things they go through, these they
lament, because of this the wretched ones throw themselves by necessity before
men, becoming suppliants of everyone who happens by. Often I have wept over
this grim spectacle, often I have been out of patience toward nature itself,
and now at the memory I am confounded. I saw a pitiable passion, I saw a
spectacle full of tears; men lie along the roads of the men passing by, dead
men; or rather no longer men, but of those who were once men =9.118= wretched
remnants, needing certain tokens and marks in order to be believed. For they
do not have it in them to be recognized as men from the characters of nature;
alone of all hating themselves, alone keeping the birthday through a curse;
for they reasonably hate that day which began for them such a life. Men who
are even ashamed to name themselves from the common appellation, lest by the
sharing of the name they should insult the common nature through themselves;
always living with wailings, having an unceasing subject of the dirges. For as
long as they see themselves, they always have the occasions of mourning, being
at a loss as to what they lament more, the things of the body that no longer
are or the things that remain; how much the disease has consumed beforehand or
how much is left to the disease; that they see such things upon themselves or
that they are not even able to see, the sight having been darkened in the
passion; that they have such things to narrate concerning themselves or that
they cannot even narrate the passions, having been deprived of the voice from
the passion; that they use such food or that they do not even <receive> that
easily, the disease hindering the eating by the destruction of the parts about
the mouth; that they suffer the misfortunes of the dead in sensation or that
even of the sensations themselves they have been further deprived. For where
among them is the sight? And where the smell? And where the touch? And where
the rest of the sense-organs, which the rot grazes upon as the passion little
by little devours? Because of these things they wander through all the places,
just as the irrational creatures moving to the more abundant pasture; carrying
about the misfortune as provision of the traffic for food and holding out to
all =9.119= the disease instead of a supplication, and needing those who lead
by the hand through sickness, and propping one another up through the want.
For being weak each by himself they become a support to the other, using one
another's members in place of those that are lacking; for they do not appear
one by one, but even the misery has something wise toward the device of life,
their wishing to be seen with one another. For being pitiable each even by
themselves, that they may make men more compassionate, they become an addition
of the passion to one another, contributing the wicked contribution to the
common fund, another gathering pity for themselves through another misfortune.
One stretching out mutilated hands, another showing a swelling belly and
another a ruined face and another the leg rotting away, and another, having
stripped whatever part of the body it happens, has shown the passion in that.
What then? Is so much enough toward not sinning at all against the law of
nature, this very thing, to make a tragedy of the passions of nature and to
set out the disease in word and to be affected toward the memory? Or is there
need for us also of some work that displays toward such people the
compassionate and the love of one another? For what the shadow-paintings are
toward the things that truly are, this the words are when divorced from the
works. For the Lord says that salvation is not in the speaking, but in the
doing of the works of salvation. Therefore we must take hold for ourselves of
the commandment concerning them. For let no one say this, that it is enough to
furnish food to those settled far off somewhere from our life on some
outskirt; for such a judgment has not a certain display of pity and
compassion, but is some fair-faced device for the men to be utterly =9.120=
banished from our life. Then are we not ashamed of our own life, if we make
pigs and dogs roof-mates? Nor does the hunter often keep the puppy from his
own bed. The farmer also knows how to greet the calf with a kiss. And this is
not yet much, but with his own hands the traveler washes the feet of the ass
and lays the hand upon the wounds and cleans away the dung and cares for the
bed; and shall we make the one of the same kind and of the same race more
dishonored even than the irrational creatures themselves? Not these things,
no, brothers; let this vote not be ratified against men. It is fitting to
remember who we are, concerning whom we take counsel; that men concerning men,
having nothing peculiar beyond the common nature upon themselves. One is the
entrance into life for all; one is the manner of life for all, food and drink;
the activity of living is of like manner; the construction of the bodies is
one, and the overthrow of life; everything compound comes to dissolution; none
of the things that have been put together has a firm constitution, our body
having been stretched around the breath for a little in the manner of a
bubble; then we have been quenched, leaving no trace of this temporary puff to
life. The memorials are in stelae and stones and inscriptions, and not even
these lasting forever. Reckoning these things then upon yourself, Do not be
high-minded, as the apostle says, but fear; it is unclear whether you are not
legislating the harshness for yourself. Do you flee, tell me, the one who has
fallen sick? Having what to accuse? That the fluid has been corrupted in him
and some rotting humor has been sown in the blood, the black bile having been
poured into the fluid? For these things one can hear from physicians giving
the physiology of the passions. What then does the man wrong, if the nature,
being a flowing and unstable substance, =9.121= has slipped toward this form
of sickness? And do you not see also upon healthy bodies, that one otherwise
well-conditioned has often met with a blister or a carbuncle or some such
passion, the moist substance having been heated beyond what is due about the
part and having suffered through this both inflammation and redness and some
pus-like destruction? What then? Do we not make war on the part of the flesh
that has fallen sick? On the contrary; we turn all the healthy part of the
body toward the healing of the one that has fallen ill. Therefore the passion
is not loathsome. For otherwise even upon ourselves, when the part has
suffered, the healthy would be alienated from the healing of the one that is
in pain. But what is the cause that sets us apart from such people? What is
this? The not fearing the threat of the one who said, Depart from me into the
eternal fire; for inasmuch as you did not do it to one of these, he says, you
did not do it to me. For if they thought these things to be so, they would not
have had such a judgment concerning those who are sick, so as to thrust them
away from themselves and to think the zeal concerning the wretched a pollution
of our life. Therefore if we hold the one who has promised to be faithful, we
must take hold of the commandments, without which it is not possible to be
deemed worthy of the things hoped for. A stranger and naked and in want of
food, sick and shut in, and this one is all things to you, as many as have
been said beforehand in the gospel. He goes about a wanderer and naked, and
being ill and in need of the necessities through the poverty that follows the
passion. For the one who neither has anything from home nor is able to become
a hired man is of necessity wholly without the things serviceable for life,
and a prisoner bound by the sickness. Therefore you have the whole fullness of
the =9.122= commandments fulfilled in these, and the Lord of all himself
becoming indebted to you through the love of men toward this one. Why then do
you contend against your own life? For not to wish to have the God of all as a
housemate with oneself is nothing other than for someone to become strong
against himself. For as through the commandment he is housed in, so through
the harshness he is separated. Take, he says, my yoke upon you; and he calls
yoke the working of the commandments. Let us obey the one who commands; let us
become the yoked beast of Christ, binding ourselves with the yoke-straps of
love. Let us not shake off such a yoke; it is kind, it is light, it does not
rub the neck of the one who has come under it, but it smooths. Let us sow upon
blessings, says the apostle, that we may also reap upon blessings. The ear
will sprout abundant from such a sowing; deep is the standing crop of the
commandments of the Lord; high are the offspring of the blessing. Do you wish
to learn to how great a height the increase of the offspring is stretched? It
touches the heavenly heights themselves; for as many things as you do to
these, you bear fruit to the heavenly treasures. Do not despair of the things
said, nor reckon the friendship of such people contemptible. The hand has been
mutilated, but it is not weak toward alliance; the foot has been ruined, but
it is not hindered from running toward God; the eye has flowed out, but it
sees through the soul the good things that are unseen. Do not then look upon
the shapelessness of the body. Wait a little and you will see what is more
unbelievable than every wonder; for not as many things as have happened about
the flowing nature, these also remain forever. But when the soul has been
freed from the intertwining with the corruptible and earthly, then it is
adorned in its own beauty. And a proof: that =9.123= rich man, the luxurious
one, did not loathe the hand of the poor man after this life, but he thought
it worthy that the once-corrupted finger of the poor man become for him a
servant of the drop of water, desiring to lap with the tongue itself the
moisture about the finger of the poor man; he would not have desired this, if
he saw the unpleasantness from the body in the character of the soul. How many
recantations is it likely the rich man sang in vain in the change of life? How
many times to bless the poor man for the ill-lot in this life? How many times
to blame his own portion as having been allotted to wealth for the harm of the
soul? And if indeed it were permitted to live again, in what things would he
have accepted to come to be again? In those who are fortunate according to
this life or in those who are ill-fated? But it is not unclear that he would
have preferred the lot of those who fare badly. For he thinks it worthy that
someone from the dead become a messenger to the brothers, that they too,
having been harmed by the arrogance of wealth, may not be brought down in the
luxury of the flesh into the same gulf of Hades, having slipped through the
smoothness of pleasure. Why then are we not made temperate by such narratives?
Why do we not traffic in the fair traffic which the divine apostle counsels?
Your abundance, he says, for their lack, that the surplus of their relief in
the life after these things may suffice also for us toward salvation. If then
we wish to receive something useful, let us first furnish it; if to be
relieved after these things, let us now give rest. If to be received by them
into the eternal tents, let us now receive them in ours; if to have the wounds
of the sins healed, let us ourselves also do this to the bodies of those who
have been mistreated. For blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain
mercy. =9.124= But perhaps someone will say that the commandment is good for
later; but now he is wary of a certain transmission and communion of the
passion, and because of not suffering any of the unwanted things he thinks one
must flee the approach of such people. These are words and pretexts and
fictions and certain fair-faced coverings of slackness concerning the
commandments of God. But the true is not so; for no fear underlies the working
of the commandment. Let no one heal the evil with evil. For how many can one
see from youth even until old age occupying themselves with the healings of
these and having dimmed nothing of the natural good condition of the body
through such zeal? For it is not likely that these things come to be. But
since some of the diseases, such as the plague-bearing onsets and as many such
as hang from an outside cause, when they come to be from corruption of air or
of water, are suspected by the many as passing also from those already taken
to those who draw near, (not even there, as I think, the passion producing the
sickness in the healthy one from transmission, but the common onset bringing
on the likeness of the sickness) the disease had a cause as passing also from
those already taken to the rest. But here, the construction of such a passion
being constituted from within, and the blood undergoing a certain destruction
from the pouring-in of the destructive humors, the passion is limited in the
one who is in pain; and that these things are so, it is possible to learn also
from this; does any communion of the better condition come from those in good
health to those who are sick, even if they remain very persistently at the
healing? These things are not so. So then it is likely also conversely that
the passion does not pass from those who are weak to those in good health. If
then =9.125= the gain of the commandment is so great that through it a kingdom
of heaven is prepared, and no damage is attached to the body of the one who
heals, what is there left that hinders the commandment of love from being
active? But you say that it is toilsome to force the habit of the many that
naturally loathes such things? Well; I agree with the word, that it is
toilsome. And what else of the things accomplished according to virtue will
you show painless? The divine law ordered many sweats and toils of the
heavenly hopes and pointed out to men the way toward life as hard to travel,
having narrowed it from everywhere with the more toilsome and rougher
practices. For narrow and afflicted, he says, is the way that leads away into
life. What then? Shall we therefore neglect that hope of the good things,
because it is not possible to acquire it with ease? Let us ask youth, whether
temperance does not seem toilsome, or whether to use the desires without shame
is not much more longed for than the continent way of life. Shall we then
because of this hold to the one because it is sweet and easy, and shrink from
the roughness of virtue? These things do not seem so to the lawgiver of life,
who forbade the broad and downhill and spacious way according to life. For
enter, he says, through the narrow and afflicted gate. Therefore here too let
us reckon this also as one of the things accomplished through toil, to bring
into habit the commandment that has been alienated from life, and let us heal
the natural turning-away of the healthy by persistence in the matter. For
habit is terrible even at working a certain pleasure =9.126= through
persistence into the things that seem more difficult. Let no one then say that
it is toilsome, but that what is accomplished is beneficial to those who
pursue it. And since the gain is great, the toil is to be overlooked because
of the gain. And what is for the time toilsome will become sweet in time
through habit. And if one must add this also to the things said, that even
beside this very life the compassion toward the unfortunate is beneficial to
those who are healthy; for a fair contribution of pity is, for those who have
mind, stored up beforehand in the misfortunes of others. For since all that is
human is administered by one nature and no one has in himself any sure pledge
of continual good fortune, it is fitting always to remember the evangelical
injunction that counsels to do whatever we wish that men may do to us. While
then you sail well, stretch out a hand to the one who has been shipwrecked.
Common is the sea, common the surge, common the disturbance of the waves;
reefs and rocks and crags and the rest of the evils of the shipwreck of life
furnish equal fear to those who sail. While you are without passion, while you
sail through the sea of life without danger, do not pass unmercifully by the
one who has struck. Who is surety for you of the continual fair sailing? You
have not yet put in to the harbor of rest, you have not yet stood outside the
waves, life has not yet been set for you upon the firm. You are still borne
through life at sea. Such as you show yourself to the one who has been
unfortunate, such you will prepare the fellow-sailors for yourself. But may we
all arrive at the harbor of rest, having fair weather by the Holy Spirit
toward the proposed sailing of life. And may the working through the
commandments be present to us and the rudder of love; being steered through
which may we reach the land of the =9.127= promise, in which is the great
city, whose craftsman and maker is our God, to whom be the glory and the might
unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
