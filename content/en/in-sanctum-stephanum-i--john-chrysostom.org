On Saint Stephen, I.
=63.929=
The memory of the contests they have finished honors the renowned athletes;
let our speech also adorn them, as far as it can, by proclaiming their
achievements through praise of them =63.930=. For the recollection of their
virtue is schooling enough for our own ways. Let us therefore begin our
encomia of the Master's =63.931= athletes with the one who first opened the
stadium of piety, and who first began the athletic exercises. The grace of the
Spirit anointed him, and the heavenly president of the games trained him for
the wrestling: not boxing with the body, nor putting forth his hands for the
match, but sharpening his tongue against those who make war with their hands.
Let us then look at the athlete, how in all things he was shown an imitator of
the Master. For he had the same antagonists who had also been insolent against
that One; yet the saving Passion did not sate their cruelty. Those who
formerly poured out the Master's blood still thirst for blood, and those who
worked the Master's murder think the same against the servants.
Let us therefore bring forward in our speech the proven athlete. Even in the
games of those outside, when the crown-winners are judged, they are accustomed
to bring the chosen and the pre-selected into the wrestling, so that the
renowned crown-winner may be proclaimed the equal in strength. But here say:
the first champion, the unconquerable tower of piety (and if a man should
address him with all these titles he would not miss the mark) became, through
his own labors, a teacher of those who contended after him. Having first
seized the crowns, he showed even to those who followed that the enemies were
easy to handle, aiming only at the body and leaving the soul unwounded. Do not
fear, it says, those who kill the body but are not able to kill the soul.
Remembering then that divine voice which anointed him for the wrestling, the
athlete thus stripped for the contests. Wounding with the tongue those who
were shooting at the body with stones, he was shown by his words more powerful
than their missiles. With the body he received the wounds from the stones, but
with his words he wounded more bitterly those who were casting, and he
provoked them to the war against him, so that they might shorten for him the
journey that sends him up to the heavens. And consider with me the difference
of the missiles. They were burying the body with stones, but he, with the
words supplied to him by the Spirit, was aiming at their heart. For no one, it
says, was able to withstand the wisdom and the spirit by which he spoke. O
tongue louder than a trumpet! O soul that nothing can daunt, firmer than any
adamant! He saw the Jewish people assembling against the athlete, and as if he
were gazing at a mere shadow, so he despised those present. Receiving the
showers of stones against the body he rejoiced, as if some tower were being
built for him by the stones, so that through it he might reach heaven.
What then could be more powerful than that speaking rock? What could appear
firmer than the pillar of the Church? Faith kept him unbent. Leaning as upon
some most sure supports, so he remained unwounded, his body patterned with
wounds, but his soul made fair by endurance, and with his own beauty rivaling
heaven itself.
For as that heaven is shown most beautiful, embroidered with the chorus of the
stars, so also this marvelous athlete, wearing the Master's tunic, was made
fair by the wounds, as if clothed in varied garments, and he rejoiced, bearing
the marks of the Lord in the body as the Master's own stamps. The stones did
not turn the athlete about, but as some plant that reaches to heaven, rooted
in the earth, so he was kept unbent, receiving the casts of the stones and not
falling. For that prophetic saying would fit him well, that The righteous
shall flourish as a palm tree. The body then was made purple, and was flowing
round with streams of blood, as he received wave upon wave of stones, but far
more was he being made a rock by =63.932= faith. For he was looking toward the
heavenly president of the games, who stretches out the hand to his own
athletes and draws them toward heaven, where the prizes of the contests are
laid up, where the proclamation of the crowns takes place; and perhaps he
cries with Paul: So I run, not as uncertainly; so I box, not as beating the
air. I am struck, and I wound with words.
Remember, he says, your fathers who stoned the prophets. For which of them, he
says, did your fathers not persecute? You have become heirs of a wicked
succession, having emulated their madness. As your fathers, so you also. You
have inherited the paternal bloodshed. These are the words of the crown of the
Churches; such are the shafts of the crown-winner against the adversaries. And
as one kindling a flame in a grove, so also the protomartyr provoked their
madness still more against himself. For he saw the one he longed for, and he
longed for the one he beheld, and perhaps he uttered such things toward them:
You aim at the outer garment, and you send the stones against the body; but it
has escaped you, as it seems, that you are fulfilling my desire.
For I also, even before Paul, hasten to put off the outer man, that with a
naked soul I may fly up to heaven, and having crossed the ethereal sea, I may
reign together with the Master. This voyage alone has no shipwreck; this
seafaring keeps those who sail from every storm. My vessel sails without
danger, for I have a heavenly helmsman, who steers with his intelligible
rudders. Make my body purple with wounds; for your fathers also made Joseph's
tunic purple with blood, as you also do. For it is fitting that what was said
by the martyr be spoken to them continually. You have imitated those who left
you the evil inheritance; but they too, having sold Joseph, missed their hope,
and you who are stoning the bodily temple are building a firmer, more
beautiful temple. I am moved to see its gateways; for it is set from earth to
heaven. For these words are fitting for Stephen, who leapt forward from the
apostolic company to the contests, and with a swifter course reached the end
of martyrdom, who contended on earth and was proclaimed in the heavens; who
conquered the enemies as they spoke, and prayed for them as they stoned,
imitating the Master in this also, and he says to the president of the games:
Lord Jesus Christ, receive my spirit. My contest is finished, he says. Receive
therefore the crown-winner in the heavens; receive, O giver of the prizes, the
athlete. For I run toward the crowns, and I hasten to receive the prizes of
the contests here.
With so much the protomartyr has feasted us, shown renowned both in words and
in wrestling, the summit of the martyrs, the crown of the Churches, who was
eager to overtake the Master in the course, and who obeyed the one calling in
the heavens through that divine voice: Where I am, he says, there also is my
servant. Set your mind on the things above, it says, where Christ sits at the
right hand of the Father. He longed for that royal throne; he ran toward those
prizes of the upward calling; he longed for those royal gifts which the
heavenly king distributes to his own contestants. What speech then would
suffice to honor worthily so great and so high an athlete, who is proclaimed
by his deeds and has left the memory of his own achievements as an indelible
monument? Let us emulate his longing, and imitate his virtue, that we too may
enjoy the crowns laid up, by the grace of the only-begotten Son of God, with
whom to the Father be glory together with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and
unto the ages. Amen.
