* Book 1

You who were named by my first Muse, and must be named by my last, Maecenas,
though I have been shown enough and already given the wooden sword, you seek
to shut me again in the old school. Age is not the same, nor mind. Veianius,
his arms hung on Hercules' door-post, lies hidden away in the country, lest he
beg the crowd so often from the edge of the sand. There is one who dins into
my well-purged ear again and again: "Be wise in time: loose the aging horse,
lest at the last he stumble, a laughingstock, and strain his flanks." So now I
lay down verses and the rest of the playthings; I care for what is true and
fitting, I ask after it, and I am wholly in this; I store and set in order
what I may soon draw forth. And lest you chance to ask under what leader,
under what household god I take shelter: bound to swear by no master's words,
I am carried as a guest wherever the storm snatches me. Now I become active
and am plunged in the civic waves, a guardian and rigid attendant of true
virtue; now I slip back by stealth into Aristippus' precepts and try to
subject things to myself, not myself to things.

As night is long to those whom a mistress deceives, and the day seems long to
those who owe a day's work, as the year is slow to wards whom a mother's hard
watch presses: so sluggish and unwelcome the times flow for me that delay the
hope and the plan of doing in earnest that which profits the poor equally, the
rich equally, and neglected will harm boys and old men equally. It remains
that I rule and soothe myself with these first elements. You may not be able
to strain your eye as far as Lynceus, yet you would not on that account, being
blear-eyed, scorn to be anointed; nor, because you would despair of the limbs
of unconquered Glycon, would you refuse to keep your body from knotted gout.
It is something to go forward to a certain point, if it is not given to go
beyond.

The breast boils with avarice and miserable desire: there are words and tones
by which you may soothe this pain and lay down a great part of the disease.
You swell with love of praise: there are sure atonements which, when the
little book has been read through thrice in purity, will be able to restore
you. The envious man, the irascible, the idle, the drunken, the lover: no one
is so wild that he cannot grow tame, if only he lend a patient ear to culture.
Virtue is to flee vice, and the first wisdom is to have been without folly.
You see with how much toil of mind and body you avoid what you believe the
greatest evils, a slender income and a shameful defeat at the polls; as a
restless merchant you run to the farthest Indians, fleeing poverty through
sea, through rocks, through fire: will you not be willing to learn and hear
and trust a better man, so as not to care for those things which you foolishly
admire and desire? Who, a fighter around the villages and the crossroads,
would scorn to be crowned at great Olympia, if he had the hope, if he had the
terms, of the sweet palm without the dust?

Silver is cheaper than gold, gold than virtues. "O citizens, citizens, money
must be sought first; virtue after coin!" This Janus, from the highest to the
lowest, teaches in public; this the young and the old chant back as their
lesson, with satchel and tablet hung from the left arm. You have spirit, you
have character, you have tongue and good faith, but six or seven thousands are
wanting to the four hundred: you will be of the crowd. But the boys at play
say, "You will be king, if you do right." Let this be your wall of bronze: to
be conscious of no guilt, to turn pale at no fault.

Tell me, please, is the Roscian law better, or the boys' ditty that offers a
kingdom to those who do right, and was sung by the manly Curii and Camilli?
Does he advise you better who says, "Make a fortune, a fortune; if you can,
rightly; if not, a fortune by any means," so that you may sit nearer and watch
Pupius' tearful poems, or he who, present at your side, urges and fits you to
answer proud Fortune free and upright? But if the Roman people should chance
to ask me why I do not enjoy the same judgments as I do the same colonnades,
and neither follow nor flee what it itself loves or hates, I will repeat what
the wary fox once answered the sick lion: "Because the footprints frighten me,
all looking toward you, none back." You are a beast of many heads. For what am
I to follow, or whom? Part of men are eager to farm the public contracts;
there are those who hunt greedy widows with scraps and fruit and catch old men
to send into their fishponds; for many a fortune grows by secret usury. But
granted that different men are held by different affairs and pursuits: can the
same men last a whole hour approving the same things?

"No bay in the world outshines pleasant Baiae," if the rich man has said it,
the lake and the sea feel the love of the hurrying master; if a vicious
craving has made the omen for him, "Tomorrow, workmen, you will take the tools
to Teanum!" The marriage bed is in the hall: he says nothing is prior, nothing
better than a celibate life; if it is not, he swears it is well only for the
married. With what knot are you to hold Proteus as he changes his face? What
of the poor man? Laugh: he changes garrets, beds, baths, barbers; hiring a
boat he is just as seasick as the rich man whom a private trireme carries. If
I have met you with my hair dressed by an uneven barber, you laugh; if by
chance a worn undershirt is under a new tunic, or if the toga sits uneven, you
laugh: what, when my judgment fights with itself, scorns what it sought, seeks
again what it lately dropped, seethes and is at odds with the whole order of
life, pulls down, builds, changes square for round? You think it is my
ordinary madness and you do not laugh, nor believe I need a doctor or a
guardian given by the praetor, though you are the protector of my affairs and
grow angry at a wrongly cut nail of a friend who hangs upon you and looks to
you. In sum: the wise man is less than Jove alone, rich, free, honored,
handsome, in short a king of kings, especially sound, except when phlegm is
troublesome.

While you, greatest Lollius, are declaiming at Rome, I have read again at
Praeneste the writer of the Trojan war, who says more plainly and better than
Chrysippus and Crantor what is fair, what is base, what is useful, what is
not. Hear why I have so believed, unless something keeps you away. The story
in which, because of Paris' love, Greece is told to have been dashed against a
barbarian world in a lingering war contains the passions of foolish kings and
peoples. Antenor moves that they cut off the cause of the war: what of Paris?
He says he cannot be forced to reign in safety and live happy. Nestor is in
haste to settle the strife between the son of Peleus and the son of Atreus;
love burns the one, anger indeed burns both in common. Whatever the kings
rave, the Achaeans are beaten. By sedition, by tricks, by crime and lust and
anger, there is sinning within the Ilian walls and without.

Again, he has set before us Ulysses as a useful example of what virtue and
wisdom can do, he who, tamer of Troy, looked with foresight upon the cities
and manners of many men, and through the wide sea, while he made ready a
return for himself and for his companions, bore many hardships, unsinkable in
the adverse waves of things. You know the Sirens' voices and Circe's cups; if,
foolish and greedy, he had drunk them with his companions, he would have been
shameful and witless under a mistress who was a harlot, and would have lived
an unclean dog or a sow fond of mud. We are a number, and born to consume the
fruits, the worthless suitors of Penelope, and the youth of Alcinous, occupied
more than is right in caring for the skin, to whom it was a fine thing to
sleep to the middle of the day and to lead care to rest at the sound of the
cithara.

Robbers rise by night to cut a man's throat; will you not wake to save
yourself? And yet if you will not when sound, you will run when dropsical; and
unless you call for a book with a lamp before day, if you do not bend your
mind to studies and honorable things, you will be tortured awake by envy or by
love. For why do you haste to take away what hurts the eye, and if anything
eats the mind, put off the time of cure to a year? He who has begun has half
the deed; dare to be wise; begin! He who puts off the hour of living rightly
is the countryman who waits for the river to run dry; but it glides and will
glide, rolling on for every age.

Money is sought, and a wife for the begetting of blessed children, and
uncultivated woods are tamed by the plough: let him to whom enough has fallen
wish for nothing more. Not house and farm, not a heap of bronze and gold, has
drawn fever from the sick master's body, nor cares from the mind; the
possessor must be well, if he thinks to use well the goods he has gathered. He
who desires or fears, him house and property help as painted tablets help the
blear-eyed, as poultices help the gout, as citharas help ears aching with
gathered dirt. Unless the vessel is clean, whatever you pour in turns sour.
Scorn pleasures; pleasure bought with pain does harm. The greedy man always
wants; seek a fixed bound for your wish. The envious man grows thin at
another's fat things; Sicilian tyrants have not found a greater torment than
envy. He who will not govern his anger will wish undone what pain and the mind
have persuaded, while he hastens by force to take penalties from unavenged
hate. Anger is a brief madness: rule the mind; unless it obeys, it commands;
restrain it with reins, restrain it with a chain.

The master shapes the horse, docile at the tender neck, to go the road the
rider shows; the hunting pup, from the time it barked at a deer skin in the
hall, serves in the woods. Now take words to a pure breast, boy; now offer
yourself to better men. The jar will keep long the scent with which it was
once imbued when new. But if you delay, or if you press strenuously ahead, I
neither wait for the slow nor press upon those who go before.

Julius Florus, I am at pains to know on what shores of the earth Claudius,
stepson of Augustus, is on campaign. Do Thrace and the Hebrus bound with snowy
fetter delay you, or the straits running between neighboring towers, or the
rich plains and hills of Asia? What works is the studious cohort building?
This too I care to know. Who takes it upon himself to write the deeds of
Augustus? Who is spreading wars and peaces into a long age? What of Titius,
soon to come upon Roman lips, who did not grow pale at draughts of the
Pindaric spring, daring to scorn the lakes and open streams? How is he? How
does he remember us? Is he eager, with the Muse's blessing, to fit Theban
measures to Latin strings, or does he rage and swell in the tragic art? What
is Celsus doing for me? He has been warned, and must be warned much, to seek
private resources and to avoid touching whatever writings Palatine Apollo has
received, lest, if by chance the flock of birds should come some day to
reclaim their feathers, the little crow, stripped of stolen colors, move a
laugh. What do you yourself dare? About what thyme do you flit, agile? Yours
is no small talent, nor uncultivated and shamefully rough. Whether you sharpen
your tongue for causes, or prepare to answer on civic law, or compose a
lovable song, you will bear the first prizes of victorious ivy. But if you
could leave the cold poultices of cares, you would go where heavenly wisdom
led you. This work, this study, let us, small and great, hasten on, if we wish
to live dear to the fatherland and to ourselves. You must also write back
this: whether Munatius is as much in your care as is fitting; or does a badly
stitched grace come together in vain and is torn open? But whether hot blood
or ignorance of things vexes you, wild with untamed neck, wherever in the
world you live, unworthy to break a brotherly pact, a votive heifer is feeding
against your return.

Albius, candid judge of my satires, what shall I say you are doing now in the
Pedan country? Writing what may surpass the little works of Cassius of Parma,
or creeping silent among the healthful woods, caring for whatever is worthy of
a wise and good man? You were not a body without a breast: the gods have given
you beauty, the gods have given you riches and the art of enjoying them. What
more would a fond nurse wish for her sweet charge, who can be wise and say
what he feels, and to whom grace, fame, and health fall in plenty, and a neat
living with a purse that does not fail? Amid hope and care, amid fears and
angers, believe that every day has dawned as your last; the hour that will not
be hoped for will come upon you welcome. You will visit me, fat and sleek,
with skin well cared for, when you want to laugh, a hog from Epicurus' herd.

If you can recline as a guest on Archias' couches and are not afraid to dine
on greens from a modest dish, I will wait for you at home at sundown,
Torquatus. You will drink wine drawn in Taurus' second consulship, between
marshy Minturnae and Petrinum of Sinuessa. If you have anything better, send
for it, or bear the command. The hearth has long been shining, and the
furnishings are clean for you. Send away light hopes and contests of riches
and Moschus' case: tomorrow, a festival for Caesar's birthday, gives pardon
and sleep; it will be allowed without penalty to stretch the summer night with
kindly talk. What is fortune to me, if it is not granted to use it? He who is
sparing from care for an heir, and too severe, sits next to the madman. I will
begin to drink and scatter flowers, and I will suffer even to be held rash.
What does drunkenness not unseal? It opens what is covered, bids hopes be
ratified, pushes the idle to battles, takes the load from anxious minds,
teaches arts besides. Whom have the full cups not made eloquent? Whom have
they not made free in cramped poverty? These things I am ordered to see to,
fit for it and not unwilling: that no shameful coverlet, no dirty napkin
wrinkle the nose; that both tankard and platter show you yourself; that among
trusted friends there be none who carries words outdoors; that like meet and
be joined with like. I will take Butra for you and Septicius, and Sabinus,
unless a prior dinner and a more winning girl keep him. There is room for more
shadows too; but too crowded feasts are pressed by smelling goats. Write back
how many you wish to be, and, dropping your affairs, slip out by the back door
past the client keeping the hall.

To marvel at nothing is almost the one and only thing, Numicius, that can make
and keep a man happy. There are those who can look on this sun and the stars
and the seasons withdrawing at fixed moments, imbued with no fear: what do you
think of the gifts of the earth, what of the sea that enriches the farthest
Arabs and Indians, what of the playthings, the applause and the gifts of the
friendly Quirite, in what way, with what feeling and face, do you believe they
should be looked on? He who fears the opposites of these almost marvels in the
same way as he who desires: dread is troublesome on both sides; a sudden sight
frightens both alike. Whether he rejoices or grieves, desires or fears, what
does it matter, if, whatever he has seen better or worse than his hope, he is
numb with eyes fixed, in mind and in body? Let the wise man bear the name of
madman, the just of unjust, if he seek Virtue herself beyond what is enough.

Go now, gaze up at silver and old marble and bronzes and arts; marvel at
Tyrian colors with gems; rejoice that a thousand eyes watch you while you
speak; busy, seek the forum in the morning and your roof at evening, lest
Mutus reap more grain from dowered fields, and (shame, that he was born of
worse stock) lest he be more marvelous to you than you to him. Whatever is
under the earth, time will bring into the open, and will bury and hide what
shines. When the portico of Agrippa and the Appian Way have seen you well
known, it still remains to go where Numa and Ancus have come. If side or
kidneys are assailed by acute disease, seek flight from the disease. You wish
to live rightly: who does not? If Virtue alone can give this, go to it
bravely, dropping the delights. You think Virtue is words and a grove is
firewood: take care that another does not occupy the harbor, that you do not
lose the Cibyratic and the Bithynian business; let a thousand talents be
rounded, then as many again, then let a third follow, and the part that
squares the heap. Of course queen Money gives a wife with a dowry, and good
faith, and friends, and birth, and beauty, and Persuasion and Venus adorn the
well-coined man. The king of the Cappadocians, rich in slaves, is poor in
cash: do not you be this man. Lucullus, they say, asked if he could furnish a
hundred cloaks for the stage, said, "How can I so many? Still, I will look,
and I will send as many as I have." A little later he writes that he has five
thousand cloaks at home; they might take part or all. A house is poor where
many things do not overflow, and escape the master, and profit thieves.
Therefore if property alone can make and keep a man happy, be first to take up
this work again, last to lay it down.

If appearance and favor make a man fortunate, let us buy a slave who may
dictate names, who may poke the left side and force the right hand to reach
across the stepping-stones: "This man has much weight in the Fabian tribe,
that one in the Veline; this man, if he likes, will give the fasces and,
importunate, will snatch the curule ivory from whom he wills." Add "brother,"
add "father": according to each man's age, adopt each wittily. If he who dines
well lives well, it is light: let us go where the gullet leads; let us fish,
let us hunt, as once Gargilius, who in the morning ordered nets,
hunting-spears, and slaves to pass through the packed forum and the people, so
that one mule out of many, with the people watching, might bring back a bought
boar. Then let us bathe, undigested and swollen, forgetful of what is fitting
and what is not, worthy of the Caerite wax, the vicious crew of Ithacan
Ulysses, to whom forbidden pleasure was better than the fatherland. If, as
Mimnermus judges, nothing is pleasant without love and jests, live in love and
jests. Live, farewell. If you have learned anything righter than these things,
share it candidly; if nothing, use these with me.

Having promised you I would be in the country five days, I am missed, a liar,
for the whole of Sextilis. And if you wish me to live sound and rightly well,
you will give me, Maecenas, fearing to be sick, the pardon you give me when I
am sick, while the first fig and the heat adorn the undertaker with black
lictors, while every father and little mother grows pale for the boys, and
officious busyness and little forensic labor bring on fevers and unseal wills.
But if winter spreads snows on the Alban fields, your bard will go down to the
sea and spare himself and, drawn in, will read; he will visit you again, sweet
friend, with the Zephyrs, if you allow, and the first swallow.

Not in the way the Calabrian host bids one eat pears have you made me rich.
"Eat, please." "It is already enough." "But take as much as you will."
"Kindly, no." "You will carry little gifts not unwelcome to the small boys."
"I am as bound by the gift as if I were sent away loaded." "As you like; you
will leave these to be eaten by the pigs today." The prodigal and the fool
give what they scorn and hate; this crop has borne ingrates and will bear them
every year. The good and wise man says he is ready for the worthy, and yet he
is not ignorant of what bronze differs from lupines. I will show myself worthy
even in proportion to the praise of him who deserves it. But if you will not
have me go away anywhere, you will give back my strong side, the black hair on
a narrow brow, you will give back sweet speech, you will give back becoming
laughter, and to mourn amid the wine the flight of forward Cinara.

A thin little fox had by chance crept through a narrow crack into a bin of
grain, and, fed, was trying again to go out with a full body, in vain. To whom
a weasel from afar said, "If you wish to escape from there, you will seek the
narrow hole lean, which you entered lean." If I am addressed with this image,
I resign all; I neither praise the sleep of the people when I am stuffed with
fatted fowl, nor do I exchange my leisure for the richest riches of the Arabs.
You have often praised me as modest, and you have heard "king" and "father" to
your face, nor more sparingly in word when absent: look whether I can put back
the gifts gladly. Not badly Telemachus, offspring of patient Ulysses: "Ithaca
is not a place fit for horses, as it is neither stretched in level spaces nor
prodigal of much grass: son of Atreus, I will leave your gifts, more fit for
you." Small things become the small: already not royal Rome, but empty Tibur
pleases me, or unwarlike Tarentum.

Philippus, strenuous and brave and famous in causes to be pleaded, while he
was returning from his duties about the eighth hour and, already old,
complaining that the Carinae were too far from the Forum, caught sight, they
say, of a close-shaved man in the empty shade of a barber, gently cleaning his
own nails with a little knife. "Demetrius" (this boy was not awkward in taking
Philippus' orders), "go, seek and bring back from what home he is, who, of
what fortune, of what father or of what patron." He goes, returns, and reports
that the name is Volteius Mena, a crier, of slender census, without crime,
known both to hurry in season and to rest, both to seek and to use, rejoicing
in small companions and a sure hearth and the games and the Campus after
business is cut off. "I should like to ask of the man himself whatever you
report: tell him to come to dinner." Mena truly would not believe it, marveled
silently to himself. Why many words? "Kindly, no," he answers. "Would he
refuse me?" "He refuses, the shameless man, and either neglects you or dreads
you." Philippus in the morning comes upon Volteius selling cheap junk to the
tunic-clad crowd and is first to bid him hail. The other apologizes to
Philippus for his labor and hired bonds, that he had not come to the house in
the morning, last that he had not seen him coming. "Think I have so forgiven
you, if you dine with me today." "As you like." "Then you will come after the
ninth hour: now go, increase your property strenuously." When they had come to
dinner, having spoken what should be said and what should not, he is at last
sent off to sleep.

When he had often been seen to run down like a fish to the hidden hook, a
client in the morning and already a sure guest, he is ordered to go as
companion to the suburban fields at the proclaimed Latin festival. Set on the
Gallic cobs he does not cease to praise the Sabine field and sky. Philippus
sees and laughs, and while he seeks rest for himself, while he seeks laughs
from every side, while he gives seven thousand sesterces and promises seven as
a loan, he persuades him to buy a little farm. He buys. Not to delay you with
long windings beyond what is enough, from sleek he becomes a countryman and
rattles of furrows and vineyards only, prepares elms, dies upon his pursuits,
and grows old with love of having. But when the sheep have perished by theft,
the goats by disease, the crop has belied hope, the ox is killed by ploughing,
offended by the losses he snatches the nag in the middle of the night and,
angry, makes for Philippus' house. As soon as Philippus saw him rough and
unshorn, "You seem to me, Volteius," he says, "too hard and too intent." "By
Pollux, you would call me wretched, patron, if you wished," he says, "to put
my true name on me. What I beg and beseech you by your Genius and your right
hand and the household gods, give me back to my former life!"

He who has once seen how much what was given up excels what is sought should
return in time and seek again what was left. It is true that each man measure
himself by his own last and foot.

Muse, when asked, report to Celsus Albinovanus that I rejoice and that his
affairs go well, companion and secretary of Nero. If he asks what I am doing,
say that, threatening many fine things, I live neither rightly nor pleasantly;
not because hail has crushed the vines and heat has bitten the olive, nor
because the herd is sick in far fields; but because, less sound in mind than
in the whole body, I should wish to hear nothing, to learn nothing that might
relieve the sick man; I take offense at faithful doctors, I grow angry at
friends, why they haste to keep me from a fatal lethargy; I will follow what
has harmed, I will flee what I believe would profit; at Rome I love Tibur,
weathercock, at Tibur Rome. After this, ask how he is, on what terms he
manages his affairs and himself, how he pleases the young man and the cohort.
If he says "Rightly," first rejoice, then remember to instill this precept
into his ears: "As you bear fortune, Celsus, so we shall bear you."

Septimius, Claudius, understands, of course, alone how much you make of me.
For when he asks and forces me by prayer, naturally, that I try to praise him
to you and introduce him, as worthy in mind and house of Nero who chooses
honorable things, when he thinks I perform the office of a closer friend, he
sees and knows what I can do more strongly than I myself. I have said many
things indeed why I might go away excused; but I feared I might be thought to
have invented my powers as smaller, a dissembler of my own resource,
convenient to myself alone. So I, fleeing the reproaches of a greater fault,
have come down to the prizes of a city brow. But if you praise the shame laid
down at a friend's orders, write this man of your flock and believe him brave
and good.

We lovers of the country bid Fuscus, lover of the city, hail, in this one
thing of course much unlike, but in the rest almost twins, with brotherly
minds (whatever the one denies, the other also), we nod alike like old and
known doves. You keep the nest; I praise the streams of the pleasant country
and the rocks smeared round with moss and the grove. What would you have? I
live and I reign, as soon as I have left those things which you raise to
heaven with favoring rumor, and like a priest's runaway I refuse the cakes; I
already want bread, better than honeyed pancakes.

If one ought to live in agreement with Nature, and if first an area must be
sought for setting a house, do you know a place better than the blessed
country? Is there where winters are warmer, where a more welcome breeze
soothes both the rage of the Dog and the moments of the Lion, when once the
furious one has received the sharp Sun? Is there where less envious Care tears
sleep apart? Does the grass smell worse or shine less than Libyan pebbles?
Does purer water in the streets strain to burst the lead than that which
hurries with a murmur down a sloping stream? To be sure, a wood is nourished
among varied columns, and the house is praised that looks out on long fields.
You will drive Nature out with a fork, yet she will always run back, and,
victorious, will stealthily break through sick fastidiousness.

Not he who, clever, does not know how to match Sidonian purple with fleeces
drinking the dye of Aquinum, will take a loss more certain or nearer the
marrow than he who cannot distinguish the false from the true. Whom second
things have delighted more than is right, changed things will shake. If you
marvel at anything, you will put it down unwilling. Flee great things: under a
poor roof one may outrun kings and the friends of kings in life. A stag,
better in fight, was driving the horse from the common grass, until the lesser
in the long contest implored the help of man and received the bit; but after
the violent victor departed from the enemy, he did not throw the rider from
his back, nor the bit from his mouth. So he who, fearing poverty, lacks
liberty, better than metals, will carry a master, shameless, and will be a
slave forever, because he will not know how to use a little. To whom his own
property will not fit, as a shoe once, if it is larger than the foot, will
trip, if smaller, will burn.

Happy in your lot you will live wisely, Aristius, nor will you send me away
unchastised when I seem to gather more than enough and not to rest. Collected
money commands or serves each man, worthy rather to follow the twisted rope
than to lead it. I was dictating these things to you behind the crumbling
shrine of Vacuna, happy in the rest, except that you were not with me.

What did Chios seem to you, Bullatius, and known Lesbos, what neat Samos, what
Sardis the palace of Croesus, what Smyrna and Colophon? Greater or less than
fame? Do they all look cheap beside the Campus and the Tiber stream? Or has
one of the Attalid cities come into your vow, or do you praise Lebedus from
hate of sea and roads? You know what Lebedus is: a village more deserted than
Gabii and Fidenae; yet I would wish to live there, and, having forgotten my
people, to be forgotten by them too, and to watch Neptune raging far from the
land. But neither will he who seeks Rome from Capua, sprinkled with rain and
mud, wish to live in an inn; nor does he who has gathered cold praise ovens
and baths as fully furnishing a fortunate life; nor, if a strong south wind
has tossed you on the deep, would you therefore sell the ship across the
Aegean sea. To a man who is sound, Rhodes and fair Mytilene do what a cloak
does at the solstice, a light garment in snowy airs, the Tiber in midwinter, a
furnace in the month of Sextilis. While it is allowed and Fortune keeps a
kindly face, let Samos and Chios and Rhodes be praised at Rome in absence.
Take with a grateful hand whatever hour a god has made fortunate for you, and
do not put off sweet things to a year, so that wherever you have been you may
say you have lived gladly. For if reason and prudence, not a place that
commands a sea poured wide, take away cares, they change their sky, not their
mind, who run across the sea. A strenuous idleness exercises us: with ships
and with chariots we seek to live well. What you seek is here, it is at
Ulubrae, if an even mind does not fail you.

If you enjoy rightly the Sicilian fruits of Agrippa which you gather, Iccius,
it is not that a greater plenty can be given you by Jove. Take away
complaints: for he is not poor to whom the use of things is at hand. If it is
well with your belly, if with your side and your feet, royal riches will be
able to add nothing greater. If by chance, abstaining from things set in the
middle, you live on herbs and nettle, you will live on so, even if the liquid
stream of Fortune should gild you at once, either because money does not know
how to change nature, or because you think all things less than virtue alone.
We marvel if Democritus' flock ate his little fields and tilled ground, while
his mind, swift without the body, is abroad; when you, amid such a scab and
contagion of gain, have no small wisdom and still care for high things: what
causes check the sea, what tempers the year, whether the stars wander and
stray of their own will or under orders, what presses the moon's orb into
darkness, what brings it forth, what the discordant concord of things wills
and can do, whether Empedocles or the acumen of Stertinius is mad. But whether
you butcher fish or leek and onion, use Pompeius Grosphus, and if he asks
anything, offer it of yourself; Grosphus will beg nothing but what is true and
fair. The market of friends is cheap when something is wanting to the good.
Yet that you may not be ignorant of how Roman affairs stand: the Cantabrian
has fallen by Agrippa's valor, the Armenian by that of Claudius Nero;
Phraates, lesser on his knees, has accepted the law and command of Caesar;
golden Plenty has poured the fruits of Italy from a full horn.

As I taught you often and long when you were setting out, Vinnius, you will
deliver the sealed volumes to Augustus, if he is well, if he is glad, if, in
short, he asks; lest by zeal for us you offend and, a busy minister with
violent service, import hatred upon the little books. If by chance the heavy
pack of my paper burns you, throw it down rather than fiercely dash the
saddlebags where you are ordered to carry them, and turn your father's surname
Asina into a laugh and become a fable. You will use your strength through
gods, rivers, bogs. As soon as, victor of your purpose, you have arrived
there, you will keep the burden so placed that you do not by chance carry the
little bundle of books under your arm as a countryman a lamb, as drunken
Pyrria a ball of stolen wool, as a tribesman guest his slippers with his
little cap. And do not tell the crowd that you have sweated in carrying songs
that might delay the eyes and ears of Caesar. Asked with much prayer, press on
further. Go; farewell; take care you do not stumble and break the charges.

Bailiff of the woods and of the little farm that restores me to myself, which
you disdain, inhabited by five hearths and accustomed to send five good
fathers to Varia, let us contend whether I more bravely pull thorns from the
mind or you from the field, and whether Horace or his property is better.
Though the piety and care of Lamia delay me, as he mourns a brother, grieving
inconsolably for a brother snatched away, yet mind and spirit bear me there
and love to break the bars that stand in the way of the course. I call him who
lives in the country happy, you him who lives in the city. To whom the other's
lot is pleasing, his own of course is hateful. Each fool unfairly blames the
undeserving place: the fault is in the mind, which never escapes itself. You,
a drudge, were seeking the country with silent prayer; now, a bailiff, you
wish for the city and the games and the baths. You know that I agree with
myself and leave sad whenever hated business drags me to Rome. We do not
marvel at the same things; that is where it disagrees between me and you. For
what you believe deserted and inhospitable wastes, he who feels with me calls
pleasant, and hates what you think fair. The arch and the greasy cookshop
strike into you a longing for the city, I see, and because that nook will bear
pepper and incense sooner than a grape, and no neighboring tavern is at hand
that can offer you wine, nor a flute-girl harlot at whose noise you may dance,
heavy on the earth; and yet you urge fields long untouched by mattocks, and
you care for the unyoked ox and fill him with stripped leaves; the stream adds
work to the lazy man, if rain has fallen, to be taught with much mass to spare
the sunny meadow.

Come now, hear what divides our harmony. Him whom thin togas and shining hair
became, whom you know pleased greedy Cinara though he brought no gift, whom
you know a drinker of clear Falernian from the middle of daylight, a short
dinner pleases, and sleep on the grass near a stream. It is not shame to have
played, but not to cut off the play. There no one files my advantages with a
sidelong eye, none poisons them with dark hate and a bite. The neighbors laugh
at me moving clods and stones. You prefer to gnaw urban rations with the
slaves; you rush by vow into their number: the sharp groomsman envies you the
use of wood and cattle and garden. The ox wishes for saddle-cloths, the lazy
nag wishes to plough. I will judge that each gladly practice the art he knows.

What the winter of Velia is, Vala, what the sky of Salernum, what manner of
men the region, and what the road (for Antonius Musa makes Baiae superfluous
for me, and yet makes me hated by them, when I am soaked in cold water through
the middle of the cold; the village groans that the myrtle groves are left and
that the sulphurs said to drive out a lingering disease from the nerves are
scorned, envious of the sick who dare to put head and stomach under the
springs of Clusium and seek Gabii and the cold country. The place must be
changed, and the horse driven past the known lodgings. "Where are you heading?
My journey is not to Cumae or Baiae," the rider, angry at the left rein, will
say; but the horse's ear is in his bitted mouth); which people the greater
plenty of grain feeds; whether they drink collected rains or perennial wells
of running water (for I care nothing for the wines of that shore: on my own
farm I can bear and suffer anything; when I have come to the sea, I require a
generous and mild wine, which may drive cares away, which may flow with rich
hope into my veins and mind, which may furnish words, which may commend me as
a young man to a Lucanian girlfriend); which tract will raise more hares,
which boars; which waters hide more fish and sea-urchins, so that from there I
may be able to return home fat and a Phaeacian: it is fair that you write to
us, and that we credit you.

Maenius, when he had bravely used up his mother's and father's goods, began to
be held a city wit, a wandering scurra, not one who kept a fixed stall, not
one who, unbreakfasted, would distinguish citizen from enemy, savage in
inventing any insults against anyone, the ruin and tempest and pit of the
meat-market, whatever he had gotten he gave to his greedy belly. When he had
taken nothing or little from the patrons of naughtiness and the timid, he
would dine on platters of tripe, cheap and of lamb, enough for three bears; of
course so that, a corrected Bestius, he might say the spendthrifts' bellies
should be burned with white-hot plate. The same man, whatever greater prey he
had gotten, when he had turned it all into smoke and ash, would say, "By
Hercules I do not marvel if some eat up their goods, since there is nothing
better than a fat thrush, nothing fairer than a roomy sow's womb." This, of
course, I am. For I praise safe and little things when means fail, brave
enough among cheap stuff; but when something better and richer befalls, the
same man, I say that you alone are wise and live well, whose money is seen
founded on shining villas.

Lest you ask, best Quinctius, whether my farm feeds its master with ploughland
or enriches him with the berries of the olive, with fruit or with meadows or
with elm dressed in vines, the shape and site of the land will be written to
you talkatively. Continuous mountains, unless they were parted by a shady
valley, but so that the coming sun looks on the right side, and, departing,
warms the left with fleeing chariot. You would praise the temper. What if the
kind thorns bear red cornel and plums? If oak and holm-oak help the flock with
much fruit, the master with much shade? You would say Tarentum, drawn nearer,
was in leaf. A spring too, fit to give a name to a stream, so that neither a
colder nor a purer Hebrus winds through Thrace, flows useful to a weak head,
useful to the bowels. These sweet retreats, even, if you believe it, pleasant,
keep me sound for you in the September hours.

You live rightly, if you care to be what you are heard to be. All we at Rome
have long been tossing you as blessed; but I fear you may believe anyone more
than yourself about you, and may think another than the wise and good man
blessed, and, if the people keep saying you are sound and rightly well, you
may hide a secret fever toward the time of eating, until a tremor falls on
greasy hands. A bad shame hides the uncured sores of fools. If someone should
tell you of wars fought by you on land and sea, and soothe empty ears with
these words: "Whether the people would wish you more safe, or you the people,
let Jupiter keep in doubt, who consults both for you and for the city," you
could recognize the praises of Augustus. When you suffer yourself to be called
wise and amended, do you answer, tell me please, in your own name? "Of course
I am delighted to be called a good and prudent man, I and you." He who gave
this today, if he wills, will take it tomorrow, as if he has conferred the
fasces on an unworthy man, he will pull them off likewise. "Put it down, it is
mine," he says: I put it down and withdraw sad. If the same man should cry
thief, deny that I am chaste, contend that I have pressed a father's neck with
a noose, should I be bitten by false reproaches and change colors? False honor
helps, and lying infamy frightens, whom except the faulty and the man who
needs healing?

Who is the good man? "He who keeps the decrees of the fathers, who keeps the
laws and like things, by whose judgment many great suits are cut, by whose
surety property and by whose witness causes are held." But the whole house and
the whole neighborhood sees this man ugly within, handsome with a fair hide.
"I have not done theft nor run away," if a slave should say to me, "You have
your price, you are not burned with thongs," I say. "I have not killed a man."
"You will not feed ravens on a cross." "I am good and honest." The Sabine
shakes his head and keeps denying. For the cautious wolf fears the pit, and
the hawk the suspected snares, and the kite the covered hook. The good hate to
sin from love of virtue. You will admit nothing into yourself from fear of
punishment: let there be hope of escaping notice, you will mix sacred with
profane. For when you snatch one from a thousand pecks of beans, the loss is,
not the crime, milder to me by that pact. The good man, whom the whole forum
and every tribunal watches, whenever he placates the gods with pig or ox, when
he has said "Father Janus!" clearly, clearly "Apollo!", moves his lips fearing
to be heard: "Fair Laverna, grant me to deceive, grant me to seem just and
holy; throw night on my sins and a cloud on my frauds."

Who is better than a slave, who more free than the miser, when he stoops
himself at the crossroads for a fixed penny, I do not see; for he who will
desire will also fear; further, he who will live fearing will never be free to
me. He has lost his arms, he has deserted the post of Virtue, who always
hastens in increasing property and is overwhelmed by it. When you can sell a
captive, do not kill him; he will serve usefully; let him feed, a hard man,
and plough, let him sail and winter as a merchant in the middle of the waves,
let him profit the grain-market, let him carry grain and stores. The good and
wise man will dare to say: "Pentheus, ruler of Thebes, what unworthy thing
will you force me to bear and suffer?" "I will take away your goods." "Of
course flock, property, beds, silver: you may take them." "I will hold you in
manacles and fetters under a savage keeper." "The god himself, as soon as I
will, will loose me." I think he means this: "I shall die." Death is the last
line of things.

Although, Scaeva, you consult enough for yourself by yourself and know on what
terms at last it is fitting to use greater men, learn what a little friend
still needing to be taught judges, as if a blind man should wish to show the
way; yet see if we too say anything that you may care to have made your own.
If welcome quiet and sleep to the first hour delight you, if dust and the
noise of wheels, if the inn hurts you, I will order you to go to Ferentinum.
For joys do not fall to the rich alone, nor has he lived badly who, born and
dying, has escaped notice. If you wish to profit your own and to treat
yourself a little more kindly, you will approach dry to the anointed. "If he
could dine on greens patiently, Aristippus would not wish to use kings." "If
he who marks me knew how to use kings, he would scorn greens." Teach which of
these men's words and deeds you approve, or, being younger, hear why
Aristippus' opinion is better. For he so eluded the biting Cynic, they say: "I
play the scurra for myself, you for the people; this is righter and far more
splendid. That a horse may carry me, that a king may feed me, I do a service;
you ask for cheap things, but you are less than the giver, though you bear
yourself as needing no one." Every color and status and circumstance became
Aristippus, attempting greater things, generally even with present things. On
the other side, him whom patience clothes with double rag, I shall marvel if a
changed road of life will become him. The one will not wait for a purple
cloak; dressed in anything he will go through the most crowded places, and
will wear either mask not unbecomingly. The other will avoid a cloak woven at
Miletus worse than a dog and a snake; he will die of cold if you do not bring
back the rag. Bring it back and let him live, inept.

To manage affairs and show captured enemies to the citizens touches the throne
of Jove and attempts heavenly things. To have pleased leading men is not the
last praise. It does not fall to every man to go to Corinth. He sat who feared
he might not succeed. "So be it. What of him who arrived: did he do it like a
man?" And yet here is, or nowhere, what we seek. This man dreads the burden as
greater than small spirits and a small body; this man goes under it and bears
it through. Either virtue is an empty name, or the man who tries rightly seeks
honor and reward. Those who are silent about their poverty before their king
will carry off more than the one who asks: it differs whether you take
modestly or snatch; and yet this was the head of the matter, this the source.
"My sister is undowered, my mother a poor little woman, and the farm neither
saleable nor firm to feed," he who says this cries, "Give food!" Another sings
along, "And to me!" The loaf will be split by a divided gift. But if the crow
could feed silent, he would have more of the feast and much less of quarrel
and envy.

He who, taken as companion to Brundisium or pleasant Surrentum, complains of
the ruts and the bitter cold and the rains, or weeps over a broken box and
stolen travel-money, brings back the known tricks of a harlot, often weeping
that a necklace, often that an anklet has been snatched from her, so that soon
no faith is given to losses and true griefs, nor does he who has once been
mocked in the streets care to lift a pretender with a broken leg, though many
a tear flow for him, and though he swear by holy Osiris: "Believe, I am not
playing; cruel ones, lift the lame man!" "Seek a stranger," the hoarse
neighborhood shouts back.

If I know you well, most free Lollius, you will fear to offer the look of a
scurra, having professed yourself a friend. As a matron will be unlike a
harlot and of another color, a friend will differ from an unfaithful scurra.
There is a vice diverse from this vice, almost greater, a rustic harshness,
unbecoming and heavy, which commends itself with shaven skin and black teeth,
while it wishes to be called sheer liberty and true virtue. Virtue is a mean
of vices and drawn back from both sides. The one, more prone to compliance
than is right, a mocker of the lowest couch, so dreads the rich man's nod, so
repeats voices and picks up falling words, that you would believe a boy was
giving back dictation to a savage master, or a mime was playing second parts.
The other often quarrels about goat's wool, fights armed for trifles: "Of
course, that first faith should not be mine, and that I should not bark out
fiercely what truly pleases me! Another lifetime's price is cheap." For what
is in dispute? Whether Castor or Dolichos knows more; whether Minucius' road
or Appius' leads better to Brundisium.

Him whom ruinous Venus, whom headlong dice strip, whom glory both clothes and
anoints above his powers, whom an importunate thirst and hunger for silver
hold, whom shame and flight of poverty hold, a rich friend, often equipped
with ten more vices, hates and dreads, or, if he does not hate, he rules and,
like a pious mother, wishes him to be wiser than herself and prior in virtues,
and says things nearly true: "My means (do not contend) suffer folly; your
property is very small. A narrow toga becomes a sound companion; cease to vie
with me." Eutrapelus, to whomever he wished to harm, would give precious
clothes: "For now, happy, with the fair tunics he will take new counsels and
hopes, he will sleep into the light, he will put a whore before honorable
duty, he will feed others' coins, at the bottom he will be a Thracian
gladiator or will drive a grocer's nag for pay."

You will never probe his secret, and you will cover what is entrusted,
tortured both by wine and by anger. You will not praise your own pursuits or
blame another's, nor, when he wishes to hunt, will you compose poems. So the
grace of the twin brothers, Amphion and Zethus, sprang apart, until the lyre,
suspected by the stern one, fell silent. Amphion is thought to have yielded to
a brother's ways: you, yield to the gentle commands of a powerful friend, and
whenever he leads out into the fields beasts of burden loaded with Aetolian
nets, and dogs, rise and put off the gloom of the uncivil Muse, so that you
may dine alike on dishes bought with labors: a solemn work for Roman men,
useful to fame and to life and to the limbs; especially when you are well and
can surpass a dog in running or a boar in strength. Add that there is none who
handles manly arms more handsomely; you know with what shout of the ring you
sustain the battles of the Campus; last, as a boy you bore savage service and
the Cantabrian wars under the leader who now unsticks the standards from
Parthian temples and, if anything is wanting, assigns it to Italian arms. And
lest you draw back and be absent without excuse, though you care to have done
nothing outside number and measure, sometimes you trifle on the paternal farm:
the army divides the skiffs, the Actian fight is brought back under your lead
by the boys in hostile fashion; the adversary is your brother, the lake is the
Adriatic, until swift Victory crowns one or the other with leaf. He who has
believed you agree with his pursuits, a favorer, will praise your play with
both thumbs.

That I may advise at once (if you need any adviser), see often what you say of
each man and to whom. Flee the questioner: for he is the same as a chatterer,
nor do open ears keep faithfully what is entrusted, and a word once sent forth
flies irrevocable. Let no maid or boy ulcerate your liver within the marble
threshold of a venerable friend, lest the master of a fair boy or a dear girl
bless you with a small gift or, inconvenient, distress you. Look again and
again of what sort he is whom you commend, lest soon another's faults strike
shame into you. We are deceived, and sometimes we introduce one not worthy:
therefore him whom his own fault will press, being deceived, cease to protect,
so that you may keep and protect one known through and through, if charges
assail him, who trusts in your defense. When he is gnawed round with Theon's
tooth, do you not feel the dangers that will come to you a little later? For
your affair is at stake when the next wall burns, and neglected fires are wont
to take strength. The culture of a powerful friend is sweet to the
inexperienced; the experienced man fears. You, while your ship is on the deep,
see to this, lest a changed breeze carry you backward. The sad hate the
cheerful, the jesting hate the sad, the swift hate the sedate, the slack hate
the agile and busy; drinkers of Falernian from the middle of the night hate
him who refuses the cups held out, though you swear you dread the night heats.
Take the cloud from the brow: often the modest man takes the look of the
obscure, the silent of the bitter.

Among all things you will read and question the learned, by what reasoning you
may be able to lead life gently through, whether poor desire always agitates
and vexes you, whether dread and hope of things moderately useful, whether
teaching prepares virtue or Nature gives it, what lessens cares, what makes
you a friend to yourself, what purely calms, honor or a sweet little gain, or
a secret journey and the path of a life that escapes notice. As often as the
cold stream Digentia refreshes me, which Mandela drinks, a village wrinkled
with cold, what do you think I feel? What do you believe, friend, I pray? Let
me have what I now have, even less, and let me live for myself what remains of
life, if the gods will that anything remain; let there be a good supply of
books and of fruit provided for the year, and let me not float, hanging on the
hope of a doubtful hour. But it is enough to pray Jove, who puts and takes
away, that he give life, that he give means; I will prepare an even mind for
myself.

If you believe old Cratinus, learned Maecenas, no poems can please long or
live that are written by water-drinkers. Since Liber enrolled the unsound
poets among Satyrs and Fauns, the sweet Camenae have generally smelled of wine
in the morning. Homer, a wine-lover, is convicted by his praises of wine;
father Ennius himself never leapt forth to arms that must be told except when
drunk. "I will assign the Forum and Libo's well to the dry; I will take
singing from the severe." As soon as I issued this, the poets have not ceased
to contend with night wine and to stink by day. What? If someone with grim
face, wild, and bare foot, and with a weaver of a scanty toga, should imitate
Cato, would he represent the virtue and ways of Cato? The tongue that rivaled
Timagenes burst Iarbitas, while he is eager and strains to be held a city wit
and eloquent. The example deceives that is imitable in vices: if they chanced
on paleness, they would drink bloodless cumin. O imitators, servile flock, how
often your uproar has moved bile in me, how often a jest! I, a leader, first
set free footprints through the empty; I did not press with my foot another's.
He who will trust himself, a leader, will rule the swarm. I first showed
Parian iambs to Latium, following the numbers and the spirit of Archilochus,
not the matter and the words that drove Lycambes. And lest you therefore crown
me with scantier leaves, because I feared to change the measures and the art
of the song, masculine Sappho tempers the Muse of Archilochus with her foot,
Alcaeus tempers it, but unlike in matter and order, nor does he seek a
father-in-law to smear with black verses, nor does he knot a noose for a
betrothed with a famous song. This man, spoken by no mouth before, I, a Latin
lyre-player, made common. It delights me, bringing things unrecorded, to be
read by freeborn eyes and held by freeborn hands.

Would you like to know why the ungrateful reader praises and loves my little
works at home, and, unjust, presses them outside the threshold? I do not hunt
the votes of the windy crowd with outlay of dinners and the gift of a worn
garment; I do not, hearer and avenger of noble writers, think it worth my
while to canvass the tribes of grammarians and the platforms. Hence those
tears. "Writings unworthy of packed theatres I am ashamed to recite, and to
add weight to trifles," if I have said, "You laugh," he says, "and you keep
those things for Jove's ears: for you trust that you alone drip poetic honey,
beautiful to yourself." At these things I fear to use the nostrils, and, lest
I be cut by a struggling sharp nail, "That place displeases," I cry, and I ask
for a truce. For play has begotten a trembling contest and anger, anger fierce
enmities and a funeral war.

You seem, book, to look at Vertumnus and Janus, of course that you may stand
for sale, smoothed with the pumice of the Sosii. You hate keys and seals
welcome to the modest; you groan to be shown to few and praise what is common.
Not so nursed: flee where you long to go down. Once sent out there will be no
return for you. "What have I done, wretched? What did I want?" you will say,
when someone has hurt you, and you know you are being rolled into a small
compass, when the full lover grows languid. But if the augur is not foolish
from hate of the sinner, you will be dear at Rome until age deserts you. When,
handled by the hands of the crowd, you have begun to grow dirty, you will
either feed idle moths in silence, or you will flee to Utica, or bound you
will be sent to Ilerda. The monitor not heard will laugh, like him who in
anger pushed a badly obedient ass over the rocks: for who would labor to save
one unwilling? This too awaits you, that stammering old age occupy you
teaching boys their letters at the ends of the streets. When a mild sun has
brought you more ears, you will tell that I, born of a freedman father and in
slender means, spread wings larger than the nest, so that as much as you take
from birth you add to virtues; that I pleased the first men of the city in war
and at home, of small body, early gray, fitted for suns, swift to anger, yet
so as to be appeasable. If by chance anyone asks you my age, let him know I
have completed four times eleven Decembers, in the year that Lollius drew
Lepidus as colleague.

Since you alone bear so many and so great affairs, protect Italian things with
arms, adorn them with manners, amend them with laws, I should sin against the
public good if I delayed your times with a long talk, Caesar. Romulus and
father Liber and Pollux with Castor, after mighty deeds received into the
temples of the gods, while they tended the lands and the race of men, settled
harsh wars, assigned fields, founded towns, wept that the hoped-for favor did
not answer their merits. He who crushed the dread hydra and subdued the known
portents by fated labor found that envy is tamed by the last end. For he burns
by his own brightness who outweighs the arts placed below him; when
extinguished, the same man will be loved. To you present we lavish timely
honors, and we set altars at which your divinity must be sworn, confessing
that nothing will arise at another time, nothing such has arisen. But this
people of yours, wise and just in one thing, by preferring you to our leaders,
you to the Greeks, estimates the rest by no similar reasoning and manner, and,
unless it sees things removed from the earth and done with their own times, it
scorns and hates; so favoring of the old that it would keep saying the tables
forbidding wrong, which the board of ten sanctioned, the treaties of the kings
equalled either with Gabii or with the rigid Sabines, the books of the
pontiffs, the aged volumes of bards, were spoken by the Muses on the Alban
mount.

If, because the most ancient writings of the Greeks are also the best, Roman
writers are weighed in the same balance, there is no reason for us to say
much: there is nothing hard inside an olive, nothing hard outside a nut; we
have come to the height of fortune; we paint and we play the lyre and we
wrestle more learnedly than the anointed Achaeans. If a day, as wines, makes
poems better, I should like to know what year claims a price for papers. A
writer who fell a hundred years ago, must he be referred among the perfect and
old, or among the cheap and new? Let a limit shut out quarrels. "He is old and
worthy who completes a hundred years." What of him who perished less by one
month or year, among whom shall he be referred? Among the old poets, or among
those whom both the present and a later age would spit out? "That man indeed
will be placed honorably among the old who is younger by a short month or a
whole year." I use the permission, and like hairs of a horse's tail I pluck
little by little and take away one, take away still one, until he falls,
eluded by the reasoning of the collapsing heap, who goes back to the fasti and
estimates virtue by years and marvels at nothing except what Libitina has
hallowed.

Ennius, both wise and brave and a second Homer, as the critics say, seems to
care lightly where his promises fall, and his Pythagorean dreams. Is Naevius
not in our hands, and does he not stick in our minds, almost recent? So holy
is every old poem. As often as it is disputed which is prior to which,
Pacuvius carries off the fame of the learned old man, Accius of the lofty; the
toga of Afranius is said to have fitted Menander, Plautus to hurry to the
pattern of Sicilian Epicharmus, Caecilius to surpass in gravity, Terence in
art. These mighty Rome learns by heart, and these, packed in a narrow theatre,
it watches; it has these and counts them poets from the age of the writer
Livius to our time. Sometimes the crowd sees straight, there are times when it
sins. If it so marvels at and praises the old poets that it puts nothing
before them, compares nothing with them, it errs. If it believes they say some
things too anciently, most things harshly, confesses many things slothfully,
it both has taste and sides with me and judges with equal Jove. I do not, for
my part, attack, nor do I think the poems of Livius must be destroyed, which I
remember Orbilius, fond of the rod, dictating to me as a small boy; but that
they should seem emended and fair and differing least from finished work, I
marvel. If among them a becoming word has by chance flashed out, and if one
verse and another is a little neater, it unjustly draws and sells the whole
poem.

I am indignant that anything is blamed not because it is thought coarsely or
gracelessly composed, but because recently, and that not pardon is asked for
the ancients, but honor and rewards. If I should doubt whether Atta's play
rightly or not walks through saffron and flowers, almost all the fathers would
cry that shame has perished, when I try to blame what grave Aesopus, what
learned Roscius acted; either because they hold nothing right except what has
pleased themselves, or because they think it shameful to obey their juniors,
and to confess that what they learned beardless must be destroyed as old men.
Now he who praises the Salian song of Numa, and that which he does not know
along with me, wishes to seem to know it alone; he does not favor and applaud
buried talents, but attacks ours, and, jealous, hates us and ours. But if
novelty had been as hateful to the Greeks as to us, what would now be old? Or
what would public use have that it might read and wear out man by man?

As soon as Greece, wars laid down, began to trifle and to slide into vice with
fortune even, now it burned with zeal for athletes, now for horses, it loved
craftsmen of marble or ivory or bronze, it hung face and mind on a painted
tablet, now it rejoiced in pipers, now in tragic actors; as if a girl-child
were playing under her nurse, what it eagerly sought, in time it left, full.
What pleases or is hated that you would not believe changeable? This good
peaces and favoring winds have had. At Rome it was long sweet and solemn to be
awake in the morning with the house opened, to bring forth rights for a
client, to lay out cautious money with good names, to hear elders, to tell a
younger by what means property could grow, ruinous lust be lessened. The
fickle people has changed its mind and is hot with one zeal for writing; boys
and severe fathers, hair bound with leaf, dine and dictate poems. I myself,
who affirm that I write no verses, am found more lying than the Parthians,
and, awake before the risen sun, I call for pen and papers and cases. He who
does not know a ship fears to drive a ship; none dares give southernwood to
the sick except who has learned; what belongs to doctors, doctors promise;
craftsmen handle craftsmen's tools: unlearned and learned, we write poems
everywhere.

Yet gather how many virtues this error and this light madness have. The poet's
mind is not rashly greedy; he loves verses, he studies this one thing; he
laughs at losses, flights of slaves, fires; he does not think out any fraud
against a partner or a boy ward; he lives on husks and second bread; though
sluggish and bad at soldiering, useful to the city, if you grant this, that
great things are helped by small things too. The poet shapes the tender and
stammering mouth of a boy, already now twists the ear from obscene talks, soon
also forms the breast with friendly precepts, corrector of harshness and envy
and anger, reports things rightly done, equips rising times with known
examples, comforts the poor and the sick. From where would the chaste girl,
ignorant of a husband, with chaste boys, learn prayers, if the Muse had not
given a bard? The chorus asks help and feels present divinities, bland, it
implores heavenly waters with a learned prayer, turns away diseases, drives
off dreaded dangers, obtains both peace and a year rich in fruits. By song the
gods above are placated, by song the Manes.

The old farmers, brave and blessed with little, after the grain was stored,
easing in festival time the body and the mind itself that bore hard things in
hope of an end, with partners in work and boys and a faithful wife, used to
appease Earth with a pig, Silvanus with milk, the Genius mindful of a short
life with flowers and wine. Fescennine licence, found through this custom,
poured rustic insults in alternate verses, and liberty, received through
returning years, played lovably, until already the joke, grown savage, began
to turn into open rage and to go threatening with impunity through honest
houses. Those attacked by the bloody tooth were pained; there was care also
for the untouched over the common condition; nay even a law and a penalty were
brought, which would not have anyone described in a bad song: they changed
their measure, reduced by fear of the club to speaking well and delighting.
Captured Greece captured the fierce victor and brought arts into rustic
Latium. So that rough Saturnian number flowed away, and elegance drove out the
heavy poison; but for a long age there remained, and today remain, traces of
the country. For late he applied acumen to Greek papers, and, quiet after the
Punic wars, began to ask what Sophocles and Thespis and Aeschylus would bring
that was useful. He tried the thing too, whether he could turn it worthily,
and he pleased himself, by nature sublime and keen: for he breathes the tragic
enough and dares happily, but he thinks blotting shameful, unskilled, and
fears it.

Comedy is believed to have least sweat because it fetches the matter from the
middle, but it has so much more burden as it has less pardon. See in what way
Plautus keeps the part of the loving youth, how that of the attentive father,
how that of the treacherous pimp, how great a Dossennus he is among greedy
parasites, how he runs over the boards with a sock not tightened. For he is
eager to send a coin down into the cash-boxes; after this, careless whether
the play fall or stand on a straight ankle. Him whom Glory has carried to the
stage in a windy chariot, a slow spectator kills, a busy one inflates: so
light, so small is that which undermines or restores a mind greedy of praise.
Farewell to the plaything, if a denied palm brings me back lean, a given one
fat. Often too this puts to flight and frightens a bold poet, that those more
in number, lesser in virtue and honor, unlearned and stupid and ready to fight
it out, if the knight disagrees, demand in the middle of the songs either a
bear or boxers: for in these the little people rejoices. But already the whole
pleasure of the knight too has migrated from the ear to uncertain eyes and
empty joys. For four hours or more the curtains are kept down, while squadrons
of horse and troops of foot flee; soon the fortune of kings is dragged with
hands twisted back, chariots hurry, carriages, wagons, ships, captive ivory is
carried, captive Corinth. If he were on earth, Democritus would laugh, whether
a diverse kind, a panther confused with a camel, or a white elephant turned
the faces of the crowd; he would watch the people more attentively than the
games themselves, as offering himself far more spectacles; but he would think
the writers were telling a little fable to a deaf ass. For what voices have
prevailed to overcome the sound which our theatres give back? You would think
the Garganian grove was lowing, or the Tuscan sea; with so great a noise the
games are watched, and arts and foreign riches, smeared with which the actor,
when he has stood on the stage, right hand runs to left. "Has he said anything
yet?" "Nothing at all." "What then pleases?" "Wool that has imitated violets
with Tarentine dye."

And lest you by chance think that I, what I myself refuse to do, praise
spitefully when others handle it rightly, that poet seems to me able to go
along a stretched rope, who vainly wrings my breast, irritates, soothes, fills
me with false terrors, like a magician, and now places me at Thebes, now at
Athens. But come, give a brief care also to those who prefer to trust
themselves to a reader rather than bear the fastidiousness of a proud
spectator, if you wish to fill the gift worthy of Apollo with books and to add
a spur to bards, that they may seek green Helicon with greater zeal. We poets
indeed often do many evils to ourselves (that I may cut my own vineyards),
when we give you a book when you are anxious or tired; when we are hurt if any
one of our friends has dared to blame one verse; when we roll back places
already recited, unasked; when we lament that our labors do not appear, and
poems drawn out with a fine thread; when we hope the matter will come to this,
that as soon as you have learned we are fashioning songs, you, kind, of
yourself send for us and forbid us to be in want and force us to write.

But yet it is worth the work to know what kind of temple-keepers Virtue, tried
in war and at home, has, not to be entrusted to an unworthy poet. That
Choerilus was pleasing to king Alexander the Great, who with uncultivated and
ill-born verses brought back Philips accepted, a royal coin; but as inks
handled send back a mark and a stain, so writers generally smear splendid
deeds with a foul song. The same king, who bought a poem so ridiculous so
dear, prodigal, forbade by edict that anyone besides Apelles paint him, or
that another than Lysippus lead bronzes imitating the face of brave Alexander.
But if you should call that fine judgment in seeing arts to books and to these
gifts of the Muses, you would swear he was born in the thick air of the
Boeotians. Yet neither do Virgil and Varius, poets dear to you, disgrace your
judgments of them and the gifts which they have borne with much praise of the
giver, nor are faces more expressed through bronze signs than through a bard's
work the manners and minds of famous men appear. Nor would I prefer talks
creeping along the ground to composing deeds, and telling the sites of lands
and rivers, and citadels set on mountains, and barbarian kingdoms, and wars
finished through the whole world under your auspices, and the bars holding
Janus, guardian of peace, and Rome feared by the Parthians with you as prince,
if I could also as much as I should wish; but neither does your majesty
receive a small song, nor does my shame dare to attempt a matter which my
powers refuse to bear. But officiousness foolishly presses him whom it loves,
especially when it commends itself by numbers and art; for he learns more
quickly and remembers more gladly that which someone derides than that which
he approves and venerates. I do not care for a service that weighs me down,
and I neither wish to be set forth anywhere in wax with a face fashioned for
the worse, nor to be adorned with verses badly made, lest I blush, presented
with a stupid gift, and together with my writer, stretched in a covered box,
be carried down into the street that sells incense and scents and pepper and
whatever is wrapped in inept papers.

Florus, faithful friend to good and famous Nero, if someone by chance should
wish to sell you a boy born at Tibur or Gabii, and should deal with you thus:
"He is both white and fair from the top of the head to the bottom of the
ankles, he will become and will be yours for eight thousand coins, a home-born
slave fit for services at the master's nod, imbued with a little Greek
letters, suitable for any art; you will imitate whatever you like in wet clay;
nay he will even sing, unskilled but sweet to a drinker. Many promises lessen
faith, when he who wishes to push out saleable wares praises more fully than
is right. No thing presses me; I am poor in my own bronze. None of the
slave-dealers would do this for you; not rashly would just anyone get the same
from me. Once he stopped, and, as happens, hid on the stairs fearing the
hanging strap. Give the coins, if the exception of flight does not hurt you:"
he would carry the price, safe from penalty, I think; you bought a faulty one
knowingly; the law was told you. Do you nevertheless pursue this man and delay
him with an unfair suit? I told you, as you were setting out, that I was lazy,
I told you I was almost maimed for such offices, lest you, savage, quarrel
that no letter was returning to you. What then have I profited, if you
nevertheless attack the laws that side with me? You complain besides on this,
that, a liar, I do not send the songs you expected.

A soldier of Lucullus, having gathered travel-money with many hardships, while
he snored at night, weary, had lost it to a penny. After this, a violent wolf,
angry at himself and the enemy alike, keen with fasting teeth, threw down a
royal garrison from a place, they say, highly fortified and rich in many
things. Famous for that deed he is adorned with honorable gifts, and he
receives besides twice ten thousand sesterces of coin. By chance about this
time the praetor, wishing to overthrow I know not what fort, began to exhort
the same man with words that could add spirit even to the timid: "Go, good
man, where your virtue calls you, go with fortunate foot, about to bear great
rewards of merits. Why do you stand?" After this that shrewd man, however
rustic: "He will go, he will go there where you wish, who has lost his belt,"
he says.

It fell to me to be nursed at Rome and to be taught how much angry Achilles
had harmed the Greeks. Good Athens added a little more art, of course that I
might wish to distinguish the straight from the crooked and to seek the true
among the woods of Academus. But hard times moved me from the pleasant place,
and the civil tide of war carried me, unskilled, into arms that would not
answer the muscles of Caesar Augustus. From there, as soon as Philippi first
dismissed me, humble with clipped wings and poor of paternal hearth and farm,
bold poverty drove me to make verses: but, having what is not wanting, what
hemlocks will ever be able to purge me enough, unless I think it better to
sleep than to write verses?

The years as they go plunder single things from us; they have snatched jests,
Venus, feasts, play; they strain to wring away poems: what do you wish me to
do? Last, not all marvel at and love the same things: you rejoice in song,
this man is delighted with iambs, that man with Bion's talks and black salt.
Three guests seem to me to disagree almost, demanding much that is diverse
with varied palate. What shall I give? What shall I not give? You refuse what
another orders; what you seek is truly hateful and sour to two. Besides the
rest, do you think I can write poems at Rome among so many cares and so many
labors? This man calls me as a sponsor, this man to hear writings, all offices
left; this man lies on the hill of Quirinus, this man at the far end of the
Aventine, each to be visited; you see intervals humanely convenient. "But the
streets are clean, so that nothing stand in the way of those meditating." A
heated contractor hurries with mules and porters, a huge machine now twists a
stone, now a beam, grim funerals wrestle with strong wagons, this way a rabid
dog flees, this way a muddy sow rushes: go now and meditate tuneful verses
with yourself. The whole chorus of writers loves the grove and flees the city,
duly a client of Bacchus rejoicing in sleep and shade: do you wish me, among
noises night and day, to sing and to follow the narrowed footprints of bards?
A talent that has chosen empty Athens for itself and given seven years to
studies and grown old with books and cares, comes out more silent than a
statue for the most part and shakes the people with a laugh: here, in the
middle of the waves of things and the storms of the city, should I think it
worth while to join words that will move the sound of the lyre?

There was at Rome a rhetor, brother of a lawyer, so that each heard mere
honors in the other's talk, as if this one were a Gracchus to that, that one a
Mucius to this. How much less does that madness vex clear poets? I compose
songs, this man elegies. Wonderful to see, and a work chased by the nine
Muses! Look first with how much pride, with how much effort we look round the
temple empty for Roman bards! Soon also, if by chance you are free, follow and
hear from afar what each brings and by what he knits a crown for himself. We
are cut and with as many blows we use up the enemy, Samnites in a slow duel
until the first lights. I depart an Alcaeus by his vote; he by mine who? Who
but Callimachus? If he has seemed to ask more, he becomes Mimnermus and grows
with the chosen surname. I bear many things to placate the irritable race of
bards, when I write and, a suppliant, catch the votes of the people; the same
man, studies finished and mind recovered, I would stop my open ears with
impunity against those who read.

They are laughed at who compose bad songs; but they rejoice while writing and
venerate themselves, and of themselves, if you are silent, blessed, they
praise whatever they have written. But he who will desire to have made a
lawful poem will take with his tablets the mind of an honest censor; he will
dare, whatever words will have too little splendor and will be without weight
and will be borne unworthy of honor, to move from their place, though they
withdraw unwilling and are still turning within the inner rooms of Vesta; long
obscured from the people the good man will dig up and bring into the light
fair names of things, which, spoken by ancient Catos and Cethegi, shapeless
neglect and deserted age now press; he will adopt new ones which father Use
has produced. Violent and liquid and most like a pure river he will pour
wealth and will bless Latium with a rich tongue; he will check what
luxuriates, he will lighten with sound culture what is too rough, he will take
away what lacks virtue, he will give the look of one playing and will be
wrung, as one who now is moved as a Satyr, now as a rustic Cyclops. I would
prefer to be seen a delirious and idle writer, while my bad things delight me
or at last deceive me, than to be wise and snarl.

There was at Argos a man not ignoble, who believed he was hearing marvelous
tragic actors, a happy sitter and applauder in an empty theatre; who would
keep the other duties of life in a right manner, a good neighbor indeed, a
lovable host, courteous to his wife, who could forgive slaves and not go mad
at a broken seal of a flask, who could avoid a crag and an open well. When
this man, restored by the means and cares of kinsmen, drove out the disease
with hellebore and the bile with neat wine, and returned to himself: "By
Pollux, you have killed me, friends, you have not saved me," he says, "from
whom pleasure has been so wrung, and the most welcome error of the mind taken
by force."

Of course it is useful to be wise, with trifles thrown away, and to yield
timely play to boys, and not to follow words to be measured on Latin strings,
but to learn by heart the numbers and modes of a true life. Therefore I speak
these things with myself and silently recall: If no plenty of water would
finish your thirst, you would tell the doctors: that, the more you have
gotten, the more you desire, do you dare confess to none? If a wound were not
made lighter for you by a shown root or herb, you would flee to be cured by a
root or herb that profited nothing: you had heard that from whom the gods gave
property, from him crooked folly departed; and though you are no wiser from
the time you are fuller, do you nevertheless use the same monitors? But if
riches could make a man prudent, if less desirous and fearful, you would of
course blush if anyone more greedy than you lived on earth. If that is one's
own which someone has bought with scale and bronze, some things, if you
believe the lawyers, use makes over; the field that feeds you is yours, and
Orbius' bailiff, when he hoes the crops soon about to give you grain, feels
you the master. You give coins, you receive grape, chickens, eggs, a jar of
strong drink: of course in that way you little by little buy the field,
perhaps bought for three hundred thousand coins or even above. What does it
matter whether you live on what was lately counted or long ago?

The buyer of Arician and Veientine land once dines on bought greens, though he
thinks otherwise; he heats the bronze with bought logs under the cold night;
but he calls it his own all the way where the poplar planted on fixed bounds
flees neighboring quarrels; as if anything were one's own which, at the point
of a movable hour, now by prayer, now by price, now by force, now by last
death, changes masters and yields into other rights. So, because perpetual use
is given to none, and heir comes upon the heir of another as wave upon wave,
what do villages profit, or granaries? Or Lucanian woods added to Calabrian
pastures, if Orcus reaps the great with the small, not to be moved by gold?
Gems, marble, ivory, Tuscan figures, tablets, silver, clothes dyed with
Gaetulian purple, there are who do not have them, there is who does not care
to have them. Why one of two brothers prefers to rest and play and be anointed
rather than Herod's fat palm-groves, the other, rich and importunate, from the
rising of light to the shade tames a wooded field with flames and iron, the
Genius knows, the companion born with us who tempers the natal star, the god
of human nature, mortal for each single head, changeable in face, white and
black.

I will use and from a modest heap, as much as the matter will demand, I will
take, nor will I fear what an heir may judge of me, because he has not found
more than what was given; and yet the same man, I shall wish to know how much
the simple and cheerful differs from the spendthrift, and how much the sparing
disagrees with the greedy. For it differs whether you scatter your things
prodigal, or neither make expense unwilling nor labor to get more, and rather,
as a boy once at the festive Quinquatrus, enjoy a scant and welcome time in
haste. Let filthy poverty be far from the house: I, whether I am carried in a
great ship or a small, shall be carried one and the same. We are not driven
with swollen sails by a favoring north wind; yet we do not lead our age with
adverse south winds, in strength, talent, appearance, virtue, place, property,
last of the first, always before the last. You are not greedy: go. What? Have
the rest already fled together with that vice? Is your breast without empty
ambition? Is it without fear of death and anger? Dreams, magic terrors,
miracles, witches, night spectres and Thessalian portents, do you laugh at
them? Do you number your birthdays gratefully? Do you forgive friends? Do you
become milder and better as old age approaches? What does it help you that one
thorn out of many has been taken? If you do not know how to live rightly, give
way to those who do; you have played enough, eaten enough, and drunk: it is
time for you to go away, lest a wanton age, more decently, laugh and jostle
you who have drunk more largely than is right.
