* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The condition of the present times also calls forth this warning of ours, that
we ought not to wonder at these heresies, whether because they exist, for they
were going to be, and were foretold, or because they overthrow the faith of
some, for they exist for this, that faith by having a trial might also have a
proving. Vainly therefore and without thought most people are scandalized by
this very thing, that heresies have so much power.

How much, if they had not existed? When something has been allotted to exist
in every way, it receives a cause on account of which it exists. This obtains
a force through which it exists, so that it cannot not exist.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Fever, in short, among the other deadly and torturing ways of taking a man
off, we neither wonder at because it exists, for it does exist, nor because it
takes a man off, for that is what it is for. In the same way, if we are
terrified that heresies, brought forth for the wasting and death of faith, can
do this, the first thing is that we be terrified that they exist, which, while
they exist, have the power, and while they can, have existence. But fever, as
an evil both from its cause and from its power, as is known, we rather
abominate than wonder at, and, as far as is in us, we take precaution, not
having its abolition in our power. Heresies, however, bringing eternal death
and the heat of a greater fire, some prefer to wonder that they can do this
rather than to avoid them so that they cannot, though they have the power of
avoiding. For the rest they will have no power, if people do not wonder that
they have so much power. For either while they wonder they are furnished into
scandal, or because they are scandalized they therefore wonder, as if what has
so much power came from some truth. A marvel, no doubt, that evil should have
its own strength; except that heresies have much power among those who have no
power in the faith. In a fight of boxers and gladiators most often a man does
not win because he is strong, or because he cannot be beaten, but because the
one who was beaten had no strength: so much so that the same victor,
afterwards matched with a man in good strength, even goes away beaten. Not
otherwise do heresies have what power they have from the weaknesses of certain
people, having no power if they run against a faith in good strength.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

These weaker people are also accustomed to be built into ruin even from
certain persons captured by heresy. Why did that woman, or that man, most
faithful and most prudent and most practiced in the church, pass over to that
side? Who, saying this, does not himself answer himself, that those whom
heresies could change are to be judged neither prudent nor faithful nor
practiced? And this is a marvel, I suppose, that someone proved beforehand
should afterwards fall away? Saul, good beyond the rest, is afterwards
overthrown by envy. David, a good man according to the heart of the Lord, is
afterwards guilty of slaughter and of shame. Solomon, gifted by the Lord with
every grace and wisdom, is led by women into idolatry. For it was reserved for
the Son of God alone to remain without offense. What then? If a bishop, if a
deacon, if a widow, if a virgin, if a teacher, if even a martyr has fallen
from the rule, will heresies on that account seem to obtain the truth? Do we
prove the faith from persons, or persons from the faith? No one is wise, no
one faithful, no one greater, except a Christian. But no one is a Christian
except one who has persevered all the way to the end. You, as a man, know each
person from the outside. You think what you see. But you see only as far as
you have eyes. But the eyes of the Lord, he says, are high. Man looks on the
face, God on the heart. And therefore the Lord looks, and therefore he knows
who are his, and he roots out the plant which he did not plant, and he shows
the last from the first, and he carries a fan in his hand to cleanse his
threshing-floor. Let the chaff of a light faith fly off as much as they will,
by whatever blast of temptations; by so much the purer mass of grain will be
stored in the Lord's barns. Were not some of the disciples themselves
scandalized and turned aside from the Lord himself? And yet the rest did not
on that account think they too must depart from his steps, but those who knew
that he was the word of life and had come from God persevered in his company
all the way to the end, though he had calmly offered it to them, if they too
wished to depart. It is a smaller thing if some Phygelus and Hermogenes and
Philetus and Hymenaeus also left his apostle: the betrayer of Christ himself
was from among the apostles. Do we wonder at his churches if they are deserted
by some, when the things we suffer show us to be Christians after the example
of Christ himself? They went out from us, he says, but they were not of us. If
they had been of us, they would certainly have remained with us.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Rather let us be mindful both of the Lord's pronouncements and of the
apostolic letters, which both foretold to us that heresies would come and laid
down that they are to be fled, and, just as we are not terrified that they
exist, so let us not wonder that they can do that on account of which they are
to be fled. The Lord instructs us that many will come, ravening wolves under
sheep's skins. What are these sheep's skins, if not the outward surface of the
Christian name? Who are the ravening wolves, if not the crafty senses and
spirit, lurking within to attack the flock of Christ? Who are the false
prophets, if not false preachers? Who the false apostles, if not adulterous
evangelists? Who antichrists in the meantime and always, if not rebels against
Christ? This heresies will be, harassing the church no less by perversity of
doctrines than the antichrist will then pursue it by atrocity of persecution;
except that persecution also makes martyrs, heresy only apostates. And
therefore heresies too had to exist, that those who are approved might be made
manifest, both those who have stood in persecutions and those who have not
gone off course into heresies. For it does not help that those who change the
faith into heresy be understood as approved; as they interpret it for
themselves from the other side, because he said elsewhere, Examine all things,
hold what is good. As if it were not allowed that all things, badly examined,
should strike by error upon the choice of some evil.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Further, if he rebukes dissensions and schisms, which without doubt are evils,
he also adds heresies in the same breath. What he joins to evils he certainly
professes to be an evil, and indeed a greater one, since he says he believed
concerning schisms and dissensions for this reason, because he knew that
heresies too had to exist. For he shows that by the sight of a heavier evil he
easily believed concerning the lighter ones; certainly not that he believed
concerning evils because heresies were good, but that he might warn in
advance, concerning temptations of a worse mark as well, that one must not
wonder at those which he said tend to make the approved manifest, namely those
whom they could not deprave. In short, if the whole chapter has the sense of
keeping unity and checking separations, and heresies no less tear away from
unity than schisms and dissensions, without doubt he has set heresies too in
that condition of reproof in which he has also set schisms and dissensions:
and by this he does not make those approved who have turned aside into
heresies, since he most of all rebukes being turned aside from things of this
kind, teaching that all should speak one thing and be of the same mind, which
even heresies do not allow.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And no longer on this, if it is the same Paul who also elsewhere numbers
heresies among the fleshly crimes, writing to the Galatians, and who suggests
to Titus that a heretical man is to be refused after a first correction,
because such a one is perverse and sins, so as to be condemned by himself. But
in almost every letter, driving in that adulterous doctrines are to be fled,
he taxes heresies. Whose works are adulterous doctrines, called heresies by a
Greek word, from the sense of choice, by which a person uses them either for
setting them up or for taking them up. Therefore he also said the heretic was
condemned for himself, because he has also chosen for himself that in which he
is condemned. For us truly it is allowed to indulge nothing from our own
judgment, and not even to choose what someone has brought in from his own
judgment. We have the apostles of the Lord as authors, who did not themselves
choose anything from their own judgment to bring in, but faithfully assigned
to the nations the discipline received from Christ. And so even if an angel
from heaven were to evangelize otherwise, he would be called anathema by us.
The Holy Spirit had already then provided that there would be in a certain
virgin(??) Philumene an angel of seduction, transfiguring himself into an
angel of light, by whose signs and tricks Apelles, led on, brought in a new
heresy.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

These are the doctrines of men and of demons, born for itching ears from the
talent of secular wisdom, which the Lord, calling it foolishness, chose the
foolish things of the world for the confusion even of philosophy itself. For
that is the material of secular wisdom, a rash interpreter of the divine
nature and arrangement. In short, the heresies themselves are furnished from
philosophy. From there the aeons, and I know not what infinite forms, and the
trinity of man in Valentinus; he had been a Platonist: from there Marcion's
better god from tranquillity; he had come from the Stoics: and that the soul
is said to perish is observed from the Epicureans: and that the restoration of
the flesh is denied is taken from one school of all the philosophers: and
where matter is equaled with God, it is the discipline of Zeno: and where
something is alleged of a fiery god, Heraclitus comes in. The same material is
rolled about among heretics and philosophers, the same inquiries are
entangled: whence evil, and why? and whence man, and how? and, what Valentinus
lately proposed, whence God? namely from enthymesis and ectroma. Miserable
Aristotle, who instituted dialectic for them, the craftswoman of building and
destroying, shifting in opinions, forced in conjectures, hard in arguments, a
worker of contentions, troublesome even to herself, rehandling all things so
that she may have handled nothing at all. Hence those fables and endless
genealogies, and unfruitful questions, and words creeping like a cancer, from
which the apostle, checking us, testifies by name that philosophy ought to be
guarded against, writing to the Colossians, See that there be not someone
circumventing you through philosophy and empty seduction, according to the
tradition of men, beyond the providence of the Holy Spirit. He had been at
Athens, and he had known that human wisdom, an affecter and interpolator of
the truth, from debates, itself too divided into its own heresies by a variety
of sects fighting one another. What then is there to Athens and to Jerusalem?
what to the academy and to the church? what to heretics and to Christians? Our
institution is from the porch of Solomon, who himself had also handed down
that the Lord is to be sought in simplicity of heart. Let those see to it who
have brought forth a Stoic and Platonic and dialectic Christianity. We have no
need of curiosity after Christ Jesus, nor of inquiry after the gospel. When we
believe, we desire nothing beyond believing. For this we first believe, that
there is not anything we ought to believe beyond.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

I come therefore to that article which both our people put forward for
entering on curiosity and the heretics drive in for bringing in scrupulosity.
It is written, they say, Seek, and you shall find. Let us remember when the
Lord uttered this word. I think in the very first-fruits of his teaching, when
it was still doubted among all whether he was the Christ, and when not even
Peter had yet pronounced him the Son of God, when even John had ceased to be
sure about him. Rightly therefore it was then said, Seek and you shall find,
when he who was not yet known still had to be sought. And this so far as
concerns the Jews. For the whole speech of that taunt belongs to them, who had
where they might seek Christ. They have, he says, Moses and Elijah, that is
the law and the prophets preaching Christ, according to what he says openly
elsewhere: Search the scriptures, in which you hope for salvation; for they
speak of me. This will be, Seek and you shall find. For it is plain that the
things that follow also fit the Jews: Knock and it shall be opened to you. The
Jews had formerly been with God; then, cast out for their offenses, they began
to be outside God. The nations, however, were never with God, except a drop
from a bucket and dust from a threshing-floor, and always outside. So one who
is always outside, how will he knock there where he never was? what door does
he know, at which he was never received nor ever cast out? or rather did he
knock, and does he know the door, who knows that he was inside and was driven
outside? Also, Ask and you shall receive, fits him who knew from whom it was
to be asked, from whom also something had been promised, from the God, namely,
of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, whom the nations no more knew than any promise of
his. And therefore he was speaking to Israel: I am not sent, he says, except
to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. He had not yet thrown the children's
bread to the dogs, he had not yet commanded them to go into the way of the
nations. Since at the end he commands that they should go to teach and to
baptize the nations, soon to obtain the Holy Spirit the Paraclete, who would
lead them into all truth. And this therefore he does then. But if the
apostles, destined as teachers for the nations, were themselves also going to
obtain the Paraclete as teacher, much more did Seek and you shall find lie
idle toward us, to whom the doctrine was going to come of itself through the
apostles, and to the apostles themselves through the Holy Spirit. All the
Lord's sayings, indeed, are set for all; they have passed through the ears of
the Jews to us. But most of them, directed at persons, have not constituted
for us the property of a warning, but an example.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Grant now of my own accord from that step. Let it have been said to all, Seek
and you shall find; yet here too the sense demands to contend with the helm of
interpretation. No divine word is so loose and scattered that the words only
are defended, and the reason of the words is not established. But in the first
place I set this down, that one certain thing has been instituted by Christ
which the nations ought in every way to believe, and therefore to seek, so
that they may be able, when they have found it, to believe. Further, of one
certain institution there cannot be an infinite inquiry.

One must seek until you find, and believe when you have found, and nothing
more, except to keep what you have believed, while you believe this besides,
that there is not another thing to be believed, and therefore not to be sought
either, when you have found and believed that which was instituted by him who
does not command you to inquire anything other than what he instituted. If
anyone doubts about this, it will stand that what was instituted by Christ is
with us. Meanwhile from confidence of the proof I go ahead, warning certain
people that nothing is to be sought beyond what they have believed, that this
is what they ought to have sought, lest they interpret Seek and you shall find
without the discipline of reason.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Now the reason of this saying stands in three articles, in the matter, in the
time, in the manner. In the matter, that you consider what is to be sought; in
the time, when; in the manner, how far. Therefore what Christ instituted is to
be sought, certainly as long as you do not find it, certainly until you find
it. But you have found it when you have believed. For you would not have
believed if you had not found, just as you would not have sought except in
order to find. Seeking therefore for this, that you may find, and finding for
this, that you may believe. By believing you have fixed every extension of
seeking and finding. This limit the very fruit of seeking has set for you.
This ditch he himself has marked out for you who does not want you to believe
anything other than what he instituted, and therefore not to seek it either.
For the rest, if because so many other things have also been instituted by
others, we ought on that account to seek as far as we can find, we always seek
and never believe at all. For where will there be an end of seeking? where a
station of believing? where a discharge of finding? With Marcion? But
Valentinus too proposes, Seek and you shall find. With Valentinus? But Apelles
too will knock at me with this pronouncement, and Hebion and Simon and all in
order have nothing else by which, insinuating themselves to me, they may bind
me to themselves. I shall therefore be nowhere, while I meet Seek and you
shall find everywhere, and as if nowhere; as one who has never laid hold of
that which Christ instituted, which ought to be sought, which must be
believed.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

One errs without punishment unless one also offends; although to err is also
to offend. One wanders without punishment, I say, who deserts nothing. But if
I have believed what I ought to have believed, and I think something else must
be sought again, I certainly hope that something else is to be found, in no
way going to hope that except because either I had not believed, I who seemed
to have believed, or I have ceased to believe. So deserting my faith I am
found a denier. Let me have said it once: no one seeks except one who either
has not had or has lost. That old woman had lost one of ten drachmas, and
therefore she sought: yet when she found it, she ceased to seek. The neighbor
did not have bread, and therefore he knocked: yet when it was opened to him,
and he received, he stopped knocking. The widow was asking to be heard by the
judge, because she was not admitted: but when she was heard, she pressed only
so far. So there is an end both of seeking and of knocking and of asking. For
to him who asks it shall be given, he says, and to him who knocks it shall be
opened, and for him who seeks it shall be found. Let him see to it who always
seeks, because he does not find. For he seeks there where it will not be
found. Let him see to it who always knocks, because it will never be opened.
For he knocks there where no one is. Let him see to it who always asks,
because he will never be heard. For he asks from one who does not hear.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Even if we must still seek, and always, yet where ought one to seek? Among the
heretics, where all things are foreign and adverse to our truth, to whom we
are forbidden to go? What servant hopes for rations from a stranger, not to
say from an enemy of his master? What soldier looks for a donative and pay
from unallied, not to say from enemy kings, unless plainly a deserter and a
runaway and a rebel? Even that old woman was seeking the drachma within her
own roof, even that knocker was beating at a neighbor's door, even that widow
was appealing to a judge who was not an enemy, though hard. No one can be
instructed from there whence he is destroyed; no one is enlightened by him by
whom he is darkened. Let us therefore seek in our own and from our own and
concerning our own, and only that which can come into question with the rule
of faith unhurt.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

Now the rule of faith, that from this point we may profess what we defend, is
that by which it is believed that there is one God only and none other besides
the founder of the world, who produced all things from nothing through his
Word first of all sent down; that Word called his Son, seen in the name of God
in various ways by the patriarchs, always heard in the prophets, last brought
down from the Spirit of God the Father and from power into the virgin Mary,
made flesh in her womb and born from her, gone forth as Jesus Christ,
thereafter preached a new law and a new promise of the kingdom of heaven, did
mighty works, crucified, rose on the third day, caught up into the heavens,
sat at the right hand of the Father, sent the vicarious power of the Holy
Spirit, who should lead those who believe, going to come with brightness to
take up the saints into the fruit of eternal life and of the heavenly promises
and to judge the profane with perpetual fire, a resuscitation of either part
having been made with restoration of the flesh. This rule, instituted by
Christ, as will be proved, has among us no questions, except those which
heresies bring in and which make heretics.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

For the rest, while its form remains in its own order, seek and treat as much
as you like and pour out every lust of curiosity, if anything seems to you
either to hang by ambiguity or to be shaded by obscurity. There is certainly
some brother, a teacher gifted by the grace of knowledge; there is someone
among the practiced, someone who has lived with you as a curious man, seeking
with you; he will know at last that it is better for you to be ignorant, lest
you come to know what you ought not, because you know what you ought. Your
faith, he says, has made you safe, not exercise of the scriptures. Faith has
been placed in the rule; it has a law and salvation from observation of the
law: but exercise consists in curiosity, having glory alone from the study of
skill. Let curiosity yield to faith, let glory yield to salvation. At any rate
either let them not make a noise, or let them be quiet. Against the rule to
know nothing is to know all things. Even if heretics were not enemies of the
truth, even if we were not warned in advance that they are to be fled, what
sort of thing is it to confer with men who themselves still profess that they
are seeking? For if they are still truly seeking, they have found nothing
certain yet, and therefore, whatever they seem to hold in the meantime, they
show their doubt as long as they seek. And so you, who likewise seek, looking
to those who themselves also seek, doubtful to the doubtful, uncertain to the
uncertain, a blind man, must of necessity be led into a pit by the blind. But
when for the sake of deceiving they pretend that they are still seeking, so
that they may insinuate their treatments to us by the injection of anxiety,
then when they have come to us they at once defend what they said had to be
sought: we must already refute them in such a way that they may know we are
deniers not of Christ but of them. For when they are still seeking, they do
not yet hold; but when they do not hold, they have not yet believed; but when
they have not yet believed, they are not Christians. But when they do hold and
believe, they still say one must seek, in order to defend. Before they defend,
they deny what they confess they have not yet believed, while they seek. Who
therefore are not even to themselves Christians, how much more to us? Who come
through deceit, what faith do they dispute? For what truth do they advocate
who introduce it from a lie? But they themselves deal from the scriptures, and
from the scriptures they persuade! From somewhere else, I suppose, they could
speak of the things of faith, except from the letters of faith?

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

We come therefore to the proposition. For this we were directing, and this we
were building beforehand by the preface of the address, that from this point
we may engage on that about which the adversaries challenge. They put forward
the scriptures, and by this audacity of theirs they at once move some. But in
the engagement itself they tire the strong, they capture the weak, they send
the middle away with a scruple. This step therefore we block most of all, of
not admitting them to any disputation about the scriptures. If these are those
forces of theirs, so that they may be able to have them, it ought to be looked
into to whom the possession of the scriptures belongs, lest he be admitted to
them to whom it in no way belongs.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

I would have brought this in from a plan of distrust or from a zeal for
entering on the constitution otherwise, unless that reason had stood first
which is this, that our faith owes obedience to the apostle, forbidding us to
enter on questions, to lend ears to new voices, to meet a heretic after one
correction, not after a disputation. So far did he forbid disputation, marking
correction as the cause of meeting a heretic, and this one only, because he is
not a Christian, lest he should seem to be chastised in the manner of a
Christian once and again and under two or three witnesses, since he is to be
chastised for this reason, that one ought not to dispute with him, then
because a meeting of the scriptures would profit nothing, unless plainly so
that one may go into an upset of the stomach or of the brain.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

This heresy does not receive certain scriptures, and if it receives any, it
twists them both by additions and by subtractions toward the arrangement of
its own institution, and if it receives them, it does not receive them whole,
and if it presents them whole to some extent, it nonetheless converts them by
invented diverse expositions. An adulterous sense shouts against the truth as
much as a corrupting pen also does. Vain presumptions of necessity refuse to
acknowledge those things by which they are convicted. They rest on those
things which they have composed from falsehood and which they have taken from
ambiguity. What will you advance, you who are most practiced in the
scriptures, when if you defend anything it is denied from the other side, if
you deny anything it is defended? And you indeed will lose nothing except your
voice in the contention, you will gain nothing except bile from the blasphemy.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

But he, if there is anyone for whose sake you go down into an engagement of
the scriptures, so that you may confirm him who is doubting, will he turn
aside to the truth, or rather to heresies? Moved by this very thing, that he
sees you have advanced nothing, with the adverse part at an equal step of
denying and defending, in a certainly equal state, he will depart more
uncertain from the altercation, not knowing which heresy he should judge.
These things they themselves also have, of course, to twist back upon us. For
they too must say that adulteries of the scriptures and lies of expositions
are brought in rather by us, who likewise defend the truth for themselves.

* Chapter 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

Therefore one must not challenge to the scriptures, nor must the contest be
set in those things in which the victory is either none or uncertain or too
little certain. For even if a comparison of the scriptures did not so turn out
as to set both parts equal, the order of things demanded that that be first
proposed which now alone is to be disputed: to whom the faith itself belongs,
whose the scriptures are, from whom, and through whom, and when, and to whom
the discipline has been handed down by which people become Christians. For
where the truth of the Christian discipline and faith has appeared to be,
there will be the truth of the scriptures and of the expositions and of all
the Christian traditions.

* Chapter 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

Christ Jesus, our Lord, let it be permitted to say in the meantime, whoever he
is, of whatever God the Son, man and God of whatever matter, teacher of
whatever faith, promiser of whatever reward, while he was acting on earth,
himself pronounced what he was, what he had been, what will of the Father he
was administering, what he determined man must do, whether to the people
openly or apart to the disciples, from whom he had chosen twelve principal men
at his side, destined as teachers for the nations. And so, one of them having
been struck off, as he was departing to the Father after the resurrection he
commanded the remaining eleven to go and teach the nations, to be baptized
into the Father and into the Son and into the Holy Spirit. Immediately
therefore the apostles, whose name means sent, having taken by lot a twelfth,
Matthias, into the place of Judas from the authority of the prophecy which is
in the psalm of David, having obtained the promised power of the Holy Spirit
for mighty works and for speech, first through Judaea, the faith in Jesus
Christ having been attested and churches instituted, then having set out into
the world they promulgated the same doctrine of the same faith to the nations.
And likewise they founded churches in each city, from which the remaining
churches have thereafter borrowed the shoot of the faith and the seeds of the
doctrine, and borrow them daily, so that they become churches. And by this
they too will be counted apostolic as offspring of the apostolic churches.
Every kind must be registered to its origin. And so the many and great
churches are that one first church from the apostles, from which all are. Thus
all are first and all apostolic, while they all prove the one, by the unity of
the communication of peace and the appellation of brotherhood and the token of
hospitality, which rights no other reason governs than the one tradition of
the same sacrament.

* Chapter 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

From this therefore we direct the prescription: if the Lord Christ Jesus sent
apostles to preach, other preachers are not to be received than those Christ
instituted, because neither does any other know the Father except the Son and
he to whom the Son has revealed him, nor does the Son seem to have revealed to
any others than the apostles whom he sent to preach, namely what he revealed

errors, not so much to be wondered at as if it were difficult and
inexplicable, since from the secular scriptures too an example of a facility
of this kind is at hand. You see today a wholly other fable composed from
Virgil, the matter fitted to the verses and the verses to the matter. In short
H(??)sidius Geta has most fully sucked a Medea tragedy out of Virgil. A
certain kinsman of mine from the same poet among the other leisures of his pen
has unfolded the Tablet of Cebes. Homeric centos also they are accustomed to
be called who from the songs of Homer stitch into one body their own works in
the centonian manner from many things composed here and there. And certainly
the divine literature is more fertile for the facility of any matter. Nor do I
risk saying that the scriptures themselves too were so arranged from the will
of God as to supply materials to the heretics, since I read that there must be
heresies, which cannot be without the scriptures.

It will follow, by whom is the understanding interpreted of those things which
make for heresies? From the devil, of course, whose parts it is to invert the
truth, who rivals even the very things of the divine sacraments with the
mysteries of idols. He too baptizes certain people, certainly believers and
his faithful; he promises from the washing an exposition of offenses: and if I
still remember, Mithra signs his soldiers there on the foreheads; he also
celebrates an offering of bread, and he brings in an image of the
resurrection, and under a sword he redeems a crown. What of this, that he also
sets a chief pontiff in one marriage? he has virgins too, he has continents
too. For the rest, if we roll out the superstitions of Numa Pompilius, if we
consider the priestly offices and insignia and privileges, if the sacrificial
ministries and instruments and vessels of the sacrifices themselves and the
curiosities of expiations and vows, has not the devil plainly imitated that
exactness of the Jewish law? He therefore who so rivalrously affected to
express, in the businesses of idolatry, the very things from which the
sacraments of Christ are administered, certainly also the same man and by the
same talent desired and was able to fit the instruments too of the divine
things and of the holy Christians, sense from senses, words from words,
parables from parables, to a profane and rival faith. And therefore no one
ought to doubt that the spiritual things of wickedness, from which heresies
also come, have been sent in by the devil, nor that heresies stand apart from
idolatry, since they are of the same author and of the same work of which
idolatry also is. They either invent another God against the Creator, or, if
they confess one Creator, they set him forth otherwise than he is in the true.
And so every lie which they speak about God is in some way a kind of idolatry.

I will not omit a description of even the heretical conversation itself, how
futile, how earthly, how human it is, without gravity, without authority,
without discipline, as agreeing with their faith. First it is uncertain who is
a catechumen, who a faithful man; they approach equally, they hear equally,
they pray equally, even ethnics, if they have come in; they will throw what is
holy to dogs and pearls to swine, though not true ones. They want simplicity
to be a prostration of discipline, whose care among us they call pandering.
They also mix peace everywhere with all. For it makes no difference to them,
though treating diverse things, provided they conspire to the overthrow of one
truth. All swell, all promise knowledge. Catechumens are perfect before they
are fully taught. The heretical women themselves, how forward they are! who
dare to teach, to contend, to do exorcisms, to promise healings, perhaps even
to baptize. Their ordinations are rash, light, inconstant. Now they place
neophytes, now men bound to the age, now our apostates, so that they may bind
them by glory, because they cannot by truth. Nowhere is there easier
advancement than in the camps of rebels, where to be there itself is to
deserve. And so another is bishop today, tomorrow another; today a deacon who
tomorrow is a reader, today a presbyter who tomorrow is a layman. For they
also enjoin priestly functions on laymen.

But of the administration of the word what shall I say, when this is their
business, not of converting ethnics, but of overturning our people? This glory
they hunt more, if they work a ruin of those who stand, not an elevation of
those who lie. Since even their very work does not come from their own
building, but from the destruction of the truth, they undermine ours so that
they may build their own. Take from them the law of Moses and the prophets and
the Creator God, they have no accusation to speak. So it comes about that they
more easily work ruins of standing buildings than constructions of lying
ruins. To these works only they act humble and bland and submissive. For the
rest they do not even know reverence for their own presidents. And this is why
there are almost no schisms among the heretics, because, though they exist,
they do not appear. For the unity itself is a schism. I lie, if they do not
even vary among themselves from their own rules, while each one likewise
modulates by his own judgment what he has received, just as he who handed it
down composed it from his own judgment. The progress of the thing recognizes
its own nature and the custom of its origin. The same was allowed to the
Valentinians which was allowed to Valentinus, the same to the Marcionites
which was allowed to Marcion, to renew the faith from their own judgment. In
short, heresies thoroughly inspected are all found dissenting in many things
from their own authors.

Most do not even have churches; without a mother, without a seat, orphans in
faith, exiles, they wander in their own cheapness.

The dealings of the heretics have also been marked with magicians rather than
with very many, with mountebanks, with astrologers, with philosophers, given
to curiosity of course. Seek and you shall find, they remember everywhere. So
far even from the kind of conversation the quality of the faith can be
estimated. Discipline is the index of doctrines. They deny that God is to be
feared. And so all things are free and loose for them. But where is God not
feared, except where he is not? Where God is not, neither is there any truth.
Where there is no truth, such a discipline too is deserved. But where God is,
there is fear toward God, which is the beginning of wisdom. Where there is
fear toward God, there is gravity, honest and astonished diligence and anxious
care and a tested choice and a deliberated communication and a deserved
promotion and a religious subjection and a devoted attendance and a modest
procession and a united church and all things of God.

Accordingly these testimonies of a stricter discipline among us accede to the
proof of the truth, from which it profits no one to turn aside who remembers
the future judgment, at which we all must stand before the tribunal of Christ,
rendering an account first of the faith itself. What then will they say who
have defiled it by heretical adultery, the virgin handed over by Christ? I
believe they will allege that nothing was ever pronounced to them by him or by
his apostles about crooked and perverse doctrines going to be, and commanded
about guarding against them and abominating them. They will rather acknowledge
their own fault and that of their people, who did not build us up beforehand.
They will add besides many things about the authority of each heretical
teacher; that those men especially confirmed the faith of their doctrine,
raised the dead, restored the weak, signified future things, so that they
might deservedly be believed apostles. As if this too were not written, that
many would come who would even put forth the greatest mighty works for
fortifying the deceit of a corrupted preaching. And so they will deserve
pardon. But if, mindful of the Lord's and the apostolic scriptures and
denunciations, they have stood in an intact faith, I believe they will be in
danger concerning pardon, the Lord answering: I had plainly foretold that
teachers of deceit would come in my name and even of prophets and of apostles,
and I had commanded my disciples to preach the same things to you; namely when
you would not believe. I had once delegated the gospel and the doctrine of the
same rule to my apostles; afterwards it pleased me to change some things from
there. I had promised a resurrection, even of the flesh; but I thought again,
lest I should not be able to fulfill it. I had shown myself born from a
virgin, but afterwards it seemed shameful to me. I had spoken of a Father who
makes the sun and the rains; but another better father adopted me. I had
forbidden you to lend an ear to heretics; but I erred. It befits those to
think such things who go off course and do not guard against the danger of the
faith of truth. But now indeed it has been handled by us generally against all
heresies, to be driven off from a conference of the scriptures by certain and
just and necessary prescriptions. For the rest, if the grace of God has
granted it, we will also answer some specially. These things in the faith of
the truth, to those who read them through at leisure, the peace and grace of
our God Jesus Christ forever.
