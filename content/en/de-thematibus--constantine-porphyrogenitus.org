A painstaking work of Constantine the emperor, son of Leo, concerning the
themes belonging to the empire of the Romans Whence they got the names and
what the appellations of these signify and that some of them are archaic and
others acquired a new appellation [First then of the so-called Anatolikon, if
it seems good, let us make mention; here the themes of the East, that is of
Asia Minor. προλογ.1 First theme the so-called Anatolikon Not according to the
supposition of the many, as it appears to me, did the appellation of the
themes come about. For it is not some ancient thing, nor did any of those who
wrote history mention such a naming as they are called now; but formerly and
at the beginning there were certain tagmata and legions registered by nation,
as the legion of the forty martyrs [was called Keraunobolos] and another of
the Marmaritai and another Pisidian and another Thessalian and another
otherwise named, being under a dux and a leader and often also a praepositus.
And these things when? When the emperors campaigned with the people and laid
the yoke of Roman slavery on those who rose against it and all but besieged
the whole inhabited world that was disorderly and contradictory, as Caesar
Julius, as the admirable Augustus, as that Trajan the renowned, as the great
among emperors Con stantine and Theodosius and those after them who embraced
Christianity and piety. For it was not reasonable, when the emperor was
present in the camp, for a general to give orders; for they were under leaders
and taxiarchs and all the affairs of the wars hung upon the counsel of the
emperor; and the whole people looked to one and only the emperor. But when the
emperors ceased from campaigning, then they also appointed generals and
themes. And to this the rule of the Romans has come down until today. But now,
the Roman empire having been narrowed both in the east and in the west and
having been amputated from the reign of Heraclius the Libyan, those who ruled
from him, not having where and how they should use their authority, cut their
own rule and the tagmata of the soldiers into certain small parts, especially
Hellenizing and throwing away the ancestral and Roman tongue. For they called
the chiliarchs Longini and the hecatontarchs centurions and the now generals
counts. For the name itself of the theme is Greek and not Roman, being named
from the positioning. First then of the so-called Anatolikon, if it seems
good, let us make mention; here the themes of the East, that is of Asia Minor.
ˉˉΑ. First theme the so-called Anatolikon The Anatolikon theme then is so
called, not because it is of the upper and first east, whence the sun rises,
but because toward us who inhabit Byzantium and the land of Europe it is
called eastern. But toward those who inhabit the Mesopotamia of Syria and
great Asia, in which dwell Indians and Ethiopians and Egyptians, it is called
western middle and Asia Minor; for the East, as we said, belongs to Indians
and Ethiopians and Egyptians and the rest who inhabit the East. Nevertheless
in order that we may not speak at length and seem to some to work wonders
concerning the naming of the themes, we shall speak more briefly as the truth
itself has it. The Anatolikon theme then, as it is now named, has had its
whole habitation from five nations. For it has made its beginning from the
town called Meros, and it is called Phrygia Salutaria as far as Ikonion. And
the parts neighboring the Isaurians toward the Taurus are called Lykaonia. And
the parts toward the sea and the south and the mountains of the so-called
Psychron and as far as Attaleia itself are called Pamphylia. And the upper and
inland parts are called Pisidia. And the parts from Akroinos as far as Amorion
are called Phrygia Kapiatiane. And the parts toward


leaning toward the sea and bounding Caria are called Lycia. As many then as
are inland and neighboring the Taurus and extending as far as the borders of
Cappadocia are called Anatolika; for they are parts of the Anatolikon theme.
Herethen is the limit of the Anatolikon theme, having its beginning from
Meros, which is the end of the Opsikion, and terminating as far as the borders
of Isauria in length; and in breadth it has on the left certain parts of the
Bucellarios and the beginning of Cappadocia; and on the right both Isauria and
the beginning of the parts of the Kibyrrhaiote. Behold then the Anatolikon
theme; and at the beginning it was not thus; but into chiliarchies and
pentakosiarchies [and hecatontarchies and pentekontarchies] the soldierly
tagma was divided, and one certain proconsul ruled all the tagmata, as in the
later times, and down to Justinian a magister was sent to the tagmata as
exarch. And witness is the tomb lying in Smyrna of a certain Publius the
proconsul, writing thus: "Publius proconsul, ruler of Ionia, Phrygia, Aeolis,
Maeonia, Lydia, the Hellespont, Mysia, Bithynia, Tarsia, Galatia, of the
Mariandynoi, of Pontus, Paphlagonia, Cappadocia the lesser and the greater,
and of Isauria and Lykaonia and as far as the borders of the Taurus and of
lesser Armenia." Behold the rule of one proconsul. And there came to be also
in the later times another certain master of soldiers, patrician in rank, by
name Jordanes, whose are the silver relief missoria which lie in the imperial
vestiarion, having an inscription of this sort: "Of Jordanes, master of
soldiers of the East and of the remaining nations under Asia Minor." And this
man too was monarch of the same East, that is of Asia Minor, and he flourished
and was in repute in the days of Arcadius the emperor. As we said before then,
the theme now called Anatolikon is inhabited by five nations, Phrygians,
Lykaonians, Isaurians, Pamphylians, Pisidians. By nation then there was a
chief of them when they were free and before the yoke and the rule of the
Romans fell upon them. But when the yoke of the Romans fell and their empire
increased, they were all enslaved and became mixed under one rule. And they
were divided and became themes when the rule of the Romans began to be docked
and amputated and contracted little by little by the godless Hagarenes; and
down to Justinian the emperor they were all under one rule, and in the reign
of Maurice; and witness is Belisarius himself, who was sole general of all the
East. But when the Hagarenes began to campaign against the Romans and to
destroy their countries and cities, the emperors of the time were forced to
cut the one rule into small pieces, and one they assigned to a certain general
and called Anatolikon, because, as we said before, toward us who inhabit
Byzantium it lies toward the east. And of old there was a chief by nation; and
they were governed as was their custom, some by democracies, others being
subject to tyrants. But having come under the empire of the Romans they were
divided into provinces and hegemonies and ducates and the so-called
consulares, that is counselors. And some of them were also princes, as it is
described in the martyrdoms of the saints, and indeed also scriniarii. And the
prefects were twofold, some of the praetoria, others of cities; and those of
the praetoria served the emperors when they campaigned, both providing the
food of the soldiers ready and arranging the camps of the emperors and
clearing the roads. And these things are those of the Romans; but when the
Persians ruled and after them the Macedonians, all Asia was divided into
satrapies, and there was a satrap of the Lydians, another of the Phrygians,
another of the Ionians, another of the Pamphylians, another of the Carians,
another of the Paphlagonians, another of the Bithynians, and simply all
so-called Asia Minor, as it seemed good to the Persian, and then to the
Macedonians, had been partitioned and arranged, and one ruled the public
affairs, another the


military. And these things to this extent. Here then let the limit of the
theme of the East be. And the so-called tourmarchai were appointed to the
service of the generals. And such a dignity signifies the one who has under
himself five hundred bow-bearing soldiers and three hundred peltasts and one
hundred dexiolaboi. For thus it lies in the book of John of Philadelphia the
one called Lydos. ασια.2 ˉˉΒˉ. Second theme the one addressed as Armeniakon
The theme called Armeniakon does not have the name as its own, nor is its
appellation some ancient one, but from the neighboring and co-dwelling
Armenians it acquired the appellation. And I think I may say that under
Heraclius the emperor and the later times it inherited such an appellation;
for neither Strabo the geographer mentioned such a naming, although being a
Cappadocian by race from the city of Amaseia, nor Menippos who registered the
stadia of the whole inhabited world, nor indeed Skylax of Karyanda, nor any
other of those who have written history, nor Pausanias of Damascus himself.
And such a naming appears more recent; for neither Procopius, nor indeed
Agathias, nor Menander, nor Hesychios the Illustrious mentioned such a name,
those who composed the chronicles under the reign of Justinian. For the
ancients cut Cappadocia into three parts; both into Kataonia beginning from
Melitene, and the part from the Taurus named Taurike, and the inland itself,
in which Caesarea the city, the metropolis of the Cappadocians, is founded.
And the ancients call both great and lesser Cappadocia, great that from
Caesarea and the Taurus and as far as the Pontic sea, which the river Halys
bounds from the west, and Melitene from the east. And this is great
Cappadocia. And of these witness is Polybius, who wrote the Roman history, who
bounds Cappadocia from both the Taurus and Lykaonia and as far as the Pontic
sea; for Neocaesarea itself and Koloneia and Melitene are all called
Cappadocia. And the name is Persian; for a certain Persian man *** in a hunt
to Artaxerxes the king, or I do not know to some other; a lion having met
seized the horse of the king. And the Persian happening to be found at the
meeting of the beast, having drawn the akinakes, rescued the king who was in
small danger and killed the lion. This Persian then, having gone up upon a
certain most high mountain and having looked round all the land as much as a
human eye looks about both to the east and to the west, to the north and to
the south, received all as a gift from the king. And these things Polybius
relates. And it is inhabited toward Koloneia by Armenians, and toward the
inland, where Amaseia is and the so-called Dazymon, by the so-called
Leukosyroi; nevertheless all the Cappadocians and those who inhabit as far as
the Pontic sea [Herodotus] Strabo calls Leukosyroi, for distinction from the
Syrians living beyond the Taurus and Cilicia who are addressed as Melanosyroi.
And lesser Cappadocia, the one now ranking as a theme, which is neighboring to
Lykaonia and crosses as far as Caesarea itself, and is broadened as far as the
beginning of the Bucellarioi, and ends toward the east as far as Rhodentos
itself and the fortress called Loulon and Podendos itself. And the parts
beyond are parts of Cilicia, of which Tarsos and the hearth of Mopsos and
Adana begin. And this is lesser Cappadocia, the one lately ranking in the name
of a theme. And in the upper times, whether of Justinian or of certain other
of the emperors, Cappadocia having been cut into three parts, the inland was
called Charsianon, from a certain Charsios a ruler who at that time excelled
in the war against the Persians, and because of this it has ranked in the
order of a theme and a generalship until now; and the lower and toward the sea
was called Armeniakon, because it is neighboring to the Armenians; and the
upper and


neighboring to the Lykaonians and the Taurus, lesser Cappadocia now, as we
said, ranking in the name of a theme. And it was under kings down to Augustus
Caesar. And it was under the Persian authority, before the kingdom of Rome
increased. And it also became autonomous when Ariarathes the king ruled it,
from whom the lake that bears salt, which they lately in barbarian fashion
call Karateia, was named Ariaratheia. And after Ariarathes Archelaus the
kinsman of Herod the king of Judaea reigns over it. And after this man, the
kingship having ceased, it changed its form into a province by the Romans, and
a proconsul both taxed it and arranged it. And there are distinguished cities
in the first province four: Caesarea the metropolis, named from the great
Caesar Julius, which formerly was called Mazaka, from Mousoch the first
ancestor of the Cappadocians; second city Nyssa; third Therma, fourth
Regepodantos. And of second Cappadocia there are cities under a hegemon eight:
Tyana, Faustinaupolis, Kybistra, Nazianzos, Sasima, Parnasos, Regedora,
Mokissos and a fortress called Koron. And of third Cappadocia, which is now
named Armeniakos, there are cities seven: Amaseia, Ibora, Zalichos, Andrapa,
Aminsos Neocaesarea, Sinope. And the race of the Cappadocians has been cried
out by all for bad character, so that even a proverb of this sort has been
brought out concerning them: "three kappas are worst: Cappadocia Crete
Cilicia." And in epigrams: Cappadocians are base always; and if they get a
belt, baser; and for the sake of gain basest; but if twice and thrice they lay
hold of a great carriage, then indeed they become basest upon basest. And
otherwise: A wicked viper once bit a Cappadocian; but she herself also died
having tasted the venom-casting blood. And from this land thus blasphemed such
men sprouted, or rather to say, they rose like many-lighted stars, renowned
for wisdom, zealots of the faith of the Christians, Gregory the Wonderworker
the bishop of Neocaesarea, and Phaidimos of Amaseia and Basil the renowned who
under Licinius was martyred for the faith of the Christians and Basiliskos the
archbishop of Komana who himself also was perfected by martyrdom, with whom
the relic of John Chrysostom was laid, and Basil the Great the bishop of
Caesarea; and the Theologian Gregory, who inherited the bishopric of
Nazianzos, now laid in the church of the Apostles with the patriarchs of this
city by the pious and Christ-loving emperor Constantine, and the brothers of
the great Basil, both Peter and Gregory and Naukratios, and Kaisarios the
brother of Gregory the Theologian. And these things of Cappadocia as in
epitome. ασια.3 ˉˉΓˉ. Third theme of the Thrakesioi The now so-called theme of
the Thrakesioi of old and at the beginning was named Asia Minor, and the one
who ruled it, a proconsul, was called Asiarch. And that I may not speak of
those outside, the martyr of the truth himself, Luke the evangelist and
apostle, in the Acts of the Apostles says the same, mentioning Alexander who
was then first of the Ephesians, calling him Asiarch. And they were surnamed
Thrakesioi from a cause of this sort; in the days of Alyattes the king of the
Lydians, a certain man with a wife and his own children, setting out from
Mysia the country of the Thracians, –of which Homer also makes mention saying
thus: Of the close-fighting Mysians and the noble Hippemolgoi– crossed over
into the parts of Asia into the country called Lydia and settled near the city
of Sardis. When then the king was sitting toward the wall of the city, the
wife of the Thracian was passing, carrying on her head a jar, and in her hands
a distaff and spindle, and behind toward the belt a horse was bound; and on
the crown the jar was full of water, and in her hands she was working spinning
the spindle from the distaff, and behind toward the belt the horse was
following having been watered from the spring. The king having seen this


wondered greatly and asked whence and who and of what city she was. And she
answered that she was Mysian by race from ***; and this is a small town of
Thrace. The king then, taking a starting-point from the woman, having made an
embassy to the king of Thrace named Kotys, from there took men as settlers
with women and children, a sufficient crowd. From this race those inhabiting
Asia Minor were named Thrakesioi, because they are labor-toiling and laborious
and handicraftsmen. And the whole theme of the Thrakesioi is composed of races
of this sort: Lydians, Maeonians, Carians, Ionians; and Ionians are called
those who inhabit both Miletos and Ephesos; and those of the inland Sardianoi,
both Lydians and Maeonians and Carians and the inhabitants of lesser Phrygia.
And thus the Thrakesioi. And this history Nicholas of Damascus writes in his
eighteenth book, he who became secretary of Herod the king. And they were
named Thrakesioi because they are from the land of the Thracians, as we said.
And there are cities about Asia twenty: first Ephesos, second Smyrna, third
Sardis, fourth Miletos, fifth Priene, sixth Kolophon, seventh Thyateira,
eighth Pergamon, ninth Magnesia, tenth Tralleis, eleventh Hierapolis, twelfth
Kolossai now called Chonai, where there is a renowned church of the archangel
Michael, thirteenth Laodikeia, fourteenth Nyssa, fifteenth Stratonikeia,
sixteenth Alabanda, seventeenth Alinda, eighteenth Myrina, nineteenth Teos,
twentieth Lebedos, [twenty-first Philadelphia] and certain others. And these
things concerning this. ασια.4 ˉˉΔ. Fourth theme the one named Opsikion The
theme called Opsikion has the appellation known to all; for opsikion is said
in the Roman tongue, which signifies in the voice of the Greeks those who go
before in front of the emperor in good order and honor. Whence the one leading
the Opsikion does not even rank as general, but is addressed both as count and
by the naming and the additional name. And the circuit of the Opsikion is
inhabited by so many tribes and nations, beginning from the sea, that is the
gulf of Astakos, and the cape of Daskylion, and as far as Triton and the
mountains of Sigriane and Prokonnesos itself and as far as Abydos itself and
Kyzikos and Parion. And the lower parts and by the sea beginning from the gulf
of Astakos and the Pylai and as far as the inland of the Mysian so-called
Olympos and the country of the so-called Dagotthenoi and Prousias itself the
Bithynians inhabit; and the parts toward the south of Olympos and as far as
the river Rhyndakos the Mysians inhabit; and the parts toward the sea and
Kyzikos both Phrygians and Graikoi inhabit, having been enriched in the naming
from the river Granikos; in which took place the first battle of Alexander of
Macedon against the generals of the Persians. And above it is stretched as far
as Dorylaion and Kotyaeion itself, from which is that most named and ancient
Aesop who first introduced myth-making into life, and as far as the town of
Meros toward the east. And toward the west it ends at Abydos, and in breadth
from the river Rhyndakos and as far as the town called Domateros. And thus the
Opsikion. And we have said its naming because of going before in front of the
emperor and arranging and directing his roads and stations. And the nations
that complete the Opsikion are so many: Bithynians, Mysians, Phrygians,
Dardanians, who are also called Trojans. And the theme called Opsikion has
distinguished cities so many: first Nicaea which also ranks as metropolis,
second Kotyaeion, third Dorylaion, fourth Medaeion, fifth Apameia, sixth
Myrleia, seventh Lampsakos, eighth Parion, ninth Kyzikos, tenth Abydos. ασια.5

theme of Optimatos has no


communion with themes; for it has been taken on for service alone, because it

fifth Teion. And the nations named in part are so many: Galatai, Mariandynoi,
Bithynians. ασια.7 ˉˉΖ. Seventh theme the one addressed as Paphlagonia And the
