HOMILY. When Saturninus and Aurelianus were sent into exile, and Gainas went
out from the city; and concerning the love of money.
=52.413=
αʹ. I have been silent a long time, and after a long interval I have returned
again to your love; but not from any =52.414= sloth and sluggishness of body,
but stilling the tumults, putting the waves to sleep, setting the storm to
rights, =52.415= drawing up those who were shipwrecked, hastening to lead by
the hand those who were being submerged toward harbor and calm. For I am a
common father of all, and it is necessary to care not only for those who
stand, but also for those who have fallen; not only for those borne with a
fair wind, but also for those tossed by the surge; not only for those in
safety, but also for those in dangers. For these reasons, then, I was absent
during the time that has passed, yielding, going about, exhorting, entreating,
beseeching the lords that the calamity be dissolved. And since those gloomy
things have come to an end, I have returned again to you who are in safety,
who are sailing with much calm. I went to those men so as to calm the storm; I
returned to you, so that a storm might not arise. I went away to those, that I
might deliver them from the grievous things; I have come to you, that you
might not fall into the gloomy things. As one must care not only for those who
stand, but also for those who have fallen, so again not only for those who
have fallen, but also for those who stand: for those, that they may rise, for
these, that they may not fall; for those, that they may be delivered from the
terrible things that hold them, for these, that they may not fall into the
grievous things that are expected. For nothing of human affairs is firm,
nothing unmoved, but it imitates a raging sea, and every day it travails with
strange and harsh shipwrecks. All things are full of tumults and confusion;
all things reefs and precipices; all things shoals and sunken rocks; all
things fears, and dangers, and suspicions, and tremblings, and agonies. No one
has confidence in anyone; each fears his neighbor. That time is perhaps
present which the prophet, sketching it, said: Trust not in friends, and hope
not in leaders; each of you shall keep away from his neighbor; Guard against
her that lies in your bosom, so as not to communicate anything to her. Why?
Because it is an evil time. Because every brother will utterly trip with the
heel, and every friend will walk deceitfully. There is no friend that is safe,
no brother that is firm. The beauty of love has been taken away; a civil war
holds all things, a civil war, and this one is not even open, but shadowed.
Countless masks everywhere. Many are the skins of sheep, innumerable wolves
hidden everywhere; and one would live more safely among enemies than among
those who seem to be friends. Those who yesterday were paying court, who were
flattering, who were kissing hands, have all at once now appeared as enemies,
and having thrown off the masks, they have become more bitter than all
accusers, charging and slandering for the very things for which they formerly
were acknowledging thanks.
βʹ. What then is the cause of all these things? The love of money, the madness
about the love of silver, the incurable disease, the furnace that is never
quenched, the tyranny stretched out everywhere over the inhabited world. For
this reason we too, what we said before, we will not cease saying even now,
although many formerly accused us, and said these things: Will you not cease
arming your tongue against those who are rich? Will you not cease continually
warring against such men? Do I war against these? Do =52.416= I arm myself
against them? Do I not say and do all things on their behalf, while they
themselves have sharpened the swords against themselves? Has not the trial of
the facts shown how I, who was rebuking and continually charging, was seeking
what was profitable, while they were rather enemies, who rebuke us for saying
these things? See our words come forth upon the facts. Did I not always say
that wealth is a runaway, going from this man to that? And would that it only
passed over, and did not also kill; would that it only changed its place, and
did not also destroy. But now it both leaves them desolate of its protection,
and hands them over to the sword, and leads them to the pit, being a harsh
traitor, and warring most of all against those who love it. It is an
ungrateful runaway, an implacable murderer, an untamable beast, a cliff sheer
on every side, a reef full of unceasing waves, a sea cut through by countless
winds, a tyrant giving harsh commands, a master harsher than some barbarian, a
relentless enemy, an irreconcilable foe, never dissolving the hatred toward
those who possess it.
γʹ. But poverty is not such, but the contrary of what has been said. For it is
an inviolable place, a calm harbor, unceasing safety, luxury freed from
dangers, unmixed pleasure, an untroubled life, a waveless life, unconquerable
plenty, mother of philosophy, a bridle of arrogance, a taking-away of
punishment, a root of humility. For what reason, then, tell me, do you flee
this, and pursue that which is hostile, the murderer, harsher than every
beast? For such is the love of money; such is the madness about riches. Why do
you settle a continual enemy with yourself for all time? Why do you make the
beast savage, which one ought to tame? And how, he says, might it become
tractable? If you would bear with my words, even now when the falls are still
ringing in the ears, when the calamity is at its height, when all are in
tumult and dejection. How then will the beast be delivered from being a beast?
For I can change it, if you are willing; for such is the strength of words.
How then might it be changed from its savagery? If we learn how it becomes
harsh. How then does it become harsh? Just as lions, just as leopards, just as
bears, when they are shut in and confined in darkness, stir up their spirit,
increase their wrath; so also wealth, when it is shut in and buried, roars
more than a lion, and always startles one up; but if you lead it out of the
darkness, and scatter it into the bellies of the poor, the beast becomes a
sheep, the plotter a protector, the reef a harbor, the shipwreck a calm. For
you may see this also in the case of ships: when the cargo is beyond measure,
it sinks the vessel; but when it is proportionate, it is borne with a fair
wind. This same thing happens also in our houses: when you gather money beyond
need, a small assault of wind, and any chance circumstance of unexpected
affairs, sinks the vessel with all hands; but if you lay up as much as need
requires, even if a storm come on, you easily run through the waves. Do not
then desire the more, lest you fall from the whole; nor gather what is
superfluous to need, lest you lose the =52.417= things that are toward need;
nor overstep the accustomed bounds, lest you be stripped of all that you have
at once; but cut away the superfluous, that you may be rich in the necessary
things. Do you not see that husbandmen also prune the vine, so that all the
strength may be shown not in the leaves and in the shoots, but in the root of
the plant? Do this yourself also; cut away the leaves, and spend all your
eagerness upon the bearing of the fruits. But if you are not willing in
prosperity, expect days of adversity; in the calm look for a storm; in health
wait for disease; in wealth expect poverty and beggary. For remember, it says,
a time of famine in a time of plenty, poverty and want in a day of wealth. For
if you are so disposed, you will both administer wealth with much sobriety,
and you will bear poverty when it comes with much courage. For what was not
expected, when it has fallen on one, throws into tumult and disturbs; but what
was hoped for, when it has come to pass, does not bring on much tumult. These
two good things, then, you gain: neither to be drunk and out of your mind in
prosperity, nor to be thrown into tumult and disturbed at the change toward
the contrary, especially if you are going to be always watching for the
contrary; for the expectation suffices in place of the trial. What I mean is
this: are you rich? expect poverty every day. For what reason and why? Because
this expectation will be able to benefit you most of all. For he who expects
poverty, being in wealth, is not puffed up, is not made vain, does not melt
away, does not long for what belongs to others; for the fear of the
expectation, being present like some pedagogue, makes sober, stills the mind,
and does not let the evil shoots that come from the love of money grow,
hindering them and cutting them away with the fear of the contrary as with
some sickle.
δʹ. This one greatest good, then, you will gain; and another not less than
this, that even when it has arrived you do not fear. Let the expectation of
the painful things go before, then, so that the trial of the grievous things
may not arrive; for this reason the trial comes, because the expectation is
not present; since if that were setting the man right, there would be no great
need of this. And Jonah the prophet is a witness of this in the case of the
Ninevites: as those men, expecting by the prophet's prediction to fall into an
incurable calamity, turned back the wrath by the expectation of the evils that
were to come; but the Jews, not being persuaded by the prophet who spoke of
the taking of Jerusalem, underwent the terrible things. For a wise man, having
feared, turned aside from evil; but the fool mixes with it, being confident.
He who expects to become poor, while living in prosperity, would not quickly
become poor; for what you were not willing to gain from the expectation, this
you will learn well from the trial. Being in wealth, then, look for poverty;
being in prosperity, look for famine; being in glory, expect dishonor; living
in health, wait for disease. Continually consider the nature of human affairs,
disposed no better than the streams of rivers, more swiftly falling than smoke
dissolving into air, and more worthless than a shadow =52.418= running by. If
you philosophize these things, neither will the good things be able to puff
you up, nor the grievous things to humble you; if, when the good things are
present, you do not cling to them too much, neither when they have gone will
you be pained. If you accustom your mind in the expectation of the contrary,
often the contrary things will not even be present, and if they should arrive,
they will not lay hold of you too much.
εʹ. And that you may learn that I do not say these things by guesswork, I wish
to tell you an ancient history. There was a certain man, wonderful and great,
and celebrated everywhere in the inhabited world, the blessed Job, the athlete
of piety, the crowned one of the inhabited world, who went through all the
contests, who set up countless trophies against the devil. This man became
rich and poor, glorious and easily despised, with many children and childless;
this man was in royal courts, and he was also on a dunghill; this man was in a
splendid robe, and after that in a plague of worms; this man enjoyed countless
attendances, and after these things he endured countless insults, servants
rising up, friends reproaching, a wife plotting. All things at the beginning
flowed in upon him as from springs, abundance of money, greatness of power, an
array of glory, peace and safety, honors and attendances, and health of body,
and good fortune of children, and there was nothing grievous there; wealth was
present to him with safety, and prosperity that did not fail, and very
reasonably; for God walled him round from every side. But later all those
things flew away, and countless storms then took lodging in that house, and
all of them one after another and continuous, and all of them with excess. For
all that he had was snatched away all at once; servants, children were buried
by a violent and untimely death, slaughtered at the table and the banquet, not
with knife and sword, but by an evil wind that shook the house down. And a
wife after these things was arming her own devices, and bringing them against
the just man; and servants, and friends, some were spitting into his face, as
he himself says: For they did not spare spittle from my face; and others were
trampling, and he was being cast out of the whole house, and his dwelling
thereafter was the dunghill, and springs of worms were bursting forth, and the
adamant was running with blood and ichor from every side, and taking a
potsherd he scraped the ichors, becoming his own executioner; and pains one
after another, and tortures that could not be borne, and night harsher than
day, and day more dreadful than night, as he says: When I lie down, I say,
When will it be day? and when I rise, I say again, When will it be evening?
and I am full of pains from evening until morning. And all things precipices,
and reefs, and the one who would comfort was no one, but those who trampled
upon him were countless; yet even so, in so great a storm and in waves truly
unbearable, he stood toward all things noble and not overturned; and the cause
of these things was this, as I said, that when he was rich, he looked for
poverty; when he was healthy, he waited for disease; when he became father of
so many children, he expected that he would also all at once be childless. And
this fear he was always unrolling beside himself, and this agony he was always
feeding, knowing the =52.419= nature of human affairs, and reckoning their
being only for a season. Therefore he also said: The fear which I feared has
come upon me, and the danger which I was dreading has met me. For he was
always going toward it in his thoughts, awaiting it, expecting, and looking
for it; for this reason, when it arrived, it did not throw him into confusion:
I was not at peace, nor was I quiet, nor did I rest, but wrath came upon me.
He did not say, I am not at peace, nor I am not quiet, but, I was not at
peace, in the time that has passed. For even if the affairs were preparing him
to think great things, yet the expectation of the grievous things did not let
me be quiet. Even if the excessive prosperity was making him live in luxury,
yet the agony of the things expected was driving away the security; even if
the present things were forcing him to enjoy, yet the care of the things
looked for was cutting this short. For this reason, what he had practiced in
his thoughts, these things, seeing them come to pass in the facts, he bore
nobly; the wrestling-bouts long practiced, and the things taken in advance by
expectation, seeing these present, he was not thrown into confusion. For that
he did not cling too much even to the good things when they were present, hear
what he says: If I also rejoiced when much wealth came to me, if I set gold as
my strength, if I trusted in a costly stone, if I also laid my hand upon
=52.420= countless things. What are you saying, O man? Did you not rejoice
when wealth was flowing round you? Not at all, he says. Why? Because I knew
its instability and its flowing away, I knew the possession was not lasting.
And I see, he says, the sun shining forth, and going into eclipse, and the
moon waning; for it is not in them. And what he means is this: If the stars in
heaven, even those that shine continually, receive some change, the sun being
eclipsed, and the moon waning, how is it not the last madness to think that
the things on earth are lasting, and fixed? For this reason he neither
rejoiced too much when they were present, nor was he harshly pained when they
had gone, since he knew their nature well. And we too, hearing these things,
beloved, let us not be crushed by poverty, nor puffed up by wealth; but
keeping the mind unchanged in the change of affairs, let us pluck the fruit
that comes from philosophy, that we may both enjoy the pleasure here, and
obtain the good things to come; of which may it come to pass that we all
obtain them, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ.
