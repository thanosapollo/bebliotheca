To Delos edited by Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff
#+END_QUOTE

When, O my heart, will you sing holy Delos, nurse of Apollo? Truly all the
Cyclades, holiest of the islands that lie in the sea, are well-hymned; but
Delos wants to carry the first prize from the Muses, because she first washed
Phoebus, lord of songs, and swaddled him and praised him as a god. As the
Muses hate the singer who does not sing of Pimpleia, so Phoebus hates whoever
forgets Delos. Now I will allot Delos her share of the song, that Cynthian
Apollo may praise me for caring for his dear nurse. She is windy and pathless,
wave-beaten, more a run for shearwaters than for horses, and she is fixed in
the sea. The great swell rolling round her wipes off much foam of Icarian
water; therefore sea-faring fishermen settled her. Yet it is no cause for envy
that she be named among the first, whenever the islands gather to Ocean and to
Titan Tethys, and she always leads the way. Behind her tracks Phoenician
Cyrnus follows, no mean island, and Macris of the Abantes, land of the
Ellopians, and lovely Sardo, and the isle to which Cypris first swam from the
water, and keeps it safe as her landing-fee. Those are fenced with sheltering
towers, but Delos is Apollo's. What rampart is stouter? Walls and stones would
fall under the blast of Strymonian Boreas; but a god is forever unshaken. Dear
Delos, such is the helper who has encompassed you. But if too many songs
circle about you, in what song shall I weave you? What is it dear to your
heart to hear? How at the very first the great god, striking the mountains
with the three-pronged sword the Telchines made for him, worked the islands in
the sea, and thrusting from below he levered them from their lowest roots and
rolled them into the sea? And those he rooted from their base in the deep,
that they might forget the mainland. But necessity did not crush you: free you
floated on the open waters, and your name of old was Asteria, since you leapt
the deep trench from heaven, fleeing the marriage of Zeus, like a star. Until
then golden Leto had not yet mixed with you; until then you were still Asteria
and not yet called Delos. Often sailors coming from the town of fair-haired
Troezen toward Ephyra inside the Saronic gulf caught sight of you, and coming
back from Ephyra some no longer saw you there, but you had run beside the
sharp passage of the narrow Euripus that flows with a roar, and that same day,
refusing the water of the Chalcidian sea, you swam as far as Sunium, the
Athenian headland, or to Chios, or to the island's water-soaked breast of
Parthenia (for it was not yet Samos), where the neighboring nymphs of Ancaeus,
the Mycalessides, entertained you. But when you offered your soil as a
birthday-place to Apollo, seafarers set this name on you in return, because
you no longer floated unseen, but in the waves of the Aegean sea you set the
roots of your feet. Nor did you tremble before Hera in her anger. She roared
terribly against all women in childbed who were bringing forth children to
Zeus, and against Leto above all, because she alone was about to bear to Zeus
a son dearer than Ares. Therefore she herself kept a lookout inside the
aether, driven in a great and unspeakable rage, and she barred Leto worn with
birth-pangs. Two watchers sat for her, overseeing the earth: furious Ares,
sitting armed on the high peak of Thracian Haemus, guarded the floor of the
mainland, and his two horses were stalled beside the seven-chambered cave of
Boreas; and the other, a lookout over the wide islands, sat, the daughter of
Thaumas, having darted to Mimas. There they remained as threateners over
whatever cities Leto set her course toward, and they turned them from
receiving her.

Arcadia fled, and the holy mountain of Auge, Parthenium, fled, and old Pheneus
fled behind. All the land of Pelops that lies beside the Isthmus fled, except
Aegialus and Argos; for she did not tread those paths, since Hera had been
allotted Inachus. Aonia too fled that one course, and Dirce and Strophia
followed, holding the hand of their father, dark-pebbled Ismenus, and far
behind came Asopus, heavy-kneed, because he had been stained by a thunderbolt.
And the autochthonous nymph Melia, whirled out of the dance, stopped, and her
cheek went pale, panting for her coeval oak, when she saw the hair of Helicon
shaking. My goddesses, tell, Muses: did the oaks truly come into being when
the nymphs did? /The nymphs rejoice when rain makes the oaks grow; the nymphs
weep again when the oaks have leaves no more./ Apollo, still under the bosom,
was dreadfully angered at them, and he spoke, threatening Thebes with no idle
threat: "Thebes, why, wretched one, do you call up the doom already at
your door? Do not yet force me against my will to prophesy. Not yet is
the tripod-seat at Pytho my care, nor has the great serpent died; still that
dread-jawed beast, creeping down from Pleistus, wreathes snowy Parnassus in
nine coils. Even so I will say something clearer than from the laurel. Flee
farther. Swift I will overtake you, bathing my bow in blood. You have been
allotted the children of a foul-tongued woman. You will not be my dear nurse,
nor will Cithaeron. May I be the care of the pure, and of the pure." So he
spoke. And Leto turned and went back. But when the Achaean cities refused her
as she came, Helice the companion of Poseidon and Bura, the cattle-stall of
Dexamenus son of Oeceus, she turned her feet back toward Thessaly. Anaurus
fled, and great Larisa, and the Cheironian heights; Peneius too fled, coiling
through Tempe. Hera, even then your heart still lay without pity, and you were
not broken nor did you take pity, when stretching out both arms she spoke such
things in vain: "Thessalian nymphs, race of a river, tell your father to put
his great flood to sleep. Twine about his beard, begging that the children of
Zeus be born in the water. Phthiotian Peneius, why now do you vie with the
winds? O father, you have not mounted a prize-horse. Are your feet always so
swift, or is it only against me they grow light, and have you been made to fly
all at once today?" He did not hear. "O my burden, where shall I carry you?
For my wretched sinews have given out. Pelion, bridal-chamber of Philyra, but
you stay, stay, since even the wild beasts on your mountains, the lionesses,
have often set down their raw birth-pangs." Then Peneius answered her,
shedding tears: "Leto, Necessity is a great goddess. I do not refuse your
pangs, lady (I know other women in childbed who have washed from me); but Hera
has threatened me unsparingly. Look what a watcher holds the lookout from the
highest mountain, who could easily drag me from the depths. What shall I do?
Is it sweet to you that Peneius perish? Let the fated day come. I will endure
for your sake even if I must flow forever with a thirsty ebb of my streams and
be called alone the most dishonored among rivers. See, here I am. Why more?
Only call Eileithyia." He spoke and drew back his great current. But Ares was
about to lift the peaks of Pangaeum from their roots and cast them into the
eddies and hide the streams. From on high he thundered and struck the shield
with the point of his spear; it rang a war-note. The mountains of Ossa
trembled, and the Crannonian plain, and the ill-blown skirts of Pindus, and
all Thessaly danced for fear: such was the roar that rang from the shield.

As when the recesses of Aetna, the mountain smouldering with fire, are all
shaken when the giant under the earth, Briareus, shifts to the other shoulder,
and the furnaces roar under the fire-tongs of Hephaestus, and the works with
them, and the fire-wrought cauldrons and tripods falling on one another cry
terribly: such then was the rattle of the well-rounded shield. But Peneius did
not give way again; he stayed as strong as at first, and he stopped his swift
eddies, until the daughter of Coeus called to him: "Save yourself and rejoice,
save yourself. Do not suffer evil for my sake because of this pity; there will
be a return of thanks for you." She spoke, and when she had toiled much she
went on to the islands in the sea. They did not receive her as she came: not
the Echinades, holding a sleek harbor for ships, nor Corcyra, most hospitable
of the rest, since Iris, in great haste from high Mimas, turned them all far
away. Under her cry they fled all together down the current, whichever she
met. Then she came to primeval Cos, the Meropian island, the holy recess of
the heroine Chalciope. But this word of her child held her back: "Do not,
mother, bear me there. I do not blame the island nor grudge it, since it is
sleek and well-pastured, if any other is. But from the Fates another god is
owed to her, the highest race of the Saviors; under his diadem, not unwilling,
both continents will come to be ruled by a Macedonian, and the lands that sit
in the seas, as far as the west and from where the swift horses carry the sun.
And he will know the ways of his father.

And one day a common struggle will come upon us later, when they raise a
barbarian knife against the Hellenes and Celtic Ares, later-born Titans from
the farthest west, and they rush like snowflakes or equal in number to the
stars when they are herded most thickly through the air; forts and [lost] and
the Crisaean plains and mainlands [lost] are thronged all around, and they see
the rich smoke of a neighbor burning, and no longer by hearing only, but
already they look upon the ranks of the enemy beside the temple, and already
beside my tripods swords and shameless belts and hateful shields, which will
set an evil road for the Galatians, a mindless tribe. Of these some will be my
prize, and others by the Nile, having seen their wearers breathe out in the
fire, will lie as the prizes of a king who has toiled many labors. Ptolemy who
shall be, I show you these oracles. You will praise greatly, all the days
after, the prophet still in the womb. But you, mother, take this in: there is
a slender island visible in the water, wandering on the seas. Her feet are not
in one place, but she swims on the tide like a stalk of asphodel, here south
wind, here east, wherever the sea carries her. Carry me there; for you will
come to her, and she will be willing." While he spoke so much the islands in
the sea ran away.
Asteria, lover of song, you came down from Euboea, to look on the circling
Cyclades, not long ago, but still Geraestian seaweed followed behind you.

steep. She loosed her girdle, and she leaned her shoulders back against the
trunk of the palm, worn by grim helplessness; and a wet sweat ran through her
skin. Gasping she said: "Why do you weigh down your mother, child? Here for
you, dear one, is an island floating on the sea. Be born, be born, child, and
come gently from the womb." Nymph of Zeus, heavy in anger, you were not going
to be long without news. Such a messenger ran to you, and still panting she
spoke, and the speech was mixed with fear: "Honored Hera, far foremost of
goddesses, I am yours, all things are yours, and you sit as true queen of
Olympus, and we fear no other female hand; and you, queen, will know the cause
of your anger. Leto is loosing her girdle inside an island.

All the others hated her and did not receive her; but Asteria called her by
name as she was passing, Asteria, the evil sweepings of the sea; you know it
yourself. But, dear one, for you can, defend your servants, lady, who tread
your ground by your command." She spoke and sat under the golden throne like a
hound, the hunting hound of Artemis, which when it has ceased from the swift
chase sits by her tracks, its ears very straight, always ready to take the
goddess's cry. Like her the daughter of Thaumas sat under the throne. She
never forgets her own seat, not even when sleep lays his forgetful wing upon
her, but there against the edge of the great throne, tilting her head a
little, she sleeps aslant. Never does she loose her girdle nor her swift
hunting-boots, lest her mistress speak some sudden word. And she, grievously
outraged, addressed her: "So now, O shames of Zeus, wed in secret and bring
forth in hiding, not even where poor mill-women labor in hard birth, but where
the seals of the sea give birth, on lonely reefs. Against Asteria I bear
no grudge for this fault, nor will I treat her as I would wish; I am that far
from it. Very wrongly she granted a favor to Leto. But I revere her greatly,
because she did not tread my bed, and chose the sea instead of Zeus." She
spoke. And the swans, singing attendants of the god, leaving
Maeonian Pactolus, circled Delos seven times, and they sang over the childbed,
birds of the Muses, most songful of things that fly. Hence the child later
bound so many strings on the lyre, as many times as the swans sang over the
birth-pangs. An eighth time they sang no more, and he leapt forth, and the
Delian nymphs, race of an ancient river, sang the holy song of Eileithyia far,
and at once the brazen aether echoed back the piercing cry. Nor did Hera take
it ill, since Zeus had taken out her anger. Then all your foundations became
gold, Delos, and the round lake flowed gold all day, and the natal shoot of
olive put on golden foliage, and deep Inopus, coiling, flooded with gold. And
you yourself took the child from the golden ground and set him in your lap,
and you uttered this word: /O great one of many altars, of many cities,
bearing much, rich continents and you islands that dwell around: I myself am
such as you see, hard to plow, but from me Delian Apollo shall be called, and
no other of lands shall be so loved by another god, not Cenchris by Poseidon
lord of Lechaeum, not the Cyllenian hill by Hermes, not Crete by Zeus, as I by
Apollo; and I shall no longer be a wanderer./ So you told it; and he drew the
sweet breast.

Therefore from that time you are called holiest of islands, nurse of Apollo.
Neither Enyo nor Hades nor the horses of Ares tread you. But yearly
tithe-bearing first-fruits are always sent, and all cities lead up choruses,
those that have set their lots toward the dawn and those toward evening and
those through the middle, and those who have their homes above the northern
shore, a longest-lived blood. They first carry you straw and holy handfuls of
ears; these the Pelasgians at Dodona, lying on the ground, servants of the
unsilent cauldron, receive first of all as they come out from far away. Second
they come to the Holy City and the mountains of the Malian land; from there
they sail across to the good Lelantian plain of the Abantes; and the voyage
from Euboea is no longer long, since your harbors are neighbors. First to
bring you these from the fair-haired Arimaspi were Upis and Loxo and fortunate
Hecaerge, daughters of Boreas, and the males who then were the best of the
youths; nor did they come home again, but they became well-fated, and those
were never without glory. Truly the Delian girls, when the well-sounding
wedding-song murmurs through the maidens' rooms, bring the coeval hair to the
maidens, and the boys, first-fruits of the first harvest of down, carry it to
the youths. Asteria of incense, the islands have made a circle around and
about you and thrown themselves about you as a chorus. Neither silent nor
soundless does curling-haired Hesperus look down on you, but always he sees
you ringing all around. The men sing the nome of the Lycian old man, which the
seer Olen brought you from Xanthus; the girls of the chorus strike the firm
ground with the foot. Then too the holy image of ancient Cypris is heavy with
garlands, the far-heard image which Theseus once set up with the youths when
he sailed back from Crete. They, having fled the hard bellowing and the wild
son of Pasiphae and the winding seat of the crooked labyrinth, danced a circle
about your altar, lady, as the lyre-playing rose, and Theseus led the chorus.
From there the sons of Cecrops send to Phoebus the ever-living holy things of
the pilgrim-ship, the tackle of that vessel. Asteria of many altars, of many
prayers, what merchant sailor of the Aegean has passed you by with a running
ship? The winds that blow on him are never so great, and need leads the voyage
as quickly as can be, but they have swiftly furled the sails and have not gone
aboard again before they have circled your great altar under blows, beaten,
and bitten the holy trunk of the olive with their hands turned behind them.
These the Delian nymph found as toys for the child at play and as laughter for
Apollo. O hearth of islands, well-hearth'd, hail to you yourself, and may
Apollo hail, and Leto whom you brought to childbed.
