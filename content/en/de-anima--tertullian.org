* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Having treated with Hermogenes only of the origin of the soul, inasmuch as he
presumed even this to have been constituted from the supply of matter rather
than from the breath of God, now turned to the remaining questions I shall
seem for the most part about to fight with the philosophers. Even in Socrates'
prison there was skirmishing on the state of the soul. I do not yet know this
first, whether at a fitting time for the master, even if nothing of the place
matters. For what would Socrates' soul then clearly taste, the sacred ship
already returned, the hemlock of condemnation already drained, death already
present, certainly stricken to some motion, according to nature, or driven out
of itself, if not according to nature? For although calm and tranquil, which
neither the weeping of a wife at once a widow nor the sight of children
thereafter orphans had bent by the law of piety, yet in this at least moved,
that it not be moved, its very constancy was shaken against the concussion of
inconstancy. But what else would any man condemned by injustice taste besides
the solace of injustice? much less a philosopher, an animal of glory for whom
injustice is not even to be consoled but rather insulted. In short after the
sentence, to his wife meeting him and crying out in womanly fashion, You have
been unjustly condemned, Socrates! he had already answered even from
congratulation, But you wanted it justly? Whereby there is nothing to wonder
at if even in prison, eager to break the ensnaring palms of Anytus and
Melitus, in the very presence of death he claims immortality of the soul by a
necessary presumption for the frustration of the injustice. So far had all
that wisdom of Socrates then come of set purpose from a consulted equanimity,
not from confidence of discovered truth. For to whom is truth discovered
without God? to whom is God known without Christ? to whom is Christ explored
without the Holy Spirit? to whom is the Holy Spirit fitted without the
sacrament of faith? Indeed Socrates was more easily driven by a different
spirit. Since they say a daemon clung to him from boyhood, a very bad
pedagogue in truth, even if after the gods and with the gods daemons are
ranked among poets and philosophers. For not yet had the proofs of Christian
power come forth, which alone expose that most pernicious force, never good,
nay the artificer of all error, the advocate-away of all truth. But if
therefore Socrates was wisest according also to the suffrage of the Pythian
daemon, namely doing business for his own partner, how much worthier and more
constant is the assertion of Christian wisdom, to whose afflatus the whole
force of daemons yields? This wisdom from the school of heaven, freer indeed
to deny the gods of the age, which would not prevaricate by ordering a cock to
be rendered to Aesculapius, nor bringing in new daemons, but driving out old
ones, nor corrupting youth, but forming it with every good of modesty, and
therefore enduring the unjust sentence not of one city but of the whole world
for the name of truth, so much the more hated as it is fuller, so that it does
not swallow death from a cup through a habit of pleasantness, but drains it
from the patibulum and live-burning through every device of cruelty, meanwhile
in this darker prison of the age among its own Cebeses and its own Phaedos, if
anything about the soul is to be examined, it will direct it to the rules of
God. Certainly you show no other more potent demonstrator of the soul than the
author. From God you should learn what you have from God, or not even from
another, if not from God. For who will reveal what God has covered? From there
it must be inquired whence also it is safest to be ignorant. It is better not
to know through God, because he has not revealed, than to know through man,
because he himself has presumed.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Plainly we shall not deny that philosophers have sometimes felt next to our
things. The testimony of truth is the event itself. Sometimes even in a storm,
with the traces of sky and strait confused, some harbor is hit upon by a
prosperous error, sometimes even in darkness certain entries and exits are
caught by a blind felicity. But even by nature very many things are suggested
as from the public sense with which God has deigned to endow the soul. Having
obtained this, philosophy, for the glory of its own art, has inflated it by
zeal (no wonder if I have so spoken) of eloquence, learned to construct and
destroy anything, and more persuading by speaking than by teaching. It imposes
forms on things, now equalizes them, now privates them, from certain things
prejudges uncertain, challenges to examples, as if all things were to be
compared, prescribes all things, even with properties diverse among likes,
reserves nothing for divine license, makes the laws of nature its own
opinions: I would bear it, if it were itself natural, so as to be proved
possessor of nature from partnership of condition. It has indeed seemed to
itself to have drawn even from those letters which they think sacred, because
antiquity has esteemed most authors even gods, much more deified men, as the
Egyptian Mercury, to whom Plato especially accustomed himself, as the Phrygian
Silenus, to whom, led by shepherds, Midas handed over his huge ears, as
Hermotimus, to whom the Clazomenians conferred a temple when dead, as Orpheus,
as Musaeus, as Pherecydes the master of Pythagoras. But what if the
philosophers have also rushed upon those things which among us are condemned
by the confession of apocrypha, sure that nothing is to be received that does
not conspire with the genuine prophetic apparatus, already born in that very
age, since we shall remember also false prophets and much earlier apostate
spirits, who by cunning of such talents too have equipped every face of the
age? Finally if it is credible that every inquirer of wisdom from the business
of curiosity has even gone to the prophets themselves, yet you would find more
diversity among philosophers than society, since even in the society itself
their diversity is caught. Since whatever true things and consonant with the
prophets they either commend from elsewhere or suborn elsewhere, with the
greatest injury of truth, which they make either to be helped by false things
or to patronize them. This therefore will have committed us and the
philosophers in this matter especially, that they sometimes clothe common
opinions with their own argumentations, contrary somewhere to our rule,
sometimes fortify their own opinions with common argumentations, agreeable
somewhere to their rule, so that truth is almost shut out from philosophy by
its own poisonings upon it, and therefore by either title of society,
adversary of truth, we are pressed both to free common opinions from the
argumentations of philosophers and to separate common argumentations from
their opinions, by recalling the questions to the letters of God, excepting
plainly those which without the snare of any prejudgment it will be allowed to
take up for simple testimony, because even from rivals testimony is sometimes
necessary, if it does not profit the rivals. Nor am I ignorant how great a
wood this matter is among the philosophers according to the number even of the
commentators themselves, how many varieties of opinions, how many
wrestling-grounds of views, how many offshoots of questions, how many
entanglements of expeditions. But I have also inspected medicine, sister, as
they say, of philosophy, claiming this business for herself too, as that to
which the reason of the soul seems more to belong through care of the body.
Whence she also most resists her sister, because by treating the soul as it
were face to face in her own domicile she knows it better. But let the
ambition of the preeminence of each look to itself. Philosophy has had freedom
of talent and medicine necessity of craft for stretching out retractions about
the soul; uncertain things are sought widely, presumed things are disputed
more widely. As great as the difficulty of proving, so great the labor of
persuading, so that deservedly that dark Heraclitus, noticing vaster mists
among the examiners of the soul, from weariness of questions pronounced that
he had by no means found the boundaries of the soul by entering every way. But
for the Christian few things are needed for knowledge of this matter. For both
certain things are always in few, and it is not allowed him to seek more than
what it is allowed to be found; for the apostle forbids infinite questions.
Further it is not allowed to find more than what is learned from God; but what
is learned from God is the whole.

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And would that it had been necessary for no heresies to exist, so that the
approved also might shine forth. We should experience nothing at all with the
philosophers even on the soul, patriarchs, so to speak, of the heretics. Since
even by the apostle already then philosophy was foreseen as a concussion of
truth; for having experienced at Athens a tongued city, when he had tasted all
the tavern-keepers of wisdom and eloquence there, from there he conceived that
warning edict. For likewise also the reason of the soul through philosophized
doctrines of men mixing waters with wine. Some deny the soul immortal, others
affirm it more than immortal, some dispute of substance, others of form,
others of each disposition, these draw its state from elsewhere, these lead
its exit elsewhere, according as either the honor of Plato or the vigor of
Zeno or the tenor of Aristotle or the stupor of Epicurus or the grief of
Heraclitus or the fury of Empedocles has persuaded. Divine doctrine, I think,
failed by arising from Judaea rather than from Greece. Christ also erred by
sending fishermen sooner than a sophist to the proclamation. If therefore any
things in this manner from the fumes of philosophy becloud the white and pure
air of truth, those will have to be unclouded by Christians, both by striking
the original argumentations, that is the philosophical, and by opposing the
heavenly definitions, that is the Lord's, so that both those by which the
ethnics are taken by philosophy may be destroyed, and these by which the
faithful are shaken by heresy may be beaten back. One encounter already
decided against Hermogenes, as we have prefaced, because we claim the soul
from the breath of God, not from matter, fortified even there by the
unobscurable rule of divine determination, And God, he says, blew a breath of
life into the face of man, and man was made into a living soul, certainly from
the breath of God, of this nothing more is to be revolved. It has its own
title and its own heretic. For the rest I shall draw the beginning from here.

:PROPERTIES:

:END:

After the definition of origin the question of state is suffered. For it is
consequent that, having professed the soul from the breath of God, we assign
it a beginning. This Plato shuts out, wanting the soul unborn and unmade. But
we teach it both born and made from the constitution of a beginning. Nor have
we at once gone astray by saying both, because of course one thing is born,
another made, as the former belongs to animals. But differences, by having
their own places and times, sometimes have also commerces of passivity. It
therefore takes that even a making be posited to have come about, since
everything that in any way receives being is generated. For even the maker
himself can be called parent of the made; so even Plato uses. Therefore as far
as our faith of a made or born soul, the opinion of the philosopher has been
driven off by the authority also of prophecy.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

He will have summoned some Eubulus and Critolaus and Xenocrates, and in this
place Aristotle, friend of Plato. Perhaps they will be built up more for
taking away the corpulence of the soul, if they have not looked at others on
the contrary, and indeed more, claiming a body for the soul. Nor do I mean
those only who fashion it from manifest corporeals, as Hipparchus and
Heraclitus from fire, as Hippon and Thales from water, as Empedocles and
Critias from blood, as Epicurus from atoms, if even atoms force corpulences
from their coming-together, as Critolaus and his Peripatetics from some fifth
substance I know not, if even that is a body, because it includes bodies, but
I also allege the Stoics, who, preaching the soul a spirit almost with us,
inasmuch as breath and spirit are nearest between themselves, will
nevertheless easily persuade that the soul is a body. In short Zeno, defining
the soul as a connate spirit, instructs in this way. That, he says, by whose
departure the animal dies, is a body: but by the departure of the connate
spirit the animal dies, therefore the connate spirit is a body; but the
connate spirit is the soul, therefore the soul is a body. Cleanthes also wants
likeness to answer to parents in children not only by the lineaments of the
body but also by the marks of the soul, namely from the mirror of manners and
talents and affections, but that the soul also takes likeness and unlikeness
of body. And so a body is liable to likeness or unlikeness. Likewise that the
passions of corporeals and incorporeals do not communicate among themselves.
Further both that the soul suffers with the body, for which when injured by
blows, wounds, ulcers it condoles, and the body with the soul, for which when
afflicted by care, anguish, love it coheres through the detriment of the
partner vigor, whose shame and fear it may testify by blush and pallor.
Therefore the soul is a body from communion of bodily passions. But Chrysippus
also stretches a hand to it, establishing that corporeals cannot at all be
left behind by incorporeals, because they are not even touched by them (whence
also Lucretius, For nothing can touch and be touched unless a body), but the
body when left behind by the soul is affected by death. Therefore the soul is
a body, which unless corporal would not leave the body behind.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

These the Platonists confuse by subtlety rather than by truth. Every body,
they say, must necessarily be either animate or inanimate. And if it is
inanimate, it will be moved from without, but if animate, from within. But the
soul will be moved neither from without, as not being inanimate, nor from
within, as that which rather itself moves the body. And so it does not seem to
be a body, which is not moved from some region in the form of corporeals. To
this we shall first wonder at the incongruity of the definition challenging to
those things which do not fit the soul. For the soul cannot be called an
animate body or inanimate, since it is itself that which either makes the body
animate, if it is present, or inanimate, if it is absent from it. And so what
it makes cannot be itself, so as to be called animate or inanimate. For soul
is said by the name of its substance. But if what is soul does not take being
called an animate body or inanimate, how will it be challenged to the form of
animate and inanimate bodies? Next if it belongs to a body to be moved from
without by something, but we have shown above that the soul is moved also by
another, when it prophesies, when it rages, certainly from without, when by
another, deservedly what will be moved from without by another according to
the proposition of the example I shall acknowledge a body. For if to be moved
by another belongs to a body, how much more to move another? But the soul
moves the body, and its efforts appear from without outside. For from it is it
that the feet are driven into walking and the hands into contact and the eyes
into sight and the tongue into utterance, as by a puppet motion agitating the
surface from within. Whence this force to an incorporeal soul? whence to an
empty thing to propel solids? But how do the bodily and intellectual senses
seem divided in man? They say that the qualities of bodily things, as of
earth, as of fire, are reported by bodily senses, as by touch, as by sight,
but that those of incorporeals are met by intellectual ones, as of kindness,
as of malice. And so it is agreed that the soul is incorporeal, whose
qualities are comprehended not by bodily but by intellectual senses. Plainly,
if I shall not have shut out the step of this definition. For behold I show
incorporeals subjected to bodily senses, sound to hearing, color to sight,
odor to smell, by whose example the soul also accedes to the body, lest you
say that therefore they are reported through bodily senses because they accede
to corporeals. Therefore if it is agreed that incorporeals also are
comprehended by corporeals, why should not the soul also, which is corporal,
be reported by incorporeals? At least let the definition be shut out. Among
the more notable argumentations will be also that, that they judge every body
to be nourished by corporeals, but the soul as incorporeal by incorporeals,
namely the studies of wisdom. But this step too will not stand, even Soranus,
most instructed author of methodic medicine, answering that the soul is
nourished also by corporeals, in short that when failing it is often propped
by food. Why not? by whose removal it altogether slips from the body. So even
Soranus himself, having commented most fully on the soul in four volumes, and
having experienced all the opinions of the philosophers, claims a corporal
substance of the soul, even if he has cheated it of immortality. For it is not
of all to believe what is of Christians. As therefore Soranus himself shows by
facts that the soul is nourished by corporeals, so let the philosopher also
exhibit it fed by incorporeals. But no one ever poured honeyed water from the
eloquence of Plato into one hesitating at the soul's exit, or stuffed crumbs
from the minute-speech of Aristotle. But what will so many and so great souls
of rustics and barbarians do, who lack the aliments of wisdom, and yet are
strong in untaught prudence, and without academies and Attic porticoes and the
prisons of Socrates, finally fasting from philosophy, nevertheless live? For
the aliments of studies do not profit the substance itself, but the
discipline, because they do not make the soul fatter, but more adorned. But it
is well that the Stoics also affirm the arts corporal. So far even thus the
soul is corporal, if it is believed to be nourished even by arts. But the
enormous intent of philosophy is often accustomed not even to look before the
feet. So Thales into the well. It is accustomed also, by not understanding its
own opinions, to suspect a corruption of health. So Chrysippus to hellebore.
Something of the kind I think happened to him when he denied two bodies in
one, alienated from the prospect and recollection of pregnant women, who daily
bear not single bodies, but even twins and triples in the circuit of one womb.
There is found even in civil law a certain Greek woman who brought forth a
fivefold of sons, at once mother of all, multiple parent of a unique fetus,
numerous childbed of a unique womb, who, packed with so many bodies, I had
almost said a people, was herself the sixth body. Universal condition will
testify that bodies about to proceed from bodies are already there whence they
proceed. What is from another must necessarily be second. Further nothing is
from another except while it is being born; but then there are two.

As far as the philosophers these things are enough, because as far as ours
they are from abundance; for whom the corporality of the soul will shine in
the gospel itself. A soul of a certain one sorrows among the inferi, and is
punished in flame, and is tortured in the tongue, and from the finger of a
happier soul implores the solace of dew. Do you think that exit of the
rejoicing poor man and the grieving rich man an image? and why is the name of
Lazarus there, if the thing is not in truth? But even if it is to be believed
an image, it will be a testimony of truth. For if the soul did not have a
body, the image of the soul would not take the image of a body, nor would
scripture lie about bodily members, if they were not. But what is that which
is transferred to the infernal after divorce of the body, which is detained
there, which is reserved to the day of judgment, to which even Christ by dying
descended? I think, to the souls of the patriarchs. But why, if the soul is
nothing under the earth? For it is nothing, if it is not a body. For
incorporeality is free from every kind of custody, immune both from punishment
and from fostering. For that through which it is punished or fostered, this
will be a body. I shall render of this more fully and more opportunely.
Therefore if the soul tastes beforehand any torment or solace in the prison or
inn of the inferi, in fire or in Abraham's bosom, the corporality of the soul
will have been proved. For incorporeality suffers nothing, not having that
through which it can suffer, or if it has it, this will be a body. For
inasmuch as every corporal is passible, so far what is passible is corporal.

It is otherwise abrupt and absurd therefore to exempt something from the
census of corporeals because it does not equal the rest of bodily examples.
And where are the private discriminations of properties, through which the
magnificence of the author is signed from the diversity of the same works, so
that they may be as discrete as they are equal, as friendly as also rival?
Since even the philosophers themselves agree that all things consist from
contraries according to the friendship and enmity of Empedocles. Thus
therefore even if corporeals lie opposite to incorporeals, they themselves
also so differ among themselves that the difference enlarges their species,
does not change the genus, so that they are corporeals, thus many to the glory
of God, while various, thus various, while diverse, thus diverse, while to
these other senses of qualities, to those others, while to these other
aliments, to those others, while these invisible, those visible, while these
heavy, those light. For they say that therefore also the soul is to be
reported incorporeal, because when it has departed the bodies of the dead are
made heavier, when they ought to be lighter, the weight of one body having
been exempted, if the soul is a body. For what, says Soranus, if they deny the
sea a body, because outside the sea a ship is made immobile and heavy? How
much more strongly therefore is the body of the soul, which afterward carries
about a body of so great weight with lightest mobility? For the rest even if
the soul is invisible, it is according to the condition of its own body and
according to the property of substance and according to the nature also of
those to whom it has been allotted to be invisible. Owls do not know the sun
with their eyes: eagles so sustain it that they judge the nobility of their
young from the audacity of the pupils; otherwise they will not rear as
degenerate him whom the ray of the sun has turned away. So there is something
invisible to one, not to another, which is not therefore incorporeal because
force of sight does not avail equally. For the sun is a body, since it is
fire; but what the eagle confesses, the owl may deny, yet not prejudging the
eagle. Just so also the body of the soul is invisible to flesh, if it happens,
but to the spirit it is visible. Thus John, made in the spirit of God, beholds
the souls of the martyrs.

When we assert a body of the soul of its own quality and of its own kind,
already this condition of property will prejudge of the other accidents of
corpulence that these also are present to it, which we have shown a body, but
even themselves of their own kind according to the property of the body, or
even if they are not present, this is of the property, that those which are
present to other bodies are not present to the body of the soul. And yet we
shall not inconsistently profess that all the more solemn things and in every
way due to corpulence are present to the soul also, as habit, as limit, as
that threefold distantive, I mean length, and breadth, and height, by which
the philosophers measure bodies. What now that we also give the soul a figure?
Plato unwilling, as if it were endangered concerning the immortality of the
soul. For he affirms everything figured composite and constructed: but every
composite and constructed thing dissoluble; but the soul immortal, therefore
indissoluble, as immortal, and unfigured, as indissoluble, otherwise composite
and constructed, if figured; as if figuring it in another way by intellectual
forms, beautiful by justice and the disciplines of philosophy, but deformed by
contrary arts. But we inscribe even corporal lines to it, not only from
confidence of corporality through estimation, but also from constancy of grace
through revelation. For because we acknowledge spiritual charismata, after
John also we have deserved to obtain prophecy. There is today among us a
sister allotted the charismata of revelations, which she suffers in the church
among the Lord's solemnities through ecstasy in the spirit; she converses with
angels, sometimes even with the Lord, and sees and hears sacraments, and
discerns the hearts of certain ones, and submits medicines to those desiring.
Now indeed according as scriptures are read or psalms are sung or addresses
are brought forth or petitions are delegated, so from there matters are
supplied to the visions. Perhaps we had been discussing something about the
soul, when that sister was in the spirit. After the solemnities were
transacted, the people dismissed, by which custom she is accustomed to report
to us what she has seen (for they are also most diligently digested, so that
they may even be proved), Among other things, she said, a soul was shown to me
corporally, and it seemed a spirit, but not of empty and vacant quality, nay
such as would even promise to be held, tender and lucid and of airy color, and
a form in all things human. This is the vision, and God is witness, and the
apostle a fit sponsor of charismata to be in the church: will you not believe
even if the thing itself shall have persuaded of the particulars? For if the
soul is a body, without doubt among those things which we have professed
above. Likewise also the property of color adheres to every body. What other
color therefore will you estimate for the soul than airy and lucid? Not that
air is its very substance, even if this seemed good to Aenesidemus and
Anaximenes, I think according to certain ones even to Heraclitus, nor that it
is light, even if this pleased Heraclides of Pontus. For thunder-stones are
not therefore of ignited substance because they flash with ruddy redness, nor
is the material of beryls therefore watery because they wave with strained
brightness. For how many other things does color associate, nature dissociate?
But since everything thin and translucent is rival of air, this will the soul
be, inasmuch as it is breath and a drawing of spirit, since before the very
subtlety of thinness it is endangered concerning the faith of corporality.
Thus also conceive already from your own sense no other figure to be assigned
to the human soul besides the human, and indeed of that body which each has
carried about. Let contemplation of the primordial meanwhile induce us to know
this. For recall, when God had blown into the face of man a breath of life,
and man had been made into a living soul, that whole breath was certainly at
once transmitted through the face into the interior and diffused through all
the spaces of the body and at the same time densified by divine aspiration
expressed within by every line which, densified, it had filled, and as it were
frozen in a mold. From there therefore both the corpulence of the soul was
solidified from densification and the figure formed by expression. This will
be the inner man, another the outer, doubly one; having also those eyes and
his own ears, with which Paul ought to have heard and seen the Lord, having
also the rest of the members, through which he both uses in thoughts and
functions in dreams. Thus also the rich man among the inferi has a tongue, and
the poor man a finger, and Abraham a bosom. Through these lines the souls of
the martyrs under the altar also are understood. For from the beginning in
Adam the soul, concreted and configured to the body, has made the seed both of
the whole substance and of this condition.

It belongs to the state of faith to determine the soul simple according to
Plato, that is uniform, at least by the name of substance. Let the arts and
disciplines look to themselves, let the figures also look to themselves. For
certain ones want another natural substance, spirit, to be in it, as if it
were one thing to live, which comes from the soul, another to breathe, which
is done by the spirit. For also not all animals have both present. For most
live only, not also breathe, because they do not have the organs of spirit,
lungs and arteries. But of what sort is it in the examination of the human
soul to look to the arguments of the gnat and the ant, when God the artificer
has tempered vital things proper to all animals according to the disposition
of each kind, so that no conjecture is to be taken from there? For neither
will man, if he is structured with lungs and arteries, therefore breathe from
one source, live from another, nor will the ant, if it lacks members of this
kind, therefore be denied to breathe, as living only. But to whom has so much
lain open in the works of God, that he has presumed these to be lacking to
anyone? That Herophilus, physician or butcher, who cut up six hundred that he
might scrutinize nature, who hated man that he might know him, I do not know
whether he clearly explored all his internals, death itself changing what had
lived, and death not simple, but itself wandering among the artifices of
cutting. The philosophers have reported for certain that gnats and ants and
moths lack lungs and arteries. Tell me, most curious inspector, do they have
eyes for seeing? Nay they even go where they will, and avoid and seek what
they know by seeing; designate the eyes, denote the pupils. But moths also eat
out: demonstrate the jaws, produce the grinders. But gnats also sound, not
even in the darkness of the ears blind: show together the trumpet and the
lance of that mouth. Any animal even of one point must be nourished by
something: exhibit the members of transmitting, cooking, and defecating food.
What then shall we say? If it is lived through these, these will be in all
certainly that will live, even if they are not seen, even if they are not
grasped according to smallness. You would believe this more, if you recall God
so great an artificer in modest things as also in greatest. But if you do not
think such minute little bodies take the talent of God, even thus you may
acknowledge his magnificence, that he has instructed modest animals to live
nevertheless without necessary members, with even sight saved without eyes,
and eating without little teeth, and digestion without bowels, just as also
certain ones walk without feet by a flowing impulse, as snakes, and by a
rising effort, as worms, and by a foaming crawl, as snails. So also why would
you not think they are breathed without the bellows of lungs and without the
pipes of arteries, so as to embrace as a great argument that therefore spirit

could have run without the soul, it would not have belonged to the soul to
breathe, but only to live. But indeed to live is to breathe, and to breathe is
to live. Therefore this whole both to breathe and to live belongs to that
whose also to live belongs, that is to the soul. In short if you separate
spirit and soul, separate also the works. Let both do something in the
discrete, the soul apart, the spirit apart; let the soul live without spirit,

sharpen or blunt. Doctrines, disciplines, arts and experiences, businesses,

as to fix their trinity in single properties; because a good tree does not
bear bad fruits nor a bad good, and no one gathers figs from thorns and grapes
from brambles. Therefore if it is so neither will God be able to raise sons of

sometime by nature sons of wrath, and in these you also were, but you have
been washed. But holy sentences will never discord. For a bad tree will not
give good fruits, if it is not grafted, and a good will give bad, if it is not
cultivated. And stones will become sons of Abraham, if they are formed into
the faith of Abraham. And offspring of vipers will make fruit of repentance,
if they have spat out the poisons of malignity. This will be the force of
divine grace, more potent certainly than nature, having in us lying under it
the free power of choice, which is called autexousion; which since it is
itself also natural and mutable, wherever it is turned, nature is converted.
But that the autexousion is in us naturally we have already shown both to
Marcion and to Hermogenes. What now, if the condition of nature will so have
to be defined, that it be determined double, of the born and the unborn, of
the made and the unmade? and so that whose nature has stood born and made will
take change; for it will be able both to be reborn and to be remade. But the
unborn and unmade will stand immobile. Which since it belongs to God alone, as
alone unborn and unmade and therefore immortal and inconvertible, it is
absolute that the nature of all the rest born and made is convertible and
changeable, so that, even if a trinity were to be ascribed to the soul, it
would be assigned from change of accident, not from institution of nature.

Let us first dispute of sleep, afterward how the soul runs death. Sleep is
certainly not extranatural, as it pleases certain philosophers, when they
assign it from those two causes which seem to be held besides nature. The
Stoics affirm sleep a resolution of sensual vigor, the Epicureans a diminution
of animal spirit, Anaxagoras with Xenophanes a weariness, Empedocles and
Parmenides a refrigeration, Strato a segregation of connate spirit, Democritus
a want of spirit, Aristotle a withering of the heat around the heart. I never
presume I have so slept as to acknowledge anything from these. For neither is
it to be believed that sleep is weariness, rather contrary to weariness, which
of course it takes away, since man is more refreshed by sleep than fatigued.
Further sleep is not always conceived from fatigue, and yet when it is from
that, that already is not. But neither shall I admit a growing-cold or some
withering of heat, since so far bodies grow warm with sleep, that the
dispensation of foods through sleep would not easily proceed with heat to be
hastened and rigor to be delayed, if we were refrigerated by sleep. It is more
that even sweat is an index of seething digestion. In short we are said to
concoct, which is an operation of heat, not of cold. Likewise a diminution of
animal spirit or a want of spirit or a segregation of connate spirit the
immortality of the soul does not allow to be believed. The soul perishes if it
is lessened. It remains, if it happens, that with the Stoics we determine
sleep a resolution of sensual vigor, because it procures rest of the body
alone, not also of the soul. For the soul as always mobile and always
exercised never succumbs to rest, alien of course from the state of
immortality; for nothing immortal admits an end of its work, but sleep is an
end of work. In short to the body to which mortality belongs, to it alone rest
flatters an end of work. He therefore who will doubt of the naturalness of
sleep, has indeed dialecticians leading into doubt the whole discretion of
naturals and extranaturals, so that he may know that even what he has thought
to be this side of nature can be claimed for nature, from which they have been
so allotted to be, that they seem to be held this side of it, and certainly
either all things nature or no nature. But with us that will be able to be
heard which the contemplation of God suggests, the author of all things of
which it is asked. For we believe, if anything is nature, that it is some
rational work of God. Further reason goes before sleep, so apt, so useful, so
necessary, that without it no soul would suffice, the recreator of bodies, the
reintegrator of forces, the prover of healths, the peacemaker of works, the
physician of labors, to enjoying which legitimately day yields, night makes
the law, taking away even the color of things. But if sleep is vital,
salutary, auxiliary, nothing of this kind is not rational, nothing not
natural. Thus even physicians relegate every contrary to the vital, salutary,
auxiliary outside the natural hinges. For even by judging the rival healths of
sleep, the phrenetic and the cardiac, besides nature they have prejudged sleep
natural; even in lethargy noting the not natural they answer by a natural
testimony, when it is in its own tempering. For every nature is rescinded
either by defraudation or by enormity, is conserved by the property of
measure. Thus that will be natural in state, which can be made not natural by
departure or excess. What, if you even exempt eating and drinking from the
lots of nature? for even in these there is a very great preparation of sleep.
Certainly with these from the beginning of his nature man has been imbued. If
you learn with God, that fountain of the race, Adam, drank sopor before he
thirsted rest, slept before he labored, nay before he even ate, nay before he
even spoke, so that they may see sleep a natural index more principal than all
naturals. From there we are led even to review it already then as an image of
death. For if Adam was giving a figure of Christ, the sleep of Adam was the
death of Christ about to sleep into death, so that from the injury equally of
his side the true mother of the living, the church, might be figured.
Therefore also sleep so salutary, so rational, is fashioned even into an
exemplar of public and already common death. For God has willed, having
otherwise wrought nothing without exemplars in his disposition, by a Platonic
paradigm more fully daily to drive with us the lines especially of the human
beginning and end, stretching a hand to faith to be more easily helped through
images and parables, as of speeches, so also of things. He therefore proposes
to you a body struck down by the friendly force of sopor, prostrated by the
bland necessity of rest, immobile in situation, such as it lay before life,
and such as it will lie after life, as a testation of the plastic and of
burial, expecting the soul as not yet conferred and as already snatched. But

you the resurrection of the dead. This will be both the natural reason of
sleep and the rational nature. Even through the image of death you are
initiated into the faith, you meditate hope, you learn to die and to live, you
learn to watch, while you sleep.

prove by things, when in exorcisms it sometimes affirms itself someone from
the parents of its man, sometimes a gladiator or a beast-fighter, as also
elsewhere a god, caring nothing more than this itself to shut out what we
preach, lest we easily believe that all souls are reduced to the inferi, so
that they may disturb the faith both of judgment and of resurrection. And yet
that daemon, after it has tried to circumvent the bystanders, conquered by the
instance of divine grace unwillingly confesses that which is in the true. Thus
even in that other species of magic, which is already believed to pluck
resting souls from the inferi and exhibit them to sight, no other force of
fallacy is more operative. Plainly, because even a phantasm is presented,
because even a body is affixed; nor is it great for him to circumscribe the
exterior eyes to whom to blind the interior sight of the mind is very easy.
Finally to Pharaoh and the Egyptians the dragons of the magical rods seemed
bodies. But the truth of Moses has swallowed the lie. Many things certainly
even against the apostles Simon and Elymas magicians; but the plague of
blindness was not from prestiges. What new is rivalry of truth from an unclean
spirit? Behold today so great a presumption of art extols the heretics of the
same Simon, that they even pledge themselves to move the souls of the prophets
from the inferi. And I believe because they can by a lie. For neither was it
then less allowed the pythonic spirit to fashion the soul of Samuel, Saul
consulting the dead after God. Far otherwise be it that we believe the soul of
any holy one, much more of a prophet, extracted by a daemon, having been
taught that Satan himself is transfigured into an angel of light, much more
into a man of light, even about to assert himself God at the end, and about to
edit more portentous signs to overturn, if it can be done, the elect. He has
doubted, if it happens, then to assert himself a prophet of God, and certainly
to Saul, in whom he himself was already remaining. Do not think it was another
who was administering the phantasm, another who was commending, but the same
spirit both in the false prophetess and in the apostate easily to lie what it
had made believed, through whom the treasury of Saul was there where also his
heart, where of course God was not. And therefore through whom he believed he
would see, he saw, because through whom he saw, he also believed. If even of
nocturnal images it is opposed that the dead have often been seen not in vain
(for even the Nasamones by remaining at the tombs of parents capture proper
oracles, as Heraclides writes or Nymphodorus or Herodotus, and the Celts for
the same cause pass the night at the tombs of brave men, as Nicander affirms),
we do not more truly suffer the dead in dreams than the living, but the dead
by the same reason as even the living and all things which are seen. For they
are not true because they are seen, but because they are fulfilled. The faith
of dreams is reported from effect, not from sight. But that to no soul at all
the inferi lie open the Lord has sufficiently sanctioned in that argument of
the resting poor man and the groaning rich man from the person of Abraham,
that a reporter of the infernal disposition cannot be relegated from there,
which even then could have been allowed, so that Moses and the prophets might
be believed. But even if the virtue of God has recalled some into bodies, into
documents of its right, not therefore will it be communicated to the faith and
audacity of magicians and the fallacy of dreams and the license of poets. Nay
in the examples of resurrection, when the virtue of God either through
prophets or through Christ or through apostles represents souls into bodies,
by a solid and handleable and satiated truth it has been prejudged that this
is the form of truth, so that you may judge every incorporeal exhibition of
the dead prestiges.

All souls therefore with the inferi, you say? Will you nill you, even
punishments already there and refreshments; you have the poor man and the rich
man. And because I have deferred I know not what to this part, now opportunely
in the close I shall render. For why would you not think the soul both
punished and fostered among the inferi meanwhile under expectation of either
judgment in a certain usurpation and candidate of it? Because its business
ought to be saved, you say, in divine judgment without any foretaste of
sentence; then because even the restitution of the flesh is to be waited as of
a consort of works and of wages. What then will be done in this time? shall we
sleep? But indeed souls do not even in the living sleep; for sleep is of
bodies, whose even death itself with its mirror sleep. Or do you want nothing
to be done there, whither universal humanity is drawn, whither all hope is
sequestered? Do you think judgment is tasted, or begun? precipitated, or
preministered? Now indeed how most iniquitous even among the inferi, if both
for the guilty it is still well there, and for the innocent not yet? What, do
you want there to be more after death a confused hope and playing with
uncertain expectation, or a review of life already and an ordination of
judgment bristling? But does the soul always expect the body, that it may
sorrow or rejoice? Does it not even of its own suffice itself to either title
of passion? How often with the body uninjured is the soul alone tortured by
bile, wrath, weariness, often not even known to itself? How often likewise
with the body afflicted does the soul seek for itself a furtive joy, and
secede from the then importune society of the body? I lie if it has not been
accustomed even of the very tortures of the body both to boast and to rejoice
alone. Look back to the soul of Mucius, when he loosed his right hand to the
fires; look back to that of Zeno, when the torments of Dionysius pass it by.
The bites of beasts are ornaments of youth, as in Cyrus the scars of a bear.
So far the soul knows even among the inferi both to rejoice and to sorrow
without flesh, because even in flesh and uninjured if it will it sorrows, and
injured if it will it rejoices. If this from its own choice in life, how much
more from the judgment of God after death? But neither does the soul share all
works with the ministry of the flesh; for even sole thoughts and naked wills
divine censure pursues. He who has looked to concupisce has already committed
adultery in the heart. Therefore even on account of this it is most congruent
that the soul, though the flesh not expected, be punished for what it has
committed not associated with the flesh. Thus also on account of pious and
benevolent thoughts, in which it did not need the flesh, it will be recreated
without the flesh. What now, if even in carnal things she is prior who
conceives, who disposes, who commands, who impels? And if sometimes unwilling,
yet she first treats what she is about to act through the body. Never finally
will conscience be later than the deed. Thus it also competes to this order
that she first weigh the wages to whom they are first owed. In sum, since we
understand that prison, which the gospel demonstrates, the inferi, and
interpret the last quadrant even a modest transgression to be paid there by
the delay of resurrection, no one will doubt that the soul weighs something
with the inferi, the fullness of resurrection through the flesh also saved.
This even the paraclete has most frequently commended, if anyone shall have
admitted his speeches from acknowledgment of the promised charismata. To
every, as I think, human opinion upon the soul from the doctrine of faith
having engaged we have satisfied a just at least and necessary curiosity. But
to the enormous and idle there will be lacking only to learn as much as it
shall have pleased to inquire.
