36. The man of the Spirit knew the things of the Spirit exactly; and for that
reason he thought he ought to take no low view, nor to fight by factions and
prejudices, assigning more to favor than to God, but to look to one thing
only: the benefit of the Churches, and the common salvation. Therefore he
wrote, he admonished, he knit together the people and the priests, both the
rest and those who belonged to the sanctuary; he bore witness, he voted, he
ordained even while not yet present; he granted his gray hair the right to
exercise authority among strangers as among his own. And at last, since the
ordination had to be made canonical for him, and one was lacking from the
number of those who make the proclamation, he tore himself from the couch,
crushed by old age and disease, and made for the city with a young man's rush;
or rather he was carried, his body dead and barely breathing, persuaded that,
even if he had to suffer something, that zeal would be a fair winding-sheet
for him. And here too a sign takes place that is not disbelieved: he is
strengthened by the toil, he grows young with eagerness, he manages the
affair, he takes his place in the array, he sets the man upon the throne, he
is escorted home, no longer using the litter as a coffin, but as a divine ark.
And the long-suffering whose praise I just now left off, this he shows here
even more. For when his fellow ministers could not bear the shame of defeat,
and the old man's power in the affairs, and for that reason were vexed at him
and abusive, he was able by what he endured to rise even above these, finding
gentleness his greatest ally, and not returning abuse when abused. For what
would be terrible, if having won in the facts he should bear being beaten by
the tongue? Therefore he so won those men too by long-suffering, taking time
also as an ally of his judgment, that they apologized, they fell at his feet,
turning their indignation into wonder. They were ashamed of what had gone
before; they used him as patriarch, and lawgiver, and judge, having thrown the
hatred away.
37. And of the same zeal was also the stand against the heretics, =35.1036=
when they made expedition against us with the imperial impiety, hoping to
overthrow us too and add us to the others, who had already been enslaved in
almost every case. For neither here did he contribute some small part for us,
both from himself and perhaps from us; whom he led against those men, as
against most bitter beasts, not ignoble hounds, training them toward piety.
One thing I blame in both of you (and do not be vexed at my frankness; for I
will burst out with what grieves me, even if it is rather burdensome), that
when I was disgusted at the evils of life, and in love with solitude, as I do
not know if anyone else of those in our time has been, and striving as fast as
I could to turn aside from this common surge and dust, and to save myself into
safety, somehow you, bringing me, handed me over to this burdensome and
plotting marketplace of souls, through the fair name of the priesthood. From
which already some evils have happened to me, and others are expected. For
already to have suffered also makes one somehow despondent toward the future,
even if reason persuades something else, suggesting the better.
38. Nor would I pass by this among his good things. In all things he was
enduring, and stronger than the flesh that clothed him; but suffering in his
last illness, which attacked together with old age, and this a chronic and
precarious one, illness he had in common with other men; but this was no
longer common, but very much his own, and in keeping with his other marvels.
For there was not a time when he was not constrained by the suffering, and
many times in the day, and sometimes even in the hour, he was strengthened by
the liturgy alone; and the suffering withdrew, as if banished by a command.
And having lived almost a hundred years, beyond the Davidic bounds concerning
our span, and of these themselves forty-five in the priesthood, a measure of
human life, then in a good old age he closed his life. And this, how? In the
words and postures of prayer, leaving behind no trace of vice, but very many
memorials of virtue. Therefore the reverence for him also is more than falls
to man, on the tongues and in the minds of all; and it is not easy to find
anyone who, remembering him, does not also greet the image, as the Scripture
has it, having set a hand upon the mouth. =35.1037= Such then was his life,
and such the completion and perfection of that life.
39. And since a memorial of that man's greatness of soul also had to be left
behind for life, what was more needed than this temple, which he raised both
for God and for us, using a little from the people in addition, but
contributing the more from his own house? A work not worthy of silence, in
size beyond most, and in beauty almost beyond all; joining itself in eight
equal sides, and raised into height by the beauties of two-storied columns and
porticoes, and by the figures upon them not falling short of nature; and
flashing down from above with a heaven, and lighting the eyes round about with
rich springs of light, as truly a dwelling-place of light; and being circled
by ambulatories set on both sides, of the most splendid material, equiangular,
and enclosing a large space in the midst; and shining forth with the graces of
gates and vestibules, and from afar welcoming those who approach. And I do not
yet speak of the outer adornment, the beauty and size of squared stone, fitted
even to a hair, both as much as is marble in bases and capitals, which have
taken the angles, and as much as is of the country, falling short in nothing
of the foreign; and belts of many kinds and varied, both projecting and
inwoven, from foundation to peak, which injures the spectator, limiting the
view. How would the discourse present in so brief a space a work of so much
time, and toil, and of so great a hand? Or will it suffice to say this much,
that of many works adorning the other cities, both private and public, this
even alone was able to make us celebrated among the greater number. But so
much for the temple. And since there was need also of a priest, this too he
gives from his own house; whether equal to the temple, I cannot say, except
that he gives him. And since there was need also of sacrifices, these too he
adds, the sufferings of the son, and his endurance in the trials; that God
might have from him a reasonable whole burnt offering, a spiritual victim well
consumed in place of a legal sacrifice.
40. What do you say, O father? Are these things sufficient, and do you receive
the returns of the toils which you labored upon my words, =35.1040= this
praise, whether a send-off or a funeral oration? And do you give the discourse
peace, in the old manner, and here too set this limit, fleeing satiety, that
it may be well measured? Or what addition do you seek? You set the limit, I
know; for it is sufficient. But grant me to add this: Make known to us where
in the world you are in glory, and the light about you, and receive your
yokefellow after a little, and the children whom you buried before; and
receive me too in the same tents, having suffered either nothing more, or
little in this life; and before the tents, in this sweet stone, which you set
up for both, honoring even here your holy namesake; and have pardon for the
discourse, both what was passed over, and what was taken on for your sake. And
may you lead through without danger, especially every flock and all high
priests, of whom you were called father, and particularly him who was forced
by you, and overpowered by a coercion both paternal and spiritual, that I may
not blame you in all things for the tyranny.
41. And what do we seem to you, O judge of my words and motions? If these
things have been spoken sufficiently, and worthily of your desire, vote this,
and we accept it; and your vote is altogether God's. But if far short of that
man's glory, and of your hope, the ally is near. Let fall your voice, awaited
by his good things, as a seasonable rain; and altogether he has you as a
debtor of the greatest things, both as a shepherd a shepherd, and as a father
the son in grace. What wonder, if having thundered through you to the
inhabited world, he himself should also enjoy something of your voice? What is
needed still? To join in the funeral reflections with the spiritual Sarah, the
yokefellow and coeval of our great father Abraham.
42. The nature of God and of men is not the same, O mother; or rather, wholly,
of divine things and of earthly. With those there is the unchangeable and
immortal, both of being itself, and of as many things as belong to being; for
what is firm belongs to what is firm. But how is it with what is ours?
=35.1041= It both flows and is corrupted, and at one time and another takes
another change. Life then and death, these which are so called, seeming to
differ from one another most, in a way pass into one another and take one
another's place. For the one, beginning from corruption which is our mother,
and traveling through corruption which is the constant departure from what is
present, ends in corruption which is the dissolution of this life; and the
other, having a release from the evils here, and often transferring to the
life above, I do not know if it would be properly called death, having the
fearful more in the name than in the fact; and we are in danger of suffering
in a way an irrational passion, fearing the things that are not fearful, and
treating as preferable the things that it is right to fear. One life: to look
toward the life. One death: sin; for it is destruction of a soul. And the
other things, of which some are proud, are a vision of dreams playing against
the things that are, and deceptive phantoms of a soul. If we are thus, O
mother, we shall neither be proud of life, nor be too much pained at death.
What terrible thing then have we suffered, if from here we have passed over to
the true life? if, having been released from turnings, and vertigos, and
satieties, and the shameful tribute, we shall be with the things that stand
and do not flow, small lights, dancing round the great light?
43. But does the sundering grieve you? But let hope gladden you. But is
widowhood terrible? Yet to him it is not terrible. And where is the good of
love, giving the easier things to itself, assigning the more difficult to the
neighbor? And what is terrible at all to her who will be loosed after a
little? The appointed term is near, the painful is not lasting. Let us not
load the lightest things with ignoble reasonings. We have been deprived of
great things, for we also obtained them; deprivation is of all, obtaining is
not of many. Let not this bend us, but let that console. For it is more
reasonable that the better should conquer. You bore the loss of children,
still in their prime, and fit for living, most manfully and most
philosophically; bear also the laying down of an aged body already weary of
life, even if the tension of the soul preserved for him the senses most free
of suffering. But do you need the guardian? And where is this Isaac of yours,
