* CLEODAMUS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of spring, O Myrson, or of winter or of autumn
or of summer, what is sweet to you? And what more do you pray to come?
Is it summer, when all is completed that we toil at?
Or sweet autumn, when hunger is light for men?
Or even winter hard to work? Since even in winter many
being warmed are charmed by idleness and by sloth;
or has fair spring pleased you more? Tell, what your mind
chooses? For leisure has granted us to talk.

* MYRSON
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

It is not fitting for mortals to judge the works of gods;
for all these are holy and sweet; but for your sake
I will declare, Cleodamus, what was sweeter to me than the others.
I do not wish it to be summer, since then the sun roasts me.
I do not wish autumn, since the season begets disease.
To bear cruel winter: I fear snow and frost.
May thrice-longed-for spring be with me through the whole year,
when neither cold nor sun weighs us down.
In spring all conceives, all things of spring sprout sweet,
and night for men is equal and like the dawn [lost]

A fowler still a boy in a wooded grove
hunting birds saw the unapproachable Eros
sitting upon a branch of boxwood; and when he perceived him,
rejoicing because the bird appeared great to him,
joining all his rods together upon one another
he watched for Eros leaping this way and that.
And the boy, distressed when no end met him,
throwing down the rods came to an old ploughman,
who taught him this craft, and he told him,
and showed him Eros sitting. But the old man
smiling moved his head and answered the boy:
/spare the hunt, and do not go after this bird./
/Flee far. The beast is evil. You will be blessed,/
/so long as you do not catch him; but if you come to the measure of a man,/
/this one who now flees and leaps away of himself from himself/
/coming suddenly will sit upon your head./

Great Cypris still stood by me as I slept,
leading infant Eros from her fair hand
nodding toward the ground, and she spoke this tale to me:
/taking Eros, dear cowherd, teach him to sing for me./
So she spoke; and she went away, and I, as much as I sang pastoral,
foolish, taught Eros as one wishing to learn,
how Pan found the cross-flute, how Athena the pipe,
how Hermes the tortoise-lyre, how sweet Apollo the cithara.

These things I taught him thoroughly; but he paid no heed to the tales,
but himself sang me love-songs, and taught me
the desires of mortals and immortals and the works of his mother.
And I forgot as much as I taught Eros,
and as many love-songs as Eros taught me, I was taught them all.

The Muses do not fear wild Eros
but they love him from the heart and follow him at the heel.
And if then someone having an unloving soul sings,
they slip away from him and do not wish to teach;
but if someone whose mind is shaken by Eros sings sweetly,
to him they all flow forward, greatly hastening.
I am witness that this tale has become true for all.
For if I sing another mortal or some one of the immortals,
my tongue stammers and no longer sings as before;
but if again I sing something toward Eros and toward Lycidas,
then also my voice, rejoicing, flows through my mouth.

[lost]  I do not know, nor is it fitting to toil at what we have not learned.
If my little songs are fair, these too alone
will set glory for me, which Fate gave me before;
but if these are not sweet, why should I toil toward more?
For if for life a double time had given us
either the son of Cronus or many-turning Fate, so as to accomplish

the one toward merriment and joys, and the other upon toil,
it would perhaps be to receive good things later after toiling.
But if the gods have nodded that one time come into life
for men, and this short and lesser than all,
to how much, O wretched ones, do we toil toward labors and toward works,
and until when do we throw our soul toward gains and toward crafts,
always desiring much more wealth?
Have we all then forgotten that we were born mortal,
and that we received a short time from Fate? [lost]

Blessed are those who love, when they are equally loved in return.
Blessed was Theseus when Peirithous was present,
even if he went down into unrelenting Hades.
Blessed was Orestes among the harsh Inhospitable ones,
because Pylades had chosen common paths with him.
Blessed was the Aeacid Achilles while his companion lived;
blessed was he dying, because he warded off dread fate for him.

Hesperus, golden light of lovely Foam-born,
Hesperus, dear holy ornament of dark-blue night,
so much dimmer than the moon, as much as you are outstanding among stars,
hail, friend, and to me leading a revel toward a shepherd
give you light instead of the moon, because that one

today beginning set more quickly. I do not come for theft,
nor that I may trouble those traveling by night;
but I love; and it is fair also to help one who has fallen in love.

Gentle Cyprogeneia, child of Zeus or of the sea,
why so much do you trouble mortals and immortals?
I have spoken a little; why now were you so hated even to yourself,
as to bear Eros so great an evil to all,
wild, without affection, in mind nothing like his form?
And for what did you grant him to be winged and far-shooting,
so that we might not be able to escape him, being bitter?
