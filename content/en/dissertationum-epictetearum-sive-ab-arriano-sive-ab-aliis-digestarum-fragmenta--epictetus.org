* 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

"What do I care," he says, "whether existing things are composed of atoms, or
of indivisibles, or of fire and earth? Is it not enough to learn the nature of
good and evil, and the measures of desires and aversions, and of impulses and
refusals as well, and using these as rules to manage the affairs of life, and
to let go the matters that are beyond us, which perhaps the human mind cannot
grasp, and even if one should grant that they are perfectly graspable, still
what is the use of grasping them? And must we not say that those who assign
these things as necessary to the philosopher's discourse are taking trouble in
vain?" "Is the precept at Delphi, then, Know thyself, also superfluous?"
"This, at any rate, is not," he says. "What, then, is its force? If someone
commanded a dancer in a chorus to know himself, would he not attend to the
command by turning to his fellow dancers and to the harmony with them?" "He
says so." "And if a sailor? And if a soldier? Does a human being, then, seem
to you to have been made as an animal on his own, or for community?" "For
community." "By whom?" "By nature." "What nature is, and how it administers
the whole, and whether it exists or not: is it no longer necessary to busy
ourselves with these things?"

Of the same.

Everything obeys the cosmos and serves it: earth and sea and sun and the other
stars, and the plants and animals of the earth. Our body too obeys it, sick or
healthy, whenever the cosmos wills, young or aging and passing through the
other changes. So it is reasonable that what is in our power, that is,
judgment, should not be the only thing that strains against it. For it is
strong and superior, and has deliberated better on our behalf, administering
us together with the whole. And besides, resistance is irrational, and it
accomplishes nothing except straining in vain and falling into pain and grief.

* 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Of Rufus, from the discourses of Epictetus on friendship.

Of the things that exist, God has set some in our power and some not in our
power. In our power is the fairest and most serious thing, that by which he
himself is happy: the use of impressions. For when this is done rightly it is
freedom, a smooth current, good cheer, and steadiness, and this also is
justice and law and temperance and all virtue. All the rest he made not in our
power. Therefore we too must agree with God, and dividing affairs in this way,
pursue in every way what is in our power, and leave what is not in our power
to the cosmos, and if it should need the children or the country or the body
or anything at all, yield them gladly.

And who of us does not admire that deed of Lycurgus the Lacedaemonian? For
when he had been maimed of one eye by one of the citizens, and had received
the young man from the people so that he might punish him however he himself
wished, he held off from this, and having educated him and shown him as a good
man, he led him into the theater. And when the Lacedaemonians were wondering,
/this man, then,/ he said, /having taken from you as insolent and violent, I
give back to you as reasonable and a man of the people./

Of Rufus, from the discourses of Epictetus on friendship.

That such is the nature of the cosmos, and was and is and will be, and what
happens cannot happen otherwise than as it now does; and that of this turning
and change not only humans have a share, and the other animals upon the
earth, but also the divine things, and by Zeus the four elements themselves
turn up and down and change, and earth becomes water and water air, and this
again changes into aether; and the same manner of change is from above
downward. If someone tries to incline his mind toward these things and to
persuade himself to receive what is necessary willingly, he will live his
life very moderately and in tune.

From the hortatory discourses of Arrian.

But indeed Socrates, when Archelaus was sending for him as one who would make
him rich, bade them report to him that /at Athens four choenixes of barley
meal are for sale for an obol, and springs of water flow./ For if what I have
is not enough for me, still I am enough for these, and so those too are enough
for me. Or do you not see that Polus did not play the tyrant Oedipus more
melodiously or more pleasantly than the wanderer and beggar at Colonus? Then
will the noble man appear worse than Polus, failing to play well every role
the divine has placed upon him? And will he not imitate Odysseus, who even in
rags was no less distinguished than in the thick purple cloak?

* 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

Of Arrian.

There are some high-spirited people who act mildly, quietly, and as it were
without anger, doing all the same things that those also do who are strongly
carried by passion. One must therefore guard against the fault of these as
well, as being much worse than straining in anger. For the latter quickly have
their fill of the punishment, but the former stretch it out for a long time,
like those who have a slight fever.

From the memorabilia of Epictetus.

"But I see," someone says, "the good and noble perishing by hunger and cold."
"And do you not see those who are not good and not noble perishing by luxury
and swagger and vulgarity?" "But it is shameful to be fed by another." "And
who, you wretch, is fed from himself, other than the cosmos? At any rate,
whoever accuses providence because the wicked do not pay the penalty, because
they are strong and rich, does something like saying, of those who have lost
their eyes, that they have not paid the penalty because their nails are sound.
For I say that they differ much more, virtue † from vice, than eyes differ
from nails."

From the memorabilia of Epictetus.

In Rome the women have Plato's Republic in their hands, because he holds that
women should be common. For they attend to the words, not to the man's
meaning: that he does not, after bidding one man marry and live with one
woman, then wish the women to be common, but removing such a marriage and
introducing another form of marriage. And on the whole people delight in
furnishing excuses for their own faults; since philosophy, at any rate, says
that it is not even fitting to stretch out the finger at random.

Of the same.

Charming, he said, are those who pride themselves on things not in our power.
/I,/ he says, /am better than you; for I have many fields, and you are wasted
with hunger./ Another says /I am a consular./ Another /I am a procurator;/
another /I have curly hair./ But a horse does not say to a horse that /I am
better than you; for I have acquired much fodder and much barley, and I have
golden bits and embroidered saddle-cloths,/ but that /I am swifter than you./
And every animal is better and worse by its own virtue and vice. Has a human
being alone, then, no virtue, so that we must look to hairs and clothes and
grandfathers?

Of Epictetus.

Therefore it is just to praise Agrippinus, because though a man of the
greatest worth he never praised himself, but if someone else also praised him,
he would blush. This man, he said, was such that he would always write a
praise of the difficulty that happened to him: if he had a fever, of fever; if
he were in ill repute, of ill repute; if he were in exile, of exile. And once,
he said, when he was about to lunch, the messenger saying that Nero orders him
to go into exile stood by, and he said /well then,/ he said, /we shall lunch
at Aricia./

:PROPERTIES:

Agrippinus, when governing, used to try to persuade those being condemned by
him that it was fitting for them to be condemned. For I do not, he said, give
my verdict as their enemy, nor as a robber, but as a caretaker and guardian,
just as the physician also consoles the one being cut and persuades him to
offer himself.

* 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

Of Epictetus.

Nature is wonderful and, as Xenophon says, fond of living things. At any rate
we love and tend the body, the most unpleasant and filthiest of all things;
for if we had to tend the neighbor's body for five days only, we would not
endure it. For see what it is, having risen at dawn, to rub another's teeth
and, having done one of the necessary things, to wash those parts. It is truly
wonderful to love a thing for which we perform so many services each day. I
stuff this sack; then I empty it; what is heavier than this? But I must serve
God. Therefore I remain and endure washing this wretched little body, stuffing
it, covering it; and when I was younger, it used to order me something else as
well, and nevertheless I endured it. Why then do you not endure, when nature
that gave us the body takes it away? "I love it," he says. "Is it not, then,
as I was just saying, that nature has given you even the loving of it itself?
And the same nature says /let it go already and have no more trouble./"

Of the same.

If a young man ends his life †, he accuses the gods †, because, when he ought
already to have been at rest, he has trouble, and no less, when death
approaches, he wants to live and sends to the physician and begs him to leave
nothing of eagerness and care. Wonderful, he said, are humans, wanting neither
to live nor to die.
