* Chapter 1

Amphion the Theban and Arion of Methymna were both skilled in song, but both
are myth; and this song is still sung by a chorus of Greeks, by the art of
music the one luring a fish, the other walling Thebes. And another Thracian
sophist (this too is a Greek myth) tamed the wild beasts by bare song and even
transplanted the trees, the oaks, by his music. I could tell you yet another
myth brother to these, and a singer, Eunomus the Locrian and the Pythian
cicada. A Greek festival was being assembled at Pytho over a dead serpent,
Eunomus singing a funeral song for a reptile; hymn or dirge of a snake the
song was, I cannot say. And there was a contest and Eunomus was playing the
cithara in the season of heat, when the cicadas under the leaves were singing
over the mountains, warmed by the sun. They were singing, then, not to the
dead serpent, the Pythian, but to the all-wise God an autonomous song, better
than the nomoi of Eunomus. A string snaps for the Locrian; the cicada flies
onto the yoke; it trilled as upon a branch on the instrument; and the singer,
fitting himself to the cicada's song, filled in the missing string. So it is
not by the song of Eunomus that the cicada is led, as the myth would have it,
having set up at Pytho a bronze Eunomus with the cithara itself and the
Locrian's fellow contestant; but it flies willingly and sings willingly. And
to the Greeks it seemed to have become an actor of music.

How is it, then, that you have trusted empty myths, supposing animals to be
charmed by music? While the bright face of truth, it seems, appears to you to
be only a fiction, and has fallen under the eyes of unbelief. Cithaeron, then,
and Helicon and the mountains of the Odrysians and of the Thracians, precincts
of error, have been deified and celebrated in song because of the mysteries.
For my part, even if they are myth, I can hardly bear so many disasters staged
as tragedy; but for you even the records of evils have become dramas, and the
actors of the dramas spectacles of delight. Come then, let us shut up the
dramas and the Lenaean poets, already quite drunk, crowning them somewhere
with ivy, raving beyond measure in a Bacchic rite, together with the satyrs
and the mad thiasos, and with the rest of the chorus of daemons, on Helicon
and Cithaeron now grown old; and let us bring down from above, from heaven,
truth together with most radiant wisdom onto the holy mountain of God and the
holy prophetic chorus. And let her, flashing light as far-shining as can be,
illuminate on every side those who roll in darkness, and set men free from
error, stretching out the highest right hand, understanding, toward salvation.
And let those who have lifted their heads and looked up leave Helicon and
Cithaeron, and dwell on Zion: /for from Zion shall go forth a law, and the
word of the Lord from Jerusalem,/ a heavenly Word, the genuine contestant
crowned in the theatre of the whole world. And my Eunomus sings not the nomos
of Terpander, nor that of Capion, nor yet the Phrygian or Lydian or Dorian,
but the eternal nomos of the new harmony, the one that bears the name of God,
the new song, the Levitical, /that charms pain and wrath, and brings
forgetfulness of all ills./ A sweet and true drug of persuasion is mixed into
the song.

To me, then, that Thracian Orpheus and the Theban and the man of Methymna seem
to have been men who were no men, deceivers, who, under a pretext of music,
ruined life, raging with a kind of artful sorcery toward corruptions,
celebrating insolence in rites, deifying griefs, the first to lead men by the
hand toward the idols, and indeed with stones and woods, that is, with statues
and paintings, they built up the clumsiness of custom, yoking that truly
beautiful freedom of those who had lived under heaven into the last slavery by
songs and charms.

But my singer is not of that kind, nor has he come to dissolve at long last
the bitter slavery of the daemons who play the tyrant, but, transferring us to
the gentle and humane yoke of piety, he calls back again to the heavens those
who have been thrown down to earth. He alone, at any rate, of all who have
ever been, tamed the most troublesome beasts, men: birds, the light-minded
among them; reptiles, the deceivers; lions, the passionate; swine, the
pleasure-loving; wolves, the rapacious. And stones and woods are the foolish;
and besides, a man baptized in ignorance is more insensible than stones. Let a
prophetic voice come forward as our witness, in concert with truth, pitying
those worn out in ignorance and mindlessness: /for God is able from these
stones to raise up children to Abraham./ He, having pitied the great ignorance
and the hardness of heart of those who had been turned to stone toward the
truth, raised up a seed of piety that perceived virtue from those stones, the
nations that had believed in stones. Again, then, certain venomous and shifty
hypocrites who waylay righteousness he has somewhere called /offspring of
vipers/; but if any of these snakes should repent willingly, following the
Word he becomes a /man of God/. And other /wolves/ he allegorizes, clothed in
sheepskins, hinting at the rapacious in human shapes. And all these wildest
beasts, then, and such stones, the heavenly song itself has transformed into
tame human beings. /For we too were once foolish, disobedient, going astray,
serving pleasures and various desires, living in malice and envy, hateful,
hating one another,/ as the apostolic writing says; /but when the kindness and
love of mankind of God our Saviour appeared, not from works of righteousness
which we had done, but according to his mercy he saved us./

See how great a power the new song has had: it has made men out of stones and
men out of beasts. And those who were otherwise dead, who had no share in the
life that truly is, when they became hearers of the song only, came to life
again. This, you see, also ordered the universe tunefully and stretched the
discord of the elements into an arrangement of concord, so that the whole
world might become a harmony for him; and he let the sea go free, yet forbade
it to mount upon the earth, and conversely he made the earth firm as it was
being carried, and fixed it as a boundary of the sea; and indeed he also
softened the rush of fire with air, as if blending a Dorian harmony with a
Lydian; and he tamed the harsh cold of air by the interweaving of fire, mixing
these lowest notes of the whole tunefully. And the unstained song, support of
the whole and harmony of all things, stretched from the middles to the
extremes and from the extremities to the middles, fitted this universe, not
according to the Thracian music, the one akin to Jubal, but according to the
ancestral will of God, which David emulated. And he who is from David and
before him, the Word of God, disdaining lyre and cithara, the lifeless
instruments, and fitting this world and indeed the little world, man, his soul
and his body, with the Holy Spirit, sings to God through the many-voiced
instrument and sings along with the instrument, man. /For you are a cithara
and a flute and a temple to me/: a cithara because of the harmony, a flute
because of the spirit, a temple because of the Word, so that the one may
sound, the other breathe, and the last may contain the Lord. And indeed David
the king, the citharist, of whom we made mention a little earlier, was
exhorting toward the truth and turning away from idols; far was he from
hymning the daemons, who were driven by his true music, by which, when Saul
was being possessed, he, singing only, healed him. A beautiful breathing
instrument the Lord wrought, man, according to his own image; and he himself,
to be sure, is an all-harmonic instrument of God, tuneful and holy, wisdom
beyond the world, heavenly Word.

What, then, does the instrument, the Word of God, the Lord, and the new song,
want? To open the eyes of the blind and to open the ears of the deaf and to
lead by the hand those who limp in both feet or wander into righteousness, to
show God to mindless men, to stop corruption, to conquer death, to reconcile
disobedient sons to a father. Humane is the instrument of God: the Lord
pities, trains, exhorts, admonishes, saves, guards, and as a surplus wage of
our learning he promises us the kingdom of the heavens, enjoying from us this
only, that we are saved. For vice feeds on the destruction of men, but truth,
like the bee, harming none of the things that are, exults only in the
salvation of men. You have, then, the promise; you have the love of mankind:
take your share of the grace.

And do not take my saving song to be new in the way a vessel or a house is;
for it was /before the morning star/, and /in the beginning was the Word, and
the Word was with God, and the Word was God/; but error is old, and truth
appears new. Whether, then, mythical goats teach that the Phrygians are
ancient, or again the poets who write the Arcadians as older than the moon, or
again those who dream that the Egyptians were the first to disclose this
earth, gods and men; yet not one of these was before this world, but before
the foundation of the world we were, we who, because we had to be in it, had
been generated earlier for God, we the rational formations of the Word of God,
through whom we are ancient, because /in the beginning the Word was/. But that
the Word was from above, a divine beginning of all things, he was and is; and
that now he has received the name long since consecrated, worthy of power,
Christ, he has been called my new song.

The Word, then, Christ, is the cause both of our being long ago (for he was in
God) and of our being well; and now this same Word has appeared to men, he who
alone is both, God and man, cause of all good things for us; from whom, being
taught to live well, we are sent on to eternal life. For according to that
inspired apostle of the Lord, /the saving grace of God has appeared to all
men, training us, that denying ungodliness and worldly desires we may live
soberly and justly and piously in the present age, awaiting the blessed hope
and appearing of the glory of the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ./
This is the new song, the appearing that has now shone forth in us of the Word
who was in the beginning and who was before being; and the Saviour who was
before being has appeared just now, he who is in that which is has appeared,
because /the WordWord ὃς MSS. was with God,/ a teacher; the Word by whom all
things have been created has appeared, and having granted life in the
beginning after the forming, as Creator, he taught living well when he
appeared as teacher, that later, as God, he might furnish living always.

And he did not first pity us for our error now, but from above, from the
beginning, and now, when we were already being destroyed, appearing, he has
saved us round about. For the wicked and creeping beast, enchanting, still
enslaves and maltreats men even now, punishing, as it seems to me, in a
barbarian way, they who are said to bind captives to dead bodies, until they
rot with them. This wicked tyrant and dragon, then, those whom he can
appropriate from birth, fastening them to stones and woods and statues and
such idols with the wretched bond of superstition, bringing them, as the
saying is, living, has buried them with himself, until they are corrupted
together. For which reason (for one is the deceiver, from above bringing Eve,
and now already the other men as well, down toward death) one also is himself
our ally and helper, the Lord, signalling beforehand from the beginning
prophetically, and now already also clearly calling to salvation.

Let us flee, then, obeying the apostolic command, /the ruler of the authority
of the air, the spirit now at work in the sons of disobedience,/ and let us
run to the Saviour, the Lord, who both now and always has been exhorting to
salvation, through wonders and signs in Egypt, and in the wilderness through
the bush and through the cloud that followed the Hebrews by grace of love of
mankind, like a maidservant. With fear of these things he exhorted the
hard-hearted; and already also through Moses the all-wise and through Isaiah
who loves the truth and through the whole prophetic chorus he turns those who
have ears toward the Word more rationally; and in one place he reviles, and in
another he also threatens; and some of men he also laments; and for others he
sings, just as a good physician of sick bodies plasters some, smooths others,
pours on others, and divides some even with iron, burning others, and in some
cases even sawing off, if somehow it is possible, even at the cost of a part
or a limb, to make the man healthy. Many-voiced, then, is the Saviour, and of
many ways toward the salvation of men: threatening he admonishes, reviling he
turns, lamenting he pities, psalming he invites, through a bush he speaks
(those men needed signs and wonders) and with fire he frightens men, kindling
the flame from a pillar, a proof at once of grace and of fear: if you obey,
the light; if you refuse to hear, the fire. But since flesh is more honourable
than both pillar and bush, prophets speak after those things, the Lord himself
speaking in Isaiah, himself in Elijah, himself in the mouth of prophets; but
you, if you do not believe the prophets, and take both the men and the fire to
be myth, the Lord himself will speak to you, /who, being in the form of God,
did not count it robbery to be equal with God; but he emptied himself,/ the
pity-loving God, longing to save man; and already the Word himself speaks to
you clearly, putting unbelief to shame, yes I say, the Word of God become man,
so that you too may learn from a man, how at last a man may become God.

Then is it not strange, friends, that God is always exhorting us toward
virtue, while we shrink from the benefit and put off salvation? For does not
John also invite toward salvation, and become altogether an exhorting voice?
Let us inquire of him, then: /who and from where among men are you?/ He will
not say Elijah, and he will deny that he is Christ; but he will confess a
voice crying in the wilderness. Who, then, is John? To take it in a figure,
let it be permitted to say, an exhorting voice of the Word crying in the
wilderness. What do you cry, O voice? /Tell us too./ /Make straight the ways
of the Lord./ John is a forerunner, and the voice a forerunner of the Word, a
comforting voice, preparing beforehand toward salvation, a voice exhorting
toward an inheritance of the heavens; through which the barren and the
wilderness is no longer fruitless.

An angel's voice foretold this pregnancy to me; that too was a forerunner of
the Lord, bringing good news to a barren woman, as John to the wilderness.
Through this voice of the Word, then, the barren is rich in children and the
wilderness bears fruit. The two forerunner voices of the Lord, of an angel and
of John, hint to me at the salvation laid up, that when this Word has appeared
we may carry off the fruit of rich childbearing, eternal life. Bringing both
voices, then, to the same point, the writing makes the whole clear: /let her
who does not bear hear; let her who is not in labour break forth in voice,
because the children of the desolate are more than of her who has the
husband./ An angel was bringing us good news; John was exhorting us to
understand the farmer, to seek the husband. For one and the same is this man,
the husband of the barren, the farmer of the wilderness, who filled both the
barren and the wilderness with divine power. For since the children of the
well-born were many, but the Hebrew woman, many-childed from of old, was
childless through disobedience, the barren takes the husband and the
wilderness the farmer; then the one of fruits, the other of believers, and
both mothers through the Word; but for the unbelieving even now both barren
and wilderness remain. John, the herald of the Word, was in this way inviting
them to become ready for the presence of God, of Christ, and this was what the
silence of Zacharias hinted, awaiting the forerunner fruit of Christ, so that
the light of the truth, the Word, might release the mystic silence of the
prophetic riddles, having become gospel; but if you long to see God truly,
take your share of purifications befitting God, not of laurel leaves and
certain fillets variegated with wool and purple, but binding on righteousness
and putting around you the leaves of self-control, busy yourself about Christ;
/for I am the door,/ he says somewhere; which those who wish to understand God
must learn thoroughly, so that he may open for us all at once the gates of the
heavens; for the gates of the Word are rational, opened with a key of faith;
/no one has known God, except the Son and he to whom the Son may reveal him./
And a door, I know well, that has been shut until now, the one who opens it
later reveals the things within and shows what it was not possible even to
know before, except for those who have gone through Christ, through whom alone
God is beheld.

Do not, then, busy yourselves about godless inner shrines, nor about mouths of
chasms full of marvel-mongering, or a Thesprotian cauldron, or a Cirrhaean
tripod, or a Dodonaean bronze; and leave the old stump honoured in desert
sands, and the oracle there, withered together with its oak, to myths that
have grown old. The Castalian spring, at any rate, has been silenced, and
another spring at Colophon, and the other mantic streams have likewise died,
and, empty of the vapour, late indeed, yet they have been exposed, having
flowed away together with their own myths. Recount to us also the useless
oracles of the rest of divination, or rather of madness: the Clarian, the
Pythian, the Didymean, that of Amphiaraus, the † Apollo, that of Amphilochus,
and if you wish, also the portent-watchers and the bird-watchers and the
judges of dreams, unholy with them; and set together beside the Pythian,
bringing them, the flour-diviners and barley-diviners and the ventriloquists
still honoured among the many; yes, and let the inner shrines of Egyptians and
the necromancies of Tyrrhenians be given over to darkness. These are truly mad
sophistries of unbelieving men and gambling-houses of unmixed error; partners
of this sorcery are goats trained for divination and ravens taught by men to
give oracles to men.

And what if I should catalogue the mysteries for you? I will not dance them
out, as they say Alcibiades did, but I will strip quite well, according to the
word of truth, the sorcery hidden in them, and those who are called your gods,
whose mystic rites they are, as upon the stage of life, I will roll out for
the spectators of the truth. The Bacchi celebrate raving Dionysus in rites,
conducting the sacred madness with raw-eating, and they complete the
distributions of flesh of the murders, wreathed with the snakes, crying Euoi,
that Eva through whom the error followed; and a sign of Bacchic orgies is a
consecrated snake. Immediately, at any rate, according to the precise speech
of the Hebrews, the name Hevia, aspirated, is interpreted the female snake;
and Deo and Kore have already become a mystic drama, and Eleusis holds the
torch for their error and rape and grief.

And it seems to me that the orgies and the mysteries must be etymologized, the
one from the wrath of Deo that came about toward Zeus, the others from the
pollution that happened concerning Dionysus; and if also from some Myon of
Attica, whom Apollodorus says was destroyed in a hunt, I do not grudge it:
your mysteries have been glorified with tomb-honour. It is possible also
otherwise for you to understand the mysteries as myth-eries, the letters
corresponding; for such myths hunt, if any others do, the most barbarous of
Thracians, the most mindless of Phrygians, the superstitious of Greeks.
Perish, then, the one who began this deceit for men, whether Dardanus, who
showed forth the mysteries of the Mother of the gods, or Eetion, who
underpinned the orgies and rites of the Samothracians, or that Phrygian Midas,
who learned from the Odrysian, then distributed to those under him an artful
deceit. For Cinyras the islander of Cyprus would never persuade me, who dared
to hand over the wanton orgies about Aphrodite from night to day, ambitious to
deify a citizen harlot. And Melampus the son of Amythaon others say brought
over from Egypt to Greece the festivals of Deo, a grief sung. These I would
call first-evils, fathers of godless myths and of destructive superstition,
who planted in life the mysteries, a seed of vice and corruption.

And now, for it is time, I will expose your orgies themselves, full of deceit
and marvel-mongering. And if you have been initiated, you will laugh the more
at these honoured myths of yours. I will speak openly the hidden things, not
ashamed to say what you are not ashamed to worship. She, then, who is
/foam-born/ and /Cyprus-born/, dear to Cinyras (I mean Aphrodite, the /lover
of genitals, because she appeared from genitals,/ those cut-off genitals of
Ouranos, the lewd ones, which after the cutting forced the wave), how
Aphrodite becomes for you a fruit worthy of shameless members: in the rites of
this sea-pleasure a lump of salt and a phallus are given to those initiated
into the adulterous craft as a token of the birth; and those being initiated
bring coin to her, as lovers to a courtesan.

And the mysteries of Deo are the amorous entwinings of Zeus with mother
Demeter, and the wrath (I do not know what I should say next, of mother or of
wife) of Deo, for whose sake she is said to have been addressed as Brimo, and
the supplications of Zeus and a drink of gall and heart-tearings and
unspeakable doings; the Phrygians complete the same for Attis and Cybele and
the Corybantes. And they have babbled that Zeus, tearing off the ram's twins,
brought them and threw them into the midst of Deo's lap, paying a false
penalty of the violent entwining, as if he had castrated himself. The symbols
of this initiation, set out as a surplus, I know will move laughter even in
you who are not inclined to laugh, because of the exposures: /I ate from a
drum; I drank from a cymbal; I carried the kernos; I went under the bridal
chamber./ Are these symbols not insolence? Are the mysteries not mockery? And
what if I should add also what remains? Demeter conceives, and Kore is reared,
and this begetter Zeus mingles again with Pherrephatta, his own daughter,
after the mother Deo, forgetting the former pollution (Zeus is father and
corrupter of a girl) and he mingles, having become a snake, which he was,
exposed. At any rate a symbol of the Sabazian mysteries for those being
initiated is the god through the lap; and this is a snake, dragged through the
lap of those being completed, an exposure of the incontinence of Zeus.
Pherrephatta too conceives a child in bull-form; to be sure, some idolatrous
poet says, /bull is of a snake and father of a bull a snake, on the mountain
the hidden thing, cowherd, the goad,/ a cowherd's goad, I think, calling the
fennel-wand, which the Bacchi wreath. Do you want me to recount to you also
Pherrephatta's flower-gatherings and the basket and the rape by Aidoneus and
the chasm of the earth and Eubouleus's swine swallowed down with the two
goddesses, for which reason at the Thesmophoria, making pits, they throw in
pigs? This mythology the women celebrate variously city by city, Thesmophoria,
Skirophoria, Arretophoria, staging in many ways the rape of Pherrephatta.

For the mysteries of Dionysus are completely inhuman: him, still a child,
while the Curetes were dancing around him with armed movement, and Titans
sneaking in by a trick, deceiving him with childish playthings, these Titans
tore apart, still an infant, as Orpheus the Thracian, the poet of the Rite,
says: /cone and rhombos and toys with bent limbs, and beautiful golden apples
from the clear-voiced Hesperides./ And it is not useless, for condemnation, to
set before you the useless symbols of this rite: knucklebone, ball,
spinning-top, apples, rhombos, mirror, fleece. Athena, then, having stolen the
heart of Dionysus, was addressed as Pallas from brandishing (pallein) the
heart; and the Titans, who also tore him apart, setting a certain cauldron on
a tripod and throwing in the limbs of Dionysus, first boiled them; then,
piercing them on spits, /they held them over Hephaestus./ And Zeus appearing
later (if he was a god, perhaps sharing somehow in the steam of the roasting
meats, of which your gods confess they /receive the privilege/) maltreats the
Titans with a thunderbolt and deposits the limbs of Dionysus with his child
Apollo to bury. And he, for he did not disobey Zeus, carrying them to
Parnassus, deposits the torn corpse.

And if you wish also to behold the orgies of the Corybantes, these, having
killed the third brother, covered the head of the corpse with a purple cloth
and, having crowned it, buried it, carrying it on a bronze shield under the
foothills of Olympus. And these, to speak concisely, are the mysteries:
murders and tombs; and the priests of these, whom those who care to call them
Anaktotelestai do call, add marvel-mongering to the disaster, forbidding
celery to be set wholly rooted on a table; for they think that the celery has
grown from the blood that flowed from the Corybantic one; just as, to be sure,
the women keeping the Thesmophoria also take care not to eat the seeds of the
pomegranate that have fallen to the ground, thinking the pomegranates have
sprouted from the drops of the blood of Dionysus. And calling the Corybantes
Cabeiri they announce a Cabeiric rite; for these two brother-killers, taking
up the chest in which the private part of Dionysus was stored, brought it down
to Tyrrhenia, merchants of a glorious cargo; and there they spent their time,
being fugitives, depositing with the Tyrrhenians the much-honoured teaching of
piety, private parts and a chest, to worship. For which reason some not
unreasonably wish Dionysus to be addressed as Attis, deprived of private
parts.

And what wonder if the barbarian Tyrrhenians are completed with passions so
shameful, when among Athenians and the rest of Greece, I am ashamed even to
say, the mythology about Deo is full of shame? For Deo, wandering in search of
her daughter Kore around Eleusis (this is the place of Attica), grows weary
and sits down by a well in grief. This is forbidden even now to those being
initiated, so that the completed may not seem to imitate the one wailing. At
that time the earth-born were dwelling in Eleusis; their names were Baubo and
Dysaules and Triptolemus, and still Eumolpus and Eubouleus; Triptolemus was a
cowherd, Eumolpus a shepherd, Eubouleus a swineherd; from whom this
hierophantic race of the Eumolpidae and of the Kerykes flourished at Athens.
And (for I will not leave off from saying) Baubo, having received Deo as a
guest, offers her a kykeon; and when she refuses to take it and is unwilling
to drink (for she was in mourning), Baubo, becoming very pained, as if
overlooked, pulls up her private parts and shows them to the goddess; and Deo
is delighted at the sight and at last receives the drink, pleased with the
spectacle. These are the hidden things of the Athenian mysteries. These, you
see, Orpheus also writes. And I will set before you the very verses of
Orpheus, so that you may have the mystagogue as witness of the shamelessness:
/so speaking she drew up her robes, and showed all the shape of the body, not
even seemly; and the child was Iacchus, and he laughed and tossed it with his
hand under Baubo's lap; and when the goddess smiled, she smiled in her heart,
and received the bright vessel in which the kykeon lay./ And the password of
the Eleusinian mysteries is: /I fasted, I drank the kykeon, I took from a
chest, having workedhaving tasted Lobeck. I put it away into a basket and from
a basket into a chest./ Fine spectacles, and befitting a goddess. Worthy,
then, of night are the completions, and of fire, and of the /great-hearted/,
or rather vain-minded, people of the Erechtheidae, and of the rest of the
Greeks besides, whom /there awaits, when they have died, what they do not even
hope./ To whom, then, does Heraclitus of Ephesus prophesy? /To
night-wanderers, magi, Bacchi, Lenae, mystai;/ to these he threatens the
things after death, to these he prophesies the fire: /for the mysteries
customarily observed among men are initiated in an unholy way./
Law, then, and empty assumption are the mysteries, and a deceit of the dragon
worshipped, of those who turn to spurious piety the initiations that are truly
uninitiated and the rites that are uncelebrated. And what sort are the mystic
chests too? For one must strip their holy things and speak out the
unspeakable. Are these not sesame cakes and pyramids and balls of wool and
many-navelled cakes and lumps of salt and a snake, the orgy of Dionysus
Bassareus? And not pomegranates besides these, and fig-branches and
fennel-wands and ivies, and besides also phthois-cakes and poppies? These are
their holy things. And further the secret symbols of Earth Themis: marjoram,
lamp, sword, a woman's comb, which is, to speak euphemistically and
mystically, a woman's member. O the evident shamelessness. Of old, for men who
were temperate, night was a silent covering of pleasure; but now for those
being initiated night is a spoken trial of incontinence, and the fire,
torch-led, exposes the passions. Put out, O hierophant, the fire; be ashamed,
torch-bearer, of the torches; the light exposes your Iacchus; permit night to
hide the mysteries; let the orgies be honoured with darkness. Fire does not
play a part; it is commanded to expose and to punish.

These are the mysteries of the godless; and I reasonably call godless those
who have not known the God who truly is, and who shamelessly worship a child
torn apart by Titans and a little woman in mourning and members truly
unspeakable from shame, held in a double atheism: a first, according to which
they are ignorant of God, not recognizing as God the one who truly is, and
another, this second error, thinking those who are not as if they are, and
naming as gods these who are not truly, or rather who are not even, having
obtained only the name. For this reason, you see, the apostle also exposes us,
saying /and you were strangers of the covenants of the promise, having no hope
and godless in the world./

Many good things befall the king of the Scythians, whoever Anacharsis was.
This man shot down his own citizen who was imitating among the Scythians the
rite of the Mother of the gods among the Cyzicenes, beating a drum and
sounding a cymbal and having hung from his neck some begging-priest thing, as
having become unmanly among Greeks and a teacher of the female disease to the
other Scythians. For which reasons (for it must in no way be hidden) it comes
upon me to wonder in what way they have called atheists Euhemerus of Acragas
and Nicanor of Cyprus and Diagoras and Hippon the two of Melos and that
Cyrenaean besides these (Theodorus is his name) and certain many others, who
have lived temperately and have seen more sharply than the rest of men the
error about these gods, even if they have not understood the truth itself, yet
have suspected the error, which is no small spark of understanding toward
truth that springs up as a seed; of whom one charges the Egyptians, /if you
think them gods, do not lament them nor beat yourselves; but if you mourn
them, no longer consider these to be gods,/ and the other, taking Heracles
made from wood (and he happened to be boiling something at home, as is
likely), /come now, O Heracles,/ he said; /now already it is time for you, as
for Eurystheus, so also for us to perform this thirteenth labour and to
prepare this relishrelish Cobet. this MSS. for Diagoras./ And then he put him
into the fire as wood.

Extremes of ignorance, then, are atheism and superstition, from which one must
be eager to remain outside. Do you not see Moses, the hierophant of the truth,
commanding that one crushed and one cut off not assemble, and further the one
from a harlot? And he hints through the former at the godless manner deprived
of the divine and generative power, and through the remaining third at the one
who inscribes many falsely named gods instead of the only God who is, just as
the one from the harlot inscribes many fathers in ignorance of the father
toward truth. And there was a certain innate ancient communion of men toward
heaven, darkened by ignorance, but leaping out somewhere suddenly from the
darkness and shining, as that saying spoken by someone: /do you see this
boundless ether on high, holding earth around in wet arms?/ and /O vehicle of
earth and having a seat upon earth, whoever you are, hard to guess to look
upon,/ and as many other such things as the children of poets sing.

And thoughts that have missed and been led aside from the straight, truly
destructive, have turned aside the heavenly plant, man, from heavenly diet and
have stretched him out upon earth, persuading him to attend to earthly
formations. For some, immediately deceived about the sight of heaven and
having trusted sight alone, observing the motions of the stars, wondered and
deified them, naming the stars gods from running (thein), and they worshipped
the sun, as Indians, and the moon, as Phrygians; and others, plucking the
cultivated fruits of the things that grow from earth, addressed wheat as Deo,
as Athenians, and the vine as Dionysus, as Thebans. Others, having observed
the exchanges of vice, make gods of the repayments, worshipping even the
disasters. From here the poets around the stage have invented the Erinyes and
the Eumenides and Palamnaioi and Prostropaioi, and still Alastores. And
already some of philosophers themselves, after the poetic ones, make
idol-forms of the passions among you, Fear and Eros and Joy and Hope, just as,
to be sure, also Epimenides of old set up altars of Insolence and
Shamelessness at Athens; and others, starting from the things themselves, are
deified for men and formed bodily, a certain Dike and Clotho and Lachesis and
Atropos and Heimarmene, and Auxo and Thallo, the Attic ones. A sixth
introductory manner of deceit that procures gods is that according to which
they number the twelve gods; of whom Hesiod sings his own theogony, and as
many things as Homer theologizes. And last there remains (for these manners
are seven in all) the one that starts from the divine beneficence that comes
about toward men. For not understanding the God who does good, they invented
certain saviours, Dioscuri, and Heracles averter of evil, and Asclepius a
physician.

These are the slippery and harmful deviations from the truth, dragging man
down from heaven and overturning him into a chasm. And I wish to show you at
close quarters the gods themselves, of what sort they are and if they are any,
so that you may at last cease from the error, and run back again to heaven.
/For we too were somehow children of wrath, as also the rest; but God, being
rich in mercy, because of his great love with which he loved us, being already
dead in trespasses, made us alive together with Christ./ For the Word is
living, and he who has been buried with Christ is raised together with God.
And those still unbelieving are named /children of wrath/, nourished on wrath;
but we are no longer nurslings of wrath, we who have been torn from error,
darting toward the truth. In this way, you see, we who were once sons of
lawlessness have now, through the love of mankind of the Word, become sons of
God; and to you even your own poet Empedocles of Acragas slips in: /therefore,
being at a loss in hard evils, you will never rest your spirit from wretched
griefs./ Most things, then, have been mythologized and invented about gods for
you; and as many as have been supposed even to have happened, these have been
written about shameful men who have lived licentiously: /and you walk in
vapour and madness, and leaving the straight right path you have gone the one
through thorns and stakes. Why do you wander, mortals? Cease, vain ones, leave
the darkness of night, and take hold of light./ These things the prophetic and
poetic Sibyl charges us; and truth also charges, stripping from this striking
and astonishing crowd of gods the masks, exposing the opinion-makings by
certain identifications of names.

Immediately, at any rate, there are those who write three Zeuses, the one of
Aether in Arcadia, and the remaining two children of Cronos, of these the one
in Crete, the other again in Arcadia. And there are those who suppose five
Athenas, the one of Hephaestus, the Athenian; the one of Nile, the Egyptian; a
third of Cronos, the inventor of war; a fourth of Zeus, whom the Messenians
have surnamed Coryphasia from the mother; and upon all the one of Pallas and
Titanis daughter of Oceanus, who, having impiously sacrificed her father, has
been adorned with the paternal skin as with a fleece. And indeed Aristotle has
a first Apollo of Hephaestus and Athena (here then Athena is no longer a
virgin), a second in Crete the one of Cyrbas, a third the one of Zeus, and a
fourth the Arcadian one of Silenus; this one has been called Nomios among
Arcadians; besides these he catalogues the Libyan one of Ammon; and Didymus
the grammarian brings upon these a sixth, the one of Magnes. And how many even
now are Apollos, innumerable mortal and perishable men, who have been called
similarly to those mentioned before? And what if I should tell you the many
Asclepii, or the Hermeses numbered, or the Hephaestuses mythologized? Lest I
seem even superfluous, flooding your ears with these many names? But their
fatherlands and their crafts and their lives, and their tombs besides, expose
them as having been men.

Ares, at any rate, even among the poets honoured as far as possible, /Ares,
Ares, bane of men, blood-stained, stormer of walls,/ this changeable and
unamiable one, as Epicharmus says, was a Spartiate; and Sophocles knows him as
a Thracian; and others as an Arcadian. And Homer says this one was bound for
thirteen months: /Ares endured, when Otus and strong Ephialtes, children of
Aloeus, bound him in a strong bond; and he was bound in a bronze jar thirteen
months./ Many good things may the Carians have, who sacrifice dogs to him. And
let the Scythians not cease sacrificing asses, as Apollodorus says and
Callimachus, /Phoebus is assigned the sacred rites of asses among
Hyperboreans./ And the same elsewhere: /sleek ass-slaughters delight Phoebus./
And Hephaestus, whom Zeus threw from Olympus /from the divine threshold,/
falling in Lemnos was a bronze-worker, lamed in both feet, /and under him the
thin shanks moved./ You have also a physician, not only a bronze-worker, among
gods; and the physician was a lover of money, Asclepius his name. And I will
set before you your own poet, the Boeotian Pindar: /gold appearing in hands
turned even that one with lordly wage; and the son of Cronos, throwing with
both hands, swiftly took the breath of the chest, breath Pindar. breaths MSS.
and a blazing thunderbolt struckstruck Pindar. struck MSS. doom,/ and
Euripides /for Zeus, having killed my child, is cause, Asclepius, throwing

Cynosuris. And Philochorus says Poseidon is honoured as a physician in Tenos,
and that Sicily lies upon Cronos and that he has been buried there. And
Patrocles of Thurii and Sophocles the younger in certain tragedies recount
about the two Dioscuri; these two Dioscuri were two perishable men, if Homer
is sufficient for anyone to confirm what has been said: /and already the
life-giving earth held them there in Lacedaemon, in the dear fatherland./ And
let the one who wrote the Cypria poems also come forward: /Castor is mortal,
and the lot of Death has been allotted him; but Polydeuces is immortal, a
scion of Ares./ This he lied poetically; but Homer is more trustworthy than
he, having spoken about both the Dioscuri, and besides having exposed Heracles
as a phantom: for /a man, Heracles, knower of great deeds./ Homer himself,
then, knows Heracles too as a mortal man, and Hieronymus the philosopher also
describes the condition of his body, small, bristly-haired, vigorous; and
Dicaearchus, lean, sinewy, dark, hook-nosed, somewhat grey-eyed,
straight-haired. This Heracles, then, having lived fifty-two years, ended his
life, having been buried by the pyre on Oeta.

And the Muses, whom Alcman genealogizes as of Zeus and Mnemosyne, and the rest
of the poets and writers deify and worship, and already even whole cities
fence precincts, Museia, to them: these were Mysian maidservants, and Megaclo
the daughter of Macar bought them. And Macar was king of the Lesbians, and he
was always at odds with his wife, and Megaclo was angry on behalf of her
mother; why should she not be? And she buys these Mysian maidservants, so many
in number, and calls them Moisai according to the dialect of the Aeolians.
These she taught to sing and to play the cithara the ancient deeds tunefully.
And they, continually playing the cithara and charming Macar well, enchanted
him and stopped his wrath. For which reason Megaclo dedicated them in bronze
as a thank-offering on behalf of her mother and commanded them to be honoured
throughout all the sanctuaries. And such are the Muses; and the history is in
Myrsilus of Lesbos.

Hear, then, the loves of the gods among you and the paradoxical mythologies of
incontinence, and their wounds and bonds and laughters and fights, and still
slaveries and drinking-bouts and entwinings again and tears and passions and
wanton pleasures. Call for me Poseidon and the chorus of those corrupted by
him, Amphitrite, Amymone, Alope, Melanippe, Alcyone, Hippothoe, Chione, the
other countless ones; among whom, even being so many, the passions of your
Poseidon were still cramped; call for me also Apollo; this is Phoebus and a
holy prophet and a good counsellor; but Sterope does not say these things, nor
Aethusa, nor Arsinoe, nor Zeuxippe, nor Prothoe, nor Marpessa, nor Hypsipyle;
for Daphne alone escaped both the prophet and the corruption. And Zeus himself
upon all, let him come, the /father/ according to you /of men and of gods./ He
was poured out so far about the things of Aphrodite that he desired all and
fulfilled the desire into all. He was filled, at any rate, with women no less
than the goat of the Thmouitae with she-goats. And I have wondered at your
poems, O Homer: /he spoke, and the son of Cronos nodded with dark brows; and
the ambrosial locks of the lord streamed from the immortal head; and he shook
great Olympus./ You invent Zeus as august, Homer, and you attach to him an
honoured nod. But if you only show the cestus, O man, both Zeus is exposed and
the hair is put to shame. To what a point of licentiousness that Zeus has been
driven who spent so many nights of pleasure with Alcmene; for not even the
nine nights were long for the unrestrained (on the contrary his whole life was
short by incontinence), so that he might sow for us the god averter of evil.
Heracles son of Zeus, truly of Zeus, the one generated from a long night,
having laboured the twelve labours in much time, corrupted the fifty daughters
of Thestius in one night, becoming at once adulterer and bridegroom of so many
virgins. Not unreasonably, then, the poets call him /cruel/ and /doer of
unholy deeds/. And it would be long for me to recount his adulteries of every
kind and corruptions of boys. For not even from boys did the gods among you
hold back, one loving Hylas, another Hyacinthus, another Pelops, another
Chrysippus, another Ganymede. Let your women worship these gods, and let them
pray that their own husbands be such, so temperate, that they may be like the
gods, having emulated the same things; let your children be accustomed to
revere these, so that they too may become men, taking the gods as a clear
image of fornication.
But perhaps only the males among their gods dart about the things of
Aphrodite; /and the more female goddesses remained each at home in shame,/
says Homer, the goddesses being ashamed, through dignity, to see Aphrodite
caught in adultery. But they are incontinent more passionately, bound in the
adultery, Eos upon Tithonus, Selene upon Endymion, a Nereid upon Aeacus and
Thetis upon Peleus, and Demeter upon Iasion and Pherrephatta upon Adonis. And
Aphrodite, having been put to shame upon Ares, went over to Cinyras and
married Anchises and lay in wait for Phaethon and loved Adonis, and she
competed with the ox-eyed one, and the goddesses, having stripped because of
an apple, attended naked to the shepherd, to see if one of them would seem
beautiful.

Come then, let us also go round the contests in brief and let us dissolve
these tomb-festivals, Isthmia and Nemea and Pythia and the Olympia upon these.
At Pytho, then, the Pythian serpent is worshipped and the festival of the
snake is announced, Pythia; and at the Isthmus the sea spat out a pitiable
refuse and the Isthmia lament Melicertes; and at Nemea another little child,
Archemorus, has been buried, and the funeral of the child is addressed as
Nemea; and Pisa is a tomb for you, O Panhellenes, of a Phrygian charioteer,
and the Zeus of Pheidias appropriates the drink-offerings of Pelops, the
Olympia. The contests, then, it seems, were mysteries, contested over the
dead, just as also the oracles, and both have been made public. But the
mysteries at Agra and those at Halimous of Attica have been limited at Athens;
and already a world-shame are both the contests and the phalli completed for
Dionysus, badly grazing upon life.

For Dionysus, longing to go down to Hades, did not know the road, and someone
promises to tell it to him, Prosymnus by name, not without wage; and the wage
was not beautiful, but beautiful to Dionysus; and the favour was of Aphrodite,
the wage which Dionysus was asked; and the request came about for the god as
he wished, and he promises to provide it to him, if he should return,
confirming the promise with an oath. Having learned, he departed; he came back
again; he does not find Prosymnus (for he had died); Dionysus, discharging his
duty to the lover, rushes to the memorial and is wanton. Cutting, then, a
branch of fig as it happened, he fashions it in the manner of a male member
and sits upon the branch, completing the promise to the dead. As a mystic
memorial of this passion, phalli are set up city by city for Dionysus; /for if
they did not make a procession for Dionysus and sing a songsongs Heinsius:

Reasonably, then, such gods of yours are slaves, having become slaves of
passions, but even before those called Helots among the Lacedaemonians Apollo
entered a slavish yoke for Admetus at Pherae, Heracles for Omphale at Sardis,
and Poseidon and Apollo served Laomedon, like a useless household servant, not
even able, I suppose, to obtain freedom from the former master; then also they
two built the walls of Ilium for the Phrygian. And Homer is not ashamed to
show Athena appearing, saying to Odysseus /holding a golden lamp/ in her
hands; and we have read Aphrodite, like some unrestrained little maidservant,
bringing and setting the stool of the adulterer before Helen's face, so that
she may lead him on to intercourse. For Panyassis besides these also recounts
that gods served very many other men, writing somehow thus: /Demeter endured,
and famous Amphigyeis endured, and Poseidon endured, and silver-bowed Apollo
endured to serve for a yearserve Sylburg. to be about to serve MSS. beside a

To these, then, it reasonably follows to introduce these erotic and passionate
gods of yours as having human passions in every way. /For indeed they too have
mortal flesh./ And Homer confirms it, introducing Aphrodite very accurately
upon the wound crying sharply and greatly, and narrating that Ares himself,
the most warlike, was wounded by Diomedes in the flank. And Polemon says that
Athena also was wounded by Ornytus; and indeed Homer says that Aidoneus too
was shot by Heracles, and Panyassis recounts Helios as Augeas. And already
this same Panyassis also recounts Hera the yoked one as wounded by this same
Heracles /in sandy Pylos/. And Sosibius says that Heracles too was wounded in
the hand by the Hippocoontidae. And if wounds, also bloods; for the poetic
ichors are more repulsive even than the bloods, for ichor is understood as a
rotting of blood. It is necessary, then, to introduce also therapies and
nourishments for them, of which they are in need. Therefore tables and
drunkennesses and laughters and intercourses: they would not be using human
things of Aphrodite nor begetting children nor even sleeping, if they were
immortal and without need and unaging. And Zeus himself also shared a human
table among the Ethiopians, and an inhuman and lawless one when feasted at
Lycaon's the Arcadian's; he was filled, at any rate, with human flesh not
willingly. For the god did not know that Lycaon the Arcadian, his host, having
slaughtered his own child (Nyctimus his name) would set him as a relish for
Zeus. Fine Zeus the mantic, the of-guests, the of-suppliants, the mild, the
all-voiced, the of-turning-to; or rather the unjust, the lawless, the
without-law, the unholy, the inhuman, the violent, the corrupter, the
adulterer, the erotic. But there was a time when he was such, when he was a
man, and now already even your myths seem to me to have grown old. Zeus is no
longer a snake, he is not a swan, not an eagle, not an erotic man; a god does
not fly, he does not love boys, he does not love, he does not force, although
there are still many and beautiful women even now, more comely than Leda and
more in their prime than Semele, and youths more in season and more civic than
the Phrygian cowherd. Where now is that eagle? And where the swan? And where
Zeus himself? He has grown old with the wing; for he is not, I suppose,
repenting of the erotic things, nor being trained to be temperate. And the
myth is stripped for you: Leda has died, the swan has died, the eagle has
died. Seek your Zeus; do not busy yourself about heaven, but about earth. The
Cretan will recount it to you, with whom he has also been buried; Callimachus
in hymns: /for a tomb, O lord, of you the Cretans have fashioned./ For Zeus
has died (do not take it hard) as Leda, as swan, as eagle, as erotic man, as
snake.

And already even the superstitious themselves appear, unwillingly indeed, yet
understanding the error about the gods; /for they are not from an oak of old
report nor from a rock,/ but they are a race of men, and a little later they
will be found being oaks and rocks too. Staphylus, at any rate, recounts that
a certain Zeus Agamemnon is honoured at Sparta; and Phanocles in Loves or
Beautifuls that Agamemnon the king of the Greeks set up a temple of Aphrodite
for Argynnus upon Argynnus his beloved. And Arcadians turn to an Artemis
called Hanged, as Callimachus says in Aetia. And another Artemis Kondylitis
has been honoured in Methymna. And there is also a sanctuary of another
Artemis of Gout in Laconia, as Sosibius says. And Polemon knows a statue of
Gaping Apollo, and again another of Glutton Apollo honoured in Elis. There the
Eleans sacrifice to Fly-Averting Zeus; and Romans sacrifice to Fly-Averting
Heracles and to Fever and to Fear, whom they also inscribe with those around
Heracles. And I pass over Argives; Argives and Laconians worship Aphrodite
Tomb-Robber, and Spartiates revere Artemis Chelytis; since they call coughing

Do you think these things set before you by us are brought from somewhere as
interpolations? You do not even seem to recognize your own writers, whom I
call as witnesses against your unbelief, of godless mockery, O wretched ones,
who have filled your whole life, truly unlivable, with it. Has not a bald Zeus
been honoured at Argos, and another, an avenger, in Cyprus? And do not Argives
sacrifice to Aphrodite Peribasos, and Athenians to a courtesan, and Syracusans

/beautiful-rumped/? And I am already silent about Dionysus the pig-plucker;
Sicyonians worship this one, having stationed Dionysus over the female
members, revering a president of shame, the beginner of insolence. Such are
the gods to them, and such they themselves, playing among gods, or rather
mocking and insulting themselves. And how much better Egyptians, having
honoured the irrational of the animals village by village and city by city,
than Greeks worshipping such gods? For those, even if they are beasts, are not
adulterous, not wanton, and not one hunts pleasure contrary to nature. And of
what sort these are, what need is there still to say, when they have been
sufficiently exposed?

But the Egyptians, of whom I just now made mention, are scattered according to
their worships; and of them Syenites revere the phagros fish, and those who
dwell in Elephantine the maiotes (this is another fish), Oxyrhynchites

altogether better than Egyptians (I hesitate to say worse), who do not cease

irrational animals? Thessalians among you have honoured the storks because of
the custom, and Thebans the weasels because of the birth of Heracles. And what

daughter of Cletor and generated Myrmidon; and Polemon recounts that those
dwelling around the Troad revere the local mice, whom they call sminthoi,
because they gnawed through the sinews of the enemies' bows; and they surnamed
Apollo Smintheus from those mice. And Heracleides in Foundations of
Sanctuaries says about Acarnania, where the Actian cape is and the sanctuary
of Actian Apollo, that a bull is sacrificed beforehand to the flies. Nor will
I forget Samians (Samians revere a sheep, as Euphorion says) nor those of the
Phoenician Syrians who dwell there, of whom some revere doves, others fishes,
so excessively as Eleans Zeus.

So be it; since those whom you worship are not gods, it seems good to me to
examine again whether they would really be daemons, enrolled in this second
rank, as you say. For if, then, they are daemons, they are greedy and
polluted. It is possible also to find openly thus, city by city, local daemons
reaping honour, Menedemus among Cythnians, Callistagora among Tenians, Anius
among Delians, Astrabacus among Laconians. And someone is honoured also at

Median contests were at their height. And it is possible for one able to look
through even a little to see very many other daemons too; /for thrice ten
thousand are upon the much-nourishing earth immortal daemons, guardians of
mortal men./ Who are the guardians, O Boeotian, do not grudge to say. Or is it

Come then, let us add this too, that your gods would be inhuman and man-hating
daemons, and not only rejoicing in the mind-harm of men, but also enjoying
man-killings; now procuring for themselves the armed rivalries in stadia, now

demanded savage drink-offerings. Aristomenes the Messenian, at any rate,

From these, at any rate, the daemons appear lovers of mankind; and how are the
superstitious not holy in proportion? The ones being well-spoken of as
saviours, the others asking salvation from plotters against salvation.
Guessing, at any rate, that they make well-omened sacrifice to them, they have
unnoticed slaughtered men. For murder does not become a victim by the place,
nor if someone should slaughter the man for Artemis and Zeus in a supposedly
sacred place rather than in wrath and love of money, other like daemons, upon
altars or in roads, naming a sacred victim, but such a sacrifice is murder and
man-killing. Why then, O men wisest of the other animals, do we flee around
the wild beasts, and if we somewhere meet a bear or a lion, we turn aside, /as
when someone seeing a snake has started back in a mountain glen, and trembling
has taken his limbs, and he has withdrawn again/; but daemons that are
destructive and accursed and plotting and man-hating and ruinous, perceiving
and understanding them beforehand, you do not turn aside nor turn away? And
what would the wicked even tell truly, or whom would they benefit?
Immediately, at any rate, I can show you man as better than these gods of
yours, the daemons, Cyrus and Solon than mantic Apollo. Your Phoebus is
gift-loving, but not a lover of mankind. He betrayed Croesus the friend and,
forgetting the wage (so glory-loving was he), he led Croesus through the Halys
onto the pyre. So loving, the daemons guide into the fire. But, O man more
humane and more true than Apollo, pity the one bound upon the pyre, and you, O
Solon, prophesy the truth, and you, O Cyrus, command the pyre to be put out.
Be temperate at last at any rate, O Croesus, having learned by the suffering;
he whom you worship is ungrateful, he takes the wage and after the gold he
lies again. The end, then, the daemon does not tell you, but the man. Solon
does not prophesy crooked things; this oracle you will find true only, O
barbarian; this you will test upon the pyre.

Whence it comes upon me to wonder by what fantasies those first who had gone
astray, led off, announced superstition to men, legislating to revere accursed
daemons, whether that Phoroneus was or Merops or some other, who are
mythologized as first to have set up temples and altars to them, and besides
to have set sacrifices beside them. For indeed also according to later times
they invented gods whom they might worship. To be sure this Eros, who is said
to be among the oldest of the gods, not even one honoured before Charmus took

desire having become complete; and they have called the licentiousness of the
disease Eros, making a god of unrestrained desire. And Athenians did not even
know who Pan was, before Philippides told them.

Reasonably, then, superstition, having taken a beginning from somewhere, has
become a spring of mindless vice; then, not having been checked, but having
come to increase and flowed much, it is established as a craftsman of many
daemons, sacrificing hecatombs and completing festivals and setting up statues

And if besides these I should bring and set before you the statues themselves
to examine, going over them you will find the custom truly to be nonsense,
turning to /works of men's hands/ that are insensible. Of old, then, the
Scythians worshipped the akinakes, the Arabs the stone, the Persians the
river, and of the other men those still more ancient set up conspicuous woods
and stood pillars from stones; which indeed were addressed as xoana because of
being scraped from the material. To be sure in Icaros the statue of Artemis
was an unworked wood, and of Cithaeronian Hera at Thespiae a trunk cut out;
and that of Samian Hera, as Aethlius says, was formerly a plank, and later in
the archonship of Procles became statue-shaped. And when the xoana began to be
imaged after men, they reaped the name brete from mortals (brotoi). And at
Rome of old the xoanon of Ares had been a spear, says Varro the writer, the
craftsmen not yet having rushed upon this fair-faced bad craft. And when the
art flourished, the error increased. How, then, they made the stones and the
woods and, to speak concisely, the material into manlike statues, to which you
put on a show of piety, slandering the truth, is already clear from this; yet,
the place needing also some demonstration, it must not be declined. That

testing is, if you wish to learn, the Holy Spirit will interpret for you: /and
they saw my works,/ he says, /forty years; wherefore I was wroth at this
generation and I said: they always wander in the heart; and they themselves
did not know my ways; as I swore in my wrath, if they shall enter into my
rest./ See the threat; see the exhortation; see the honour; why, then, do we
still change the grace into wrath, and not, receiving the Word with opened
ears, host God in pure souls? For great is the grace of his promise, /if today
we hear his voice/; and the today grows according to each day of his, until
the today is named. And until the consummation both the today and the learning
remain; and then the today that truly is, the unfailing day of God, is
stretched together with the ages. Let us always, then, obey the voice of the
divine Word; for the today is an image of an eternal age, and the day a symbol
of the light, and the Word light for men, through whom we are illuminated
toward God. Reasonably, then, for those who have believed and obey the grace
will superabound, but for those who have disobeyed and wander according to
heart and have not known the Lord's ways, which John commanded to make
straight and prepare, at these God was wroth and he threatens; and indeed also
the end of the threat the ancient wanderers of the Hebrews say is a threat in
riddles; for they are said not /to enter into the rest/ because of unbelief,
before they themselves, having followed the successor of Moses, learned late
by deed that they would not otherwise be saved unless they had believed as
Joshua. And the Lord, being a lover of mankind, invites all men /into
knowledge of the truth/, he who sends the Paraclete. What, then, is the
knowledge? Piety; /and piety is profitable toward all things/ according to
Paul, /having a promise of life of the now and of the coming./ For how much
would you have confessed, O men, if eternal salvation were sold, that you
would buy it? Not even if someone should measure out the whole Pactolus, the
mythical stream of gold, will he number a wage worthy of salvation. Do not,
then, grow weary; it is permitted you, if you wish, to buy out the
much-honoured salvation with a household treasure, love and faith, which is a
noteworthy wage of life. This price God receives gladly. /For we have hoped
upon a living God, who is saviour of all men, especially of believers./ And
the others, grown around the world, like some seaweeds on sea-rocks, slight
immortality, just as the Ithacan old man, not longing for the truth and the
fatherland in heaven, and besides the light that truly is, but for the smoke.
And piety, likening man to God as far as possible, inscribes as a
corresponding teacher God, who alone is able to image man to God worthily. The
apostle, knowing this teaching as truly divine, /but you, O Timothy,/ he says,
/from infancy you know holy letters, which are able to make you wise unto
salvation through faith in Christ./ For truly holy are the letters that make
holy and make gods, from which letters and holy syllables the writings
composed, the compositions, the same apostle accordingly calls /God-breathed/,
/being profitable toward teaching, toward exposure, toward setting right,
toward training in righteousness, so that the man of God may be complete,
furnished toward every good work./ No one would be so struck by the
exhortations of the other holy ones as by the Lord himself the lover of
mankind; for nothing else but this only is work for him, that man be saved. He
cries, at any rate, urging toward salvation himself /the kingdom of the
heavens has drawn near/; he turns the men who draw near by fear. In this way
the apostle of the Lord too, inviting the Macedonians, becomes an interpreter
of the divine voice, saying /the Lord has drawn near/, /take care lest we be
caught empty./ And you are so fearless, or rather unbelieving, obeying neither
the Lord himself nor Paul, and that when he has been bound on behalf of
Christ. /Taste and see that the Lord is kind./ Faith will lead in, experience
will teach, the writing will train, saying /come, O children, hear me, I will
teach you fear of the Lord./ Then, as to those already having believed, he
adds concisely /who is a man who wishes life, loving to see good days?/ We
are, we shall say, the worshippers of the good, the emulators of the good
things. Hear, then, /you who are far/, hear /you who are near/; the Word has
not been hidden from some; it is a common light, it shines upon all men; no
one is a Cimmerian in the Word; let us hasten to salvation, upon the
regeneration; let us, the many, hasten to be gathered into one love according
to the union of the monadic substance. Being well-worked, let us pursue unity
analogously, seeking the good monad. And the union from many, having taken a
divine harmony from many-voicedness and scattering, becomes one symphony,
following one chorus-leader and teacher, the Word, resting upon the truth
itself, saying /Abba, the Father/; this true voice God welcomes, first
harvesting it from his children.

But it is not reasonable, you say, to overturn a custom handed down to us from
fathers. And why do we not still use the first food, the milk, to which, I
suppose, the nurses accustomed us from birth? And why do we increase or lessen
the paternal substance, and not keep the equal, as we have received it? And
why do we no longer vomit into the paternal laps, or still complete the other
things which, being infants reared under mothers, we incurred as laughter, but
we have set ourselves right, even if we did not obtain good pedagogues? Then
upon voyages the deviations, though being damaging and unsafe, yet somehow
fall sweet, and upon life shall we not, leaving the wicked and passionate and
godless custom, even if the fathers are angry, turn aside toward the truth and
seek the Father who truly is, having thrust away the custom as a deadly drug?
For this itself, you see, is the most beautiful of the things attempted, to
show you how from madness and this thrice-wretched custom piety was hated; for
so great a good, than which nothing greater has ever been given from God to
the generation of men, would never have been hated or forbidden, unless,
snatched together by the custom, then indeed having stopped your ears against
us, like stiff-necked horses refusing the rein, biting the bits, you fled the
words, longing to shake off us, the charioteers of your life, and being
carried by mindlessness upon the cliffs of destruction you supposed the holy
Word of God accursed. There follow you, therefore, according to Sophocles, the
wages of the choice, mind gone, useless ears, empty thoughts, and you do not
know that this is true more than all, that the good and pious will obtain a
good exchange, having honoured the good, and those from the opposite, the
wicked, the corresponding punishment, and upon the ruler of vice a penalty has
been hung. The prophet Zechariah, at any rate, threatens him: /the Lord who
has chosen Jerusalem rebuke you; is this not a brand plucked from fire?/ What
desire, then, still lies in men for a willing death? And why have they fled to
this death-bearing brand, with which they will be burned down, when it is
possible to live well according to God, not according to the custom? For God
grants life, but a wicked custom after the departure from here rubs on an
empty repentance together with punishment, /and having suffered a child knew,/
how superstition destroys and piety saves. Let one of you see those who serve
among the idols, filthy in hair, insulted with dirty and torn clothing,
altogether inexperienced of baths, and made beastlike with the points of the
nails, and many also deprived of the private parts, showing by deed that the
precincts of the idols are certain tombs or prisons; these seem to me to
mourn, not to worship, the gods, having suffered things more worthy of pity
than of piety. And seeing these things do you still go blind, and will you not
look up toward the master of all and Lord of the whole? And will you not take
refuge, fleeing from the prisons here, upon the mercy from the heavens? For
God from much love of mankind holds to the man, as a mother bird flies upon a
nestling falling from a nest; and if somewhere also a creeping beast should
gape around the nestling, a mother flies around wailing dear children; and God
the Father both seeks the formation and heals the fall and pursues the beast
and takes up the nestling again, urging it to fly up to the nest. Then dogs
already having gone astray, tracking with smells, have tracked out the master,
and horses, having shaken off the rider, have obeyed the master at one whistle
somewhere; /and an ox,/ he says, /knew the one who acquired it, and an ass the
manger of its lord, but Israel did not know me./ What, then, the Lord? He does
not remember evil, he still pities, he still asks repentance. And I wish to
ask you, if it does not seem strange to you that you men, having come about as
a formation of God and having received the soul from him and being altogether
of God, serve another master, and besides tend, instead of the king, the
tyrant, and instead of the good, the wicked. For who, O by the truth, being
temperate, leaving the good, consorts with vice? And who is it who, fleeing
God, lives with little daemons? And who, being able to be a son of God, is
pleased to be a slave? Or who, being able to be a citizen of heaven, pursues
Erebus, when it is possible to farm paradise and go around heaven and take a
share of the living and unstained spring, walking the air in the track of that
light-bearing cloud, as Elijah, beholding the saving rain? And they, like
worms around pools and muds, the streams of pleasure, rolling, graze
unprofitable and mindless luxuries, certain swinish men. For swine, he says,
/are pleased with mud/ rather than with clean water and /rave upon refuse/
according to Democritus. Do not, then, do not let us be enslaved nor become
swinish, but /as genuine children of light/, let us look up and look toward
the light, lest the Lord expose us as bastards as the sun the eagles. Let us
repent, then, and transfer from ignorance to knowledge, from mindlessness to
understanding, from incontinence to self-control, from injustice to justice,
from atheism to God. A beautiful risk to desert to God. And it is possible for
the lovers of justice, who pursue eternal salvation, to enjoy many other good
things too, and indeed also those which God himself hints, speaking through
Isaiah: /there is an inheritance for those who tend the Lord/; a beautiful and
lovely inheritance, not gold, not silver, not clothing, the things of the
earth, where somewhere moth and robber somewhere sink about the earth-loving
wealth, eyeing it, but that treasure of salvation, toward which we must hurry,
having become lovers of the word, and the urbane works lift with us from here
and fly with the wing of the truth. This inheritance the eternal covenant of
God puts in our hands, furnishing the eternal gift; and this affectionate
father of ours, the Father who truly is, does not cease exhorting,
admonishing, training, loving; for he does not cease saving, and he advises
the best things: /become just, says the Lord; you who thirst, go upon water,
and as many as do not have silver, walk and buy and drink without silver./
Toward the bath, toward salvation, toward the illumination he invites, all but
crying and saying: I give you earth and sea, child, and heaven and all the
animals in them I grant you; only, O child, thirst for the Father, God will be
shown you without wage; the truth is not retailed, he gives you also the
winged and the swimming and the things upon the earth; these the Father has
crafted for your thankful luxuries. A bastard will buy with silver, who is a
child of destruction, who has chosen /to serve mammon/, but to you he entrusts
your things, I mean to the genuine, to the one who loves the Father, through
whom he still works, to whom alone he also promises, saying: /and the earth
shall not be sold unto confirmation/; for it is not ratified by corruption;
/for all the earth is mine,/ and it is also yours, if you receive God. Whence
the writing reasonably brings good news to those who have believed: /and the
holy of the Lord shall inherit the glory of God and his power./ What sort of
glory, O blessed one, tell me; /which eye has not seen nor ear heard, nor has
it come up upon a heart of man; and they shall rejoice upon the kingdom of
their Lord unto the ages, amen./ You have, O men, the divine promise of the
grace, you have heard also the other threat of the punishment, through which
the Lord saves, training the man with fear and grace; why do we delay? Why do
we not turn aside from the punishment? Why do we not receive the gift? And why
do we not choose the better, God instead of the wicked, and prefer wisdom to
idolatry and exchange life for death? /Behold I have set before your face,/ he
says, /the death and the life./ The Lord tries you to choose the life, he
advises you as a father to obey God. /For if you hear me,/ he says, /and wish,
you shall eat the good things of the earth,/ the grace of obedience; /but if
you do not obey me nor wish, a sword and fire shall eat you,/ the judgment of
disobedience. /For the mouth of the Lord has spoken these things;/ a law of
truth is a word of the Lord. Do you wish me to become a good counsellor for
you? But you hear; and I, if possible, will show. You ought, O men, thinking
about the good itself, to bring on an innate faith, a sufficient witness from
home itself, choosing the best conspicuously, and not even to seek whether one
must pursue it as a labour. For even if one must be drunk, say, one must
doubt; but you get drunk before examining; and if one must insult, you do not
busy yourselves, but as quickly as possible you insult. Only, then, if one
must be pious, you seek, and if one must follow this wise God and the Christ,
this you think worthy of counsel and examination, not even having understood
what befits God, whatever it is. Believe us even as drunkenness, so that you
may be temperate; believe even as insult, so that you may live. And if you
also wish to obey, having beheld the clear faith of the virtues, come, I will
set before you as a surplus the persuasion about the Word. And you, for the
paternal customs no longer occupy you from the truth, having been catechized
beforehand, might now hear how what is after this holds; and let not some
shame of this name take you beforehand, /which greatly harms men,/ turning
aside from salvation. Having stripped, then, openly in the stadium of the
truth let us contest genuinely, the holy Word awarding, and the master of the
whole directing the contest. For the prize that lies before us is not small,
immortality. Do not, then, still take thought even if a little, what certain
rabble of the marketplace say of you, godless chorus-members of superstition,
pushed by mindlessness and madness into the chasm itself, makers of idols and
worshippers of stones; for these have dared to deify men, writing as a
thirteenth god Alexander the Macedonian, /whom Babylon exposed dead./ I
admire, then, the Chian sophist, Theocritus his name; after the end of
Alexander Theocritus, mocking the empty opinions which men had about gods,
said to the citizens: /men, take courage as long as you see the gods dying
before the men./ And he who worships the visible gods and the mixed crowd of
these generated things, and makes them partners, is far more wretched than
those daemons themselves. For God is nowhere in no way unjust as the daemons,
but as just as possible, and nothing is more like him than whoever of us
becomes as just as possible. Go into the road, then, every handicraft people,
who turn to the fierce-eyed Ergáne goddess of Zeus with standing
winnowing-fans, silly ones, craftsmen and worshippers of the stones. Let your
Pheidias and Polycleitus come, and Praxiteles again and Apelles and as many as
go after the banausic arts, earthly workers of earth. For then, a certain
prophecy says, the affairs here will be unfortunate, when they believe
statues. Let them come again, then, for I will not cease calling, the little
craftsmen. None of these, I suppose, has crafted a breathing image, nor indeed
has softened soft flesh from earth. Who melted marrow or who fixed bones? Who
stretched sinews? Who blew veins? Who poured blood into them or who stretched
skin around? And where would one of them make seeing eyes? Who breathed in a
soul? Who granted justice? Who has promised immortality? Only the craftsman of
the whole, the /best-crafting Father/, formed such an ensouled statue, us,
man; but your Olympian, an image of an image, singing far from the truth, is a
deaf work of Attic hands. For /an image of God/ is his Word (and a genuine son
of the mind is the divine Word, an archetypal light of light), and an image of
the Word is the true man, the mind in man, who is said to have been generated
/according to the image/ of God and /according to likeness/ for this reason,
being likened by the understanding according to heart to the divine Word and
in this way rational. And of the seen earth-born man the statues that are
manlike appear an earthly image and a timely impress far from the truth.
Nothing else, then, but the life seemed to me to have become full of madness,
being occupied with so great eagerness about the material. And the custom has
been fattened by empty opinion, having given you a taste of slavery and of
irrational busyness; and of lawless laws and deceitful hypocrisies ignorance
is cause, which, introducing constructions into the race of men of destructive
fates and of hated idols, having invented many forms of the daemons, has
impressed upon those who follow it a stain of a long death. Take, then,
rational water, wash, you who have been polluted, sprinkle yourselves from the
custom with the true drops; one must go up clean into heavens. You are a man,
the most common, seek the one who crafted you; you are a son, the most
particular, recognize the Father; and do you still remain in the sins, glued
to pleasures? To whom shall the Lord speak /yours is the kingdom of the
heavens/? It is yours, if you wish, of those who have had the choice toward
God; yours, if you should wish only to believe and to follow the shortness of
the preaching, which the Ninevites having obeyed, by genuine repentance
exchanged the expected capture for the beautiful salvation. How, then, he
says, shall I go up into heavens? /A way/ is the Lord, /narrow/ indeed, but
/from heavens,/ narrow indeed, but sending up into heavens; narrow on earth
overlooked, broad in heavens worshipped. Then the one unheard of the Word has
ignorance as pardon of the error, but the one thrown into ears carries also in
the soul from the judgment the disobedience, and by as much as he shall seem
more understanding, the intelligence is toward ill for him, because he has
used the understanding as an accuser, not having chosen the best; for he has
been born as a man to have it as his own toward God. As, then, we do not force
the horse to plough nor the bull to hunt, but we drag each of the animals
toward what it has been born, so, to be sure, we also invite the man, born
toward the sight of heaven, a heavenly plant truly, toward the knowledge of
God, having grasped what is his own and exceptional and particular beside the
other animals, advising him to prepare a sufficient provision of ages, piety.
Farm, we say, if you are a farmer, but know God farming, and sail, you who
love seafaring, but calling upon the heavenly steersman; knowledge has caught
you serving as a soldier; hear the general who signals just things. As, then,
weighed down with stupor and drunkenness, sober up and, looking through a
little, think what the stones worshipped by you wish, and what you spend
emptily-eager about the material; you consume both the moneys and the life
into ignorance as your living into death, having found this only as the end of
your vain hope, not even able to pity yourselves, but you do not even become
fit to obey those who pity you of the error, enslaved to a wicked custom, from
which, hung, self-chosen, until the last breath you are carried down into
destruction; /because the light has come into the world and men loved the
darkness rather than the light,/ when it is possible to wipe off the things in
the way of salvation and the vapour and the wealth and the fear, uttering this
poetic thing: where then do I carry these many moneys? And where do I myself
wander? Do you not wish, then, having thrown off these empty fantasies, to bid
farewell to the custom itself, saying to empty glory: false dreams, farewell,
you were nothing then?
For what do you think, O men, Tychon Hermes and that of Andocides and the
Uninitiated? Or is it clear to everyone that they are stones, just as Hermes
himself too. And as the halo is not a god and as the rainbow is not a god, but
passions of air and of clouds, and in the way day is not a god, nor indeed
even a year nor time completed from these, so neither sun nor moon, by which
each of the things said before is marked off. Who, then, being well-minded,
would suppose audit and punishment and justice and nemesis to be gods? For not
even an erinys nor fates nor destiny, since neither a constitution nor glory
nor wealth are gods, which painters also show as blind; and if you deify shame
and eros and Aphrodite, let shame and impulse and beauty and intercourse
follow them. No longer, then, would sleep and death reasonably be thought twin
gods among you, these being passions that come about naturally about the
animals; nor indeed would you justly say ker nor destiny nor fates goddesses.
And if strife and fight are not gods, neither Ares nor Enyo. And still if the
lightnings and the thunderbolts and the rains are not gods, how are fire and
water gods? And how also the shooting ones and the comets, having come about
through a passion of air? And let the one who says fortune a god say also
action a god. If, then, not even one of these is thought to be a god, nor
indeed of those handmade and insensible formations, and a certain providence
about us of a divine power appears, nothing else remains than to confess this,
that truly only the God who truly only exists is and subsists. But you are
like men who have drunk mandragora or some other drug, you mindless ones, and
may God grant you sometime to sober from this sleep and to understand God, and
not to imagine gold or stone or tree or action or passion or disease or fear
as God. /For thrice ten thousand are/ truly /upon the much-nourishing earth
daemons/ not /immortal/ nor indeed mortal (for they have not shared in sense,
so as also in death), but stone and wooden masters of men, insulting and
breaking faith with life through the custom. /For the earth is the Lord's,/ he
says, /and the fullness of it/; then why do you dare, luxuriating in the
things of the Lord, to be ignorant of the master? Leave my earth, the Lord
will say to you, do not touch the water which I give forth, do not take a
share of the fruits which I farm; give back, O man, the nurture-wages to God;
know your master; you are an own formation of God; and how would what is his
own justly become alien? For what has been alienated, being deprived of the
belonging, is deprived of the truth. Or do you not, in a Niobe manner somehow,
or rather, so that I may utter more mystically toward you, in the manner of
the Hebrew woman (the ancients called her Lot) turn into insensibility? We
have received this woman as turned to stone because of loving Sodom; and
Sodomites are the godless and those who turn toward impiety, hard-hearted and
silly. Think these voices to be said to you from God; for do not think stones
to be sacred and woods and birds and snakes, but not men; and much the
contrary, suppose the men truly sacred, and the beasts and the stones what
they are. For the wretched of men and miserable think God cries through a
raven and a jackdaw, but is silent through a man, and they have honoured the
raven as a messenger of God, but they pursue the man of God, not cawing, not
clucking, but speaking; woe, they attempt inhumanly to slaughter the one who
catechizes rationally and humanely, calling upon the justice, neither awaiting
the grace from above nor turning aside from the punishment. For they do not
believe God nor learn his power thoroughly. And of whom the love of mankind is
unspeakable, of this one the hatred of wickedness is uncontainable. And the
wrath feeds the punishment upon sin, and the love of mankind does well upon
repentance. And most pitiable is to be deprived of the help from God. The
maiming of eyes, then, and the deafening of hearing are more painful beside
the remaining greeds of the wicked; for the one of them has taken away the
heavenly appearance, and the other has deprived of the divine learning. And
you, maimed toward the truth and blind in the mind, deaf in the understanding,
do not feel pain, do not resent, you have not desired to see the heaven and
the maker of the heaven, nor have you sought to hear and learn the craftsman
and Father of all, joining the choice to the salvation; for nothing stands in
the way of the one hastening toward knowledge of God, not lack of education,
not poverty, not lack of glory, not lack of glory, not lack of property; nor
does anyone pray, /having wasted with bronze/, to exchange the wisdom that
truly is true, nor with iron; for this has been said well more than all: the
kind is everywhere saving; for the emulator of the just, as a lover of the one
without need, is of few needs, having treasured the blessed in no other than
in himself and in God, where not moth, not robber, not pirate, but the eternal
giver of the good things. You have reasonably, then, been likened to those
snakes whose ears have been shut toward those who charm. /For wrath is to
them,/ the writing says, /according to the likeness of the snake, as of a deaf
asp and stopping its ears, which will not hear the voice of charmers./ But you
at least be charmed of the wildness and receive the tame and our Word and spit
out the destructive venom, so that as much as possible it may be given you to
strip the corruption, as to those the old age. Hear me and do not stop the
ears nor fence the hearings, but put the things said into mind. Beautiful is
the drug of immortality; stop sometime the creeping trails. /For the enemies
of the Lord shall lick dust,/ the writing says; it says lift from the earth
into ether, look up into heaven, wonder, cease watching for the heel of the
just and hindering /the way of the truth/; become understanding and harmless;
perhaps somewhere the Lord will grant you a wing of simplicity (he has chosen
to wing the earth-born), so that leaving the holes you may dwell the heavens.
Only let us repent from a whole heart, so as to be able with a whole heart to
contain God. /Hope upon him,/ he says, /all assembly of people, pour out
before him all your hearts./ To those empty of wickedness he speaks; he pities
and fills of justice; believe, O man, a man and God; believe, O man, the one
who suffered and is worshipped. Believe, you slaves, the living God, the dead
one; all men believe only the God of all men; believe and receive a wage,
salvation; /seek out God, and your soul shall live./ The one who seeks out God
busies himself about his own salvation; you have found God, you have life. Let
us seek, then, so that we may also live. The wage of the finding is life from
God. /Let all who seek you rejoice and be glad upon you, and let them say
through all, let God be magnified./ A beautiful hymn of God is an immortal
man, being built in justice, in whom the oracles of the truth have been
engraved. For where else than in a temperate soul must justice be written?
Where love? And where shame? And where gentleness? These divine writings, I
think, having sealed them in the soul, one must consider wisdom a beautiful
starting-point for those who have turned to any part of life, and consider the
same wisdom an unstormy harbour of salvation; through which good fathers of
children are those who have run to the Father, and good sons to parents those
who have understood the Son, and good husbands of wives those who have
remembered the bridegroom, and good masters of household servants those who
have been ransomed from the last slavery. O more blessed than the error among
men the beasts; they graze the ignorance, as you, but they do not play the
part of the truth; there is not among them races of flatterers, fishes are not
superstitious, birds do not idolatrize, they are struck by one only, the
heaven, since they cannot understand God, having been thought unworthy of the
Word. Then are you not ashamed, having made yourselves more irrational than
the irrational, who through so many ages have been worn out in atheism? You
have become children, then youths, then ephebes, then men, but good never.
Even if you respect old age, having come upon the settings of life be
temperate, even if at the end of life you know God, so that the end of life
may take up for you a beginning of salvation. Grow old toward superstition,
arrive young toward piety; God will include harmless children. Let the
Athenian, then, follow the laws of Solon and the Argive those of Phoroneus and
the Spartiate those of Lycurgus, but if you register yourself of God, heaven
is your fatherland, and God a lawgiver. And what also are the laws? /You shall
not murder, you shall not commit adultery, you shall not corrupt children, you
shall not steal, you shall not bear false witness, you shall love the Lord
your God./ And there are also the fillings-up of these, worded laws and holy
words written in the hearts themselves: /you shall love your neighbour as
yourself,/ and /to the one striking you on the jaw provide also the other,/
and /you shall not desire, for by desire alone you have committed adultery./
How much better, at any rate, for men than to obtain the desires, not even to
wish to desire the beginning of what one must not. But you do not endure to
bear the austerity of salvation, and just as we are pleased with the sweet of
foods because of the smoothness of the pleasure, preferring them, but the
bitter things heal us and make us healthy, roughening the sense, but the
austerity of the drugs strengthens those weak in the stomach, so the custom
pleases and tickles, but the one pushes into the chasm, the custom, and the
other leads up into heaven, the truth, /rough/ at first, /but a good nurse of
youths/; and this women's quarters is solemn, and the council of elders
temperate; nor is it hard of approach nor impossible to take, but it is
nearest, a household-dweller of us, as the all-wise Moses says hinting,
dwelling in three parts according to us, /hands and mouth and heart./ This is
a genuine symbol of the truth being completed with the three in all, counsel
and deed and word; for do not fear this, lest the many and delightful things
appearing take wisdom from you; you yourself willingly will step over the
nonsense of the custom, just as also the children, having become men, have
thrown away the playthings. With unsurpassable speed, then, and approachable
goodwill the divine power, having shone upon the earth, has filled the all
with a seed of salvation. For the Lord would not have accomplished so great a
work in so little time without divine care, despised in sight, worshipped in
deed, the purifying and saving and mild, the divine Word, the most manifest
God truly, who has been equalled to the master of the whole, because he was
his Son and /the Word was in God/, neither when he was first preached
beforehand, disbelieved, nor when, having taken up the mask of the man and
having been formed in flesh, he played the saving drama of humanity, unknown;
for he was a genuine contestant and a fellow-contestant of the formation, and
most quickly having been given through to all men, more swiftly than sun
having risen from the paternal will itself, most easily he shone God for us,
from where he himself was and who he was, having presented through what he
taught and showed, the truce-bearer and reconciler and our Saviour Word, a
life-making spring, peaceful, poured upon all the face of the earth, through
whom, so to speak, all things have already become a sea of good things.

And a little, if you wish, look from above at the divine beneficence. The
first one once played in paradise unbound, since he was a child of God; but
when, falling under pleasure (a snake is allegorized, pleasure creeping upon a
belly, an earthly vice, being nourished into woods) he was led aside by
desires, the child becoming a man by disobedience and having refused to hear
the Father was ashamed of God. What a power pleasure had; the man unbound
through simplicity was found bound with sins. The Lord wished again to loose
this one of the bonds, and having been bound in flesh (this is a divine
mystery) he mastered the snake and enslaved the tyrant, death, and, most
paradoxical, that man who had gone astray by pleasure, the one bound with
corruption, he showed unbound with hands spread. O of a mystic wonder; the
Lord has been laid down, and man has been raised, and the one who fell from
paradise receives a greater prize of obedience, heavens. Wherefore it seems to
me, since the Word himself has come to us from heaven, that we must no longer
go upon human teaching, busying ourselves about Athens and the rest of Greece,
and Ionia besides. For if the teacher who has filled all things with holy
powers, crafting, salvation, beneficence, lawgiving, prophecy, teaching, is
for us, now the teacher catechizes all and the all has already become Athens
and Greece for the Word. For you did not believe a poetic myth writing Minos
the Cretan as a familiar of Zeus, and will you disbelieve us who have become
disciples of God, having taken up the wisdom that truly is true, which only
the peaks of philosophy hinted, but the disciples of Christ have both grasped
and proclaimed. And indeed also all, so to speak, the Christ is not divided;
he is neither barbarian nor Jew nor Greek, not male, not female; but a new man
remoulded by a holy spirit of God. Then the other counsels and suggestions are
painful and about the things in part, whether one must marry, whether one must
be a citizen, whether one must beget children; but a catholic exhortation
only, and toward the whole life clearly, in every time, in every circumstance,
stretching toward the most lordly end, the life, is piety; according to which
also only it is necessary to live, so that we may live always; and philosophy,
as the elders say, is a long-timed counsel, wooing an eternal love of wisdom;
/and a commandment of the Lord is far-shining, illuminating eyes./ Receive the
Christ, receive the seeing, receive your light, so that you may well know both
God and man. /Longed for/ is the Word who has illuminated us /above gold and
precious stone; sweetlonged for — sweet Mayor (see Psalm xviii. 11 Sept.).
sweet — longed for MSS. is he above honey and honeycomb./ For how is he not
longed for, who has made the mind buried in darkness clear and has sharpened
the /light-bearing eyes/ of the soul? For as /if there were no sun, because of
the other stars all would be night,/ so if we had not known the Word and been
illuminated by this, we would have fallen short of nothing of the fattened
birds, being fattened in darkness and nourished with death. Let us contain the
light, so that we may contain God; let us contain the light and become
disciples to the Lord. This, you see, he has also promised to the Father: /I
will recount your name to my brothers; in the midst of a church I will hymn
you./ Hymn and recount to me your Father, God; your recountings will save, the
song will train me. How until now I wandered seeking God, but since you lead
me with light, Lord, and I find God through you and I receive the Father from
you, I become your fellow-heir, since you were not ashamed of the brother. Let
us take away, then, let us take away the forgetfulness of the truth; having
brought down the ignorance and the darkness in the way as a mist of sight, let
us behold the God who truly is, having first hymned this voice to him /hail,
light/; a light from heaven has shone for us who have been buried in darkness
and shut in a shadow of death, cleaner than sun, sweeter than the life here.
That light is eternal life, and as many as have taken a share of it, live, and
the night reveres the light and, setting through the fear, yields to the day
of the Lord; all things have become an unsleeping light and the setting has
stood around into a rising. This the new creation has wished; for the /sun of
justice/ who rides over all things goes around humanity equally, imitating the
Father, who /raises his sun upon all men,/ and sprinkles the dew of the truth.
This one has transferred the setting into a rising and has recrossed the death
into life, and having snatched the man from destruction he has hung him toward
ether, transplanting the corruption into incorruption and changing earth into
heavens, the farmer of God, /signalling with the right, and raising peoples
upon a good work, reminding of a true life/, and granting us the truly great
and divine and inalienable lot of the Father, making the man a god with
heavenly teaching, /giving laws into their understanding and writing them upon
heart./ What laws does he underwrite? /That all shall know God from small unto
great, and I will be gracious,/ God says, /to them and of their sins I will
not remember./ Let us receive the laws of the life, let us obey God exhorting,
let us learn him, so that he may be gracious, let us give back even to the one
not needing a thankful wage of well-being, as some rent, the piety to God of
the dwelling here. Golden for bronze, a hundred-oxen for nine-oxen. For a
little faith he gives you so great an earth to farm, water to drink and other
to sail, air to breathe, fire to serve, a world to dwell; from here he has
granted you to send a colony into heavens; these great and so many crafted
things and gifts he has rented you for a little faith. Then those who have
believed the sorcerers accept the amulets and the charms as supposedly saving,
and you do not wish to hang around you the heavenly himself, the Saviour Word,
and having believed the charm of God to be released from passions, which are
diseases of soul, and to be torn from sin? For sin is an eternal death. Or
completely toothless and blind like the moles do you live eating nothing else
in darkness, falling around with the corruption. But there is, there is the
truth that has cried /from darkness light shall shine./ Let the light, then,
shine in the hidden of the man, in the heart, and let the rays of the
knowledge rise, showing forth and flashing the man hidden within, the disciple
of the light, the acquaintance of Christ and fellow-heir, especially whenever
the most honoured and most reverend name of a good Father for a pious and good
child arrives into knowledge, commanding gentle things and urging the child
the saving things. And the one who obeys him has the advantage in all things;
he follows God, he obeys the Father, he knew him going astray, he loved God,
he loved the neighbour, he fulfilled the commandment, he seeks the prize, he
asks the promise. And it always lies before God to save the herd of men. In
this way the good God also sent the good shepherd; and the Word, having spread
the truth, showed men the height of salvation, so that either having repented
they may be saved or not having obeyed they may be judged. This is the
preaching of the justice, a gospel to those who obey, a tribunal to those who
refuse to hear. But a trumpet that has sounded great-noised gathered soldiers
and announced war, and shall Christ, having blown a peaceful melody upon the
ends of the earth, not gather his own peaceful soldiers? He has gathered,
then, O man, the soldiery that is bloodless with blood and word, and he put in
their hands the kingdom of the heavens. A trumpet of Christ is his gospel; he
trumpeted, and we heard. Let us arm peacefully, /having put on the breastplate
of justice/ and having taken up the shield of faith and having put around the
helmet of salvation and let us sharpen /the sword of the spirit, which is a
word of God/. Thus the apostle arrays us peacefully; these are our unwounded
arms; having armed with these let us draw up against the wicked; let us quench
the fired darts of the wicked with the watery points dipped by the Word,
exchanging the well-doings with thankful blessings and honouring God with the
divine Word. /For while you are still speaking he will say,/ he says, /behold
I am present./ O of this holy and blessed power, through which God is a
fellow-citizen with men. Better, then, and more excellent to become at once an
imitator and a tender of the best substance of the things that are; for no one
will be able to imitate God except through those things by which he tends
holily, nor again to tend and revere except imitating. The heavenly and truly
divine eros, at any rate, comes to men in this way, whenever in the soul
itself somewhere the truly beautiful, being rekindled by the divine Word, is
able to flash; and the greatest, together with the genuine wishing the being
saved runs together, the choice and the life being yoked together, so to
speak. Therefore this exhortation of the truth alone has been likened to the
most faithful of friends, remaining until the last breath and becoming a good
escort with the whole and complete spirit of the soul for those who depart
into heaven. What, then, do I exhort you? I am urgent that you be saved. This
Christ wishes; in one word he grants you life. And who is this? Learn
concisely: a word of truth, a word of incorruption, the one who regenerates
the man, bringing him up into truth, the goad of salvation, the one who drives
out the corruption, the one who pursues the death, the one who has built a
temple among men, so that he may establish God among men. Purify the temple,
and leave the pleasures and the sloths like an ephemeral flower to wind and
fire, and farm the fruits of temperance understandingly, and set yourself up
as a first-fruits to God, so that you may be not only a work, but also a grace
of God; and both befit the acquaintance of Christ, both to appear worthy of a
kingdom and to have been thought worthy of a kingdom.

Let us flee, then, the custom, let us flee it as a hard cape or a threat of
Charybdis or mythical Sirens; it strangles the man, it turns away from the
truth, it leads away from the life, it is a snare, it is a chasm, it is a pit,
the custom is a greedy evil; keep the ship outside of that smoke and wave. Let
us flee, O fellow-sailors, let us flee this wave, it belches fire, it is a
wicked island heaped with bones and dead, and on it a seasonal little harlot
sings, pleasure, delighted with a public music: come hither, much-praised
Odysseus, great glory of Achaeans, stop the ship, so that you may hear a more
divine voice. She praises you, O sailor, and calls you much-hymned, and the
harlot appropriates the glory of the Greeks; let her graze the dead, a
heavenly spirit helps you; pass by the pleasure, she cowherds; and let not a
woman with a dressed rump deceive your mind, chattering wily things, seeking
your nest. Sail past the song, it works death; if you only wish, you have
conquered the destruction and, bound to the wood, you shall be loosed of all
the corruption, the Word of God will steer you, and the Holy Spirit will moor
you in the harbours of the heavens; then you will behold my God and you will
be completed in those holy mysteries and you will enjoy the things hidden in
the heavens, those kept for me, /which neither ear heard nor came up upon a
heart/ of anyone.

And indeed I seem to see two suns, and double Thebes, someone said raving to
idols, drunk with unmixed ignorance; and I would pity him playing the drunkard
and would invite the one so beside himself toward a temperate salvation,
because the Lord also welcomes a repentance of a sinner and not a death. Come,
O frantic one, not leaning on a thyrsus, not being bound with ivy, throw the
mitre, throw the fawnskin, be temperate; I will show you the Word and the
mysteries of the Word, recounting according to your image. This is a mountain
beloved by God, not lying under tragedies as Cithaeron, but dedicated to the
dramas of the truth, a sober mountain, shaded with holy woods; and on it rave
not the sisters of Semele /the thunderstruck/, the maenads, who are initiated
into an unholy distribution of flesh, but the daughters of God, the beautiful
lamb-women, prophesying the solemn orgies of the Word, gathering a temperate
chorus. The chorus are the just, the song is a hymn of the king of all; the
girls psalm, angels glorify, prophets speak, a sound of music is sent, they
pursue the thiasos with a run, the called hasten longing to receive a father.
Come to me, O old man, you too, leaving Thebes and throwing off the mantic and
the Bacchic, be led by the hand toward truth; behold I give you the wood to
lean on; hasten, Tiresias, believe; you will see; Christ shines more brightly
than sun, through whom eyes of the blind look up; night will flee you, fire
will fear, death will go; you will see the heavens, O old man, who do not see
Thebes. O of the truly holy mysteries, O of unstained light. I am torch-led
the heavens and to behold God, I become holy being initiated, and the Lord is
hierophant and seals the initiate leading with light, and he deposits with the
Father the one who has believed, kept for ages. These are the Bacchic doings
of my mysteries; if you wish, you too be initiated, and you will dance with
angels about the ungenerated and indestructible and only God who truly is, the
Word of God hymning with us. Eternal is this Jesus, one the great high priest
of one God, the same also Father, he prays on behalf of men and urges men:
/hear, countless tribes,/ or rather as many of men as are rational, both
barbarians and Greeks; I call the whole race of men, of whom I am craftsman by
will of a Father. Come to me, to be arrayed under one God and the one Word of
God, and not only have the advantage of the irrational animals by the word,
but from all the mortal I give you alone to reap immortality. For I wish, I
wish also to give you a share of this grace, furnishing the complete
beneficence, incorruption; and I grant you a word, the knowledge of God, I
grant myself complete. This I am, this God wishes, this is a symphony, this a
harmony of a Father, this a Son, this Christ, this the Word of God, an arm of
the Lord, a power of the whole, the will of the Father. O images of old, but
not all resembling, I wish to set you right toward the archetype, so that you
may also become like me. I will anoint you with the unguent of faith, through
which you throw off the corruption, and I will show the naked shape of
justice, through which you go up toward God. /Come to me all who labour and
are heavy-laden, and I will give you rest; take my yoke upon you and learn
from me, that I am gentle and humble in the heart, and you will find rest for
your souls; for my yoke is kind and my burden is light./ Let us hasten, let us
run, O God-loving and God-like statues of the Word, men; let us hasten, let us
run, let us take up his yoke, let us receive incorruption, let us love Christ
the beautiful charioteer of men; he led the colt under yoke with the old; and
having yoked the pair of men, he directs the chariot into immortality,
hastening toward God to fulfill clearly what he hinted, formerly into
Jerusalem, and now driving into heavens, a most beautiful spectacle to the
Father, an eternal son victory-bearing. Let us become, then, ambitious toward
the beautiful and God-loving men, and let us acquire the greatest of the good
things, God and life. And the Word is a helper; let us take courage in him and
let never so great a desire of silver and gold, nor of glory, come upon us, as
of the Word of the truth itself. For it is not pleasing even to God himself,
if we make the things worth most of little account, and choose rather the
insults of ignorance and mindlessness and sloth and idolatry, conspicuous, and
the last impiety. For not out of the way do the children of philosophers think
all as many as the mindless do, to work unholy things and to be impious, and
still describing the ignorance itself as a form of madness they confess
nothing else than that the many have gone mad. It does not, then, choose to
doubt or to have gone mad; but holding tightly to the truth one must follow
God with all strength, being temperate, and think all things his, as they are,
and besides, having learned that we too, the most beautiful of the
possessions, are his, entrust ourselves to God, loving the Lord God and
considering this a work beside the whole life. And if /the things of friends
are common/, and man is God-loving (for he is also a friend to God, the Word
mediating), all things, then, become the man's, because all things are God's,
and all things are common to both the two friends, of God and of man. It is
time, then, for us to call only the pious Christian both rich and temperate
and well-born and in this way an image of God with likeness, and to say and
believe him to have become /just and holy with understanding/ by Christ Jesus
and unto so much already also like God. The prophet, at any rate, does not
hide the grace, saying, /I said that you are gods and sons of the Highest
all./ For us, us he has adopted and he wishes to have been called Father of us
only, not of those who disobey. For thus somehow our things of the followers
of Christ hold: of what sort the counsels, of such sort also the words, and of
what sort the words, of such sort also the deeds, and of what sort the works,
of such sort the life; kind is the whole life of men who have known Christ.
Enough, I think, of the words, even if I have gone forth longer from love of
mankind, pouring out whatever I had from God, as inviting toward the greatest
of the good things, the salvation; for about the life that has pause nowhere
in no way the words also do not wish ever to cease being hierophants. And this
remains still for you, to choose the profitable end, either judgment or grace;
as I, at any rate, do not think it right even to doubt which of them is
better; nor indeed is it lawful for life to be compared with destruction.
