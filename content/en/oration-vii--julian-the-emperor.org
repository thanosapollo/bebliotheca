* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Many things happen in a long time: this from the comedy it occurred to me the
other day to cry out, when having been invited we were listening to a dog
barking neither piercing nor noble, but singing myths like the nurses, and not
even arranging these soundly. Immediately then it occurred to me, standing up,
to break up the gathering; but since it was necessary, as in a theater when
Heracles and Dionysus are mocked, to hear from the comedians, I endured not
for the speaker's sake but for those assembled, or rather, if one must say
something more spirited, for our own sake and so as not to seem, from
superstition rather than from a pious and reasoned mind, like the doves,
startled by the little words, to have flown up. I stayed, saying this to
myself
/Endure now, heart, and a more dog-like thing you once endured,/ endure also a
dog talking nonsense for a small portion of a day; it is not the first time
you hear the gods blasphemed; we do not manage the public things so well, we
are not so temperate for the sake of our private things, nor indeed are we so
fortunate as to keep our ears clean or at the last at least our eyes unstained
by the manifold impieties of this iron race. But since, as if we were lacking
such evils, the dog filled us with unholy words, naming the best of the gods,
which neither he ought ever to have spoken nor we to have heard, come, let us
try to teach him before you, first that it belongs to the Cynic to write
discourses rather than myths, then of what sort and which the treatments of
the myths must be made, if indeed philosophy also has any need of mythography,
and last I will speak a little concerning reverence toward the gods; for this
has become for me the cause of my coming forward before you, though I am not a
writer and though I have previously declined speaking in a crowd as one of the
burdensome and sophistical things. And a little concerning myth, as a kind of
genealogy, is perhaps not unfitting for me to say and for you to hear.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

The beginning, from where it was discovered and who first attempted to compose
the false persuasively for the benefit or entertainment of the hearers, one
would no more find than if one should attempt to seek out the first who
sneezed or cleared his throat. And if, as cavalry appeared in Thrace and
Thessaly, and archers and the lighter of the arms in India and Crete and
Caria, the practices following I think the nature of the country, so someone
supposes also concerning the other things, in which each is honored, that they
were discovered first and especially by those people: myth seems to have been
from the beginning an invention of the herd-like humans, and from that time
until now the thing remains among them in currency like some other of the
entertainments, flute and lyre, for pleasure and entertainment. For as the
birds to fly and the fish to swim and the deer to run, since they are so by
nature, need nothing of being taught, and if one binds them or shuts them in,
they try nonetheless to use those parts which they know themselves to have by
nature, these animals, so I think also the race of humans, having the soul as
nothing other than reason and knowledge as if imprisoned, which indeed the
wise call a power, turns to learning and seeking and being busy, as toward the
work most proper to itself; and to whomsoever a kindly god quickly loosed the
bonds and brought the power into activity, to this one knowledge is present at
once, but to those still bound, as I think Ixion is said to have lain beside a
cloud instead of the goddess, to these a false opinion has been fused in place
of a true one; for from there come to them these wind-eggs and prodigies, as
it were certain images and shadows of true knowledge; at any rate they
practice the falsehoods before the knowledge of true things and they teach
them very eagerly and they learn them as if, I think, something useful and
wonderful. And if one must altogether make some defense also for those who
first fashioned the myths, they seem to me, for the souls of children, as the
nurses, when they itch about teething, fasten certain leather things to both
their hands, that they may soothe their suffering, so also these men channel
these things to the little soul that is growing wings and longing to know
something more, but not yet able to be taught the truths, as watering a
thirsty field, that they may, I think, soothe their tickling and their pain.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And when such a thing advanced and was in repute among the Greeks, the poets
drew from there the fable, which differs from the myth in that it is made not
for children but for men, and has not entertainment only but also some
exhortation. For it wishes, hiding itself, both to exhort and to teach,
whenever the speaker is cautious of saying the obvious, suspecting hatred from
the hearers. Thus indeed Hesiod too appears to have made it; and after him
Archilochus, putting it about his poetry like a kind of seasoning, used myths
not infrequently, seeing, as is likely, that the subject which he was pursuing
was lacking in such entertainment, and knowing clearly that poetry deprived of
myth is epic only, and has been deprived, as one might say, of itself, for
poetry no longer remains; he plucked these seasonings from the poetic Muse,
and set them out for this very reason, that he might be thought not some
writer of lampoons but a poet.

And the Homer or Thucydides or Plato of myths, or whatever you wish to call
him, was Aesop the Samian, a slave in fortune rather than in choice, yet not
foolish nor, even in this, not a man. For him to whom the law did not grant
frankness, it was fitting to bring in his counsels sketched in shadow and
variegated with pleasure and charm, as I think among physicians the free men
prescribe what is needed, but if someone is at once a household slave in
fortune and a physician in art, he has trouble, being forced both to flatter
and to treat his master.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

If then this slavery belongs also to the Cynic, let him speak, let him write,
let anyone at all yield myth-making to him; but if he says he alone is free,
for what he will use myths I do not know. Is it that mixing the bitter and
biting of the counsel with pleasure and charm he may both benefit and avoid
taking some harm from the one benefited? But this is exceedingly slavish. Or
would one be better taught not hearing the facts themselves nor the names upon
them, according to the comic poet who calls the tub a tub? But instead of
so-and-so, what need is there to name Phaethon? And why stain unholily the
title of King Helios? And who is Pan and who is Zeus among men who walk the
earth worthy to be called so, that from there we may transfer our thoughts
upon them? And yet, even if this were possible, it was better to name the men
themselves. For was it not thus better to speak, putting human names? Or
rather not even putting them, for those which our parents put for us were
enough. But if it is neither easier to learn through the fiction nor fitting
for the Cynic to invent such things, why did we not spare the expensive
outlay, and besides also waste the time inventing and composing little myths,
then writing them out and learning them by heart?

But perhaps the argument says that the false and invented ought not, in place
of the true and uninvented, to be sung by the Cynic, who alone has a share of
freedom, in the public gatherings, yet custom has become so from Diogenes
beginning and Crates down to those after. You will find no such example
anywhere; for I leave aside for now that it in no way belongs to the Cynic who
defaces the currency to attend to custom, but to reason itself alone, and to
find what must be done from within, not to learn it from without. And if
Antisthenes the Socratic, like Xenophon, related some things through myths,
let this not deceive you; for a little later I will speak to you about this;

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

but now tell me that, by the Muses, concerning Cynicism, whether it is a kind
of madness and a life not human, but a bestial disposition of soul that thinks
nothing fine, nothing serious nor good? For Oenomaus would give many to
suppose these things about it. If you had cared at all even to go through
these things, you would have recognized it clearly in the Dog's Own Voice and
in Against the Oracles and in all simply that the man wrote. And the thing
being such that all reverence toward the gods has been destroyed, and all
human prudence dishonored, and not only the law that is synonymous with the
fine and the just trampled, but also those written as it were in our souls by
the gods, by which we all are untaughtly persuaded that there is something
divine and look toward this and I think hasten toward it, disposing our souls
toward it as I think the seeing toward the light, and besides this if the
second law also should be driven out, being sacred by nature and divine, the
one commanding to abstain from others' things wholly and altogether and
permitting these to be confused neither in word nor in deed nor in the very
hidden activities of the soul, which is for us also the leader of the most
perfect justice: is the thing not worthy of the pit? Ought not those who
praise these things, like the scapegoats, not to be driven out beaten with the
sacred wands (for that penalty is lighter than the crimes) but to perish being
stoned? For how do these differ, tell me by the gods, from those who rob in
the wilderness and have occupied the shores to harm those who put in? They
despise death, they say; as if this same madness did not attend those men
also. At any rate he who according to you is a poet and myth-teller, but as
the Pythian declared him to men using pirates, a hero and daemon, says
concerning those who plunder the sea
/As pirates who wander over the sea, staking their lives./ Why then do you
still seek another witness of the pirates' madness? Unless one might even call
those pirates more courageous than such dogs, and these dogs here more
reckless than those pirates. For the one, knowing their life to be so wicked,
put forward the wildernesses not more through fear of death than through
shame, but these walk about in the midst confounding the common laws, not by
introducing a better and purer constitution, but a worse and more disgusting
one.

And the tragedies attributed to Diogenes, being admittedly also writings of
some Cynic, and disputed in this only, whether they are of the teacher,
Diogenes, or of the pupil Philiscus: who going through them would not be
disgusted and think that an excess of unspeakable doing has not been left even
to the courtesans? And meeting those of Oenomaus; for he also wrote tragedies
similar to his own discourses, more unspeakable than the unspeakable and
beyond evils, and I no longer have what I may say about them worthily, even if
I should utter against them the woes of the Magnesians, or the Termerian, or
simply all tragedy with the satyr-play and comedy and mime, so has every
shamefulness, every madness been artistically worked to excess in those by the
man; and if from these someone thinks fit to show us of what sort Cynicism is,
blaspheming the gods, barking at all, as I said at the beginning, let him go,
let him proceed, land before land, wherever he wishes;

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

but if, as the god said to Diogenes, having defaced the currency he should
turn to the counsel spoken by the god before this, Know Yourself, which
Diogenes and Crates appear to have emulated in their deeds, this I would
already say is worth everything to a man wishing both to be a general and to
philosophize. And what did the god say, do we know? That he ordered him to
despise the opinion of the many and to deface not the truth but the currency.
And Know Yourself, in which portion shall we place it? In that of the
currency? Or shall we set this itself as the chief of truth, and as a way of
saying Deface the currency through the utterance Know Yourself? For as he who
has altogether dishonored the conventional things, and has come to truth
itself, will set down even concerning himself not by the conventional but by
the things that truly are, so I think also he who has known himself will know
precisely what he is and not what he is thought. Is then the Pythian not a
true god, and was Diogenes clearly persuaded of this, who having been
persuaded by him was shown, instead of an exile, not greater than the king of
the Persians, but, as fame has handed down, an object of envy to the very man
who overthrew the Persian power and competed with the deeds of Heracles and
was ambitious to surpass Achilles?

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

This Diogenes then, of what sort he was toward the gods and toward men, let
him be known not through the discourses of Oenomaus nor the tragedies of
Philiscus, on which having inscribed the name of Diogenes he often lied
against that divine head, but through the deeds he did.

He went to Olympia for what, by Zeus? To watch the contestants? What then? Was
it not possible also at the Isthmia and the Panathenaea to watch the same men
without trouble? But wishing there to be with the strongest of the Greeks? Did
they not go to the Isthmus? You would then find no other cause than service
toward the god. And if he was not struck with terror at the thunderbolt:
neither was I, by the gods, though I have often experienced many signs from
Zeus, struck with terror. But nevertheless I so shudder at and love and revere
and stand in awe of the gods, and I feel simply all such things toward them as
one would toward good masters, toward teachers, toward fathers, toward
guardians, toward all such things simply, that I almost rose the other day
because of your words. This then, I do not know how it came, perhaps needing
to be silent, was spoken.

And Diogenes, though poor and lacking money, walked to Olympia, but ordered
Alexander to come to him, if Dion is trustworthy to anyone. So he thought it
fitting for himself to go to the temples of the gods, but for the most kingly
of those in his time to come to his company. And what he has written to
Archidamus, are they not kingly exhortations? And Diogenes was god-revering
not only in words but also in deeds. For having chosen to dwell at Athens,
when the divine led him to Corinth, being released by the one who bought him
he no longer thought he must leave the city; for he was persuaded that the
gods cared for him and that he had been sent into Corinth not in vain nor by
some chance, but in a manner by the gods, seeing the city more luxurious than
the Athenians and needing a greater and nobler chastener.

What then? Are there not also of Crates many musical and graceful samples of
his holiness and reverence toward the gods? Hear them then from us, if you
have not had leisure to learn them from those men.
/Glorious children of Memory and Olympian Zeus,
Pierian Muses, hear me as I pray;
Give grass continually to my belly, and give it without
Slavery, which indeed has made life simple.

And make me useful to friends, not sweet.
I do not wish to gather famous money, seeking the wealth of a dung-beetle
And the substance of an ant,
But to have a share of justice and to collect a wealth
Easy to bear, easy to get, honorable toward virtue.
And having obtained these I will propitiate Hermes and the holy Muses,
Not with luxurious expenses, but with holy virtues./
Do you see that he prayed speaking well of the gods, and not, as you do,
blaspheming against them? For how many hecatombs are equal in worth to that
holiness which even the divine Euripides rightly hymned saying /Holiness, lady
of the gods, holiness;/? Or has this escaped you, that all things, both the
great and the small, when brought to the gods with holiness have equal power,
but deprived of holiness not a hecatomb, by the gods, but even the thousand-ox
offering of Olympia is expense only, and nothing else? Which I think Crates
knowing both himself honored the gods through the holiness alone which he had,
with fair speech, and taught the others not to prefer the expenses of
holiness, but holiness itself, in the rites. And these two men, being such
toward the gods, did not gather lecture-rooms nor, like the wise, associate
with their friends through images and myths; for it is said well by Euripides
/The tale of truth is simple by nature;/ for he says the false and unjust
needs shadow-painting. What then was the manner of their company? Deeds led
the words, and those who honored poverty themselves first appear also to have
looked down on their ancestral money, those who embraced freedom from conceit
first practiced cheapness through all things, those who took the tragic and
pompous out of others' lives themselves first dwelt in the marketplaces or the
precincts of the gods, and they warred on luxury even before words through
deeds, refuting by deeds, not shouting in speech, that it is possible to share
the kingship of Zeus needing nothing or very little and not being troubled by
the body, and they rebuked those who erred while the ones who had stumbled
were alive, they did not blaspheme them when dead, when even the more moderate
of enemies make truce with those who have gone. And the true Cynic has no
enemy, even if someone strike his little body, even if he drag about his name,
even if he revile and blaspheme, because enmity comes toward a rival, but what
overpasses rivalry with another is wont to be honored with goodwill; and if
someone is otherwise toward him, as I think many are toward the gods, he is
not an enemy to that one, for he is not harmful, but he himself, laying on
himself the heaviest penalty, ignorance of the better, is left deserted of
that one's protection.

But if it were now set before me to write concerning Cynicism, I would have
said still about these things what occurred to me, perhaps not less than what
has been said; but now rendering what is continuous with the purpose let us
next consider of what sort the myths that are fashioned must be. And perhaps
that inquiry also leads this attempt, to what kind of philosophy mythography
belongs. For many both of the philosophers and of the theologians appear to
have done it, as Orpheus the most ancient who philosophized in a god-inspired
way, and not a few also of those after him; and indeed Xenophon too appears,
and Antisthenes and Plato, to have used myths in many places, so that it has
appeared to us that, even if not to the Cynic, at least to some philosopher
mythography belongs.

A little then must be said beforehand concerning the parts or instruments of
philosophy. For it is no great difference whichever way one numbers the
logical with the practical and the physical; for it appears equally necessary
according to both. And of these three again each is cut into three, the
physical into the theological and that concerning the mathematical studies and
third that concerning the theory of things that come to be and perish and of
things everlasting, yet bodies nonetheless, what being is for them and what
the substance of each; and of the practical, that toward one man is ethical,
economic that concerning one house, political that concerning a city; and
further of the logical, the demonstrative through true things, the forcible
through reputable things, and the paralogistic through things that appear
reputable. There being then so many parts of philosophy, if something has not
escaped me; and it is no wonder that a soldier not over-exact nor pick to the
nail such things, since he utters them not from practice of books but from the
habit that happens to come; you at any rate will be witnesses for me, if you
should count the days, how many they are between this and the hearing that
happened to us lately and of how many occupations they are full; but, as I
said, if something has been left aside by me; and yet I think nothing is
lacking; only he who adds will not be an enemy, but a friend.

:PROPERTIES:

:END:

Of these parts then neither to the logical does mythography belong, nor of the
physical to the mathematical, but only, if at all, of the practical to that
which comes to be toward one, and of the theological to the telestic and
mystic; for nature loves to hide, and the hidden of the substance of the gods
does not bear to be thrown in naked words into impure ears. And what the
secret nature of the characters is by nature able to benefit even when
unknown; at any rate it heals not souls only but also bodies, and makes
presences of gods; this I think often happens also through the myths, whenever
into the ears of the many, who are not able to receive the divine things
purely, it is poured for them through riddles together with the stage-making
of the myths.

And it having already become clear to whom and to what kind of philosophy it
is also at times fitting to write myths; for besides the argument the choice
of the men who went before testifies to these things. Since also many things
have been told in myth by Plato concerning the things in Hades when he is
theologizing, and before him by the son of Calliope, and by Antisthenes and
Xenophon and Plato himself, when they treat certain ethical subjects, not as a
side-work but with a certain carefulness the writing of myths has been mixed
in, which you ought, if you wished, imitating, instead of Heracles to take the
name of Perseus or of some Theseus and stamp the Antisthenean type, and
instead of Prodicus's stage-making about those two gods to bring another like
it into the theater.

And since I have mentioned also the telestic myths, come now let us try to see
by themselves of what sort those fitting each of the parts must be, no longer
needing ancient witnesses in all things, but following the new tracks of a man
whom I after the gods admire and am amazed at equally with Aristotle and
Plato. And this man speaks not concerning all, but concerning the telestic,
which Orpheus who established the most holy rites handed down to us. For the
incongruous in the myths by this very thing makes a way toward the truth. For
the more paradoxical and monstrous the riddle is, so much the more it seems to
protest that one not believe the things said on the spot, but search out the
hidden things and not stand off before, under the gods as leaders, having
become manifest they initiate, or rather perfect, the mind in us and if indeed
something better than mind belongs to us, a small portion of the One itself
and the Good, having the whole impartibly, a fullness of the soul and holding
all of it together in the One and the Good through his transcending and
separate and exempt presence. But these things about the great Dionysus I do
not know how it occurred to me, reveling, to rave; and I put the ox on my
tongue; for one must say nothing about the unspeakable things. But may the
gods give the benefit of those things both to me and to the many of you, as
many as are still uninitiated in these.

But concerning the things which it is lawful both to speak and to hear and
without divine jealousy for both, every discourse that is brought forth is
composed of diction and thought. Therefore since the myth also is a kind of
discourse, it will be composed of these two. And let us consider each of them.
There is a certain simple thought in every discourse, and moreover it is also
advanced according to figure, and the examples of both are many. The one then
is simple and needs no variety, but the figured has many differences in
itself, of which, if you have cared at all for rhetoric, you are not without
understanding. Of these figures according to thought most fit the myth; only
for me it is not now to be spoken concerning the many nor concerning all, but
concerning two, the august according to thought and the incongruous. And these
same things happen also about diction. For it is somehow formed and figured by
those who do not bring it forth at random nor, like a winter torrent, drag
refuse of words from the three-ways; but of these two, whenever we invent
concerning the divine things, the words must be altogether august and the
diction as temperate and beautiful as possible and most fitting to the gods,
and nothing of the shameful and blasphemous or impious, that we may not become
leaders of such boldness for the crowd, or rather even before the crowd
ourselves anticipate having been impious about the gods. Nothing then
incongruous must there be about such dictions, but all things august and
beautiful and magnificent and divine and pure and aimed as far as possible at
the substance of the gods;

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:

but the incongruous according to thought, coming to be for the sake of the
useful, must be admitted, so that humans, not needing some reminder from
without, but taught by the things said in the myth itself, may be eager to
seek the hidden and to be busy about it under the gods as leaders. For look, I
myself have heard many saying that Dionysus is a human, since he was born from
Semele, but a god through theurgy and the telestic art, as the lord Heracles
through kingly virtue was brought up to Olympus by his father Zeus. But, my
good man, I said, you do not understand the myth that is clearly riddling. For
where is the birth, as of Heracles, so also of Dionysus, having the better and
the transcending and the exempt, yet still remaining in the measured of human
nature and somehow being made like to us? And Heracles is said to have been
born a little child and little by little the divine body increased for him,
and he is recorded to have gone to teachers, and he is said to have campaigned
and to have mastered all, yet to have toiled according to the body. And yet
these things belonged to him, but more greatly than according to a human. For
when in the swaddling-clothes he was choking the snakes and arraying himself
against the very elements of nature, heats and frosts, then against the most
resourceless and unfightable, I mean lack of food and solitude, and I think
his journey through the sea itself upon the golden cup, which I believe by the
gods is not a cup, and I have believed that he walked as upon dry land of the
sea. For what was impossible for Heracles? And what did not obey his divine
and most pure body, these so-called elements being slaves to the demiurgic and
perfective power of his unstained and pure mind? Whom great Zeus through
Providence Athena, setting this goddess as guardian over him, putting her
forth whole from his whole self, planted as a savior for the cosmos, then
brought him back through the thunderbolt fire to himself, commanding the child
under the divine token of the ethereal ray to come to him. But concerning
these things may Heracles be gracious both to me and to you.

And the things of the much-talked birth of Dionysus, which is not a birth but
a daemonic manifestation, in what do they resemble the human? His mother, they
say, carrying him, deceived by Hera being jealous, begged her lover to come to
her as he was accustomed to go to his wedded wife; then the little body not
bearing the crashings of Zeus was burned down by the thunderbolt. And all
things being fired together, Zeus commanding Hermes to snatch Dionysus and
cutting his own thigh sewed him in; then from there, when the infant had been
brought to term, Zeus in labor comes to the nymphs; and they, singing Loose
the stitch to the thigh, brought the dithyramb forth into light for us; then
the god, they say, went mad by Hera, and the Mother of the gods stopped the
disease for him, and he was at once a god. At any rate there followed him not
Lichas as Heracles, nor Iolaus nor Telamon nor Hylas nor Abderus, but Satyrs
and Bacchae and Pans and an army of daemons. Do you see how human the sowing
is through the thunderbolts, and the bringing-to-birth more human, and of both
the things said the deeds more like the human? Why then, not throwing down the
nonsense, do we not know that first about these things, that Semele was wise
in the divine things? For she was a child of Cadmus the Phoenician, and to
these the god also bears witness of wisdom, saying /And the Phoenicians
learned many roads of the blessed./

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

She seems to me then to have perceived this god first among the Greeks and,
having foretold his coming manifestation not far off, to have stirred some of
the rites about him sooner than was fitting, not bearing to wait the fated
time, then to have been consumed by the fire that flowed upon her. And when it
had been decided by Zeus to give in common to all humans a beginning of
another constitution and to change them from the nomad life toward the tamer,
Dionysus from the Indians appeared a self-visible daemon, visiting the cities,
leading with himself a great army of certain daemonic beings and giving to
humans in common to all as a token of his manifestation the plant of the
cultivated vine, from which I think, their things about life having been
tamed, the Greeks thought it worthy of this name, and they called Semele his
mother because of the foretelling, and besides also because the god honored
her, as first hierophant of the visitation still to come.

And the history being such, as one examining it accurately would search out,
those seeking which of the gods Dionysus ever is, having the true as I said,
arranged it into a myth, riddling both the substance of the god and the
conception in the intelligibles beside the father and his ungenerated birth in
the cosmos... in the whole, and the other things in order as many as were
worthy of seeking, but not easy for me to speak, perhaps both through still
being ignorant of the precise about them, and perhaps also not wishing to
