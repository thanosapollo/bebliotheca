* Iambs to Seleucus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I bid greeting to the noble and good son Seleucus, a branch of a well-born
root. And I myself pray that I may rejoice in your life, thinking highly of
your studies and of your manners. First, then, have fear and longing for God;
for God becomes for all who think well the beginning and the end of every
life. Second, practice character, child: gentle, humble, self-controlled,
firm, sweet, unenvious, just, manly, wise, grave, industrious, steady, and
temperate. For this is an ornament both to the young and to the old: to be
rich not so much in money as in character. For that is yours; but money plays,
leading astray the wealth-loving disease, being by nature given to smile on
others from others, imitating the manner of a faithless harlot who mocks many
lovers in various ways, being with them and fleeing and clinging to these and
those, but genuinely to none. For the nature of wealth has no stability, being
likened to the furious waves of the sea that swell and fall with unstable
motion. You, then, my boy, being always rich in manners, will have a treasure
not plundered by thefts, not laid open in the midst to sycophants, nor yet
drained out by the hands of tyrants or sacked by the weapons of barbarians,
but remaining within the bodiless houses, kept safely in the treasuries of the
soul, which neither the furious might of fire eats, nor will a wave of the
sea's swell cover.

Guarding this wealth that is truly yours and genuine, polish it with studies,
with the books of poets, the writings of historians, and exercising yourself
in the running eloquence of orators and the subtle cares of philosophers. But
go through all these with good sense, gathering from them wisely what is
useful, and fleeing the harm of each with judgment, imitating the work of the
wise bee, which settles on all flowers, yet harvests from each most wisely
what is useful, having nature itself as teacher. But you, from reasoning,
pluck abundantly of the things that help. And if anything brings harm,
perceiving the base, fly off at once. For the mind of men is swift-winged. So
whatever encomia of virtue they have written, hymning it, and conversely when
they blame vice, these you, having learned with zeal, keep both the sense and
the grace of the diction. But the unseemly myths they wrote about the gods in
broad nonsense, teachings of demons, myths worthy of laughter and of tears:
turn from these as from snares and traps. And having read both, the gods and
the words, gods ridiculous and words lovely, despise the pleasure-loving gods,
but honoring the words, as from one plant, both flee the thorns and pluck the
rose. And of the outside learning this is the best law for you. But to what it
is fitting to give all zeal, we shall say, a little later.

For that I wish to say to you first. The young man must flee utterly the
company of the wicked and the pleasures with them. For many there are, like
cattle full of mange and some disease, who try the simpler young, and by
rubbing against them in the deceit of knavery they wish to fill them with
wickedness as with a disease, so that by the partnership of the greater number
they may overshadow their own evils. Guard against these. For, as it seems to
Paul, evil communications corrupt good manners. Yes, and this you must keep
most strictly: hate the unseemly song of theatres, of beasts, of hippodromes,
the quarrelsome spectacle of evils, the vanities of life, the hydra of
pleasures, the unseemly lessons of wanton men for whom nothing is shameful
except to be temperate. For some of them, servants of shame, have as their art
to be clever in outrages, mimes of the ridiculous, accustomed to blows,
cutting away shame with razors before the hair, a wanton workshop of
indecency, for whom to suffer and to do all that is not lawful in the sight of
all is a part of their art. But others, a tribe more wretched than those,
dancing away the glory of males, breaking nature together with the twistings
of their limbs, men-women, male effeminates. Not men, not women, in true
speech. For the one they do not remain, the other they have not reached. For
what they are, they do not remain by manner; and what they badly wish, they
are not by nature: a riddle of prodigality and a puzzle of passions, men to
women and women to men. And what would one say of the diseases of shameful
songs and the tunes that make womanish the tension of the heart, flutes,
dances of whorish bacchanals, to which the thrice-wretched even assign honor?
Are these then worthy of praises and of spectacle and of delight, or of tears
and of groanings? Laughter plays the tyrant, and nature is adulterated, and a
various flame of pleasures is kindled. And theatres of dishonor are founded,
so that the diseases may not even be unseemly in secret, but that prizes may
be set for the evil lessons. But you, abhor these things, lest you stain your
pupils, fleeing all corruptions of the eyes, so that you may keep the pupils
for me as virgins.

And still more flee the murderous spectacles of the gluttons, whose god is the
belly. For being slaves of the belly, a most shameful disease, they serve its
evil commands. And she, as a bitter mistress of the remaining limbs, sits
within selling limbs to beasts, greedily devouring the prices, a prodigal
belly thrusting kindred limbs tyrannically into the bellies of beasts. Of
these passions sit unfeeling men as spectators. And if a man escape the
beasts, they groan as if they themselves have been more mocked than the beasts
and have sat in vain. But when a man is caught and bellows a tight cry,
shouting bitterly and scraping up dust, all pity has fled from every
spectator's sight, and with pleasure there is the greatest applause, if they
see streams of blood. For they rejoice seeing what they ought to lament, and
they give the scale of favor to the beasts, and they cheer on those who have
seized still more, whetting angers as if filling themselves and chewing men's
flesh together with the beasts. And the evil sellers of their own limbs, both
slaves of food and again food of beasts, hateful while living and wretched
when dead, have had such a bitter end of life. And of the limbs some have been
buried in beasts, some are torn pitilessly under teeth, and others half-rent
dart in various twists, still gasping, and still think there is a time for
flight and imagine a course. Do not then defile your eye with the pollutions
of raw spectacles, nor train it to look on dead men, beasts filled, running
tombs, and kinsmen lying.

And indeed the spectacle of horse-races that seems to many much tamer, this
too is a plague and a disease of souls. It splits cities, brings the people to
factions, teaches fights, whets an abusive mouth, cuts the bonds of citizens,
clashes families, shames the old, maddens the young, kindles enmities of
dearest friends, tramples laws. And a worse evil than these evils it dares. It
stirs up sorcerers as allies to the mad, helpers of victory, and feeds disease
with disease. For when they have been kindled to hot strife, their course is
at once to the sorcerers. And they in turn call the wickedness of demons as a
fellow-worker for themselves of falls, of crushings, of murders. For the army
of demons rejoices in evils. Therefore clearly even the contentious spectacle
of the hippodromes that seemed tame is a destruction of souls, a battle of
bodies, and besides these a clear harm of money. How many houses has it pulled
down at once? How many rich has it forced to beg? And how many cities formerly
well-ordered has it utterly overthrown? For when faction in its prime stained
the people's hands with the murders of rulers, it widowed the cities of men by
the law of the sword; fire and iron divided the cities, punishing slaughters
with slaughters, murders with murders. Who then of the temperate will endure
to watch a contest of sorcery, not the speed of horses, a faction that begets
murder, a disease of cities?

But you, instead of these, rejoice in studies, from which you will practice
the best character. And when you have moderately pre-exercised the mind as in
a palaestra with various writings, then contend in the God-inspired Scriptures
themselves, gathering the great wealth of the two covenants, of the old and of
the new always. For the new is a second written, and after itself it will no
longer have a third. To these gladly give all zeal, from which you will learn
to practice a good manner and to worship the true and only God. For there is a
Monad and a Trinity eternal, Father with Son and All-holy Spirit, a Trinity
distinct in persons, a Monad by nature. Therefore neither confuse the
hypostases by number, nor yet, worshipping God, cut the nature. For in nature
truly the Trinity is not cut. For it is one Trinity, one God Almighty. This is
a subtle pious mystery. For truly the way of truth is narrow, a path with
cliffs on both sides, compressed, from which, having slipped from opposite
sides, they have fallen into a deep-cliffed error: Sabellius looking toward
the Jews, and Arius imitating the idolaters, the one confusing the hypostasis
of the persons, the other dividing the essence impiously. But you,
unswervingly keep the middle way, dividing as one must, and joining as is
lawful. For the Trinity is joined without confusion, just as the Monad is
separated without cutting. For the nature is uncut, and the hypostases always
remain wholly unconfused. May you remain for me a guardian of these dogmas and
a genuine worker of the commandments, and wise in all mystical contemplations,
always advancing, never puffed up. For thus grace will bloom greater. You see
faithful Moses, the man of God, image of the life according to virtue, how
having first learned all the wisdom of the Egyptians and having been reared
even in deep wealth, he became of his own will both a fugitive and poor, and
in the deserts he exchanged the luxury in Egypt for a hired table of
shepherds, preferring a hard life to the glory of tyrants, until, judged
worthy of perfect spectacles, he saw the greatest mystery of an angel
appearing from the flame of fire of a bush. Then, having been deemed worthy
then first also to hear the voice of God, and having received authority, he
looses the slavish yoke for a groaning people and rules the whole race by the
vote of God. Having received so great a height he is not lifted up, but says
he is some thin-voiced and slow-tongued man, saying he is weak, so that
thinking humbly he may remain strong. Keep the image of this life, forming
yourself to the manner of Moses.

And the learning of the Greek discourses, as a judge bringing a lawful vote,
appoint to serve, as is fitting, the boldness of the true dogmas and the
all-wise contemplation of the Scriptures. For it is just that the wisdom of
the Spirit, being from above and having come from God, be mistress of the
lower education, as of a handmaid not puffed up in vain, but accustomed to
serve decently. For let the lower serve the wisdom of God. But that it is most
fitting for you to learn beforehand: not every book is safe that has acquired
the august name of Scripture. For there are, there are at times falsely named
books: some intermediate and neighbors, as one might say, of the words of
truth. And others spurious and very dangerous, like counterfeit and bastard
coins, which have the inscription of a king, but are adulterated, being
cheated in their materials.

For this reason I will tell you each of the God-inspired books; and so that
you may learn clearly, I will first tell those of the Old Covenant. The
Pentateuch has Creation, then Exodus, and Leviticus as the middle book, after
which Numbers, then Deuteronomy. To these add Joshua and the Judges, then Ruth
and four books of Kingdoms, and a pair of Chronicles. Ezra upon them first,
then the second. Next I will tell you five stichic books: of Job crowned with
the contests of various sufferings, and the book of Psalms, a tuneful healing
of souls, and three of Solomon the wise: Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Song
of Songs. To these add the twelve prophets: Hosea first, then Amos the second,
Micah, Joel, Obadiah, and Jonah the type of the three-day passion itself,
Nahum after them, Habakkuk, then ninth Zephaniah, and Haggai and Zechariah and
the two-named messenger Malachi. After whom learn the four prophets: the great
Isaiah who speaks boldly, and Jeremiah the compassionate, and mystical
Ezekiel, and last Daniel, the same most wise in deeds and in words. To these
some also admit Esther.

It is time for me to tell the books of the New Covenant. Receive only four
evangelists: Matthew, then Mark, to whom adding Luke as third, number John
fourth in time, but first in the height of doctrines. For I reasonably call
him a son of thunder, who sounded most greatly with the Word of God. And
receive also Luke's second book, that of the catholic Acts of the Apostles.
Next add the vessel of election, the preacher of the nations, the apostle
Paul, who wisely wrote twice seven letters to the churches: one to the Romans,
to which one must join two to the Corinthians, that to the Galatians and that
to the Ephesians, after which that in Philippi, then that written to the
Colossians, two to the Thessalonians, two to Timothy, to Titus and Philemon
one each, and one to the Hebrews. And some say that to the Hebrews is
spurious, not speaking well; for the grace is genuine. Well then, what
remains? Of the catholic epistles some say seven, and others that only three
should be received: one of James, one of Peter, and one of John's. And some
receive the three and besides them the two of Peter, and that of Jude as
seventh. And the Apocalypse of John again some admit, but the majority say it
is spurious. This would be the most unerring canon of the God-inspired
Scriptures.

If you are persuaded by these, you would escape the traps of the world, and
would cast down the vanity of hopes, and would sow the fleeing wealth with
lavish hand to the poor, remaining a safe harvest. For you will treasure in
the heavens what was sown. And may you follow Christ, the wise Word of God. O
thrice-blessed, O thrice-happy you, having appeared a luminary to many old and
young. For a pious life shines more than stars. And a chorus of prophets,
martyrs, apostles, standing around you as a household member, will crown you,
sending you forth with a victory-clap. And then you will be gladdened,
harvesting unceasing glory, dancing in the midst of angels, crown-bearing.
Farewell, and remember what has been written. Greet Olympias your nurse, the
living image of gravity, of purity, of ascesis, and the seal of faith. If you
wish, Seleucus, to learn the number of the iambs, know three hundreds, as many
tens, and three units. For I always pray, child, that you be a friend of the
Trinity.
