To pray together with the city, men of Athens, both these things and many
others is for me the pleasantest of all, and for you I know it is ready at
hand for all. But I, both when you first were considering these Sicilian
matters, thought there was need of good counsel, and now still of more; for on
one and the same day we are deliberating both about an addition of empire and
about our own safety. And the speeches which you would most gladly hear I am
not ignorant of, but the affairs have come to such a point that not even if
one wished is it possible to pass by the need. For it is far better that I
myself, if it should so happen, give offense to you while persuading and
teaching, than to look on while we fall upon ourselves. Such then is my
preparation and my judgment, and I claim to be allowed to speak with
frankness. For if it were necessary that as soon as you heard you should do
something against your judgment, perhaps it would be well to be on guard; but
since it is in your power, having considered, to choose of the things said
whichever you wish, it is not right to flee the choice, but having set both
side by side to become of the better. And apart from these things, if it were
possible to bring the affairs along by the pleasures of speeches, it would be
a misfortune not to choose to harangue in this manner; but since the affairs
do not turn out according to whatever figure the speeches take, but according
as those things happen to come to an end, so the speeches spoken at the
beginning about them have seemed to stand, one must receive those spoken from
goodwill and not for a small part of a day throw away the whole future. For
where even in the affairs themselves it is not of the well-minded to give away
the greater for the lesser, surely a temporary grace of speech is not worth
the later safety of the whole. First then I shall answer the last of the
things said; then I shall try also to make the examination of the rest, as
well as I am able. For I say that both judge rightly, men of Athens, both
those who from the beginning having this

opinion that one must not sail to Sicily hold to the same arguments, and those
who being of one vote with the many then now bring the other. For the former
from the beginning calculated what was needed, and the latter show in deed
that they have foresight. For when it was possible to give way to hopes, they
were with you, but when trial finds the truth, they set themselves toward what
appears. And it was, I think, of the contentious to wish to abide by what they
chose from the beginning, but of those who have sense and wish the city to be
saved to go with the affairs. For it is not the decrees that set right the
actions in arms, but according as the affairs of the wars stand, such must the
decrees about them become.

These then say nothing else than that since we were led on at the beginning,
for this reason even now we must go on erring, making the evil a remedy of the
evil. But I, from what I find hearing together with you, think on the contrary
that it would have been better for us if from the beginning you had voted
these things down. And indeed it is best for all men to grasp the future by
calculation; for it is a token of the two fairest things, both good fortune
and deliberating well; but if for those who miss the best it will not be
possible to change to the second from the present, that which is the greatest
of human things will be taken away, being taught by the affairs, so that the
misfortune must be endless. And indeed if someone should ask you by what
greatest thing a law differs from being a decree, you would find this, that
the laws having found the just once for all set it as common for all time, but
the decrees follow the needs; and in the same way in which the actions meet
now one way now another upon the occasions, so the occasions both make and
dissolve the decrees and the opinions; reasonably. For in that manner we
converse with the citizens, so that it is enough to utter the same throughout,
but these have been invented toward those outside and toward the fortunes of
war. Nothing of them then is firm or unmoved, nor of such a kind that one must
never dissolve it. But indeed where even the

Come, by the gods, if someone straight from the beginning had been able to set
before you all the future exactly, and this would have been, as it seems, some
one of the gods; for of men at least no one was such as to hold out against
those who then kindled you, that the affairs would come round to this so that
for us the speech about those who have gone would arise sooner than about
Sicily, is there anyone so foolish or unfortunate to whom it already occurred
that these things arrive, so as to throw the city around with so great an
expense and so many dangers? I at least would not be persuaded. Therefore the
things which, having foreknown, you would not have sent, these, having come to
know them later, you would send for the men in season, especially as not in
like circumstances, men of Athens, for the whole affairs and occasions did we
have the opinion then and shall we change the present, if you are persuaded by
me. For then even with truces existing toward the Lacedaemonians and having
won the war at home brilliantly, being in a desert of enemies, we reasonably
lived in luxury, but now you see the enemies from the wall, I suppose, and
instead of that troublesome and heavy thing, the invading into the country
each year, they have settled continuously at the gates, and there has come
round, if one must not talk nonsense, an exact siege; and of money for us some
has been spent out on the fine dispatch, and some we used up in the earlier
occasions; and slaves desert every day, and we have become fewer by as many in
all as the greatest city after us had been sufficiently equipped with. Let us
consider then whether we shall seem either to know ourselves, or to tend the
city well, if being ourselves besieged we shall attempt to do this to others
overseas, and before going out to have

And the things here are of this kind; and those there of what sort? No better

the need of necessities greater and having the dangers sharper. Ships
waterlogged, sailors in disorder, money flowing away, of the merchants most

yield nothing, unless they shall take in addition a penalty worthy of the
plot. Then we, men of Athens, shall wait for these things, and the affairs not

voted, as if this will suffice, if we do not cease playing the fool, or that
the Syracusans will carry through the contest against us according to our

one of all we have been forced to use toward them, but as if seeking an

kindled, neglecting to quench it should bring together wood and rubbish and
matter, as if by this they would check the fire?

Do not you at least, men of Athens, suffer anything more stupid, nor deceive
yourselves, like those who shut their eyes, so that they may not see what they
do not wish. But having seen where the affairs are going forward, while there
is still occasion and you stand as masters of the counsel, check them and do
not wish of two roads to see the one against you and to be ignorant of the one
for you. For not even now have the affairs been so disposed by lack of bodies
and of the rest of the preparation, that we should blame ourselves as having
put our hands to them too ignobly, but for the splendor of the expedition and
surplus everything has been done, but we have stumbled in fortune and in
having let go the greatest of the advantages, and this was the occasion, and
in not setting upon Syracuse at the height of the fear; for these things let
one accuse both Nicias and whoever one wishes; but the occasions at least have
been let go, and it is not possible

For the harbor will be shut at once and we shall all but sit in the gulfs of
the enemy. And what will they bid us do then, these now easy ones?

Or send again instead of a provisioning to the Syracusans of what they will
have been set as masters? By Zeus but they will fear this second attack here.
Rather they will take heart knowing that they mastered the former also. For
they would think they already have testimony from us ourselves that we have
despaired of the affairs being any longer in easy resource. For not as taking
heart, but as fearing, I suppose, we shall seem to have sent those now.

So. Each then by itself privately has so great a fear, both that of taking
away from the force here and that of campaigning there with any number
whatsoever. And how are these contrary to one another? From the things here
being in a bad way, heart comes to the enemy there, that if they hold out a
little they will not be in danger further, and again from faring anything
against judgment there it is for all here to share the danger. For not to say
if our expedition is worsted, or if those surpass in the whole, but if only a
false report and opinion should arrive, that we have stumbled, everything is
thrown back and has changed, and of those now listening to you by necessities
there is no one who will not at once make his own through the Lacedaemonians.
And if we shall be deprived, men of Athens, of the allies and of the revenues,
what is left for you? I at least would not say, but it is seen by all. Then
they remind us that straight from the beginning it seemed good not to fight
the Peloponnesians about the country, but to war with expeditions and
circumnavigations. But that this same man Pericles who persuading these things
was also persuading that other, not to acquire empire during the war, this
they do not wish to remember, though it is most of all reasonable. For I, men
of Athens, take it that our city so far excels all cities that it is able not
only to conquer them each by each, but also some of them by nations, yet not
ever to become stronger than human nature, nor is it reasonable to claim these
things. For example I say you would conquer the Peloponnesians alone against
them alone and against them all together, and again you would take Sicily not

Siceliots themselves. And I say the same things also about the affairs in

Peloponnesians and with those in Sicily and with Greeks and barbarians, and

outside, and that one must acquire those through these rather than attempt
those there for the sake of these in an untimely season.

And that one must not trust that argument, that the greater part of the
Syracusan force is on our side, hear. No one, men of Athens, of those in
Sicily, neither Greeks I mean nor barbarians, hates the Syracusans equally and
is well-disposed to us, but how they seem to me to be disposed I shall say.
Those men, so long as no one had arrived from us into Sicily, were displeased
at the Syracusans, as many of our allies are at us. For no one willingly, I
think, wishes to be a slave. But so great a force having appeared, and another
again being sent by us and the intention being conspicuous, they no longer
hate those more than they fear us, so that we become reconcilers for them. For
they will choose far more again their own and like, even if they must overlook
them being a little inferior, rather than the alien and newcomers. These are
what I fear and besides these, men of Athens, the false speech of the
Lacedaemonians, by which we have been slandered, as having surpassed all in
ruling by force. And most strange of all, to yield Attica itself for the sake

expedition into Egypt, which this man just now wrapped round well, which took
away two hundred of our triremes, and this when we already held a portion of
Egypt not less than all Sicily; and we did not even stop here, but time having
gone other fifty went as aid to those there, and they benefited those in
nothing and themselves perished in addition as an addition. This, this example
I shudder at, men of Athens, and neither night nor day takes away the memory
of it; and I ask all of you to guard against Sicily as against Egypt and not
to make many such examples, calculating if then someone saying these things
had persuaded, most of all from the beginning not to sail to Egypt, and if
not, at least not to send the second triremes, that not anything whatsoever
would have happened to the city as an affair, or at least the second would not
have perished in addition. But indeed if you all know that those things too
were erred by the opinion of counselors and by those who hindered having less,
see that not now too you yourselves may pay the penalty of our defeat. And
consider that not even from the many and great victories in Cyprus once did it
result for us to acquire Cyprus itself, but having let it go we withdrew. And
that the place too where the camp is is pestilential you all perhaps learn.
