* On the Praises of Saint Paul the Apostle

Of our father among the saints John, archbishop of Constantinople, the
Chrysostom, an encomium on the holy apostle Paul, Homily I.

1.1 No one would err in calling Paul's soul a meadow of virtues and a
spiritual paradise, so greatly did it bloom with grace, and so worthy of the
grace did it display the philosophy of the soul. For since he became a chosen
vessel, and had cleansed himself well, the gift of the Spirit was poured out
into him abundantly. Whence he also brought forth for us the wondrous rivers,
not four only according to the fountain of paradise, but far more, flowing
each day, not watering the earth, but rousing the souls of men to the
fruitbearing of virtue. What speech then will suffice for his achievements? Or
what tongue will be able to attain to his praises? For when one soul has
gathered and holds all the goods that are among men, and all with excess, and
not only those of men but also those of the angels, how shall we master the
magnitude of the praises? Yet we will not be silent on this account, but even
for this very reason we will speak most of all. For this too is the greatest
form of praise, that the magnitude of the achievements should, with all
surplus, defeat the ease of speech, and the defeat is more splendid for us
than ten thousand trophies.

1.2 From where then would it be timely to take hold of the praises? From where
else than from this very first point, to show that he has the goods of all?
For whether prophets displayed something noble, or patriarchs, or righteous
men, or apostles, or martyrs, he has gathered all these together with such
excess as none of those had, whatever good each possessed.

1.3 Consider: Abel offered a sacrifice, and from this he is proclaimed. But if
you bring Paul's sacrifice into the midst, it is shown better than that by as
much as heaven is better than earth. Which one then do you want me to name?
For it is not one only. For he was sacrificing himself each day, and by this
again he made the offering double: on the one hand dying each day, on the
other carrying about the dying in his body. For he was continually arrayed
against dangers, and was slain in his choice, and he so mortified the nature
of the flesh that he was disposed no less than the victims that are
slaughtered, but even far more. For he was not offering oxen and sheep, but he
was slaying himself twice each day. Therefore he also dared to say: For I am
already being poured out, calling his own blood a drink offering.

1.4 Yet he was not content with these sacrifices, but when he had consecrated
himself well, he also offered the whole inhabited world, both land and sea,
both Greece and barbarian land, and simply all the earth that the sun looks
upon; this, as if he had become some winged thing, he went through entirely,
not simply journeying, but tearing up the thorns of sins, and sowing the word
of piety, driving out error, bringing back the truth, making angels out of
men, or rather making the men angels from demons. Therefore, when he was about
to depart after the many sweats and these frequent trophies, consoling the
disciples, he said: If I am also poured out upon the sacrifice and service of
your faith, I rejoice and I rejoice with you all; therefore you also


rejoice and rejoice with me. What then could be equal to this sacrifice, which
he sacrificed after drawing the sword of the Spirit, which he brought upon the
altar that is above the heavens? But Abel was taken off, murdered by Cain, and
by this became more splendid. But I have numbered for you ten thousand deaths,
and as many as the days this blessed man lived while preaching. And if you
also wish to learn the slaughter that went as far as the trial itself, that
one fell by a brother who had neither been wronged nor been benefited, but
this one was taken off by those whom he was hastening to snatch from ten
thousand evils, and for whose sake he suffered all that he suffered.

1.5 But Noah was righteous, perfect in his generation, and alone such among
all? But Paul too was alone such among all. And that one saved only himself
with his children; but this one, when a far harsher flood had seized the
inhabited world, not fastening planks and making an ark, but composing the
epistles in place of planks, snatched from the midst of the waves not two and
three and five kinsmen, but the whole inhabited world that was about to be
drowned. For the ark was not of such a kind as to be carried about in one
place, but it reached the limits of the inhabited world, and from that time he
brings all into this chest until now. For having constructed it commensurate
with the multitude of those being saved, receiving men more senseless than the
irrational, he makes them rivals of the powers above, and in this he conquers
that ark. For that one, taking a raven, sent out a raven again, and receiving
a wolf, did not change the beastliness; but this one not so, but taking
wolves, he made them sheep, and taking hawks and jackdaws, he rendered them
doves, and casting out all irrationality and beastliness from the nature of
men, he brought in the gentleness of the Spirit, and until now this ark
remains sailing, and is not dissolved. For the storm of wickedness could not
loosen its planks, but sailing over it rather put down the surge of the storm;
and very reasonably; for these planks are anointed not with asphalt and pitch,
but with the Holy Spirit.

1.6 But all marvel at Abraham, because having heard: Go out from your land and
from your kindred, he left fatherland, and house, and friends and kinsmen, and
the command of God was everything to him. For we too marvel at this. But what
could be equal to Paul? Who left not fatherland and house and kinsmen, but the
world itself for Jesus, or rather heaven itself, and he overlooked the heaven
of heaven, and sought one thing only, the love of Jesus. And hear him making
this clear and saying: Neither things present, nor things to come, nor height,
nor depth will be able to separate us from the love of God. But that one,
casting himself into dangers, snatched his nephew from the barbarians? But
this one snatched not the nephew, nor three and five cities, but the whole
inhabited world, not from barbarians, but from the very hand of the demons,
undergoing ten thousand dangers each day, and by his own deaths acquiring much
safety for others. But there the chief of the goods is, and the crown of
philosophy, the sacrificing of the son? But here too we shall find the first
prizes belonging to Paul; for he sacrificed not a son, but himself ten
thousand times, as I said before.

1.7 What would one marvel at in Isaac? Many other things, but most of all his
forbearance, because digging wells and seeing himself driven from his own, he
did not go out against them, but even seeing them being filled in he endured,
and was always moving to another place, not always going to close quarters
with those who were grieving him, but standing aside and yielding everywhere
of his


own possessions, until he had sated their unjust desire. But Paul, seeing not
wells being filled in with stones, but his own body, did not merely yield as
that one did, but going in he was eager to lead up to heaven those who were
casting him with stones; for by as much as the spring was being filled in, by
so much more did it burst forth, and it poured out more rivers into endurance.

1.8 But Scripture marvels at this man's child for his endurance? And what
adamantine soul could display the endurance of Paul? For he did not serve
twice seven years, but his whole life for the bride of Christ, not only being
burned with the heat of the day and the frost of the night, but undergoing ten
thousand snowstorms of trials, now receiving scourges, now having his body
pelted with stones, and now fighting with beasts, now boxing with the sea, and
with continual hunger by day and night, and with cold, and everywhere leaping
beyond the pits, and snatching the sheep from the throat of the devil.

1.9 But Joseph was chaste? But I fear it may be laughter to praise Paul from
this, who crucified himself to the world, and saw not only the splendid things
in bodies, but all affairs thus, as we see dust and ash, and as a corpse would
be unmoved toward a corpse. Thus, putting to rest the leapings of nature with
exactness, he never suffered anything toward any human passion.

=1.10= All men are struck with amazement at Job? And very reasonably; for he
was a great athlete, and able to be set beside Paul himself, for his
endurance, for the purity of his life, for the testimony of God, for that
stout fight, for the wondrous victory after the fight. But Paul was not
spending many months contending thus, but many years, not melting clods of
earth from ichor and sitting on a dunghill, but continually falling into the
very noetic mouth of the lion, and wrestling with ten thousand trials, he was
firmer than any rock; not being reproached by three friends or four, but by
all the unbelieving false brethren, being spat upon, being reviled.

=1.11= But Job's hospitality was great, and his care for those in need? We too
will not contradict; but we shall find it so much more lacking than Paul's as
body stands apart from soul. For what that one displayed toward those maimed
in the flesh, this one practiced toward those mutilated in the soul, setting
right all who were lame and crippled in reasoning, and clothing those who were
naked and unseemly with the robe of philosophy; and in the bodily things too
he so surpassed him, by as much as it is far greater to help those in need
while living with poverty and hunger than to do this from abundance; and the
house of the one was open to everyone who came, but the soul of the other was
spread out to the whole inhabited world, and he received whole peoples.
Therefore he also said: You are not straitened in us, but you are straitened
in your own bowels. And the one, having sheep and oxen without number, was
ambitious toward those in need; but this one, possessing nothing more than the
body, from this very thing supplied those in need, and he cries saying: These
hands served my needs and those who were with me, having acquired from the
body's labor an income for the hungry and the famished.

=1.12= But the worms and the wounds furnished Job with harsh and unendurable
pains? I too confess it; but if you set against them Paul's scourges in so
many years, and the continual hunger, and the nakedness, and the chains, and
the prison, and


the dangers, and the plots, those from his own, those from strangers, those
from tyrants, those from the whole inhabited world, and with these the things
more bitter than these, I mean the pains for those who fall, the care of all
the churches, the burning which he endured for each of those who were caused
to stumble, you will see how the soul that bore these things was firmer than
rock, and conquered iron and adamant. What then that one suffered in the body,
this one suffered in the soul, and the despondency over each of those caused
to stumble gnawed his soul more harshly than any worm. Whence he also sent
forth continual fountains of tears, not only by day but also by night, and he
was torn more sharply than any woman in labor over each of them. Therefore he
also said: My little children, of whom I am again in travail.

=1.13= Whom would one be struck with amazement at after Job? Moses, surely.
But this one too he overshot with much to spare; for his other things too are
great, but the chief and the crown of that holy soul is that he chose to be
blotted out of the book of God for the salvation of the Jews. But that one
chose to perish together with others; but Paul chose not to perish together,
but, others being saved, himself to fall from the unbounded glory. And the one
boxed with Pharaoh, the other with the devil each day; and the one toiled for
one nation, the other for the whole inhabited world, not with sweat, but also
being flowed about from every side with blood instead of sweat, setting right
not only the inhabited world but also the uninhabited, not only Greece but
also the barbarian land.

=1.14= It was possible also to bring Jesus into the midst, and Samuel and the
other prophets; but lest we make the speech longer, let us go to their chiefs;
for when he appears better than these, no dispute is left about the others.
Who then are the chiefs? And who else after these, but David, and Elijah, and
John? of whom the one was forerunner of the former coming of the Lord, the
other of the second; therefore they also shared the name with one another.
What then is the exceptional thing of David? Humility and the love toward God.
And who more, and who not less, than Paul's soul accomplished both of these?
And what is the wondrous thing of Elijah? Is it that he shut the heaven, and
brought on famine, and brought down fire? I do not think so; but that he was
jealous for the Master, and was more vehement than fire. But if you should see
Paul's zeal, you will see it prevailing by as much as that one surpassed the
other prophets. For what could be equal to those words which, being jealous
for the glory of the Master, he spoke, that I could wish to be anathema for my
brethren, my kinsmen according to the flesh? For this reason, the heavens
being set before him, and the crowns and the prizes, he delayed and was slow,
saying: To remain in the flesh is more necessary for your sake. For this
reason he thought not even the visible creation itself, nor even the noetic,
sufficient for the display of the love and the zeal, but he sought even
another that does not exist, so as to show what he wished and desired. But
John ate locusts and wild honey? But this one spent his time in the midst of
the inhabited world as that one in the wilderness, not feeding on locusts and
wild honey, but setting a table much cheaper than this, and not even abounding
in necessary food because of his eagerness for the preaching. But this one
displayed much freedom of speech toward Herod? But this one stopped not one
and two and three, but ten thousand of that kind, or rather even much harsher
than that tyrant.

=1.15= It remains to examine him against the angels next. Therefore leaving
the earth, let us go up to the vaults of the heavens; but let no one condemn
boldness


of the speech. For if Scripture also called John an angel and the priests,
what wonder if we compare the one better than all with those powers? What then
is the great thing of those? That they obey God with all exactness; which
David also, struck with amazement, said: Mighty in strength, doing his word.
For nothing is equal to this, even if they are bodiless ten thousand times;
for what most of all makes them blessed is this, that they are persuaded by
the commands, that they nowhere disobey. This then can be seen kept by Paul
also with exactness; for he did not only do his word, but also the commands
and beyond the commands, and making this clear he said: What then is my
reward, that preaching the gospel I may set the gospel of Christ without
charge? What else did the prophet say marveling at them? Who makes his angels
spirits, he says, and his ministers a flame of fire. But this too can be seen
in Paul; for like spirit and fire he ran through the whole inhabited world,
and cleansed the earth. But he has not yet obtained heaven? For this is the
wondrous thing, that he was such on the earth, and wearing a mortal body, he
rivaled the bodiless powers.

=1.16= Of how great condemnation then would we not be worthy, when one man has
gathered all goods to himself, and we do not even strive to imitate the
smallest part of him? Thinking these things then, both let us free ourselves
from the accusation, and let us strive to come to that zeal, that we may be
able also to obtain the same goods, by the grace and love of mankind of our
Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the might now and always and unto
the ages of ages. Amen.

2.t Of the same, an encomium on the holy apostle Paul, Homily II.

2.1 What a man is at all, and how great the nobility of our nature is, and of
how much virtue this living being is capable, Paul showed most of all men; and
now he stands, from the time he came to be, with a splendid voice apologizing
to all who accuse our constitution, on behalf of the Master, and exhorting
toward virtue, and stopping the shameless mouths of the blasphemers, and
showing that the interval between angels and men is not great, if we are
willing to attend to ourselves. For not having obtained another nature, nor
having shared another soul, nor having dwelt in another world, but having been
reared in the same earth and country and laws and customs, he overshot all men
who have come to be from the time men came to be. Where then are those who say
that virtue is hard, and vice easy? For this man answers them, saying: The
momentary lightness of the affliction works out, according to excess unto
excess, an eternal weight of glory. And if such afflictions are light, much
more the pleasures from home.

2.2 And this is not only the wondrous thing in him, that by surplus of
eagerness he did not even perceive the toils on behalf of virtue, but that he
did not even pursue it for a wage. For we do not even, when wages are set
before us, endure the sweats on its behalf; but that one both without the
prizes embraced and loved it, and leaped over the things that seemed to be its
hindrances with all ease; and he blamed neither weakness of body, nor
circumstance of affairs, nor tyranny of nature, nor anything else. And yet,
having been entrusted with a care greater than that of all generals and kings
upon the earth, nevertheless he was in his prime each day. And as the dangers
were strained upon him, he possessed a fresh eagerness, and making this clear
he said: Forgetting the things behind


, and stretching forward to the things before; and when death was expected, he
called them into a sharing of this pleasure, saying: Rejoice and rejoice with
me; and when dangers and insults and all dishonor were lying upon him, he
leaped again, and writing to the Corinthians he said: Therefore I also take
pleasure in weaknesses, in insults, in persecutions;

2.3 And he also called these very things weapons of righteousness, showing
that from this too he harvested the greatest things, and was unconquerable to
the enemies from every side; and everywhere being scourged, insulted, reviled,
as if parading in triumphs, and setting up the trophies continually everywhere
on earth, thus he adorned himself, and he confessed thanks to God, saying: But
thanks be to God who always leads us in triumph. And he pursued the
unseemliness and the insult for the sake of the preaching more than we pursue
honor, and death more than we life, and poverty more than we wealth, and the
toils more than others the rests, and not simply more, but much more, and
being grieved more than others rejoice, praying for the enemies more than
others pray against them. And he reversed the order of affairs, or rather we
reversed it, but he, as God legislated, thus kept it. For these things are all
according to nature, but those the contrary. What proof of these things? Paul,
being a man, and running after these more than those.

2.4 One thing only was fearful to him and to be fled, to strike against God,
and nothing else; just as neither was anything else desirable, as to please
God; and I speak not of nothing among things present, but not even among
things to come. For do not tell me of cities and nations and kings and camps
and weapons and money and satrapies and powers; for he did not even think
these to be a spider's web; but set the very things in the heavens, and then
you will see his vehement love toward Christ. For he, toward that charm, did
not marvel at the dignity of angels, nor of archangels, nor anything else of
that kind. For he had in himself the greater than all, the love of Christ;
with this he thought himself more blessed than all. And without this he did
not even pray to become of the lordships and the principalities and the
authorities, but with this love he wished rather to be among the last and
among those being punished, than without it among the highest and those being
honored.

2.5 For one punishment for him was to fail of this love. This was Gehenna to
him, this punishment, this ten thousand evils, just as also enjoyment was to
obtain it; this life, this world, this angel, this things present, this things
to come, this kingdom, this promise, this the ten thousand goods. And he
thought nothing else of the things that do not bring one here to be either
grievous or sweet; but he so despised all things seen, as a plant that is
rotting away. And tyrants, and peoples breathing wrath, seemed to him to be
gnats; and death and punishments and ten thousand torments, children's
playthings; except if ever he endured them for Christ. For then he also
embraced these, and he so adorned himself with the chain, as Nero did not even
with the diadem on his head; and he dwelt in the prison as in heaven itself,
and he received wounds and scourges more sweetly than those who snatch the
prizes; and he loved the toils no less than the prizes, thinking the toils to
be a prize; therefore he also called them grace.

2.6 Consider. The prize was to depart and be with Christ, but to remain in the
flesh, this contest; but nevertheless he chooses this rather than that, and
says it is more necessary for him; to become anathema from Christ was a
contest and toil, or rather even beyond contest and toil; to be with him, a
prize. But he chooses this rather than that for Christ. But perhaps someone
might say that all these things were sweet


to him because of Christ. For this I too say, that the things which are causes
of despondency to us, these begot great pleasure for him. And why do I speak
of dangers and the other hardships? For he was even in continual despondency;
therefore he also said: Who is weak, and I am not weak? who is caused to
stumble, and I do not burn? Except if someone might say that even the
despondency has pleasure. For many even of those who have lost children, when
they are allowed to mourn, receive consolation; but when they are forbidden,
they are in pain; just so Paul also, weeping night and day, received
consolation; for no one so mourned his own evils as that one the evils of
others. For how do you think he was disposed when the Jews were not being
saved, praying, that they might be saved, to fall from the glory above? Whence
it is clear that their not being saved was much harsher. For if it were not
harsher, he would not have prayed for that; for he chose it as lighter, and as
having more consolation; and he did not simply wish it, but he also cried
saying: That I have grief, and pain in my heart. 2.7 Him then who each day, so
to speak, suffered for those who inhabit the

2.7 inhabited world, and for all in common, both nations, and cities, and for
each one, to whom could one be able to compare him? to what iron? to what
adamant? What would one call that soul? golden, or adamantine? for it was
firmer than any adamant, and more precious than gold and precious stones; and
it will outrun the tension of that material, and the costliness of this. To
what then could one compare it? To none of the things that are. But if gold
should become adamant, and adamant gold, then somehow it will obtain their
image. But what need have I to compare it to adamant and gold? Set the whole
world against it, and then you will see Paul's soul outweighing it. For if he
says this about those who were distinguished in sheepskins and caves and in a
small part of the inhabited world, much more would we say it about him, that
he was worth all. If then the world is not worthy of him, who is worthy?
perhaps heaven? But this too is small. For if he himself preferred the love of
the Master to heaven with those in the heavens, much more will the Master, who
is as much better than him as goodness than wickedness, prefer him to ten
thousand heavens. For he does not love us similarly, as we love him, but so
much more, as it is not even possible to set out in speech.

2.8 Consider then of how great things he counted him worthy even before the
coming resurrection. He snatched him into paradise, he led him up into a third
heaven, he made him a sharer of such unspeakable things as it is not lawful
for any of those who have obtained human nature to speak. And very reasonably;
for walking on earth, he did all things as if going about with angels; and
being bound with a mortal body, he displayed their purity, and being subject
to so many necessities, he was eager to appear no less than the powers above.
For he ran through the inhabited world as if winged, and as if bodiless he
overlooked toils and dangers, and as if already having obtained heaven, he
despised the things on earth, and as if turning about with the bodiless powers
themselves, thus he was continually awake. And yet angels were often entrusted
with various nations; but not even one of them so managed the nation which was
entrusted to him, as Paul the whole inhabited world. And do not tell me that
Paul was not the one managing these things; for I too confess it. For even if
he was not himself the one accomplishing these things, nevertheless he was not
even so outside the praises for them, since he had so prepared himself as
worthy of so great a grace. Michael was entrusted with the nation of the Jews.
But Paul with land and sea, and the inhabited world, and the uninhabited.

2.9 And I say these things not insulting angels, far be it, but showing that
it is possible for one who is a man to be with them, and to stand near them.
And for what reason


were angels not entrusted with these things? That you may have no apology when
you are idle, nor take refuge in the difference of nature while you sleep; and
otherwise the wonder also became greater. For how is it not wondrous and
paradoxical, a word leaping out from a tongue of clay, to put death to flight,
to loose sins, to set right a maimed nature, and to work the earth into
heaven? For this I am struck with amazement at the power of God, for these
things I marvel at Paul's eagerness, that he received so great a grace, that
he prepared himself such.

=2.10= And I exhort you not only to marvel, but also to imitate this archetype
of virtue; for thus we shall be able to share the same crowns with him. And if
you marvel hearing that, having accomplished the same things, you will obtain
the same, hear him saying these things: I have fought the good fight, I have
finished the course, I have kept the faith; henceforth there is laid up for me
the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will give
back to me in that day; and not only to me, but also to all who have loved his
appearing. Do you see how he calls all into the same sharing? Since then the
same things are set before all, let us all strive to become worthy of the
goods that have been promised; and let us see not only the magnitude and the
bulk of the achievements, but also the tension of the eagerness, through which
he drew so great a grace, and the kinship of nature; for he shared all the
same things with us. And thus even the things very hard to accomplish will
appear easy to us and light, and having toiled this brief time, we shall
continue wearing that unaging and immortal crown, by the grace and love of
mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the might now and
always and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

3.t Of the same, an encomium on the holy apostle Paul, Homily III.

3.1 The blessed Paul, showing the strength of human eagerness, and that we are
able to fly to heaven itself, leaving angels and archangels and the other
powers, sometimes through himself alone commands them to become imitators of
Christ, saying: Become imitators of me, as I also of Christ; and sometimes
even without himself he leads them up to God himself, saying: Become then
imitators of God, as beloved children. Then, showing that nothing so makes
this imitation as living for the common benefit and looking toward what is
useful for the whole, he added: Walk in love. For this reason, having said:
Become imitators of me, he immediately discourses about love, showing that
this virtue most of all makes one near to God; since the others at least are
more lacking than this, and all turn about men; such as the fight against
desire, the war about the belly, the array against love of money, the
wrestling against wrath; but to love, this is common to us and to God. For
this reason Christ also said: Pray for those who despitefully use you, that
you may become like your Father who is in the heavens.

3.2 This then Paul also, knowing it to be the chief of the goods, displayed
with much exactness. No one then so loved enemies, no one so benefited those
who plotted, no one suffered so many things on behalf of those who had grieved
him; for he did not even look to the things he suffered, but he considered the
common of nature, and by as much more they were made beasts, by so much more
he pitied their madness. And as a father would be disposed toward a child held
by frenzy (for by as much as the sick one is insulted and kicks harshly, by so
much more he pities him and weeps), so that one too, from the excess of the
demons


of those who were bringing these things upon him, conjecturing the disease,
was stirred toward more care.

3.3 Hear then how mildly, how compassionately he discourses with us on their
behalf, of those who scourged him five times, who stoned him, who bound him,
who thirsted for his blood, and who desired to tear him apart each day. For I
bear them witness, he says, that they have a zeal of God, but not according to
knowledge. And again, checking those who trampled upon them, he said: Do not
be highminded, but fear; for if God did not spare the branches according to
nature, lest somehow he spare not even you. For since he saw a master's
sentence gone out against them, what he was master of, this he did; he
continually wept for them, he was in pain, he hindered those who wished to
leap upon them, and from what was possible he was eager to find at least a
shadow of pardon for them. And since he could not persuade by speech because
of their unyielding and hard quality, he turned to continual prayers, saying:
Brethren, my good pleasure and my petition toward God is on their behalf unto
salvation. And he also extends good hopes to them, saying: The gifts and the
calling of God are without repentance, so as not to despair completely and
perish; all of which was of one who cared and was vehemently burning for them,
as when he says that: The deliverer will come from Zion, and he will turn
ungodlinesses from Jacob. For he was vehemently cut and bitten seeing them
perishing. Therefore he invented many consolations of this pain for himself,
sometimes saying: The deliverer will come, and he will turn ungodlinesses from
Jacob, and sometimes: So these also disobeyed your mercy that they too may be
shown mercy.

3.4 And Jeremiah also does this, forcing and being eager to find some apology
on behalf of those who have sinned, now saying: If our sins have stood against
us, do it for your own sake, and now again: The way of a man is not his, nor
will a man go and set right his journey; and elsewhere again: Remember that we
are dust. For it is a custom of those who plead for those who have sinned,
even if they have nothing reasonable to say, at least to invent some shadow of
apology, not exact ones, nor able to be drawn into doctrines, but nevertheless
consoling those who are in pain for the perishing. Let not us then examine
such apologies exactly, but considering that they are of a soul in pain,
seeking to say something on behalf of those who have sinned, thus let us
receive the things that have been said.

3.5 Was he then such toward Jews only, and not toward those outside? He was
milder than all both toward his own, and toward strangers. Hear then what he
says to Timothy: And a servant of the Lord must not fight, but be gentle
toward all, apt to teach, forbearing, in meekness educating those who oppose
themselves, if perhaps God may give them repentance unto knowledge of truth,
and they may recover from the snare of the devil, having been taken alive by
him unto his will. Do you want to see him also, how he discourses toward those
who have sinned? Hear what he says writing to the Corinthians: And I fear lest
somehow coming, I find you not such as I wish; and after a little: Lest when I
come again my God humble me toward you, and I mourn many of those who have
sinned before, and have not repented of the wantonness and uncleanness which
they practiced. And writing to the Galatians he said: My little children, of
whom I am again in travail, until Christ be formed in you. And on behalf of
the one who had committed fornication hear him, how no less than that one he
both is in pain and exhorts, saying: Confirm love toward him. And when he cut
him off, he did this with many tears. For out of much affliction, he says, and
anguish of heart I wrote


to you, not that you might be grieved, but that you might know the love which
I have more abundantly toward you; and again: I became to the Jews as a Jew,
to those under law as under law, to the weak as weak, I have become all things
to all, that by all means I may save some; and elsewhere again: That I may
present every man perfect in Christ Jesus.

3.6 Did you see a soul going beyond all the earth? He expected to present
every man, and as far as his part at least, he presented all. For as if he
himself had begotten the whole inhabited world, thus he was in tumult, thus he
ran, thus he was eager to lead all into the kingdom, healing, exhorting,
promising, praying, beseeching, frightening the demons, driving out those who
corrupt, through presence, through letters, through words, through deeds,
through disciples, through himself, setting up those who fall, establishing
those who stand, rousing those who lie on the ground, healing the crushed,
anointing the idle, shouting fearful things upon the enemies, looking sharply
upon the foes; as some excellent general, himself baggage-carrier, himself
shield-bearer, himself champion, himself supporter, himself becoming all
things to the army.

3.7 And not in the spiritual things only, but also in the fleshly he displayed
much providence, much eagerness. Hear then how on behalf of one woman he
writes to a whole people, saying: And I commend to you Phoebe, our sister,
being a deacon of the church in Cenchreae, that you receive her in the Lord
worthily of the saints, and stand by her in whatever matter she may need of
you; and again: You know the house of Stephanas; that you also be subject to
such; and again: Acknowledge such. For this too is proper to the affection of
the saints, to help even in these things. Thus Elisha also, the woman who
received him, not only benefited in the spiritual things, but was also eager
to repay in the fleshly; therefore he also said: Is there some word for you
toward the king, or toward the ruler?

3.8 And why do you marvel, if Paul furnished the recommendation from letters,
when even calling some to himself, he did not think this unworthy, to take
thought also about their travel money, and to set it down in a letter? For
writing to Titus he says: Send forward Zenas the lawyer and Apollos with
eagerness, that nothing may be lacking to them. And if, when committing them,
he sent them out so eagerly, much more, if anywhere he saw them in danger, he
would have done all. See then also writing to Philemon, how much eagerness he
makes because of Onesimus, and how understandingly, how caringly he writes.
And he who on behalf of one household slave, and this one having become a
runaway, and having stolen many of the master's things, did not decline to
compose a whole letter, consider how great he was about the others. For he
thought one thing only worthy of shame, to overlook, when something ought to
be done toward salvation. For this reason he moved all things, and he
hesitated at nothing to spend on behalf of those being saved, not words, not
money, not body; for he who gave himself over to deaths ten thousand times,
much more did not spare money, if at least it was present. And why do I say,
if at least it was present? For even when it was not present it is possible to
show that he did not spare. And do not think the saying to be a riddle, but
hear him again writing to the Corinthians: Most gladly I will spend, and be
spent out, on behalf of your souls. And addressing the Ephesians he said: You
yourselves know that these hands served my needs and those who were with me.

3.9 And being great, in the chief of the goods, in love, he was more vehement
than any flame; and as iron falling into fire becomes wholly fire, so also


he himself, kindled by the fire of love, became wholly love; and as being a
common father of the whole inhabited world, thus he imitated those who had
begotten them; or rather he even overshot all fathers, both for the sake of
bodily and of spiritual cares, giving over money, and words, and body, and
soul, and all things on behalf of those beloved. For this reason he also
called it fulfillment of law, and bond of perfection, and mother of all the
goods, and beginning and end of virtue; therefore he also said: But the end of
the charge is love from a pure heart, and a good conscience; and again: For,
you shall not commit adultery, you shall not murder, and if there is any other
commandment, it is summed up in this word, in the: You shall love your
neighbor as yourself.

=3.10= Since then love is beginning and end and all the goods, let us also be
jealous of Paul in this; for from this he became such. For do not tell me of
the dead whom he raised, nor of the lepers whom he cleansed; God will seek
none of these from you. Acquire the love of Paul, and you will have the crown
completed. Who says these things? The very nurse of love himself, this one who
also set it before signs and wonders and ten thousand other things. For since
he had accomplished it vehemently, for this reason he also knows its strength
with exactness. From this he himself also became such, and nothing so made him
worthy as the power of love; therefore he also said: Be jealous of the greater
gifts; and I show you a still more exceeding way, speaking of love, the
fairest and easy way. This then let us also walk continually, that we may both
see Paul, or rather Paul's Master, and obtain the undefiled crowns, by the
grace and love of mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and
the might now and always and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

4.t Of the same, an encomium on the holy apostle Paul, Homily IV.

4.1 The blessed Paul, who gathered us today and enlightened the inhabited
world, this man at the time of the calling was once blinded; but that maiming
of his became enlightenment of the inhabited world. For since he saw badly,
God maimed him well, so that he might look up usefully, at once furnishing him
a proof of his own power, and at once prefiguring in the passion the things to
come, and teaching the manner of the preaching, and that one must, having cast
off all these things from home, and having shut even the eyes, follow him
everywhere. Therefore he himself also cried, making this very thing clear: If
anyone thinks he is wise among you, let him become a fool, that he may become
wise; as it is not possible to look up well, unless one has first been maimed
well, and has cast out the reasonings that are his own and that trouble him,
and has entrusted the whole to faith.

4.2 But let no one hearing these things think this calling to be forced; for
he was able again to return, whence he went out. Many then, having seen other
greater wonders, turned back again, both in the New and in the Old; such as
Judas, Nebuchadnezzar, Elymas the magician, Simon, Ananias and Sapphira, the
whole people of the Jews; but not Paul. But having looked through toward the
undefiled light he strained the course, and flew toward heaven. And if you
examine for what reason he was maimed, hear him saying: For you heard my
former conduct in Judaism, that according to excess I persecuted the church
and laid it waste, and I advanced in Judaism beyond many contemporaries in my
race, being more abundantly a zealot of my ancestral traditions. Since then he
was so vehement and unapproachable,


he needed a more vehement bridle, lest being led by the rush of eagerness he
should also disobey the things being said. For this very reason, putting down
that madness of his, he first calms the waves of that violent wrath through
the maiming, and then discourses with him, showing the unapproachable quality
of his wisdom, and the superiority of the knowledge, and that he may learn
whom he is warring against, whom he is able to bear not only when punishing,
but not even when benefiting. For darkness did not maim him, but excess of
light darkened him.

4.3 And why then did this not happen from the beginning, he says? Do not seek
this, nor be curious, but yield to the incomprehensible of the providence of
God the fitness of the time. For he himself also does this, saying: But when
he who set me apart from my mother's womb was well pleased, and called me
through his grace to reveal his Son in me. Do you then also be no more
curious, when Paul says this. For then, then it was expedient, the scandals
having been taken from the midst. Next from this let us learn, that no one in
any way, neither of those before him, nor he himself, found him from home, but
Christ manifested himself. Therefore he also said: You did not choose me, but
I chose you. Since for what reason, seeing dead men being raised by his name,
did he not believe? For seeing a lame man walking, and demons running away,
and paralytics being braced, he harvested nothing; for he was not ignorant of
these things, who so busied himself about the apostles. And when Stephen was
being stoned, being present and seeing his face as the face of an angel, he
gained nothing from this. How then did he gain nothing from this? Because he
had not yet been called.

4.4 But you, hearing these things, do not think the calling to be constrained;
for God does not constrain, but he leaves them masters of choices even after
the calling. For he also manifested himself to Jews even when it was needful,
but they did not wish to receive him because of the glory from men. And if
some one of the unbelievers should say, whence is it clear that he called Paul
from heaven, and he was persuaded? for why did he not call me also? we will
say that to him: do you believe this, tell me at all, O man? Then if you
believe, it is sufficient for you as a sign. For if you disbelieve that he
called from heaven, how do you say, why did he not call me also? but if you
believe that he called, this is sufficient for you as a sign. Believe then;
for he calls you also from heaven, if you have a well-disposed soul; since if
you are ill-disposed and twisted, not even a voice being brought to you from
above will suffice for salvation.

4.5 How many times then did the Jews hear a voice being brought from heaven,
and they did not believe? how many signs did they see, both in the New and in
the Old, and they did not become better? But in the Old, these after ten
thousand wonders made a calf; but the harlot of Jericho, having beheld none of
these, displayed a wondrous faith about the spies. And in the land of the
promise these, while the signs were happening, remained more unfeeling than
stones; but the Ninevites, having beheld Jonah only, believed and repented,
and beat off the God-sent wrath. And in the New, at the very presence of
Christ, the robber, seeing him being crucified, worshiped; but these, having
beheld him raising dead men, both bound him and crucified him.

4.6 And what in our time? Did not fire, leaping out from the temple, from the
foundations in Jerusalem, spring upon those who were building, and thus stand
them off from the unlawful attempt then? but nevertheless they were not
changed, nor did they put away the hardening. And how many other wonders
happened after that then, and they gained nothing from this? such as the
thunderbolt brought down against the roof of the temple of Apollo; the oracle
of this very demon, who forced the one then reigning to move the martyr lying
nearby,


saying he was not able to speak, so long as he sees that one's chest nearby;
for it was lying from neighboring. After this again, this man's uncle, having
insulted the sacred vessels, became worm-eaten and expired; and the steward of
the royal monies, through another lawlessness done by him against the church,
having burst in the middle perished. Again the springs among us, which conquer
rivers by their stream, fled all at once and leapt away, never having suffered
this before, but when the king defiled the place with sacrifices and drink
offerings. What would one say of the famine that fell together with the king
upon the cities everywhere of the inhabited world, the death of the king
himself, that in the country of the Persians, the deceit before the death, the
army caught in the midst of the barbarians, as in some seine and nets, the
going up from there, the wondrous and paradoxical? For when the impious king
fell pitiably, and another pious one succeeded, all the terrible things were
immediately loosed; and the soldiers caught in the midst of nets and having no
way out from anywhere, God then having nodded, having been delivered from the
barbarians returned with all safety. Which of these things is not sufficient
to draw toward piety?

4.7 And are the present things not much more wondrous than these? is not a
cross preached, and the inhabited world runs toward it? is not a death
announced that is reproachful, and all leap upon it? for were not ten thousand
crucified? for were not two robbers impaled with Christ himself? for were
there not many wise? for were there not many powerful? whose name ever so
prevailed? and why do I speak of wise and powerful? for were there not famous
kings? who so overcame the inhabited world in a short time? For do not tell me
of the various and manifold heresies; for all preach the same Christ, even if
not all soundly, all worship that one crucified in Palestine, the one under
Pontius Pilate. Do these things then not seem to have a clearer proof of his
power than that voice brought down from heaven? For why did no king so
overcome as this one prevailed, and this when there were ten thousand
hindrances? for kings warred, and tyrants were arrayed, and all peoples rose
up, and nevertheless our things were not lessened, but even became more
splendid. Whence then, tell me, so great a strength?

4.8 He was a magician, he says. Then this one alone became such a magician.
You have surely heard that among Persians and Indians many magicians have come
to be, and they are still even now; but not even their name is anywhere. But
the one from Tyana, he says, that deceiver and sorcerer, he too shone. Where
and when? In a small part of the inhabited world, and for a short time, and he
was quenched quickly and perished, leaving no church, no people, no other such
thing. And why do I speak of magicians and sorcerers who were quenched? whence
did all the things of the gods cease, both the Dodonaean, and the Clarian, and
all these wicked workshops are silent and have been stopped?

4.9 Whence then do demons shudder not at this crucified one only, but also at
the bones of those slaughtered for him? and for what reason, even hearing a
cross, do they leap away? And yet they ought to mock; for is the cross
splendid and notable? On the contrary, it is shameful and reproachful. For it
is a death of a condemned man; it is a death that is the last of evils, and
accursed among Jews, and abominable among Greeks. Whence then did demons fear
it? was it not from the power of the crucified? For if they feared the thing
itself by itself, this too would be most unworthy of gods; except that many
both before him and after him were crucified, and with him also two.


What then, if someone should say, in the name of the robber who was crucified,
or of this one, or of that one, will the demon flee? By no means, but he will
even laugh. But if you add Jesus the Nazarene, they flee as from some fire.
What then would you say? whence did he prevail? that he was a deceiver? But
his commands are not such; and otherwise, many deceivers also have come to be.
But that he was a magician? But the doctrines do not witness this; and there
has often been a great crop of magicians. But that he was wise? But wise men
have often come to be many. Who then so prevailed? No one ever, not even near
in a small way.

=4.10= Whence it is clear that it was not because he was a magician, nor
because he was a deceiver, but because he was a corrector of these, and some
divine and unconquerable power, for this reason he himself also overcame all,
and he breathed into this tentmaker so great a power as the facts themselves
witness. For a man standing in the marketplace, having his craft about hides,
was so strong as to lead back to the truth Romans, and Persians, and Indians,
and Scythians, and Ethiopians, and Sarmatians, and Parthians, and Medes, and
Saracens, and simply the whole race of men, in not even whole thirty years.
Whence then, tell me, did the man of the marketplace, and standing at a
workshop, and handling a knife, both himself philosophize such things, and
persuade the others, both nations, and cities, and countries, not displaying
strength of words, but even the whole contrary, being unlearned with the last
unlearnedness? Hear him then saying, and not being ashamed: If I am even
unskilled in speech, yet not in knowledge. Not possessing money. For this too
he himself says: Until the present hour we both hunger, and thirst, and are
buffeted. And why do I speak of money, when he often did not even abound in
necessary food, nor had a garment to put around him? And that he was not even
splendid from a pursuit, this too his disciple shows, saying that: He remained
with Aquila and Priscilla because of being of the same craft; for they were
tentmakers by craft. Not notable from ancestors; for how, having such a
pursuit? Not from fatherland, not from nation. But nevertheless, having come
into the midst, and having appeared only, he threw all the things of the
opponents into confusion, he poured all together, and as fire falling into
stubble and grass, thus he burned up the things of the demons, and he
transferred all things into what he wished.

=4.11= And this is not only the wondrous thing, that he himself being such was
so strong, but that also the more of those being discipled were poor,
unskilled, uneducated, living with hunger, obscure and from obscure. And these
things he himself also preaches, and is not ashamed, speaking their poverty,
or rather not even begging for them. For I go, he says, to Jerusalem serving
the saints, and again: On the first of sabbaths let each of you set aside by
himself storing up, that when I come, collections may not then be made. And
that the more of them also consisted of unskilled men, writing to the
Corinthians, he says: See your calling, that not many wise according to the
flesh; and that from obscure: Not many, he says, well-born; and not only not
well-born, but even exceedingly cheap. For the weak things of the world, he
says, God chose, and the things that are not, that he may bring to nothing the
things that are. But unskilled and uneducated, yet somehow persuasive to
speak? Not even this. And this he himself again shows, saying: And I came to
you, not according to excellence of speech or of wisdom, announcing to you the
testimony. For I judged to know nothing among you, except Jesus Christ, and
this one crucified; and my speech and my preaching were not in persuasive
words of wisdom.

=4.12= But was what was preached sufficient to draw? But hear also about it
what he says: Since both Jews ask signs, and Greeks seek wisdom, but we preach
Christ crucified, to Jews a stumbling-block, but to Greeks foolishness. But


did he enjoy safety? But he did not even breathe at any time from the dangers.
For I was among you, he says, in weakness, and in fear and in much trembling;
and not he himself only, but the disciples also suffered the same. For you
remember, he says, the former days, in which, having been enlightened, you
endured a great contest of sufferings; this on the one hand, being made a
spectacle with reproaches and afflictions, this on the other, having become
sharers of those so suffering. For you also received the seizure of your
possessions with joy. And again writing to the Thessalonians he says: For you
suffered the same things from your own tribesmen, just as they also from the
Jews, who both killed the Lord, and their own prophets, and drove us out, and
are not pleasing to God, and contrary to all men. And writing again to the
Corinthians he said that: The sufferings of Christ abound unto you, and as you
are sharers of the sufferings, so also of the consolation, and to the
Galatians: Did you suffer so many things in vain, he says, if indeed even in
vain.

=4.13= When then both the one preaching is unskilled and poor and obscure, and
what is preached is not attractive, but even has a stumbling-block, and those
hearing it are poor and weak and nobodies, and dangers successive and
continual both to the teachers and to the disciples, and the one being
announced crucified, what was it that made it prevail? Is it not quite clear
that some divine and unspeakable power? Clear to everyone, surely. And this
can be seen also from the opposites. For when you see the opposites of these
running together, both wealth, and good birth, and greatness of fatherland,
and cleverness of rhetoric, and safety and much attendance, and the
innovations immediately quenched, but these overcoming from the opposites,
what is the cause, tell me? For the same thing happened, as if, the one
reigning not being able with camps and weapons and array to prevail over the
barbarians, some poor man, naked and alone, and not even handling a javelin,
nor having a garment, having gone in should accomplish what others with
weapons and preparation were not strong enough for.

=4.14= Do not then be ill-disposed, but each day cast the vote, and worship
the power of the crucified. For neither, if you should see someone
constructing cities, and driving ditches around, and bringing engines against
walls, and forging weapons, and enrolling soldiers, and having money without
bound, and not being able to take one city, but another attacking with a naked
body, and using hands only, overrunning not one and a second and twenty
cities, but ten thousand throughout the inhabited world, and taking them with
the men, would you say this is of human power. Thus it is clear also now. For
for this reason God allowed both robbers to be crucified, and some deceivers
to appear before him, that even from comparison the superiority of the truth
might be shown to those who are exceedingly unfeeling, and you may learn that
this one is not one of those, but the interval of this one and of those is
much and unbounded. For nothing was strong enough to overshadow his glory, not
the sharing of the same sufferings, not the running together in the same
times. For if the demons fear the cross, and not the strength of the
crucified, the pair of robbers stops the mouth of those who say these things.
And if even the difficulty of the times accomplished the whole, those about
Theudas and Judas apologize, who attempted the same things as we with also
many other of the signs, and were destroyed. For as I said, for this reason
God allowed these things, that from surplus he might show his own. For this
reason he also allowed false prophets to appear in the time of the prophets,
and false apostles in the time of the apostles, that you may learn that
nothing of his can be overshadowed.

=4.15= Shall I tell you also from another place a wondrous and paradoxical
power of preaching, and show you him even through those warring being raised
and increasing? Against this Paul


some at one time warring preached this very doctrine in Rome. For wishing to
provoke Nero who was warring against Paul, they also undertake to preach,
that, the word having been kindled more, and more disciples having come to be,
the wrath of the tyrant might become hotter, and the beast might be made wild.
And this Paul himself writing to the Philippians said: I want you to know,
brethren, that the things concerning me have come rather unto progress of the
gospel, so that the more of the brethren, having become confident by my bonds,
dare more abundantly to speak the word fearlessly. Some indeed even through
envy and strife, and some also through good pleasure, preach Christ; the ones
from faction, not purely, thinking to bring affliction upon my bonds; but the
others from love, knowing that I lie for a defense of the gospel. What then?
except that in every way, whether in pretense, or in truth, Christ is
announced. Did you see how many preached from faction? But nevertheless he
prevailed even through the opponents.

=4.16= And besides these, there were also other things striking against. For
even old laws not only did not help, but even were opposed and warred, and the
wickedness and the ignorance of those who slandered; for they have a king,
they say, the Christ. For they did not know his kingdom above, that fearful
and unbounded one, but they slandered as of their introducing a tyranny to the
inhabited world. And all in common, and each privately boxed against them; in
common, as of the constitution being taken away, and the laws being
overturned; and privately, as of each house being torn apart and dissolved.
For father then warred against child, and son denied father, and wives
husbands, and husbands wives, and daughters mothers, and kinsmen kinsmen, and
friends friends, and this war was some various and manifold one, creeping into
the houses, tearing kinsmen apart, throwing councils into confusion, troubling
lawcourts, as of the ancestral customs being dissolved, and feasts and service
of demons being overturned, which to the old lawgivers before all other things
were much sought. And with these the suspicion of tyranny also made them be
driven from every side. And one could not say that among Greeks these things
were so, but among the Jews it was quiet, but those too attacked much more
harshly; for they too accused a taking away of constitution. For he does not
cease, he says, speaking blasphemous words against this holy place and the
law.

=4.17= But nevertheless, the pyre being kindled from every side, from the
houses, from the cities, from the fields, from the wilderness, from the
Greeks, from the Jews, from the rulers, from the ruled, from the kinsmen, from
the land, from the sea, from those reigning, and all making one another wild,
and attacking more harshly than any beast, this blessed man, leaping into so
many furnaces, and standing in the midst of wolves, and being struck from
every side, not only was not buried, but he also transferred all of them unto
the truth. Shall I tell also other wars most harsh besides these: that of the
false apostles, what most of all grieved him, that of his own who were weak;
for many who believed were being corrupted; but even toward these he sufficed.
Whence and from what strength? Our weapons, he says, are not fleshly, but
powerful to God toward a taking down of strongholds, taking down reasonings
and every height being lifted against the knowledge of God. For this reason
all things were being changed, and were being reshaped all at once.

=4.18= And as, a pyre having been kindled, the thorns, being spent little by
little, yield and give way to the flame, and make the fields clean; so then
also, Paul's tongue speaking, and coming upon all things more vehemently than
fire, all things yielded


and gave way, both services of demons, and feasts, and assemblies, and
ancestral customs, and corruptions of laws, and wraths of peoples, and threats
of tyrants, and plots of his own, and knaveries of false apostles; or rather,
as when the ray rises, both darkness is driven, and beasts go down and
henceforth lurk, and robbers run away, and murderers take refuge about the
caves, and pirates stand off, and tomb-robbers withdraw, and adulterers and
thieves and housebreakers, as being about to be convicted from the ray, having
gone off somewhere far they make themselves unseen, and all things become
transparent and splendid, both land and sea, the ray from above illuminating
all, the seas, the mountains, the countries, the cities; so then also then,
the preaching having appeared, and Paul sowing this everywhere, error was
driven, and truth returned, and the smells of fat and smoke, and cymbals and
drums, and drunkennesses and revels, and fornications, and adulteries, and the
other things, which it is not even good to speak, the things accomplished in
the temples of the idols, ceased and were spent, as wax melted by fire, as
chaff spent by flame; but the splendid flame of the truth went up bright and
high toward heaven itself, being raised by the very ones who hindered, and
increasing through those who impeded; and not even danger held back its course
and the uncheckable rush, not tyranny of a most ancient custom, not strength
of ancestral customs and laws, not the hard-to-receive quality of the teaching
of the laws, not any other of the things that have been said.

=4.19= And that you may learn how great this is, threaten the Greeks, I do not
say dangers and deaths and famine, but a small loss of money, and you will see
them changing immediately. But our things are not such, but when all were
being cut down and slaughtered, and being warred everywhere, and with various
manners of wars, these things became more blooming. And why do I speak of the
Greeks present now, the cheap and contemptible? Let us bring into the midst
those then wondrous, those famed for philosophy, Plato, Diagoras, the
Clazomenian, and many others such, and then you will see the strength of the
preaching. For after the hemlock of Socrates, some went off to Megara, fearing
they might suffer the same; and others even fell from fatherland and freedom,
and they overcame of no other more than one woman; and the man of Citium,
having left the constitution in the writings, thus ended. And yet there was
then nothing hindering, not danger, not unskillfulness, but they were even
clever at speaking, and they abounded in money, and they happened to be of the
fatherland cried out among all; but they were strong for nothing. For such is
error, even when no one troubles, it flows down; such is truth, even when many
war, it is roused.

=4.20= And these things the very truth of the facts cries, and there is no
need of speeches, nor of words, the inhabited world from every side sending
forth a voice, the cities, the fields, the land, the sea, the inhabited, the
uninhabited, those on the ridges. For he did not even leave the wilderness
without a share of his benefaction, but he filled it most of all with the
goods which he came bringing us from heaven, through Paul's tongue, through
the grace placed in him. For since he furnished an eagerness worthy of the
gift, the grace also shone abundantly, and the more of the things that have
been said were accomplished through this man's tongue.

=4.21= Since then God so honored our race, as to count one man worthy to
become cause of so many achievements, let us be jealous, let us imitate, let
us strive to become according to that one we too, and let us not think this to
be impossible. For what I have often said, this I will not cease saying, that
there was in him both the same body as ours, and the same foods, and the same
soul; but the choice was great, and the eagerness


splendid; and this made that one such. Let no one then despair, let no one
give up. For if you present your mind, there is nothing hindering it from
receiving the same grace. For God is not a respecter of persons; and he
himself fashioned that one, and he himself brought you in; and as he is Master
of that one, so also of you; as he proclaimed that one, so he also wishes to
crown you. Let us then hold ourselves under and cleanse ourselves, that we
too, having received the grace abundantly, may obtain the same goods, by the
grace and love of mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and
the might unto the ages of ages. Amen.

5.t Of the same, an encomium on the holy apostle Paul, Homily V.

5.1 Where now are those who blame death, and say this passible and corruptible
body is a hindrance to them toward virtue? Let them hear Paul's achievements,
and let them cease from this wicked slander. For what harm did death do our
race? and what did corruption hinder toward virtue? Think of Paul, and you
will see that even becoming mortal benefited us the greatest things. For if
this man were not mortal, he would not have been able to say, or rather he
would not have been able to display what he said through the deeds, that: I
die daily, by your boasting, which I have in Christ Jesus. For everywhere we
need soul and eagerness, and there is nothing hindering being ranked among the
first. Was not this man mortal? was he not unskilled? was he not poor and
making his food from the labor of each day? did he not have a body subject to
all natural necessities? What then hindered him from becoming such as he
became? Nothing. Let no one then be despondent who is poor, let no one be
displeased who is unskilled, let no one be in pain of the cheap, but those
only as many as have a softened soul and a mind unnerved. For this becomes a
hindrance only toward virtue, vice of soul and softness of judgment; and
without this, nothing of the others. And this is clear from this blessed man
who has now gathered us. For as none of these things harmed this one, so the
opposites benefited those outside nothing, not cleverness of words, not
abundance of money, not distinction of race, not greatness of glory, not being
in power.

5.2 Why do I speak of the men? or rather until when do I hold the speech on
the earth, when it is possible to speak of the powers above, the
principalities, and the authorities, and the world-rulers of the darkness of
this age? for what did it benefit these to obtain such a nature? do not all
the powers come being judged through Paul, and those according to him? For do
you not know, he says, that we will judge angels, not to speak of things of
life? Let us then be in pain through nothing else, but through vice only, nor
let us rejoice and be glad through anything else, but through virtue only. If
we are jealous of this, there is nothing hindering becoming according to Paul.

5.3 For that one became such not from the grace only, but also from his own
eagerness; and for this reason from the grace, since also from the eagerness.
For both were with excess, both the things of God breathed upon him, and the
things of his own choice were present. Do you want to learn the things of God?
Demons feared his garments. But I do not marvel at this, just as not even that
diseases fled the shadow of Peter; but I marvel that he appeared doing this
wondrous thing before the grace, and from the very starting-line and the very
preludes; not even having this power, nor having received ordination, he was
so kindled with zeal toward Christ, as to stir the whole people of the Jews


against himself; who even seeing him being in so many dangers, so that even
the city was being besieged, was let down through a window through the wall,
and having been let down, not even so did he fall into hesitation, nor into
cowardice and fear, but from this he received more eagerness, yielding to the
dangers for economy, but yielding the teaching to no one, but having snatched
the cross again he followed; and yet he still had at his feet the example
according to Stephen, and he saw Jews breathing murder against him most of
all, and desiring to taste of his very flesh. Neither then did he fall
unsparingly into the dangers, nor fleeing did he become softer again. He
vehemently loved the present life because of the gain from it, and he
vehemently overlooked it because of the philosophy that came to him from the
contempt, or because of hastening to go off to Jesus.

5.4 For what I always say about him, and I will never cease saying, no one so
falling into opposite affairs practiced both unto exactness; no one then so
desired the present life, not even of those who vehemently love life; no one
so overlooked it, not even of those who with excess are dying. Thus that one
was pure of every desire, and he was passionate toward none of the things
present, but everywhere he mixed his own desire with the will of God; and now
he says it is even more necessary than the being with Christ and converse, and
now so heavy and burdensome, as even to groan and to hasten toward the
departure; For he desires those things only, which bring him gain according to
God, even if these happened to be opposite to the former. For he was some
various and manifold one, not playing a part, far be it, but becoming all
things which the need of the preaching and of the salvation of men required,
and in this imitating his own Master.

5.5 For God also appeared as a man, when it was needful that this appear; and
in fire of old when the time required this; and now in the form of a
man-at-arms and a soldier, now in the image of an old man, now in a breeze,
now as a wayfarer, now as very man, and he did not even thus decline to die.
And when I say, it was needful, let no one think there is a necessity of the
speech, but of his love of mankind only. And sometimes he sits on a throne,
sometimes upon the Cherubim. And all these things he did toward the economies
lying under. Therefore he also said through the prophet: I multiplied visions,
and in the hands of prophets I was likened.

5.6 Thus Paul also, imitating his own Master, would not have been condemned,
now becoming as a Jew, now as without law; and now he kept law, now he
overlooked law; and sometimes he held to the present life, sometimes he
despised it; and now he asked for money, now he even pushed away what was
given; and he sacrificed and was shaved, and again he anathematized those who
do it; and now he circumcised, now he cast out circumcision. And the things
that happened were opposite, but the judgment and the mind from which these
happened was vehemently consistent and joined to itself. For he sought one
thing, the salvation of those hearing and seeing these things. For this very
reason now he raises law, now he takes it down. For not only in the things he
did, but also in the things he said, he was various and manifold, not changing
the judgment, nor becoming another from another, but remaining what he was,
and handling each of the things said toward the present need. Do not then
blame him for these things, but for these very things most of all proclaim and
crown him.

5.7 Since even the physician, when you see him now burning, now feeding, and
now


using iron, now a drug, and sometimes taking away from foods and drinks,
sometimes allowing the sick one to be filled of these to satiety, and
sometimes wrapping him from every side, sometimes commanding that very one who
has been heated through to drink out a whole bowl of cold waters, you will not
condemn the change, nor the continual shifting; but then most of all you will
praise the art, seeing it applying with confidence the things that seem to us
to be opposite and harmful, and guaranteeing the safety. For this is a man of
skill. And if we accept a physician doing these opposite things, much more
must one proclaim Paul's soul, thus bringing itself toward the sick. For those
sick in souls need device and handling no less than those ailing in bodies;
and if you go toward them straight, all the things of their salvation will be
gone.

5.8 And what wonder, if men do these things, when God, who can do all things,
has used this law of healing, and does not always converse with us straight?
For since he wishes them to be good willingly, but not by necessity and force,
there was need for him also of a method, not through his own inability, away
with it, but through our weakness. For it is possible for him to nod only, or
rather to will only, and to do all things whatsoever he may wish; but we, once
having become masters of ourselves, do not endure to obey him in all things.
If then he should drag the unwilling, he will take away what he gave, I mean
the freedom of the authority. That this then may not happen, there was need
for him of many methods. And these things have not been said to us simply, but
because of the various and wise quality of the blessed Paul; so that when you
see him fleeing dangers, marvel similarly, as when you see him going to close
quarters with them; for as this is of courage, so that also of wisdom. When
you see him speaking great things, marvel similarly, as when you see him being
moderate; for as this is of humility, so that of greatness of soul. When you
see him boasting, marvel similarly, as when you see him pushing away praise;
for this too is of an unpuffed character, and that of an affectionate and
loving one; for managing the salvation of the many, he did these things.

5.9 Therefore he also said: Whether we were beside ourselves, it is for God;
whether we are sober, it is for you. For neither did any other have so many
necessities toward madness, nor was any other so pure of arrogance. Consider.
Knowledge puffs up; and we would all say this with him; and there was in him
so great a knowledge, as in none of the men who have ever come to be; but
nevertheless it did not lift him, but even in this very thing he is moderate.
Therefore he also says: We know in part, and we prophesy in part; and again:
I, brethren, do not yet reckon myself to have laid hold, and: If anyone thinks
he knows something, he has not yet known anything. Again fasting puffs; and
this the Pharisee makes clear, saying: I fast twice of the week. But this one,
not fasting, but even starving, called himself an abortion.

=5.10= Why do I speak of fasting and knowledge, when so many conversations and
so continual had come to be for him toward God, as came to be for none of
prophets, nor of apostles, and he was more humbled through these things? For
do not tell me of these that have been written; for he hid the more, and
neither did he say all, lest he put a great glory around himself, nor did he
keep silent about all, lest he open the mouths of the false apostles. For that
one did nothing simply, but all things with a just and reasonable cause; and
with so great wisdom he pursued the opposite affairs, as to obtain the same
praises everywhere. And what I say is such. A great good is to say nothing
great about oneself; but he did it so timely, as to be praised more having
spoken than having been silent. And if he had not


done this, he would have been accused more than those who praise untimely; for
if he had not boasted, he would have lost and betrayed all, and he would have
lifted the affairs of the enemies. Thus he knew to use the time everywhere,
and with a right judgment even to do the things forbidden, and so usefully, as
to be well spoken of from these no less than from the things commanded. For
Paul then being well spoken of while boasting succeeded more than some other
hiding his own achievements; for no one accomplished so many goods hiding his
own, as that one speaking out his own.

=5.11= And what is again more wondrous, that he not only spoke out, but also
stood as far as the need. For not as if the time furnished him much license,
did he again use the affair without measure, but he knew until where it was
needful to go forward. And not even this sufficed him, but so as not to
corrupt the others, nor to prepare them simply to praise themselves, he also
calls himself a fool; for he himself, the need calling, did this. And since it
was likely that the others, looking to him, would use the example simply and
in vain; which also happens in the case of physicians; for often, what this
one applied as a drug with time, another applying untimely damaged and
overshadowed the power of the drug.

=5.12= That this then may not happen here too, see with how much correction in
advance he uses, being about to boast, not once, nor twice, but often drawing
back. For I would that you endured me, he says, a little in the folly; and
again: What I speak, I do not speak according to the Lord, but as in folly. In
whatever anyone dares in folly speaking, I too dare. And having said so many
things, he was not even content with these, but again being about to step into
the praises he hides himself, saying: I know a man; and again: On behalf of
such a one I will boast, but on behalf of myself I will not boast; and after
all these things: I have become a fool, he says, you forced me. Who then is so
senseless and vehemently unfeeling, as, seeing that holy one, so great a
necessity lying upon him, hesitating and drawing back from saying something
great about himself, and like a horse going down cliffs and rearing
continually, and yet being about to manage so many things, not to flee the
affair from much surplus, and to use the affair only when time constrains?

=5.13= Do you want me to show you also another such thing of his? For this is
the wondrous thing; that he was not content with the conscience, but he also
taught us how one must pursue each of these, not constructing an apology for
himself only from the necessity of the times, but also educating the others,
so that when a time has fallen they may not flee such a thing, nor again
pursue it untimely. For through these things which he said, he all but said
these things: it is a great evil to say something great and wondrous about
oneself; and this is of last senselessness, beloved, when no necessity is
lying upon one, and a violent necessity, to adorn oneself with praises; this
is not to speak according to the Lord, but such a thing is rather a proof of
madness, and it empties for us all the wage after the many sweats and toils.
For all these things and more than these, through which he declined even when
a necessity had fallen, he said toward all. And what is greater than this,
that not even when a necessity had fallen did he pour all out into the midst,
but he hid the more and the greater. For I will come, he says, to visions and
revelations of the Lord; but I spare, lest anyone should reckon unto me,
beyond what he sees or hears something from me. And he said these things
educating all, not even when there is necessity, bringing all things
whatsoever we know for ourselves, to set them down into the midst, but as many
as are useful to those who hear.

=5.14= Since Samuel also; for it is not unfitting to remember that holy one
too, for the praises also come to be for our benefit; that one also boasted
once, and


spoke out his own achievements; but of what kind? Those which were expedient
for those who heard. For he did not stretch a long speech about chastity, nor
about humility, nor about not remembering evil, but on behalf of what? On
behalf of this, which the one then reigning most needed to learn, on behalf of
righteousness, and of having hands clean of gifts. And David again boasting,
boasts from those things able to set right the hearer. For that one too did
not speak another virtue, but he brought the bear and the lion into the midst,
and nothing else. For to lead the speech further out is of one ambitious and
arrogant; but to say these things which were going to be necessary toward the
present need, of one loving mankind and looking to the advantage of the many;
which indeed Paul also did. For he was being slandered, as not being an
approved apostle, nor having some strength. There was then a necessity through
those things to fall into these, which most of all showed his dignity.

=5.15= Do you see through how many things he educated the hearer not to boast
simply? First, through showing that he did this by necessity; second, through
also calling himself a fool, and using many apologies; third, through not
saying all, but hiding the greater, and this when there was necessity; fourth,
through putting on another mask, and saying that: I know a man; fifth, through
not bringing all the other virtue also into the midst, but that part of which
the present time most had need.

=5.16= And he was such not in boasting only, but also in insulting. And yet
this too was forbidden, to insult a brother, but he so used this also again as
needed, as to be well spoken of more than those who praise. For this very
reason also calling Galatians foolish both once and twice, and Cretans idle
bellies, and evil beasts, from this too he is proclaimed. For he also gave us
a definition and a rule, so as not to use attendance upon those neglected
according to God, but to handle the speech more strikingly. And measures of
all things lie with him; for this very reason doing and saying all things he
was well spoken of, both insulting and praising, both turning away and
attending, both lifting himself and being moderate, both boasting and calling
wretched. And why do you marvel, if insult and reviling are well spoken of,
when even murder was well spoken of and deceit and guile, both in the Old, and
in the New?

=5.17= Having then examined all these things with exactness, both let us
marvel at Paul, and let us glorify God, and let us so handle him, that we
ourselves also may obtain the eternal goods, by the grace and love of mankind
of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the might now and always
and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

6.t Of the same, an encomium on the holy apostle Paul, Homily VI.

6.1 Do you want today, beloved, leaving Paul's great and wondrous things, to
bring into the midst these which seem to some to have some handle? for we
shall see even these making him no less splendid and great than those. What
then is it that has a handle? He was seen once, he says, fearing blows; for he
was seen, when they stretched him out for the straps; and not then only, but
also at another time again in the case of the seller of purple, when he also
furnished trouble to those who wished to lead him out. For he did this
constructing nothing else than safety for himself and not quickly falling into
the same things. What then would we say? That nothing so shows him great and
wondrous as these things that have been said; such as that having such a soul,
not daring, nor desperate, and a body thus yielding to blows and trembling at
scourges, he overlooked no less than the bodiless powers all


the things that seemed to be fearful, when the time required this. When then
you see him being stretched out and fearing, remember those words through
which he went beyond the heavens, and rivaled the angels, saying: Who will
separate us from the love of God? affliction, or distress, or persecution, or
famine, or danger, or sword? Remember those words through which he says these
things are nothing, saying: For the momentary lightness of our affliction
works out for us, according to excess unto excess, an eternal weight of glory,
we not looking at the things seen, but at the things not seen. Add to these
the daily afflictions, the deaths each day; and having thought these things,
both marvel at Paul, and despair of yourself no longer.

6.2 For this very thing that seems to be weakness of nature, this is a
greatest proof of that one's virtue, that he was such not having been freed
from the necessity of the many. For since the excess of the dangers would have
furnished this supposition to many, and perhaps made them suspect that, having
become above human things, he was such; for these reasons he was allowed to
suffer, that you may learn that being one of the many according to nature,
according to eagerness he was not only above the many, but was even one of the
angels. For with such a soul and such a body he endured the ten thousand
deaths, and he despised the things present, the things to come. Therefore he
also uttered those great words, unbelievable to many: That I could wish to be
anathema from Christ on behalf of my brethren, my kinsmen according to the
flesh.

6.3 For it is possible, if we should only will, to conquer all agony of nature
by the power of eagerness; and there is nothing impossible for men of the
things commanded by Christ; for if we give over this eagerness as much as we
have, God also brings in much weight with us, and thus we shall become unspent
to all the terrible things that come upon us. For it is not fearing blows that
is worthy of condemnation, but through the fear of the blows to endure
something unworthy of piety, so that fearing blows shows the one unseizable in
the contests more wondrous than the one who does not fear. For thus the choice
shines more; for to fear blows is of nature; but through the fear of the blows
to endure nothing unseemly is of the choice setting right the defect of
nature, and mastering that weakness; since neither is being grieved an
accusation, but through the grief to say or do something of the things not
seeming good to God. For if I said that Paul was not a man, you would bring
the defects of nature into the midst well, as being about to refute the speech
by this; but if I say and confirm that he was a man, and nothing better than
us according to nature, but became better according to choice, you bring these
things forward to me in vain, or rather not in vain, but on behalf of Paul.
For you show from this how great that one was, as in such a nature to be
strong for the things above nature. And you not only lift that one, but you
also sew up the mouths of those who have collapsed, not allowing them to take
refuge in the superiority of nature, but pushing them into the eagerness from
choice.

6.4 But he also feared death once, he says? For this too is of nature. But
nevertheless this same one who feared death again said: For we too, who are in
the tent, groan being burdened. And again: We ourselves groan in ourselves.
Did you see how he brought in a power from choice counterbalancing the natural
weakness? Since even martyrs many times, being about to be led away to death,
grew pale and were filled with fear and agony; but even for this they are most
wondrous, that these too, fearing death, did not flee death for Jesus. Thus
Paul also


fearing death, does not even decline Gehenna for the longed-for Jesus, and
trembling at an end, he seeks the departing. And not this one only was such,
but their chief also, having often said that he is ready to give over his
soul, vehemently feared death. Hear then what Christ says discoursing with him
about this: But when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and
another will gird you and carry you where you do not wish, narrating the
defect of nature, not that of choice.

6.5 For nature displays its own even when we are unwilling, and it is not
possible to master those defects, not even for the one who vehemently wishes
and is eager; therefore we are harmed nothing from this, but we are even
marveled at more. For what accusation is it to fear death? and what is it not
a praise, fearing death to endure nothing slavish through the fear? For it is
not an accusation to have a nature that has a defect, but to be a slave to the
defects; since he who sets right the despite from it by the courage of choice
is great and wondrous. For by this he shows how great the strength of choice
is, and he stops the mouths of those who say, why have we not become good by
nature? for what differs this, to be by nature, or by choice? and by how much
is this better than that? by as much as it also has crowns, and a splendid
proclamation.

6.6 But is that of nature firm? But if you wish to have a noble choice, this
becomes firmer than that. Or do you not see the bodies of the martyrs being
cut by swords, and nature yielding to the iron, but the choice not giving way
to it, nor being convicted? did you not see in the case of Abraham, tell me,
choice mastering nature, when he was commanded to slay the child, and this
appearing more powerful than that? did you not see the same thing happening in
the case of the three children? do you not hear also the proverb from outside
saying, that choice becomes a second nature from habit? And I would say that
it is even first, as the things said before showed. Do you see that it is
possible even to have the firmness from nature, if choice is noble and
awakened, and to harvest more praise, at least the one who chose and willed,
than the one forced to be good?

6.7 This is most of all good, as when he says: I bruise my body and lead it in
slavery. Then most of all I praise him, seeing him not accomplishing virtue
without toil, so that his ease may not be a ground of idleness for those after
these things. And when he says again: I have been crucified to the world, I
crown his choice. For it is possible, it is possible to imitate strength of
nature by exactness of choice; and if we bring this very statue of virtue into
the midst, we shall find that the goods present to him from choice he was
eager to carry out into firmness of nature.

6.8 For he was in pain being struck, but he despised them no less than the
bodiless powers who are not in pain, as it is possible to hear his words,
which make him not even be thought of our nature. For when he says: The world
has been crucified to me, and I to the world, and again: And I no longer live,
but Christ lives in me, what else is it to say, than that he has even stood
away from the body itself? and what, when he says: There was given me a thorn
in the flesh, an angel of satan? and this is nothing else, than to show the
toil standing as far as the body; not because it did not pass within, but
because by surplus of choice he pushed it away and thrust it out. And what,
when he says many other things more wondrous than these, and rejoices being
scourged, and boasts upon the chains? What else would one say, than this which
I said, that the saying: I bruise my body and lead it in slavery, and I fear
lest somehow having preached to others I myself become unapproved, shows the
weak of nature


, and through these things, which I said, the nobility of choice?

6.9 For for this reason both are set down, that neither through those great
things you may think him to be of another nature and despair, nor through
these small things you may condemn the holy soul, but from this again, having
cast out the despair, you may lead yourself unto good hopes. For this reason
he sets down again also that of the grace of God with excess, or rather not
with excess, but with good disposition, that you may think nothing to be of
him. And he also says the things of his own eagerness, that you may not,
having thrown the whole upon God, live sleeping and snoring. And you will find
measures and rules of all things lying with him with exactness.

=6.10= But he also cursed the coppersmith, he says, Alexander once. And what
of this? For the saying was not of wrath, but of pain on behalf of the truth;
for he was not in pain for himself, but because he opposed the preaching; For
he vehemently opposes, he says, not me, but our words; so that the curse not
only showed this man's longing on behalf of the truth, but also consoled the
disciples. For since it was likely that all would be caused to stumble, those
who despitefully used the word suffering nothing, for this reason he says
these things. But he also prayed against some others once, saying: If at least
it is just with God to give back affliction to those who afflict us; not
desiring those to pay a penalty, far be it, but hastening to console those who
were despitefully used; therefore he also adds: Rest to those who are
afflicted. Since when he himself suffers something unpleasant, hear how he
philosophizes, and repays with the opposites, saying: Being reviled we bless,
being persecuted we endure, being blasphemed we exhort. And if you should say
that the things said or done on behalf of the others are of wrath, it is time
for you also that he maimed and insulted Elymas from wrath, and that Peter
killed Ananias and Sapphira from wrath. But no one is so senseless and silly
as to say these things. And we find him saying and doing many other things
too, seeming to be burdensome, and these most of all are what show his equity.
For when he gives over to satan the one in Corinth who had committed
fornication, he does it from much love and an affectionate mind; and this he
shows also from the second letter. And when he threatens Jews and says: Wrath
has come upon them unto an end, he does not do it being filled with wrath,
(for you hear him continually praying on their behalf), but wishing to
frighten and to make them more sober.

=6.11= But he insulted the priest, he says, saying: God is about to strike
you, you whitened wall. And I know that some, apologizing toward this, say
that what was said is a prophecy. And I do not accuse those who say it; for
this also happened, and thus he died. But if someone being a sharper enemy
should contradict, and doing something more curious should bring against it
saying: and if it was a prophecy, for what reason did he apologize saying: I
did not know that he is high priest? this we would say, that educating and
admonishing the others to be disposed equitably toward the rulers, as Christ
also did. For having said ten thousand speakable and unspeakable things about
the scribes and Pharisees, he says: The scribes and the Pharisees sat upon the
seat of Moses; all things then, whatsoever they may say to you to do, do. Thus
then here too Paul both kept the dignity, and announced beforehand what was
going to be.

=6.12= And if he also cut off John, this too was worthy of the providence on
behalf of the preaching. For the one entrusted with this ministry must not be
some loose one, nor collapsed, but courageous and tense, nor even to touch
this good business, unless he is going to give over his own soul ten thousand
times unto death and dangers, as Christ himself also says: If anyone wishes to
come after me,


let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me. For the one not so
disposed betrays many others too, and he benefits more being quiet by himself,
than coming forward into the midst, and receiving a burden greater than his
own power; for he destroys both himself and those entrusted besides. For how
is it not absurd, if someone who is ignorant of piloting, and the fight toward
the waves, not even when ten thousand constrain would choose to sit upon the
tillers, but the one going upon the preaching goes toward this simply and as
it happened, and without looking around accepts an affair that is a cause of
ten thousand deaths? For neither a pilot, nor the one boxing toward the
beasts, nor the one who has chosen to fight as a gladiator, nor any other,
must have the soul so arrayed toward deaths and slaughters, as the one who
undertakes the preaching. For the dangers are greater, and the opponents
harsher, and to be so slaughtered is not about chance things. For heaven lies
as the prize, and Gehenna as the penalty for those who sin, and destruction
and salvation of a soul. And not the one who undertakes the preaching only
must be so arrayed, but also simply the faithful; for he commands all to take
up the cross and follow; and if all, much more the teachers and shepherds, of
whom John also was then, the one also called Mark. Therefore he was also
justly cut out, because having stationed himself in the very front of the
phalanx, he stood very unmanfully; therefore Paul also stood him off, so that
that one's sluggishness might not cut out the course of their tensions.

=6.13= And if Luke should say that there came to be a provocation between
them, do not think this to be an accusation. For it is not to be provoked that
is harsh, but unreasonably and upon nothing just. For unjust wrath, he says,
will not be held guiltless; not wrath simply, but the unjust. And Christ
again: He who is angry with his brother in vain; not simply being angry. And
the prophet also says: Be angry, and do not sin. For if one must not use the
passion, not even when time calls, it lies in us in vain and to no purpose;
but not in vain. Therefore the craftsman also planted this toward a setting
right of those who sin, that it may stir the sluggish and slack of the soul,
that it may awaken the one sleeping and dissolved, having put the tension of
wrath in our mind as a temper to iron, that we may use it unto what is
needful. For this reason Paul also often used it, and being angry he was more
desirable than those who discourse with equity, doing all things with the
fitting time on behalf of the preaching. For neither is equity simply a good,
but when the time demands; since, if this is not present, that too becomes
sluggishness, and the wrath rashness.

=6.14= And I said all these things not apologizing on behalf of Paul; for he
does not even need our tongue; for his praise is not from men, but from God;
but that we may educate the hearers to use all things unto what is needful, as
I also said before. For thus we shall be able to gain from every side, and
with much abundance to sail down into the waveless harbor, and to obtain the
undefiled crowns, of which may it come to be that we all are counted worthy,
by the grace and love of mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the
glory and the might now and always and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

7.t Of the same, an encomium on the holy apostle Paul, Homily VII.

7.1 When those who carry the royal signs, a trumpet sounding before them and
many soldiers going before, enter into the cities, the whole people is
accustomed to run together, so as both to hear the sound, and to see the sign
being carried on high


, and the courage of the one carrying. Since then Paul also enters today, not
into a city, but into the inhabited world, let us all run together. For this
one too carries a sign, not of the king below, but the cross of the Christ
above, and there go before not men, but angels, both unto honor of what is
carried and unto safety of the one bearing. For if to those who manage their
own life, and do nothing of the common things, angels have been given as
guards by the Master of the whole, as Jacob also says: The angel who delivers
me from my youth; much more upon those entrusted with the inhabited world and
carrying so great a bulk of gifts are the powers above present. Those then who
have been counted worthy of this honor from those outside wear garments and a
golden ornament about the neck, and are splendid from every side; but this
one, wearing a chain instead of gold, carries the cross; this one being
driven, this one being scourged and starving.

7.2 But do not be gloomy, beloved. For this ornament is much better than that
and more splendid, and a friend to God; therefore even carrying he did not
grow weary. For this is the wondrous thing, that with bonds and scourges he
was more splendid than those who have the purple and the diadem. For that he
was more splendid, and the things said are not a boast, his garments made
clear. For if you should put upon a sick man ten thousand diadems and so many
purples, you will not be able to cut off even a small thing of the flame; but
those napkins of his, conversing with the bodies of the sick, make every
disease run away, and reasonably. For if robbers seeing this sign do not dare
to come on, but flee without turning, much more diseases and demons seeing
that sign flee. And he carried, not that he himself might bear it only, but
that he might make all such and teach them to carry; therefore he also said:
Become imitators of me, as you have us as a type; and again: What you heard
and saw in me, these things practice; and again: It was granted to us, not
only to believe unto him, but also to suffer on his behalf. For the dignities
of the present life appear greater then, when they stand about one only; but
in the spiritual things the contrary; then most of all the thing of the honor
shines, when it has many as sharers of the presidency, and when the one
sharing is not one, but has many who enjoy the same things. Do you see then
all as sign-bearers, and each carrying his name before nations and kings, but
he himself even before Gehenna, and before punishment. But he did not speak
thus; for those were not able to carry.

7.3 Did you see of how much virtue our nature is capable? as nothing is more
precious than a man even remaining mortal? For what greater than this can you
tell me? and what equal? and of how many angels and archangels is the one who
said this word not worthy? For he who in a mortal and perishable body gave
over all things for Christ, of which he was master, or rather of which he was
not, (for he gave over both things present, and things to come, and height and
depth and another creation), this one if he were in a bodiless nature, what
would he not have said? and what would he not have done? For I also marvel at
the angels for this, that they were counted worthy of such an honor, not that
they happened to be bodiless; since the devil too is bodiless and invisible,
but nevertheless he is more wretched than all, since he struck against the God
who made him. From this we also say men are wretched, not when we see them
wearing flesh, but when they do not use it unto what is needful; since Paul
also wore flesh. Whence then was he such? Both from home, and from God, and
for this reason from God, since from home; for God is not a respecter of
persons. And if you should say: and how is it possible to imitate those, hear
what he says: Become imitators of me, as I also of Christ. That one became an
imitator of Christ,


and you not even of the fellow-servant? that one was jealous of a Master, and
you not even of the fellow-slave? and what apology will you have?

7.4 And how did he imitate him, he says? Consider this from the beginning and
from the very preludes. For since from the divine streams he came up having so
great a fire, as not even to wait for a teacher; for he did not wait for
Peter, nor did he come to James, nor to any other, but having been lifted by
eagerness, he so kindled the city that a harsh war was fanned against him;
since even being a Jew, he did the things beyond the dignity, binding, leading
away, confiscating. Thus Moses also, no one having ordained him, hindered the
injustice of the barbarians against those of the same race. For these things
are a proof of a noble soul and a free judgment, not enduring to bear the
evils of others in silence, even if there is no one ordaining. For that he
justly leapt upon the presidency, God showed having ordained him later; which
he also did in the case of Paul. For that this one too did well then taking
hold of the word and of the teaching, this too God made clear, quickly leading
him up unto the dignity of the teachers.

7.5 For if they leapt upon the affairs for the sake of honor and presidency,
and of their own attendance, they would reasonably have been accused. But
since they loved dangers, and they drew deaths to themselves, that they might
save all the others, who is so wretched as to accuse so great an eagerness?
For that they did these things loving the salvation of the perishing, the vote
of God also made clear, and the destruction of those who loved this love badly
also made clear. Others also leapt upon rule and presidency once, but all
died, some having been burned, others having been swallowed by a splitting of
earth. For they did not do this through presidency, but through a love of the
first seat. Uzziah also leapt upon it, but this one too became unclean; Simon
also leapt upon it, but he was condemned, and he was in danger about the last
things; Paul also leapt upon it, but he was crowned, not with priesthood and
honor, but with ministry and toils and dangers. And that he ran on from much
zeal and eagerness, for this reason he is proclaimed and was splendid from the
preludes.

7.6 For as the one being ordained a ruler, if he does not pursue the affair as
needed, is worthy even of a greater punishment, so even if someone is not
ordained, but handles fittingly, I do not say the things of the priesthood,
but the things of the providence of the many, he is worthy of the whole. For
this reason this one more vehement than fire waited no day being quiet, but at
once he came up from the sacred fountain of the waters, and he kindled much
flame for himself, and he neither thought of dangers, nor the laughter and the
shame from Jews, nor being disbelieved among them, nor any other of such
things; but having taken other eyes, those of love, and another mind, he fell
in with much rush, like some winter torrent, sweeping away all the things of
the Jews, and through the Scriptures showing that this is the Christ. And yet
he did not yet have many gifts of the grace, he had not yet been counted
worthy of so great a Spirit; but nevertheless he was immediately on fire, and
he did all things with a soul that would die, and as if apologizing for the
time gone by, thus he did all things, and he busied himself throwing himself
into the part of the war that toiled most, and which was full of dangers and
fears.

7.7 And nevertheless being so daring and impulsive and breathing fire, he was
again so obedient and well-reined to the teachers as not, in so great a rush
of eagerness, to fall against them. For coming to him then boiling and raging
they said that he must go off to Tarsus and Caesarea, and he did not speak
against; they said that he must be let down through the wall, and he endured;
they advised him to be shaved, and he did not fall against; they said not to
enter into the


theater, and he yielded. Thus he was of one thing everywhere, of what was
expedient for the faithful, of the peace, of the concord, and everywhere he
kept himself for the preaching.

7.8 So that when you hear that he sends the nephew to the chiliarch, wishing
to snatch himself from the dangers, and when he calls upon Caesar, and when he
hastens into Rome, do not think the words to be of unmanliness. For he who
groaned, because he was present in this life, how would he not have chosen to
be with Christ? and he who despises the heavens and overlooks angels for him,
how would he have desired the things present? For what reason then did he do
this? That he might spend time in the preaching, and go off with many men, all
having been crowned. For he feared lest ever poor and needy of the salvation
of the many he should depart from here. Therefore he also said: To remain in
the flesh is more necessary for your sake.

7.9 Therefore also seeing the lawcourt setting down the better vote about him,
as Agrippa said toward Festus: This man could have been released, if he had
not called upon Caesar, and being bound, and being led away with ten thousand
other prisoners who had done ten thousand terrible things, he was not ashamed
at being bound together with those, but he also took thought beforehand of all
who sailed with him, and yet being confident on his own behalf and knowing
that he was in safety, and he was being brought up so great a sea being bound,
and he rejoiced as being sent forward unto a greatest rule. For not even a
small contest lay before him, the setting right of the city of the Romans. But
nevertheless he did not even neglect those in the ship, but he also ordered
those, having narrated the vision that appeared to him, from which they
learned that all who sailed with him are saved through him. And he did this,
not lifting himself, but preparing those obedient to himself. For this reason
God also allowed the sea to be stirred, that both through the things in which
he was disobeyed, and through the things in which he was heard, through all
things Paul's grace might be shown. For he also advised not to sail up, and he
was disobeyed, and there came to be a danger about the last things; and not
even so was he burdensome, but again as a father of children he took thought
beforehand, and he did all things so that no one might perish. And when he
also set foot of the Romans, there too how does he discourse with equity? how
with freedom does he stop the mouths of those who disobey? And he does not
even stand here, but from there too he ran into Spain.

=7.10= For even being in danger he was more confident, and he became more
daring from this, and not he himself only, but the disciples also through him.
For as if they saw him giving in and becoming more hesitant, perhaps they too
would have let down; so, since they saw him becoming more courageous, and
being despitefully used, and attacking more, they preached with freedom of
speech. And making this clear he said: So that the more of the brethren,
having become confident by my bonds, dare more abundantly to speak the word
fearlessly. For when the general is noble, not only slaughtering nor killing,
but also being wounded, he makes those stationed under him more bold, and more
being wounded than wounding. For when they see him mixed with blood and
carrying wounds, and not even so giving way to the enemies, but standing
nobly, and shaking a spear, and striking the opponents, and not giving in
toward the pains, they too are arrayed with more eagerness; which indeed also
happened in the case of Paul. For seeing him bound and preaching in the
prison, and being scourged, and mastering those who scourged, they received
more freedom of speech. Therefore making this also clear, he did not simply
say Having become confident, but he adds: Dare more abundantly to speak the
word fearlessly; that is, more now than when I was loosed, the brethren spoke
with freedom. Then he himself also received more eagerness; for then more he
was provoked against the


enemies, and the additions of the persecutions were additions of more freedom
of speech to him and a ground of greater confidence.

=7.11= He was shut in together then once, and he shone out so much, as even to
shake the foundations, and to throw open the doors, and to transfer the jailer
toward himself, and all but to persuade the one judging, so that that one
himself also said: In a little you persuade me to become a Christian. Again he
was being stoned, and having entered the city that was stoning he transferred
it. They called him as being about to judge, sometimes Jews, sometimes
Athenians, and the judges became disciples, the adversaries subjects. And as
fire falling into various materials increases more, and takes the underlying
material as an addition; so also Paul's tongue, with as many as it came
together, transferred them toward itself, and those warring against him,
having been caught by that one's words, quickly became food for this spiritual
fire, and through them again the word was raised, and went forward upon
others. Therefore he also said: I have been bound, but the word of God has not
been bound. They put him to flight; and the affair was a pursuit, but what
happened was a sending of teachers. And what friends and those stationed
together would have done, this the enemies did, not allowing him to be settled
in one place, but leading the physician about everywhere, through the things
in which they plotted, through the things in which they drove, so that all
might hear that one's tongue. They bound him again, and they provoked him
more; they drove the disciples, and they sent them to those who do not have a
teacher; they led him unto a greater lawcourt, and they benefited the greater
city.

=7.12= Therefore the Jews also being in pain said about the apostles: What
shall we do to these men? Through the things, they say, which we choose,
through these we increase. They gave him over to the jailer, that he might
hold him exactly; but he was bound more exactly by Paul. They sent him with
prisoners, that he might not flee; but he catechized the prisoners; they sent
him through the sea, that even unwilling they might prepare the road to be
accomplished quickly; and the shipwreck that happened became a ground of
teaching for those who sailed with him; they threatened ten thousand
punishments, that the preaching might be quenched, but it was raised more. And
as in the case of the Master the Jews said: Let us kill him, lest the Romans
come, and take away our city and the nation, and the contrary happened, (for
since they killed him, for this reason the Romans took away both their nation
and the city, and what they thought to be hindrances, these became helps of
the preaching), so also when Paul was preaching, what those brought on cutting
out the word, these increased it, and lifted it unto an unspeakable height.

=7.13= For all these things then let us give thanks to the resourceful God,
let us bless Paul, through whom these things came to be, let us pray that we
ourselves also obtain the same goods, by the grace and love of mankind of our
Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom to the Father be glory, together
with the Holy Spirit, unto the ages of ages. Amen. Save me from all who
persecute me and deliver me, lest he snatch my soul as a lion, there being no
one redeeming nor saving. And swift was the help of the girl; for she having
become unseen suddenly, the suitor went off having gained one thing only, a
horse-race of wantonness. But the bride stood by the bridegroom somewhere
singing: Justly is my help from the God who saves the upright in heart. Let us
then also learn the contests of continence against the fleshly things, let us
campaign against pleasures as the martyr, let us arm ourselves on behalf of
flesh against flesh, let us prepare the members beforehand with the glory of
the resurrection, let us not be mastered by delights that furnish the painful
things of Gehenna, let us flee the sweet, that the truly sweet may not run
away from us. Let the widow keep goodwill for the dead companion as again unto


life about to appear. Let her who lives with marriage be dead toward alien
pleasures. Let her who is rotting with fornications cool the flame of pleasure
with a marital one. Let the virgin not seek a bridegroom fairer than Christ.
Let each, saving the purity proper according to order, furnish to the soul the
contemplation of the master Christ; for Blessed, he says, are the pure in
heart, for they shall see God; to him be the glory unto the ages of ages.
Amen.
