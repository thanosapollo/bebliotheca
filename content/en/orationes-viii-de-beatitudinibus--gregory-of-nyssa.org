OF GREGORY OF NYSSA AMONG THE SAINTS ON THE BEATITUDES.
ORATION I.
And seeing the crowds, he went up into the mountain; and when he had
sat down, his disciples came to him; and opening his mouth, he taught
them, saying: Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom
of the heavens. Who then among those gathered is such as both to be a
disciple of the Word and to go up with him from the ground, from the
hollow and low thoughts, to the spiritual mountain of high
contemplation? Which has fled every shadow that comes from the high
earth mounds of vice, and being lit all around by the ray of the true
light, gives from a lookout, in the clear open sky of truth, to see all
things that are unseen to those shut in the hollow. And of what sort and
how many the things surveyed from this height are, the God Word himself,
blessing those who have gone up with him, goes through, as if pointing
with some finger, from this side the kingdom of the heavens, from the
other the inheritance of the land above; then mercy, and righteousness,
and consolation, and the kinship that comes to be toward the God of all;
and the fruit from the persecutions, which is to become a housemate of
God; and whatever else besides these is present to see, when the Word
points from above from the mountain, looked toward from the =44.1196=
high watch through the hopes. Since then the Lord goes up into the
mountain, let us hear Isaiah crying: Come, let us go up into the
mountain of the Lord; and if we are weak from sin, let us be
strengthened, as the prophecy guides, slackened hands and loosened
knees. For if we should come to be upon the peak, we shall find the one
who heals every disease and every infirmity, who takes up our weaknesses
and bears the diseases. Therefore let us too run toward the ascent, so
that with Isaiah, coming to be upon the ridge of hope, we may see from a
lookout those goods, as many as the Word shows to those who have
followed upon the height. But let the God Word open the mouth for us
too, and teach us those things whose hearing is blessedness; and let the
beginning of our contemplation be the beginning of the teaching that has
been spoken. Blessed, he says, are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the
kingdom of the heavens. If one of those who love gold should light upon
writings that report a treasure in a place; and the place that holds the
treasure should show much sweat and toil to those who desire the
possession; will he then go soft toward the toils, and grow slack about
the gain, and make it sweeter than the wealth to have brought in no
labor under the zeal? It is not so, it is not so; but he will call all
his friends toward this, and from every side, as far as he is able,
gathering to himself the help toward this by the multitude of the hand,
he makes the hidden wealth his own. This, brothers, is that treasure
which the writing reports; and the wealth is hidden under the unclarity.
Therefore let us too, the desirers of the unmixed gold, use the many
hands of the prayers, so that the wealth may come into the open for us,
and all may divide the treasure equally, and each may possess the whole.
For such is the division of virtue, that it is both portioned out to all
who lay claim to it, and the whole is present to each, not lessened
among those who share. For in the distribution of earthly wealth the one
who snatches the more does injustice to those who share equally; for he
lessens altogether the part of


the one who shares, when he has made his own more; but the spiritual
wealth does what the sun does, both dividing itself to all who see and
being present whole to each. Since then an equal gain from the toil is
hoped for each, let the cooperation toward what is sought by us through
the prayers become equal for all. First then I say one must think what
the beatitude itself is, whatever it is. Blessedness is, according to my
account, a taking in of all things thought according to the good; from
which none of the things that come into a good desire is absent. And the
thing signified from the beatitude might become more known to us also
from the setting beside of the contrary; and contrary to the blessed is
the wretched. =44.1197= Wretchedness then is the misery in the painful
and unwilled passions. And from the contrary the disposition upon each
of those who come to be in them has been divided. For it belongs to the
one being blessed to be gladdened by the things set before him for
enjoyment and to exult; and to the one being called wretched to be
distressed by the things present to him and to be pained. What is truly
to be blessed, then, is the Divine itself. For whatever we may suppose
it to be, that unmixed life is blessedness, the unspeakable and
inconceivable good, the inexpressible beauty, grace itself, and wisdom,
and power; the true light; the fountain of every goodness; the authority
lying above the all; the only thing to be loved, that which is always
the same, the continuous exultation; the everlasting gladness; about
which, saying all that one can, one says nothing of the things according
to worth. For neither does the mind reach the being, and even if we
should make room to think something of the higher things about it, the
thing thought is announced by no word. And since the one who shaped the
human being made him according to the image of God, secondarily that
which comes to be in this name according to participation of the true
blessedness would be to be blessed. For as upon bodily well formedness,
the prototype beauty is in the living and standing face, and second to
this is that shown according to imitation upon the image; so also human
nature, being an image of the blessedness that lies above, is itself
also characterized by the good beauty, when it shows upon itself the
impressions of the blessed characters. But since the filth of sin has
made the beauty upon the image useless, he came who washes us with his
own water, the living water that leaps into everlasting life, so that
we, putting away the shame from sin, might be renewed again according to
the blessed form. And just as upon the art of painting one skilled might
say to the unskilled that that face is beautiful which is composed of
such and such parts of the body; to which belongs such hair, and the
circles of the eyes, and the outlines of the brows, and the placing of
the cheeks, and each of all the things through which well formedness is
completed; so also the one who paints our soul again toward the
imitation of the only blessed, sketches in the word each of the things
that tend toward beatitude, and says first: Blessed are the poor in
spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of the heavens. But what gain will
come from the great gift, if the thought lying in the word is not made
plain to us? For even in medicine, many of the precious and hard to get
drugs remain unused and of no profit to those who do not know, until we
hear from the art toward what each of these is useful. What then is it
=44.1200= to be poor in spirit, through which it comes about to become
possessor of the kingdom of the heavens? We have learned two riches from
the Scripture: one that is pursued, and one that is condemned. The
wealth of the virtues is pursued, and the material and earthly is spoken
against; because the one becomes a possession of the soul, and this is
fitted toward the deceit of the senses. Therefore the Lord forbids
treasuring this, as laid out for moth eating and for the plot of those
who dig through walls. And he commands to have the zeal about the wealth
of the high things, which the destroying power does not touch. And by
saying moth and thief, he indicated the ravager of the soul's treasures.
If then


poverty is set over against wealth, it is altogether possible by the
analogy to be taught a double poverty too: the one to be thrown away,
and the one that is blessed. He then who is poor of temperance, or of
the precious possession of righteousness, or of wisdom, or of prudence,
or who is found a beggar and without property and poor of some other of
the costly treasures, is wretched of the poverty and pitiable of the
lack of the precious things; but he who willingly is poor of all things
thought according to vice, and has none of the devil's treasures stored
in his own treasuries, but boiling in the spirit, and through this
treasuring for himself the poverty of evils, this one would be the one
shown by the Word in the poverty that is blessed, whose fruit is kingdom
of heavens. But let us come back again upon the working of the treasure,
and let us not stand off, uncovering the hidden thing with the mining
word. Blessed, he says, are the poor in spirit. This has been said in a
way also in what is before, and now it will be said again, that the end
of the life according to virtue is likeness toward the Divine. But the
passionless and unmixed altogether flees the imitation from humans. For
it is not possible for the passionate life to be wholly likened toward
the nature that does not admit the passions. If then only the Divine is
blessed, as the Apostle names it, and the sharing of the beatitude is
for humans through the likeness toward God, and the imitation is without
a way; then blessedness is unreachable for human life. But there are
things of the Godhead that lie ready for imitation to those who will.
What then are these? It seems to me the Word names poverty of spirit the
voluntary humility. And of this the Apostle, saying it, shows us
beforehand the poverty of God as a pattern, who being rich became poor
for us, so that we might become rich by that one's poverty. Since then
all the other things, as many as are seen about the divine nature,
overshoot the measure of human nature; but humility is a kind of thing
grown with us and reared with those who go upon the ground, and who have
their constitution from earth, and who flow down into earth; having
imitated God in what is according to nature and possible for you, you
yourself put on the blessed form. And let no one think the achievement
of humility to be without toil, =44.1201= and procured with ease. On the
contrary, of every one of the things practiced according to virtue, such
a thing is more toilsome. Why? Because while the human being slept, the
one who had received the good seeds, the chief of the contrary sowing,
the tares of pride, was rooted by the enemy of our life. For by the
things through which that one dashed himself down to earth, by the same
way he threw down with himself the wretched human race toward the common
fall; and there is no other such evil of our nature as that through
pride. Since then the passion according to lifting up has somehow grown
in almost everyone who shares human nature, for this reason the Lord
begins the beatitudes from here, as if casting out of our habit some
original evil, pride, in counseling to imitate the one who willingly
became poor, who is truly blessed, so that, being likened as far as we
are able in that in which we can, from becoming poor according to
choice, we may also draw to ourselves the sharing of the beatitude. For
this mind, he says, let there be in you, which also was in Christ Jesus;
who being in the form of God did not count it plunder to be equal to
God; but emptied himself, taking the form of a slave. What is poorer
upon God than the form of the slave? What is more humble upon the king
of the beings, than to come into sharing of our poor nature? The King of
those who reign, and Lord of those who lord, willingly puts on the form
of slavery; the Judge of the all becomes tributary to those who hold
power; the Lord of the creation is brought down in a cave; the one who
has grasped the all finds no place in the lodging, but is thrown aside
in the manger of the irrational animals; the pure and unmixed admits the
filth of human nature, and going through all of our


poverty proceeds as far as the trial of death. You see the measure of
the voluntary poverty. The life tastes death; the Judge is led into a
court; the Lord of the life of the beings comes to be upon the vote of
the one judging; the King of all the hypercosmic power does not push
away the hands of the executioners. Toward this pattern, he says, let
the measure of humility look for you. And perhaps it seems to me to be
well also to look through the unreason of such a passion, so that the
beatitude may become easy for us to achieve, humility being achieved
with much ease and facility. For just as those skilled among physicians,
having first taken out the disease making cause, more easily master the
passion; so we too, having settled by reasoning the puffiness of those
swollen with conceit, shall make the road of =44.1204= humility easy for
ourselves to step upon. From where then might one more show the vanity
of conceit? From where else, than from showing the nature, what it is?
For the one looking toward himself, and not the things about himself,
would not reasonably fall into such a passion. What then is a human
being? Do you want me to say the more solemn and more precious of the
accounts? But the one who adorns our things, and arranges human well
birth toward the more boastful, traces the nature from clay in its
genealogy; and the well birth, and the solemnity of the proud one, has
kinship toward the brick. And if you should wish to speak the near and
ready account of the generation, away, do not utter about this; do not
mutter, do not uncover, as the law says, the unseemliness of your father
and mother; do not make public by the word the things worthy of
forgetfulness and of deep silence. Then do you not blush, you earthen
statue, you who in a little are dust, you who hold in yourself a short
lived puff like a bubble, becoming full of pride, and inflamed all
around with conceit, and swelling the mind with the vain thought? Do you
not see toward both limits of the life of the human being, and how it
begins, and into what it ends? But you exult in youth, and look toward
the flower of the age, and pride yourself in the season, because your
hands swell over for movement, and the feet are light toward the leap,
and the curl tosses about in the breezes, and the down sketches the
cheek, and because your clothing is over flowered with the dye of
purple, and the weavings from silks are patterned for you, patterned
with wars or hunts, or some histories, or perhaps you even look toward
the sandals carefully shining in the black, and delighting elaborately
in the lines from the needles? You look toward these, but you do not see
toward yourself? I will show you as in a mirror who you are and of what
sort you are. Have you not seen in a common grave the mysteries of our
nature? Have you not seen the successive heap of bones? skulls stripped
of fleshes, looking with a fearful and hideous look in hollowed eyes?
Have you seen mouths grinning, and the rest of the members carried
according to what has happened? If you have seen those, you have seen
yourself in those. Where are the signs of the present flower? where the
good color of the cheek? where the flower upon the lip? where the grim
beauty in the eyes shining under the wrapping of the brows? where the
straight nose, which mediates for the beauty of the cheeks? where the
hairs upon the neck? where the curls about the temples? where the hands
drawing the bow? the horsing feet? the purple? the fine linen? the
cloak? the belt? the sandals? the horse? the running? the snorting? all
the things through which your conceit is now increased? Where are these
in those, say, for which you now lift yourself and think great things?
What dream so without substance? What such phantasms from sleep? What so
=44.1205= powerless shadow fleeing the touch, as the dream of youth
appearing together and at once flying past? These things I say toward
those in youth who are foolish through the unfinished state of the age.
And what would one say about those already settled, for whom the age has
gone on, but the character is unstable, and the disease of pride
increases; and they set the name thought upon such a weakness of
character; and as a rule both the beginning and the power according to
it become the matter of pride.


For either they suffer this in it, or they prepare themselves toward it,
or the stories about it often rekindle the disease even when it has
already ceased. And what account is such as to come within their
hearing, which has been stuffed beforehand by the voice of the heralds?
Who will persuade those so disposed that they differ not at all from
those who parade upon the stage? For upon those too some face from the
art of the polished ones has been put on, and a gold spangled purple,
and the parade upon a chariot, and yet no disease of pride comes into
them from such things; but such as they were in thought before the
stage, they have kept an equal habit upon the soul even upon the parade,
and after this they are not distressed, having stepped down from the
chariot and taking off the figure. But those who parade through the
office in the stage of life, reckoning neither what was a little before
nor what is after a little, as bubbles are stretched around by the puff;
in the same way these too are swollen around by the loud voice of the
herald, and they mold about themselves the form of some alien mask,
changing the placing of the face according to nature into the unsmiling
and fearful, and a harsher sound is invented for them, being remolded
toward the beastlike for the astonishment of those who hear; they no
longer remain in the human limits, but introduce themselves into the
divine power and authority. For they believe themselves to be lords of
life and death, because of those judged among them they assign to one
the saving vote, and they condemn the other to death; and they do not
even see this, who is truly lord of human life, the one who also defines
the beginning of being and the end; and yet this alone would have been
enough toward the settling of the puffiness, to see many of the rulers
snatched from the midst of the thrones in the very stage of the office,
and carried out upon the tombs, upon which the lament took over the
voices of the heralds. How then is he lord of an alien life who is alien
from his own? And this one then, if he is poor in spirit, looking toward
the one who willingly became poor for us, and looking toward the equal
honor of the nature, would not insolence anything from that deceived
tragedy about office against the same born, truly to be blessed of the
humility of the season, having exchanged for it the kingdom of the
heavens. Do not throw away, brother, =44.1208= the other account of
poverty either, which becomes a procurer of the wealth according to
heaven. Sell, he says, all your belongings, and give to the poor, and
come, follow me, and you will have treasure in the heavens. For such
poverty too seems to me not to be out of tune with the poverty that is
blessed. See, having left all things, as many as we had, we have
followed you, the disciple says toward the Master; what then will there
be for us? And what is the answer? Blessed are the poor in spirit, for
theirs is the kingdom of the heavens. Do you want to think who is the
one who is poor in spirit? The one who has exchanged the wealth of the
soul for the bodily plenty, the one who is poor through the spirit, the
one who has shaken off the earthly wealth as some weight, so that he may
be carried above, lifted and through the air, as the Apostle says,
journeying together upon a cloud with God. Gold is a heavy thing, heavy
is every matter pursued according to wealth; but virtue is a light and
upward borne thing. But these are opposed to one another, the weight and
the lightness. Therefore it is impossible for someone to become light,
having nailed himself to the weight of the matter. If then we must step
toward the things above, let us be poor of the things below that drag
down, so that we may come to be in the things above. And what the manner
is, the psalmody guides: He has scattered, he has given to the poor, his
righteousness remains into the age of the age. The one who has shared
with the poor will set himself into the portion of the one who became
poor for us. The Lord became poor; do not you too fear the poverty. But
the one who became poor for us reigns over all the creation. Therefore
if you become poor together with the one who became poor, you will also
reign together with the one who reigns. For blessed are the poor in
spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of the heavens, of which may we too be
counted worthy in


Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom is the glory and the power, into the ages
of the ages. Amen.
ORATION II.
Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth. Those who step
toward the higher things through some ladder, when they have stepped
upon the first rung, are lifted through that one upon the one lying
above, and again the second leads the one going up to the third, and
this to the next, and this to the one after itself. And so the one going
up, always being raised from that in which he is toward that which lies
above, arrives at the topmost of the ascent. Looking toward what do I
make this preface from here? It seems to me that the order of the
beatitudes is disposed after the manner of rungs, making the ascent easy
for the word through one another. For the one who has stepped in thought
upon the first beatitude, through some necessary sequence of the
thoughts, is received by the one after that, even if the word should
seem at first to be somewhat strange. For perhaps the hearer will say
that it is not possible, as in a disposition of rungs, after the kingdom
of the heavens, for the inheritance of the =44.1209= earth to be taken;
but if the word had to follow the nature of the beings, it would have
been more sequential for the earth to have been set first of the heaven,
as if the ascent for us were going to be from here upon that. But if we
should somehow be winged by the word, and should stand above the back of
the heavenly vault, we shall find there the hyperheavenly earth, which
is laid up for inheritance to those who have lived according to virtue,
so that the order of the sequence of the beatitudes may not seem to have
been missed, heaven first, and after these earth, having been set before
us in the promises by God. For the appearing, as far as toward the
bodily sense, is all akin toward itself. For even if one thing should
seem high by the local interval, still it is below the noetic essence,
which it is impossible for the reasoning to tread, unless it has first
gone through by the word those things which the sense reaches. And if
the lot that lies above is named by the title of earth, you will wonder
at nothing; for the Word condescends to the lowliness of our hearing,
the one who came down toward us for this, since we were not able to be
raised toward him. Therefore through words and names known to us he
hands over the divine mysteries, using those voices which the custom of
human life holds. For even in the promise before this he named that
unspeakable beatitude in the heavens a kingdom; showing something of
such a sort by the word, such as the kingdom below holds? some diadems
flashing around with the gleams of stones, and well flowered purples
shining out something sweet to greedy eyes, and gateways and curtains,
and high thrones, and ranks of spear bearers standing around in a line,
and whatever else those who lift the bulk of power toward the greater
through such things add as tragedy about such a stage of life. But since
the name of the kingdom according to life is something great and above
almost all things envied among humans, for this reason he used the name
together, toward the showing of the goods that lie above; as if there
were some other thing among humans higher than the kingdom, through that
name he would altogether most have winged the soul of the hearer toward
desire of the unspeakable blessedness. For it was not possible for those
goods to be uncovered to humans by proper names, which are above sense
and human knowledge. For neither has an eye seen, he says, nor an ear
heard, nor has it gone up upon a human heart. But so that the hoped for
blessedness may not altogether escape the conjecture, as we have room
according to the lowliness of our nature, so we hear of the unutterable.
Let not then the sameness of name of the earth again after the heavens
drag down your mind upon the earth below, but if you have been raised by
the Word through the former beatitudes, and


have stepped upon the heavenly hope, busy yourself for me with that
earth, which is not the inheritance of all, except if some through the
meekness of life =44.1212= are judged worthy of that promise; which the
great David too, who among all those who had come to life in his time
was witnessed by the divine Scripture as meek and without malice, seems
to me to have thought of beforehand by the guidance of the Spirit, and
already to have held the hoped for by faith, saying I believe to see the
goods of the Lord in the land of the living. For I do not say that the
Prophet has called this earth, which grows all mortal things and
dissolves again into itself every race from itself, a land of the
living; but he knows a land of the living, upon which death has not
stepped; in which a way of sinners has not been worn, which has not
received upon itself a track of vice, which the one who sows the tares
has not cut with the plow of wickedness, which is barren of thistles and
thorns; in which is the water of rest, and the place of the green, and
the fountain divided four ways, and the vine farmed by the God of all,
and whatever else we hear through riddles from the God inspired
teaching. If then the high earth has been thought by us, contemplated
above the heavens, upon which the city of the king has been settled,
about which glorious things have been spoken, as the Prophet says, we
would no longer reasonably be strangers toward the order of the sequence
of the beatitudes. For it was not likely, I think, that this earth
should lie before as a blessing in the hopes for those who will be
snatched, as the Apostle says, in clouds into a meeting of the Lord into
air, and who are thus going to be always with the Lord. What need then
still of the earth below, for whom the life is lifted in hopes? For we
shall be snatched in clouds into a meeting of the Lord into air, and
thus we shall always be with the Lord. But let us see of what virtue the
inheritance of that earth is set forth as a prize. For blessed, he says,
are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth. What is the meek? and
about what does the Word bless the meek? for it does not seem to me that
one must think equally a virtue all things that come to be in meekness,
if only the quiet and slow should be signified through this word. For
neither among runners is the meek better than the one who hurries, nor
in boxing does the hard to move take up the crown against the opponent;
and if we run toward the prize of the upward calling, Paul advises to
stretch the speed, saying: So run, that you may take hold; since he
himself too, through a more vehement movement always, held to what is in
front, making forgetfulness of the things behind; and boxing he was easy
to move; for he saw through the assault of the one standing against him;
and he was sure about the stance, and armed with the hands, he did not
throw the weapon about the hands against some empty and substanceless
thing, but he touched the vital parts of the one wrestling against him,
bringing the blows against the body itself. Do you want to know Paul's
boxing? See the wounds of the one competing against, see the bruises of
the opponent, see the marks of the one defeated. And you are altogether
=44.1213= not ignorant of the opponent who wrestles against through the
flesh, whom he bruises through the boxing, tearing with the nails of
self control, whose members he puts to death with hunger, and thirst,
and cold, and nakedness, upon whom he lays the marks of the Lord, whom
he conquers through the running, leaving him behind himself, so that he
may not be shadowed in the eyes by the opponent running in front. If
then Paul is sharp and easy to move in the contests, and David widens
the steps running upon the enemies, and the bridegroom in the Song is
likened to a gazelle according to the easy movement, leaping through
mountains, and springing upon hills (and many such things it is possible
to say, in which the speed according to movement is found more honorable
than meekness); how here in the form of an achievement does the Word
bless the meek? For blessed, he says, are the meek, for they shall
inherit the earth; that earth altogether which is fertile of good
births, which is leafy with the tree of life, which is watered with the
fountains of the spiritual gifts, upon which the true vine sprouts,
whose farmer we hear to be the


Father of the Lord. But the Word seems to philosophize some such thing,
that there is much ease toward vice, and nature is sharp flowing toward
the worse; as the heavy of bodies remain altogether unmoved toward the
things above, but if they should be shaken off from some high ridge upon
the slope, they are carried toward what lies below with such a rush,
their own weight stretching the carrying, that the speed is beyond
account. Since then the sharpness in these things is hard, that which is
thought from the reverse would altogether be to be blessed. And this is
meekness, the habit slow and hard to move toward such impulses of
nature. For just as fire, having a nature always moved upon the things
above, is unmoved according to the contrary carrying; in the same way
virtue too, being some sharp thing toward the things above and lying
above, and never letting down from the swiftness, is fettered toward the
contrary impulse. Therefore since the swiftness toward the evils is
excessive according to our nature, well is that which is quiet in such
things blessed. For the still in these things becomes a witness of the
movement toward the things above. And it would be better to make the
account clear through the very patterns according to life: Double is the
movement of each one's choice, going forward according to authority
toward what seems good, from this toward temperance, from that toward
the unchaste. And what has been said in part of the form according to
virtue and vice, let this be thought also upon every. For the human
character is altogether split toward the contrary impulses, either anger
being divided against fairness, or conceit against moderation, or envy
against goodwill, or ill will against a loving and peaceful
co-disposition. Since then human life is material, and the passions are
about the materials, and every passion has a sharp and uncheckable
=44.1216= impulse toward the fulfillment of the will (for matter is
heavy and downward borne); for this reason the Lord blesses, not those
who live upon themselves outside passion (for it is not possible in a
material life for the wholly immaterial and passionless life to be
achieved), but he says meekness is the possible limit of virtue in the
life through flesh, and he says that being meek is enough toward
beatitude. For he does not legislate passionlessness altogether for
human nature; for it is not of a just lawgiver to command these things,
as many as nature does not admit; for such a thing is like as if someone
should move the water creatures toward the aerial life, or the reverse
upon the water, as many as live in the air; but the law should be
fitting to the proper and according to nature power. For this reason the
beatitude enjoins the moderate and meek, not the altogether passionless;
for the one is outside nature, and the other is achieved through virtue.
If then the beatitude were proposing the unmoved toward desire, the
blessing would be without profit to life and unused; for who would reach
such a thing, yoked with flesh and blood? But now he says it is not the
one who has desired according to some chance that is condemned, but the
one who has drawn the passion upon himself from forethought. For that
such an impulse should sometime be born, the weakness mixed with nature
often prepares even against will; but not to be carried aside like a
torrent by the impulse of the passion, but to stand manfully toward such
a disposition, and to push away the passion with the reasonings, this is
the work of virtue. Blessed then are those who are not sharp flowing
toward the passionate movements of the soul, but settled by the word,
upon whom the reasoning, as some bridle, checking the impulses, does not
let the soul be carried out toward disorder. And one might more see such
a thing upon the passion according to anger, how meekness is to be
blessed. For whenever a word, or some act, or a suspicion of the more
unpleasant things, stirs up such a disease, and the blood boils around
the heart, and the soul rises toward defense; just as the myths through
some drug making change the nature into forms of irrational things; so
it is possible to see suddenly a pig, or a dog, or a leopard, or some
other such beast coming to be from the anger the man. A bloodshot eye;
hair standing and bristling; a harsh voice and being sharpened upon the


words; a tongue numbed by the passion, and not serving the impulses
within; lips fixed, and not articulating the word, nor wrapping in the
mouth the moisture born from the passion, but unseemly spitting out the
foam together with the voice; such are the hands; such the feet; such
the whole disposition of the body, each of the members being
co-disposed =44.1217= with the passion. If then the one should be such;
and the other, looking toward the beatitude, should calm the disease
through the reasonings in a settled look, and a quiet voice, as some
physician treating by art one unseemly from frenzy; will you not
yourself also say, setting this beside that, that that beastlike one is
pitiable and abominable, but the meek is to be blessed, who has not
twisted his seemliness together with the vice of the neighbor? And that
the Word looks especially toward this passion is clear from his
legislating meekness for us after humility. For the one seems to hold to
the other, and the constitution of humility is as some mother of the
habit according to the meek. For if you take away conceit from the
character, the passion according to anger has no occasion to be born.
For insult and dishonor become a cause of such weakness for those who
have been angered. And dishonor does not touch the one who has trained
himself with humility. For if someone should have the reasoning
cleansed from human deceit, and should see the nothingness of the nature
with which he has been allotted, from what beginning he has the
constitution, and toward what end the short and short lived of the life
here is carried, and the filth yoked with the flesh, and the poverty of
the nature, that it is not sufficient through itself toward its own
constitution, unless it should fill the lacking with the surplus of the
irrational things; and besides these griefs and mournings and disasters,
and the many formed kinds of diseases, to which human life is subject,
of which there is no one who is by nature unfinished and free. Seeing
these things with the eye of the soul cleansed through accuracy, he
would not easily be indignant toward the lacks of honors. On the
contrary he will think a deceit the honor brought upon him upon
something by the neighbor, there being no such thing in our nature which
can have sharing toward the honorable, except according to soul only,
whose honor is not constituted from the things sought according to this
world. For to boast upon wealth, or to be solemn upon birth, or to look
toward glory, or to seem to be above the neighbor, through which human
honors are filled; all these become a taking down of the soul's honor
and a reproach, so that the one who has reasoned would not choose to
defile the pure of the soul with some such thing. And to be so is
nothing other than to be in a deep habit of humility, which having been
achieved, anger will have no entrance against the soul. And this not
being present, the quiet and still life is achieved; which is nothing
other than meekness, whose end is beatitude and inheritance of heavenly
earth, in Christ Jesus, to whom is the glory and the power, into the
ages of the ages. Amen. =44.1220=
ORATION III.
Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be consoled. We have not yet
stepped upon the peak of the mountain, but we are still according to the
foot of the thoughts; even if we have already gone past two hills,
having been led up through the beatitudes upon the poverty that is
blessed, and the meekness above this, after which the Word leads us to
the higher things, and shows us in sequence a third height through the
beatitudes; toward which we must altogether run up, having put away
every hesitation, and the sin that easily surrounds, as the Apostle
says, so that, becoming light and well girt upon the peak, we may draw
near through the soul to a cleaner light of the truth.


What then is the thing said, Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall
be consoled? The one looking toward the world will altogether laugh, and
will say these things, mocking the Word: If those spent in every
disaster according to life are blessed, then according to the sequence
those for whom life is without grief and without hurt are altogether
wretched. And so enumerating the forms of the disasters, he will
increase the laugh, bringing under the eye the evils of widowhood, and
the misery of orphanhood, the losses, the shipwrecks, the captivities
from wars, the unjust judgments in court, removals and confiscations and
dishonors, and the disasters from the diseases, such as maimings and
amputations, and the manifold outrage of the body, and if any passion is
constituted for humans according to this life, touching either body or
soul, he will go through all by the word, through which, as he thinks,
he will show as ridiculous the word that blesses those who mourn. But
we, having thought little of those who contemplate the divine thoughts
in a small souled and low way, will attempt as it is possible to look
down upon the wealth lying in the thing said through depth, so that
through this too it may become clear how great is the difference of the
fleshly and earthy mind toward the high and heavenly. It is possible
from the ready to take as to be blessed that mourning which comes to be
upon faults and sins, according to Paul's teaching about grief, who said
that there is not one form of grief, but the one worldly, and the other
worked according to God. And of the worldly grief, death is the work;
and the other works salvation from repentance for those who grieve. For
in truth such a passion of the soul is not outside being blessed, when,
having come into perception of the worse, it bewails the life in vice.
For just as upon the bodily weaknesses, for whom some part of the body
becomes paralyzed from some outrage, the lack of pain becomes a sign of
the paralyzed having been put to death; but if by some medical art the
vital perception should again be brought back to the body, they already
rejoice =44.1221= when the part is in pain, both the one who is ill
himself and those who bring the treatment, using as a proof of having
turned the passion toward health, that the member has already come into
perception of the things that sting; so whenever, as the Apostle says,
some having become past feeling have given themselves over to the life
according to sin, having become truly some dead and paralyzed of the
life according to virtue, they have no perception of the things they
do. But if the word that heals should take hold of them, as through some
hot and burning drugs (I mean the gloomy threats of the judgment that is
going to be), and should sting the heart through depth with the fear of
the things expected, fear of gehenna, and fire not quenched, and
unending worm, and gnashing of teeth, and unceasing weeping, and outer
darkness, and all such things, rubbing and warming as some hot and sharp
drugs upon the one numbed through the passions according to pleasure,
should lead him into perception of the life in which he was, it will
work him to be blessed, having made the painful perception in the soul.
Just as Paul too lashes with the word the one who raged upon the bed of
the father, until he held the sin without perception; but when the
treatment of the rebuke reached the man, as already having become
blessed through the mourning, he begins to console, so that such a one
may not, he says, be swallowed by the more abundant grief. And let this
thought too be for us not unused toward the life according to virtue for
the present contemplation of the beatitude, because of sin somehow being
excessive in the nature of humans; and the mourning from repentance has
been shown as a drug of this. But it seems to me the Word hints at
something deeper than the things said in the continuing activity of the
mourning, guiding us to think something else besides this. For if he
were showing only the regret upon the fault, it would have been more
sequential for those who have mourned


to be blessed, not those who mourn forever; as by comparison of a
pattern of the spending in the disease we bless those who have been
treated, not those who are forever being treated. For the stretching of
the treatment also co-indicates the continuous of the weakness. And
otherwise it seems to me to be well not to set it by such a thought
only, as if the Word were assigning the beatitude to those who mourn
upon sins. For we shall find many who have had a life without accusation,
and witnessed upon every better thing by the divine voice itself. For
what greed of John? what idolatry of Elijah? What small or greater fault
according to the life of those does the history know? What then? Will
the account then suppose them to be outside the blessedness, who have
neither been ill from the beginning, nor have come into need of this
drug, I mean the mourning from repentance? Or would it be absurd to
think such ones thrown away from the divine blessedness, because they
neither sinned, nor healed the sin with mourning? Or thus the sinning
would be more honorable than living without sin =44.1224=, if the grace
of the Paraclete has been allotted only to those who repent? For
blessed, he says, are those who mourn, for they shall be consoled.
Therefore following as it is possible, as Habakkuk says, the one who
sets upon the high things, let us again seek out the thought lying in
the things said, so that we may learn to what mourning the consolation
of the Holy Spirit is set forth. Let us look then in human life, what
first mourning itself is, and upon what things it is constituted.
Therefore such a thing is clear to all, that mourning is a gloomy
disposition of the soul, constituted upon deprivation of some of the
things according to the heart; which has no room to be constituted upon
those who live through in good cheer; for example, someone fares well
according to life, all the affairs are carried for him according to
stream through the pleasant things, he is gladdened upon the wife, he
exults upon the children, he has been fortified through the alliance of
the brothers, solemn according to the marketplace, honorable to those
who hold power, fearful to those set against, not to be despised by
those under the hand, easy of access to friends, leafy with wealth,
enjoying, pleasant, without grief, strong in the body; having all things,
as many as seem to be honorable according to this world; such a one is
altogether in gladness, being delighted upon each of the things present.
But if some change of this well day should take hold, bringing either a
separation of the dearest, or a loss of the things present, or some
maiming of the bodily good lot, from some wicked chance, then by the
taking away of the thing that gladdens, the contrary disposition comes
to be, which we name mourning. Therefore the account given about it is
true, that mourning is some painful perception of the deprivation of the
things that gladden. If then human mourning has been thought by us, let
the clear things become some guidance of the unknown, so that it may
become clear what the mourning that is blessed is, which the consolation
follows. For if the deprivation of the goods that are present makes the
mourning here, and one would not bewail the loss of the unknown; it is
fitting first to know the good itself, whatever it is according to
truth, and then thus to think down upon human nature. For through this
it will happen that the mourning that is blessed is achieved. For as it
is upon those who live in darkness, when the one is born in the gloom,
and the other, accustomed with the enjoyment of the light outside,
becomes shut in from some outrage, the disaster of the things present
does not take hold of both alike (for the one who knows of what he has
been deprived makes the loss of the light heavy; and the one who has not
known such a grace at all will live through without grief, reckoning
through the rearing of the gloom that he has fallen out of none of the
goods; and from this the desire of the enjoyment of the light will lead
the one into every device and invention of seeing again that of which he
was deprived from outrage; and the other will grow old living through in
the gloom, through not having known the better, judging the present a
good for himself); so also upon the present thought, the one who has
been strong to look down upon the truly good, then =44.1225= having
thought down upon the poverty of human


nature, will altogether have the soul in disaster, making mourning by
the present life not being in that good. Therefore it seems to me the
Word does not bless the grief, but the knowledge of the good, to which
the passion of grief happens, because the thing sought is not present to
life. Sequential then to examine what then that light is, with which
this gloomy cave of human nature is not lit in the present life. Or
perhaps the desire looks toward the unachievable and incomprehensible?
For what reasoning in us is such as to track the nature of the thing
sought? What signification from names and words is such as to make in us
a worthy thought of the light that lies above? How shall I name the
unseen? How shall I present the immaterial? How shall I show the
formless? how shall I take apart the unmeasured, the unquantified, the
unqualified, the unshaped; that which is found neither by place nor by
time; that which is outer of every bounding, and of every defining
phantasy; whose work is life, and the hypostasis of all things thought
according to the good; about which every high thought and name is
contemplated; godhead, kingdom, power, everlastingness, incorruption,
joy and exultation, and all that is thought and said in height? How then
is it possible and through what reasonings for such a good of ours to
come under the eye, that which is contemplated and not seen? That which
furnishes being to all the beings? itself always being, and not needing
to come to be? But so that the account may not toil in vain stretching
itself upon the uncontainable, let us cease busying ourselves with the
nature of the goods that lie above, as such a thing being impossible to
come under grasp, having gained this much only from the things sought,
as much as through the very not being able to look down upon the thing
sought, to form some thought of the greatness of the things sought. And
by as much as we believe the good to be higher than our knowledge by
nature, by so much more let us stretch the mourning in ourselves, that
the good of which we happen to have been separated is such and so great,
that we cannot even have room for the knowledge of it. Of this, however,
which overshoots every grasping power, we humans were sometime in
participation; and so great was that good above every thought in our
nature, that the human seemed to be that other, having been formed by
the most accurate likeness according to the image of the prototype. For
the things which we now contemplate about that by conjecture, all these
were also about the human being, in incorruption and blessedness; and
the self ruling and the unmastered, and the without grief and
=44.1228= unbusied of the life, and the spending in more divine things;
and the looking toward the good with the mind naked and clean of every
covering. For all these things the account of the genesis of the world
hints to us through few words, saying that the human being has been
shaped according to the image of God, and lives in the paradise, and
delights in the things planted there. And the fruit of those plants is
life and knowledge and such things. And if these things were in us, how
then is it possible, looking beside by comparison the present
wretchedness now to the blessedness then, not to groan upon the
disaster; the high has been humbled; that which came to be according to
the image of the heavenly has been made earthy; that which was ordered
to reign has been enslaved; that which was created into immortality has
been corrupted by death; that which spent in the delight of paradise has
been moved to this diseased and toilsome place; that which was reared
with passionlessness has exchanged for the passionate and perishable
life; that which was unmastered and self empowered is now lorded by such
and so many evils, that it is not even easy to number our tyrants. For
each of the passions in us, whenever it prevails, becomes master of the
one enslaved; and as some tyrant, having taken the acropolis of the
soul, harms the subject through the very ones under the hand, using our
reasonings as servants toward what seems good to itself; so anger, so
fear, cowardice, boldness, the passion according to grief and that
according to pleasure, hate, strife, unmerciful,


harshness, envy, flattery, remembering of evil and lack of feeling, and
all the passions thought in us according to the contrary, is a numbering
of some tyrants and masters, who enslave the soul as some one taken by
spear toward their own power. And if someone should also reckon the
disasters about the body, those woven together with our nature and
twisted together, I mean the various and many formed kinds of diseases,
of all of which the human was without experience according to the
beginnings, he will much more increase the tear, contemplating in
parallel the painful instead of the goods, and setting the evils beside
the better. This then the one who blesses mourning seems to teach in
secret, that the soul look toward the true good, and not be baptized
down by the present deceit of life; for it is not possible either to
live without tears for the one who has looked through the affairs
through accuracy, or to think himself to be in painful things the one
who deepens himself in the pleasures of life; as such a thing is
possible to see upon the irrational things; for whom the construction of
the nature is pitiable; (for what is more pitiable than the deprivation
of the word?) but they have no perception of the disaster, but the life
is carried through for them too according to some pleasure; and the
horse exults, and the bull dusts himself; and the pig bristles the
mane; and the puppies play, and the calves leap about, and it is
possible to see each of the animals indicating the pleasure through some
proofs, for whom if there were some thinking down of the grace of the
word, they would not have disposed their mute and wretched life in
pleasure. So also upon the humans, for whom there is no knowledge of the
goods =44.1229= of which our nature has been deprived, for these the
spending of the present life is according to pleasure. And it follows
for the one who is pleased with the present things, not to seek the
better. And the one who does not seek would not find that which comes to
be present only to those who seek. Therefore for this the Word blesses
the mourning, not judging it to be blessed through itself, but through
that which comes to be from that. And the yoking of the word shows that
the mourning is blessed for them of the reference toward the
consolation. For blessed, he says, are those who mourn and he did not
stop the word in this, but added, For they shall be consoled. Which the
great Moses seems to me to have thought of beforehand (or rather, the
Word arranging these things in that one, in the mystical observations of
the Pascha); to legislate unleavened bread for them in the festal days;
and to make bitter herbs a relish for the eating, so that through such
riddles we may learn that it is not possible to share that mystical
feast otherwise, unless the bitter herbs of this life should be mixed
willingly with the simple and unleavened life. For this the great David
too, although seeing about himself the topmost measure of human good lot
(I mean the kingdom); abundantly applies the bitter herbs to his own
life, groaning in wailing, and lamenting the stretching of the sojourn
in flesh, and failing, from the desire of the greater things, he says,
Woe is me that my sojourn has been lengthened. And elsewhere looking
intently toward the beauty of the divine tents, he says he fails from
the desire, judging it more honorable for himself to have been ranked
there in last things, than to be first among the present. And if someone
should wish to think down more accurately upon the power of this
mourning that is blessed, let him look for himself in the narrative
according to Lazarus and the rich one, in which such a doctrine is made
clearer to us more nakedly. For remember, Abraham says toward the rich
one, that you received your goods in your life; and likewise Lazarus the
evils; for this this one is consoled, and you are pained. For it seems,
since the ill counsel, or rather the evil counsel, has stood us away
from the good economy of God about the human being (for God having
legislated in our enjoyment the good unmixed of the evil, and having
forbidden to mix with the beautiful the trial of the evil, since we
willingly from gluttony were filled of the contrary (I mean having
tasted of the disobedience of the divine Word); for this it is necessary
altogether for human


nature to come to be in both, and to share in part =44.1232= both of
that which grieves and of that which gladdens. And there being two
lives, and a double life being contemplated peculiarly according to each
of the lives, and likewise also a double gladness; the one in this life,
and the other in that which lies before us according to hopes; it would
be to be blessed to store the portion of the gladness with the true
goods into the everlasting life; and to fulfill the service of the grief
in this short and seasonal life, making a loss not the being deprived of
some of the pleasant things according to this life, but the coming to be
in a falling away of the better through the enjoyment of these.
Therefore if it is to be blessed to have the gladness unending and
forever stretched in the infinite ages; and human nature must altogether
taste also of the contrary things; it is no longer hard to see together
the will of the word, why blessed are those who mourn now, for they
themselves will be consoled into the infinite ages; and the consolation
comes to be from the participation of the Paraclete. For the grace of
the consolation is a proper activity of the Spirit; of which may we too
be counted worthy, by grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom is the
glory into the ages of the ages. Amen.
ORATION IV.
Blessed are those who hunger, and thirst the righteousness; for they
shall be filled. Those who are stomach sick and of bad appetite, the
ones skilled of medicine say, when some wicked humors and superfluities
have flowed together upon the upper belly, always seem to be full and
sated, and for this to hold themselves alien toward the beneficial food,
their natural appetite having withered in the bastard fullness. But if
some care from medicine should be brought upon them, the things shut in
the hollows of the stomach having been washed out through some cutting
drug making, thus it happens, the alien no longer troubling the nature,
that the appetite of the helping and nourishing eating comes back to
them; and this is a sign of the health, no longer to admit the eating by
constraint, but with desire and impulse. What then does the preface will
for me? Since going forward through sequence the word that leads us by
the hand toward the higher of the ladder of the beatitudes, the one
according to the voice of the Prophet disposing the good ascents in our
heart, shows us after the ascents already finished such a fourth other
ascent, saying: Blessed are those who hunger and thirst the
righteousness, for they shall be filled; I think it is well, having
cleansed as it is possible the sated and filling of the soul, to make in
ourselves the appetite that is blessed of such eating and drinking;
=44.1233= for it is not possible for the human being to be strong,
unless the sufficient food underprops the power, nor to be filled of
food without the eating, nor to be nourished without appetite. Since then
power is some good according to the life; and this is kept together by
the sufficient satiety; and the satiety comes to be through eating; and
the eating from appetite; the appetite would be to be blessed for those
who live, beginning and cause of the power that comes to be in us. And
as according to this sensible food we have, not all desire the same
things, but the desire of those who share is often divided toward the
forms of the eatables, and the one is delighted with the things that
sweeten, another has the impulse toward the things that sting and heat;
and another is pleased with the salty, and another with the things that
astringe; and it often happens that the impulse of the eating does not
come to be for each according to the profitable (for having a lean
toward some passion according to some peculiarity of mixture, one feeds
the disease by the quality of the foods against one another; but if he
should have the impulse toward the things that help, he will altogether
live in health, the food


keeping together for him the good condition); so also upon the food of
the soul not all the desires lean toward the same. For some desire glory,
or wealth, or some worldly conspicuousness, for others the appetite is
busy about the table, others take up envy, as some poisonous food,
eagerly; and there are some for whom that which is beautiful by nature
comes to be in appetite. And this is beautiful by nature always and for
all, which is not chosen for the sake of some other thing, but itself
through itself desirable, always holding itself the same, and never
being blunted by satiety. For this the Word blesses not those who simply
hunger, but those for whom the desire has the lean toward the true
righteousness. What then is the righteousness? for this I think must
first be uncovered through the word, so that, the beauty according to it
having been made plain, thus the appetite may be moved in us toward the
season of that which has appeared. For it is not possible to hold
oneself desirefully toward that which does not appear, but our nature is
somehow idle and unmoved upon the unknown, unless it should take some
thought of the desirable through hearing or sight. Some then of those
who have examined such things say that righteousness is a habit
distributive of the equal, and of that according to worth for each. For
example if someone should become lord of a distribution of monies, the
one looking toward the equal, and measuring the giving with the need of
those who share, is called just; and if someone having taken the
authority of judging should not bring the vote toward some favor and
hatred, but following the nature of the affairs, should both punish the
worthy, and bring the saving vote toward those without blame, and should
make the judgment of the remaining disputes in truth, this one too is
called just. And the one assigning the tributes to those under the hand,
whenever he should lay on the tribute measured to the power, and a
master of a house, and a ruler of a city =44.1236= and a king of nations,
if each of these should lead those subjected correspondingly, not being
moved by irrational impulses from authority, but judging the subject
with straightness, and fitting himself in judgment toward the choices of
those under the hand; all such things they assign to the account of
righteousness, those who define the just by such a habit. But I, looking
toward the height of the divine legislation, conjecture that something
more than the things said is thought in this righteousness. For if the
saving word is common toward all human nature, and it is not of every
human being to be in the things said (for to reign, to rule, to judge,
to come to be in authority of monies, or of some other economy, is of
few; and the multitude is in those under the hand and being economized);
how would one accept that true righteousness to be that which does not
lie before equally in honor to all by nature? For if the aim for the
just according to the outer accounts is the equal; and the superiority
has the unequal; it is not possible to think the given account of
righteousness true, being at once convicted by the unequal according to
life. What then is the righteousness that reaches into all? Whose desire
lies before common to everyone looking according to the evangelical
table; whether someone is rich, or poor, or is a slave, or lords, or
well born, or bought with silver, no circumstance either increasing or
drawing back the account of the just. For if such a thing should be
found only in the one who is advanced according to some authority and
superiority; how is Lazarus just, the one thrown beside the gate of the
rich one, who has no matter toward such righteousness, not office, not
authority, not a house, not a table, not some other preparation toward
life, through which it is possible to work that righteousness? For if
the just is to be in ruling, or distributing, or economizing something
at all, the one who is not in those things is altogether outside the
just. How then is the one who has had none of these things, through
which righteousness is characterized according to the account of the
many, counted worthy of the rest. Therefore the righteousness to be
sought by us is that, of which the one who has desired has the enjoyment
in promise. For blessed, he says, are those who hunger the
righteousness; for they shall be filled. Many and manifold things lying
before into


participation, upon which human nature has the appetitive impulse, there
is much need for us of the science, so as to discern in such eatables
the nourishing and the poisonous, so that that which seems to be taken
up by the soul in the part of food may not work death and corruption for
us instead of the life. And perhaps it is not untimely, through some
other of the things sought according to the Gospel, to articulate the
thought about this. The one who has shared with us in all things without
sin =44.1237=, and has shared together with us in the same passions, did
not judge the hunger a sin, nor did he push away from his own trial the
passion according to it, but he received the appetitive impulse of
nature that comes to be upon the food. For remaining without food forty
days, later he hungered; for he gave when he willed a time to nature to
work its own things. But the inventor of the temptations, when he knew
the passion according to hunger having come to be in that one too,
advised to welcome the appetite with stones; and this is, to turn aside
the desire from the food according to nature upon the things outside
nature. For say, he says, that these stones may become breads. For what
has the farming done injustice? And for the sake of what have the seeds
been abominated, so that the food from these should be dishonored? And
why is the wisdom of the Creator condemned, as not fittingly nourishing

Blessed are the merciful, for they shall be shown mercy. Perhaps it is
some such thing, which through some vision Jacob was trained through a
riddle, seeing a ladder reaching from earth upon the heavenly height,
and God established upon it, such as now the teaching through the

according to virtue is formed for the patriarch by the form of the
ladder, so that he himself may both learn and guide those after him,
that it is not possible otherwise to be raised toward God, unless always
looking toward the things above, and having the desire of the high

established upon every blessedness. And altogether, as to the =44.1249=

For blessedness is as truly proper of God; therefore Jacob also

participation then of the beatitudes is nothing other than a sharing of

that which is truly to be blessed is the godhead; that which is thought

advises through his own words; and our aim is, not how we may be
persuaded to reach after the beautiful things (for this lies of itself
in human nature, to hold itself leaning toward the beautiful); but how
we may not miss the judgment of the beautiful. For in this part
especially our life is at fault, in not being able accurately to
understand what is the beautiful by nature, and what is supposed such
