Blessed indeed is the race of poets, and it is free of troubles on every side.
For not only is it allowed them to set up such subjects as they themselves may
wish each time, neither true nor sometimes even plausible, nor even having any
coherence at all, if one should wish to look rightly, but they also handle
them as they please with thoughts and arguments, some of which, if one should
take away what is before them and after them, it is not even possible to learn
what they mean by themselves. Yet when all has been said together, we have
understood and accepted them, as if content that we understood. Some they
begin and then leave the rest, as if they had condemned it; others they
deprive of a beginning; of others they take out the middle and think it
enough, as if they were some tyrants over thoughts. Then nothing is too bold
or too hard for them, but they hoist gods from the machine, and put them
aboard to sail with whom they please, and they make them not only sitting with
men, if it so happens, but also drinking with them and holding lamps and
making light. And they are so magnificent in these things, from which this
whole speech has started, that they are blessed according to Homer himself,
living at ease, whenever they make the hymns and paeans to the gods. The
strophes or periods have filled the whole. And having said sea-girt Delos, or
Zeus who delights in thunder, or the loud-roaring sea, and having gone on to
how Heracles came to the Hyperboreans, and how Iamus was an ancient seer, or
how Heracles overcame Antaeus, or having added Minos, or Rhadamanthys, or
Phasis, or

the Ister, or that they themselves are nurslings of the Muses and some
invincible in wisdom, having cried this out, they think they have hymned
sufficiently for themselves, and not even any of the private men asks more of
them. So strongly do we hold them sacred and honor them that we have even
conceded to them the very making of hymns to the gods and the addressing of
them, as if they were truly prophets of the gods. And even that whose office
it is to set forth what is fitting rightly and to handle it with examination
in all things and to go through all precision as far as is possible for a man,
we think we need not use at all toward the gods, but for all other contests we
use speech, and we praise festivals and we narrate deeds of men and wars, and
we make myths, and we contend in the lawcourts, and to all things, so to
speak, speech comes to us, but toward the gods themselves who gave it to us we
do not think it altogether right to use it. Yet concerning the rites
themselves and the sacrifices that are performed we have distinguished in
prose what must be done, having written laws. But hymns we do not think we
should make in this way. Is it that the poets need the gods, and we what it is
not lawful to say? But this at least is witnessed even by the poets
themselves, that all men have need of the gods. So it is reasonable that all
should honor them from the power that belongs to each. But are the poets alone
dear to the gods, and from them do the gods most gladly receive the gifts? Why
then did we not also make the poets alone priests of the gods? But because, by
Zeus, the prophets of the gods too, as many as are mantic, signify in measure
what must be done? Yet the greater part, by Zeus, is without measure: the
prophetess herself at Delphi, the priestesses at Dodona, Trophonius, the
dreams from Asclepius and Sarapis. But indeed it is more according to nature
for a man to use prose speech, just as also

to walk, I think, rather than to be carried riding. For measures did not come
first, and then speech and conversation were found, nor did poets come into
being and then arrange the names that must be used, but when names and prose
speech already existed, for the sake of some grace and persuasion the
craftswoman of these things, poetry, later entered in. So that if we honor
nature, we would be honoring the arrangement and will of the gods themselves,
and we would make the earlier and more ancient and, even according to the
poets themselves, the better, more honorable, by addressing with such an
address the gods who arranged all these things, just as we are not ashamed to
address ourselves without measures.

I have said these things not dishonoring the race of poets nor taking away the
rank it has, but from the very things conceded to them showing that we too
would justly add these as well, like victims besides those already there. And
if what is according to nature, of whatever you may speak, is more pleasing to
the gods, we would more reasonably please them by honoring them thus than in
that other way, and the gods would honor us more if we should give the rights
of age to the same things they did; since even measure itself grants the fair
name to the poets, but in use it is far more ours. For there the iambic verse
only measures whether it fills the beat, but here it measures out the whole
speech and truly goes through all of it, and it even begins at once from the
name. For it allows neither to overshoot nor to come short of the worth, but
commands that each be given what is due. Then it does not allow extra things
to be thrown in, which is ridiculous, for the sake of the measure; then coming
to the periods it demands self-sufficiency, the hardest of all measures, I
think, and upon all this, as I said before, it looks to see if the whole has
proportion, and if it has fulfilled the aim that was set. Just as

therefore the measures in the marketplace with which we deal are, I think,
this which is so called, yet we would not say that hitting the measure belongs
most of all to the shopkeepers, because they possess a choenix and a cotyle,
but these are the particular measures, or rather names, so to speak, of
measure, while the whole measure, toward which even these are often needed and
to which one must refer everything concerning every matter, and that which is
truly worth everything and leads to virtue, we would say is something else
both to know and to have: so also here the measures among the poets are these
particular ones, I think, yet the whole measure is not with them. And
especially one must not hesitate because of the fair name, as if the poets
alone knew the measure of speeches. For it is possible even without such
measures to hit the measure, and the opposite, just as a doctor without
weights and measures can calculate what is needed, and again one who uses
these can miss through inexperience of the matter for which the measures are
needed. I am not unaware, as I said, that it is much easier to do these things
through song than in speech, and that such contests have been conceded to the
poets; reasonably. For they have many advantages and they have full power to
do whatever they wish. But we may neither open a jar of hymns nor a Muses'
chariot nor a merchant-ship of ten thousand measures nor clouds, nor griffins
nor any such thing, nor be bold nor throw in a speech outside the matter, but
we must truly remain in the measure and remember ourselves throughout, as one
keeping rank on campaign. Nevertheless we must try at least to complete the
address, especially as we are fulfilling a vow, since we have been saved, and
at the same time it is not fitting to linger discussing measure. Well then.
For everyone at every time calls you as helper, Sarapis; grant us to speak the
speech

graciously; nothing at all is impossible for you, both to furnish a beginning
that is dear to you, and as you gave the first things, so having heard become
also the second. For all things everywhere that we would most wish to happen
to us happen through you and because of you.

Who the god is and of what nature, let it be left to the priests and the
learned of the Egyptians to say and to know; but of how many and of what kind
of goods he is shown to be the cause for men, we would praise him sufficiently
by speaking as in the present, and at the same time it is possible to examine
his nature through these same things. For if we say what someone can do and
what he distributes, we have almost said both who he is and of what nature he
is. For he is not other than such as he appears and is shown from his works.
What then are the works of Sarapis? This no longer seems to me to need a human
answer. But Homer says he would not be able, even taking ten tongues and ten
mouths, to tell the number of the ships that followed the Achaeans, unless the
Muses should tell him and lead the speech; but for me the works and gifts of
the greatest of the gods, always being accomplished toward men, would surely
be quite impossible even for one who had acquired the mouths of all men and
all human speech there is, unless some gods should truly become leaders of the
speech. And so let the gods be called again, and let us speak the works of the
god: for he wishes and grants to speak of those things by which the life of
men is administered and saved; these are the works of Sarapis. One must begin,
they say, from the beginning. These three things are observed in every man and
hold life together: soul, body, and whatever outside these comes into use.
Over all these this god is set, both leading us from the beginning into light
and into his own beginning, and taking thought for those who have been born
how each thing will be theirs, adorning our soul with wisdom, which alone

shows to men their kinship with the gods and by which we differ from the other
mortals or living things, which gave men the very notion of the gods and found
temples and rites and all honors, and further taught and established laws and
government and all devices and all arts, and gave the distinction of false and
true, and, so to speak, made life. The soul, as I said, he purifies with
wisdom, and the body he saves by giving health, without which it is possible
neither to use the goods of the soul nor to enjoy any other good fortune. And
it has such power and honor among gods and men that from the houses of the
gods it does not even go out, not before at least the stars would leave heaven
and heaven itself would leave its own place, and of men those whom it leaves
while living are more pitied than those who have died. And he alone is equally
honored by kings and equally by private men, equally by the wise and the base,
by the great and the lesser, by those who fare well and the opposite, giving
to the former the enjoyment of the happiness they have, and to the latter
becoming himself alone in place of the other hardships. And the acquisition of
wealth, which after health is most sought by men, this too Sarapis gives
without wars and battle and dangers; so throughout all of life he is gracious
to us, and no idle region has been left to this god, but with the things by
which one must think of a man, he examines all and in every part has a
benefaction, beginning from the soul and ending in the outward prosperities.
And he has made life for us like a harmony and joined it with his gifts,
making us love wisdom through health, and making health sweeter with
prosperity, and fastening the extremes with the middle as with a peg, and
securing by health both the goods of the soul and the enjoyment of what
exists. How then should we not proclaim this one

in festivals and on all days as guardian and savior of all men, a
self-sufficient god?

And indeed, holding together for men the whole measures of life and being
steward of what is livable, in this he would justly be thought to have
embraced all things and as it were to steer all our life. The citizens of the
great city by Egypt even call this one Zeus, because he has not been left
behind in surplus power, but he has come through all things and has filled the
whole. For of the other gods the powers and the honors are divided, and men
call upon different ones for different things, but he like a chorus-leader of
all has beginnings and ends. And he alone is also ready for the one who needs
something to call upon this one; so that men too are not carried in the same
way concerning the others, but some honor some gods and others others, yet
this one alone all alike count among their own. For because he has the powers
of all, some serve this one in place of all, and others, whatever they honor
for whatever purpose, also add this one as the common and chosen of the whole
earth. And not only does this one, being one, have all the powers on earth in
each, nor is he stationed like one god in one place, nor according to Homer's
lot did he take a third share, as that poet said Zeus and Poseidon and Pluto
divided, but this god is great also in the sea, and merchant ships and
triremes are steered under him, and in the aether and the clouds; that earth
and sea are his care, the argument of necessity proves. Homer then said that
the earth and Olympus were left in common to the gods, but he himself appears
as a distinguished allottee of earth and in turn a partner with each in the
others' own portions, himself one being all things to all, one able for the
same, accomplishing all deeds and all prosperities

in all times and places, holding, the poets would say, the keys of earth and
sea, since even after the necessary end of life this one still remains as
ruler for men. And one must go to him from him, that saying from home to home,
as to himself. And he is the one who divides the places to each according to
the worth of their way of life on earth and of the justice of what comes
after, by day going over the things above the earth, by night making the
judgments unseen by the living, himself savior and conductor of souls, leading
into light and receiving again, encompassing all everywhere. And if of any of
the gods, a man having mentioned Sarapis too is filled at once with cheer and
with fear. For he himself is the most loving of mankind among gods and the
most terrible, having the profitable fear for men, so that they may neither do
harm to one another nor suffer it from one another. Yet he is turned more
toward pity, and what Homer said of all the gods, that they are to be turned
and to be entreated, this one most of all confirms, so many turns he turns for
the salvation of those who always need him. And further, in sacrifices men
share with this god alone in a distinctive way the exact communion, calling
him to the hearth and setting him forth as both guest and host, so that while
others fill some contribution-feasts and others others, he is the common
filler of all contribution-feasts, holding the rank of symposium-leader for
those who always gather in the same place. As Homer said Athena herself both
poured the libation and accomplished each thing, he himself being both
fellow-libationer and the one who receives the libations, coming to the revel
and calling revelers to himself, who dancing under him the dance free of
evils, having brought home with the wreaths the good cheer, render the second
by calling upon him. And similar also is the communion with him in the other
things, of equal honor, not toward an equal, such as

of merchants and shipmasters, not only bringing up tenths, but also making him
an equal sharer, as a fellow-merchant and partner of all that is in between;
to such an extent is he mixed in among men. This is the one who is truly
steward of the winds far more than the islander whom Homer made, this one lord
both to still and to raise whichever he will; this one sent up drinkable water
in the midst of the sea, this one raised those who were lying down, this one
showed the much-sought light of the sun to those who saw, whose sacred chests
of sacred books have countless numbers. And the marketplaces, they say, and
the harbors and the open spaces of the cities are full of those recounting
each thing. But for me as I try to speak, an endless multitude of days pouring
in will keep the catalogue unfinished all the same. For his works have not
stood still, nor are they as many as have come to be, but day and night others
come on after others. And as of all time advancing each day it is not possible
to say what has been spent, but always the equal remains, so of the works the
god works it is not possible to say whether those we have received are the
more, or those that will be; so many come to be. If then someone should try to
catalogue these, he will chase what is fleeing, like those who chase the
streams of rivers from behind, and he will do the same as if he thought it
right to live all time; for he will need it entirely.

But these things others will cry at different times, some seeing them in
themselves, others in others. And let it already have been said by us in a
common speech as in a proclamation of libations. There is no fear at all that
we shall lie. What we laid down at the beginning is shown through all things:
that those things by which the human race is led are the works of Sarapis, and
that his power never escapes us, but both saves, and afterward there is the
providence from him.

He also stands over the birth and nourishment of all living things, and many
of the sacred animals, like men, live with a view to him. This one leads the
Nile in the season of summer, this one calls it back in winter, this one has
the two and forty temples throughout Egypt, this one holds together and adorns
all the temples on earth, guardian of the things seen and of the secret,
leader of men and of daimons; and he will already turn our speech toward
himself, and it is time for those who have addressed him to make an end. O you
who hold the fairest of the cities you watch over, which fills for you the
yearly festival, O light common to all men, and recently become manifestly so
to us, when the sea was flooding in and much was rising from every side and
nothing was seen but the destruction that was coming and almost already
present, you raised your hand, and you revealed the hidden heaven and you gave
earth to see and to put in to shore, so far beyond hope that even after we had
stepped ashore there was no belief. For these things much thanks to you, O
much-honored one, and now do not abandon me, but save me surely, and look
brightly on this hymn as made in such circumstances, a thank-offering for
those things before, and a supplication and entreaty concerning the things to
come, that they become sweeter and better than the present.
