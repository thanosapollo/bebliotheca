* 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

While I was airing the clusters in the sun, Mania came up, mincing and putting
on airs, and pelted me with many jests. I had long been burning for her, and I
meant to do something hot. So when I gladly caught hold of her, coming near I
left the grapes alone, and creeping up I very gladly harvested her bloom. Let
this be for you, by Pan, the great mysteries.

* 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Himeron the soft one struck his leg very hard on the rocky ground, and fever
seized him, and a swelling rose in the groin. I would rather he recovered than
that I should have four medimni of dried figs. Greet from me the ewe with the
soft wool, the one I praise to you, and the two little oxen and the dog, and
tell Mania herself to be well.

* 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Sekyle at your place wrongs me, taking from the sheaves and stealing. If she
stops, all will be well and we shall remain friends; if she keeps at the work,
I will sue you for damage. For my ancestors' tombs would reasonably groan if
I, Eupeithides of Corydallus, look on while I am insulted, and that by a slave
worth perhaps two minae.

* 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

What fine thing have you done, and what useful labor have you accomplished? I
drove in a row of vines, then planted tender young fig shoots beside them, and
round about the farmyard I set olive slips. Then I had dinner and pea
porridge, drained three stout cups, and gladly went to sleep.

* 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

My bees' hives are empty, and they have left the hearth, though until now they
were no runaways. They used to remain faithful and dwell in their own hives as
in homes, and they had a dewy meadow rich in flowers, and we feasted them with
a full banquet; and they, from their excessive industry, feasted us in return
with much fine honey, and they were never barren of this sweet labor. Now they
have gone away, though we hurt them in nothing, no, by Aristaeus and Apollo
himself. They are fugitives, their house is bereft, and the flowers in the
meadow grow old unused. When I remember their flight and their graceful dance,
I think of nothing else than that I have been robbed of daughters. So I am
angry at them: why did they leave their fosterer, who was truly a father and a
guardian and not an ungrateful keeper? I must track down their wandering, and
where they have run off and sit, and who received them, this too: for he has
those who do not belong to him. Then when I find them I will reproach the
ungrateful and faithless ones at length.

* 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And where is one to turn the stream aside, if it is neither to be thrown into
the road nor to pass over into the neighbors' land? You will not, I suppose,
order us to drink it up. It was said long ago that a bad neighbor is a bad
thing, and it is believed now not least in your case. But your violence will
get you nothing: we will not sell you the plot, and first the master will sue
you over these matters, if his mind is sound.

* 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

I do not praise you because you say you are beautiful, nor because you say you
have many lovers. Perhaps those men admire you for your looks, but you please
me because of your name, and I praise you as I praise my ancestral land, and I
have marveled at the invention of the man who so named you, so that not only
the people in the city should go mad over you, but the country folk too. What
wrong do I do in making trial of the fruit? Since, among other things, the
name is an invitation to love, and that for a man living by farming. So I have
sent you figs and grapes and new wine from the vats, from your namesake in the
country; and in spring I will send roses too, the fruit of the meadows.

* 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Whether you are in earnest about my name or joking, you yourself know; but I
do not think it fitting that you should play the coquette toward me with what
you send. Fine gifts of yours: nuts worth two obols, and insolent wine from
its youth. Phrygia might drink it; I drink Lesbian and Thasian, and I need
money. To send fruit for fruit is bringing fire upon fire. And it is no worse
for you to know this as I myself think. For my name is also the cause of my
making money from those who wish to come to me; it teaches me that the beauty
of bodies too is like fruit. So while it is in its prime, it is fitting to
receive the favor in return for it; but if it falls away, what else would we
be than a tree stripped of fruits and leaves at once? And yet nature grants
those to bloom again; a courtesan has only one harvest. So from this one must
store up for old age.

* 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

I learned late that you advised me well, teaching me to run from the
courtesans. They gape to take, and they pretend to love, and they shut you out
continually, and what is heaviest of all to me, they do not want to sleep with
you until they are overfull and sated, but they put on airs and mince, then
they go at the food again, and secretly they devour everything and gulp it
down more than the farm hands in the field, but when we are present they play
pretty. I do all I can and hurry, lifting both legs, to run from them back to
the goats again. I would have taken that Thebais who deserves the worst
destruction, while she was piping to me, lifted her by the waist, thrown her
on the little bed, and gone at the business. May the soldier who stopped me
perish. Thrasyleon, I think, was his name, or some other such name compounded
with a beast.

* 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

I have learned that your pig is a lecher. Why then did you not seize him by
force and castrate him, as we usually do with the he-goats? For this, you
know, persuades even the animals to keep quiet and be very chaste. And I am
skilled in these things, of course: I will show him whole at once, packing him
with salt and smearing him with pitch; then he will be healthier than Croton,
I suppose, and a gourd, and he will stop being in love and wearing out your
substance. Who in his right mind would keep an uncastrated wanton?

* 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

The young men keep dogs to hunt good game, but I have never seen a hare
swifter or more fleshless. It is a wonder how I even caught him. For when he
was flayed and the skin stripped off, then he became visible, or rather (for I
do not speak correctly) he is still more invisible now. And you, Tryphe, stop
giggling at me; for if your father sees you, you luckless girl, you will
certainly get something bad. I am afraid of him even when he is here; but you,
I do not know how, have grown spoiled and despise him.

* 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

We will nail up the hare's hide, Lamprias, so that this may be a trophy of
your hunts, in the manner of those great hunters, I suppose. Your good hunting
will be recorded too. Did you catch it yourself, or receive it as a gift? And
how was it even seen at first, given its smallness? The dogs must truly have
had good noses; for it could not be seen, but they scented it. And since you
began hunting you have become Hippolytus himself to us. See that Aphrodite
does not grow angry at you too for your disdain.

* 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

Country life is fine in other ways too, and especially in the gentleness of
character; for quiet and leisure produce a fine mildness in those of the
earth. But you, I do not know how, are rustic and not a good neighbor to your
neighbors. At any rate you pelt us with clods and wild pears and shout loudly
when you see a man as if chasing a wolf, and you are harsh and, as the saying
goes, a bitter neighborhood. If I were not farming my ancestral field, I would
gladly have sold it to flee a terrifying neighbor. But, my good Cnemon, put an
end to the perversity of your character, and do not let anger lead you into
forgetfulness, lest you go mad without knowing it. Let these be friendly
precepts from a friend, and a remedy for your character.

* 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

I ought to have made no answer; but since you are a busybody and force me
against my will to begin talk with you, I have at least gained this, speaking
to you through messengers and not to you yourself. So let this be for you the
so-called Scythian answer. I am mad and murderous and I hate the race of men.
Hence I pelt those who come onto the plot with clods and stones. I have
thought Perseus blessed in two ways: that he was winged and met no one, and
that he was above greeting anyone and saluting him. I envy him very much also
for that possession with which he made those he met into stones. If I had had
the good fortune to obtain it, nothing would be more plentiful than stone
statues, and I would have made you this first. What has come over you that you
try to train me and long to show me gentle, when I think such hostile thoughts
toward all? Hence I left the part of the plot by the road idle, and this of my
land is barren of fruits. But you show yourself one of those I cannot avoid,
and you are eager to have me as a friend when I am not even a friend to
myself. Why am I even a human being?

* 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

You differ in nothing from the accursed days, being so savage and solitary in
character, but even against your will you must become gentle to us, out of
respect for neighborhood and honor for the boundary gods, who are common. So I
am sacrificing to Pan, and I invite those of the Phylasians most suitable for
the rite. Among these I would want you too to come; and if you drink and share
the libations you will be somewhat milder. For Dionysus loves to wither angers
and put them to sleep, and to wake cheer; and this same god will be Paean for
you too, and will release you from unmixed bile, quenching with wine the fuel
of your wrath. And if you hear a pipe-girl perhaps, Cnemon, and fall into song
and slip into a tune, you will have something calm in your soul; and it would
not be worse if, when you are in wine, you even lift an arm to dance. And if
when drunk you should fall in with a girl calling for her maid, or trying to
find the nurse who was left behind, perhaps you will do something hot and a
youthful deed. It would not be out of place for something of that sort to
happen at Pan's sacrifice; for he too is very amorous and the sort to set upon
maidens. Unknit your brow, and relax this gloomy and cloudy look with good
cheer. This is a friend's advice, meaning well by you.

* 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

I write this back so that I may abuse you and discharge some of my anger at
you; for I especially needed you present, so that I might lay hands on you
myself. Why do you long to ruin me, why are you eager to destroy me by
inviting me to a feast and banquet? First, I shudder terribly at seeing many
and being with many; I flee a common sacrifice as cowards flee the enemy; and
I look with suspicion on wine as terribly strong to plot against the mind and
attack it. The gods, both the others and Pan, I greet and address only in
passing, and I sacrifice nothing, for I do not wish to trouble them. And you
shake pipe-girls and songs at me, you ridiculous man. I would not lay a hand
on you over these; but those other things of yours are fine too, to dance and
to keep hottest company with a girl. You seem to me ready even to leap into
fire and somersault onto swords, but be neither a friend to me when
sacrificing nor otherwise.

* 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

Did I not say that you found Plutus sharp-sighted and not blind, and you laugh
at me and the land and Fortune, since she takes care of honest men? At any
rate you have shown yourself one of those blessed people who they say in the
time of Cronus had everything from the earth of its own accord, and among them
an unstinting community prevailed, and simplicity of character, and they
inhabited one house, the whole place under heaven. Who is so fault-finding or
bad as to be vexed and think jealous thoughts when you have grown rich? May
the character of farmers never be filled with so much wickedness as to be
jealous of wealth and to have thoughts about money. Let that turn to wild
goats and to the orators in the lawcourts.

* 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

Leaving farm work and farming, our neighbor Laches has boarded a ship and they
say he sails the Aegean, and he measures other seas and rides the waves and
lives a seagull's life and fights with various winds. Cape after cape takes
him, and looking around for fat profit and thinking of sudden wealth he said a
long farewell to those little goats and his former shepherd's life. Though he
could live scantily, little by little, from the fields, he is not content with
what he has: he pictures Egyptians and Syrians and looks at the sample market,
and by Zeus he is much occupied calculating interest on interest and counting
money on money, and he inflames his mind and burns it with profit on the
double voyage. He does not think of storms or contrary winds or the
unsteadiness of the sea or the unseasonableness of the times. But even if we
gain little while laboring much, the land is far more stable than the sea, and
she is more trustworthy and has more secure hopes from herself.

* 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

I, lucky man, was sacrificing a wedding in vain and going about crowned when
there was no need, and I was flattering the gods within and without. The boy
brought the yoke down from the fields as if to bring the bride from town to
the ancestral plot; but he hired a pipe-girl he happened to be in love with,
put a bride's dress on her, and brought me back a wood-pigeon instead of a
dove, as they say, a courtesan instead of a bride. At first, being very modest
in a maidenly way and according to the custom of those being married, she hid
her trade; but with difficulty their cleverness and their devices against me
broke out. They shall not wholly despise me as if I were of brick, since I
will proclaim the fine bridegroom to the crows, unless he ceases from his
excessive luxury and ditches and breaks clods with me; and I will sell that
bride too for export, unless she herself also takes part in the work with the
Phrygian and the Thracian woman.

* 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

All fine things grow in the fields, and the earth is adorned with them and
feeds all. Some of the fruits last two years, others are seasonal dainties
that last only a little; of all these the gods are makers, and the earth
herself is mother and nurse at once. Justice and temperance also grow, and
these in the fields are the finest of trees, the most useful of fruits. So do
not despise farmers; for there is a wisdom here too, not dressed up in speech
nor beautifying itself with power of words, but very silent and declaring
virtue through life itself. If this has been written to you more wisely than
the countryside's provision, do not wonder; for we are neither Libyans nor
Lydians but Athenian farmers.
