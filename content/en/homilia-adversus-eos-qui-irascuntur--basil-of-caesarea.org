HOMILY 10.
Against those who are angry.
As with the precepts of medicine, when they come to pass with good aim and
according to the reason of the art, the benefit from them is shown especially
after the trial; so with the spiritual exhortations, whenever the precepts
especially receive the outcome as witness, then their wisdom and benefit
toward the correction of life and the perfection of those who obey is made
manifest. For hearing the Proverbs declaring expressly that "Anger destroys
even the prudent"; and hearing also apostolic exhortations: "Let all anger and
wrath and clamor be taken away from you with all malice"; and the Lord saying
that he who is angry with his brother without cause is liable to the judgment;
now when we have come to trial of the passion, not as having arisen in us, but
as having fallen upon us from without like some unexpected hurricane, then
especially we have recognized the wonder of the divine precepts. We ourselves
having given place to anger, as an outlet to a violent stream, but observing
in quiet the unseemly disturbance of those held by the passion, have
recognized in the works the good aim of the saying: that "A wrathful man is
not seemly." For whenever once the passion, having thrust aside the
reasonings, itself takes over the sovereignty of the soul, it utterly makes
the man a beast, and does not even permit him to be a man, no longer having
the help that comes from reason. For what venom is to venomous creatures, this
wrath becomes to those who have been provoked. They rage like dogs, they dart
like scorpions, they bite like serpents.
Scripture too knows to call those mastered by the passion by the names of the
beasts to which they have made themselves akin through wickedness. For it
addresses them as "dumb dogs," and "serpents," "offspring of vipers," and such
things. For those who are ready for mutual destruction, and for the harm
against those of the =31.356= same race, would reasonably be numbered with
beasts and venomous creatures, in whom there exists by nature an
irreconcilable hatred toward men.
Through wrath tongues are unbridled, and mouths ungated; hands without
self-control, insolences, reproaches, slanders, blows, and all the other
things that one could not even number, are passions that are offspring of
anger and wrath. Through wrath the sword also is sharpened; the death of a man
is dared from a human hand. Through this brothers have failed to know one
another, and parents and children have forgotten nature. For those who are
angry first fail to know themselves, then also all their intimates together.
For as torrents, flowing together toward the hollows, sweep away whatever they
meet; so the impulses of the angry, violent and unrestrainable, pass similarly
through all. Gray hair is not venerable to those in wrath, nor virtue of life,
nor kinship of race, nor previous favors, nor any other of the things held in
honor. Wrath is a short-lived madness. They even often throw themselves into
an evident evil, in their eagerness for revenge neglecting what is their own.
For as if goaded round about by a gadfly with the memory of those who have
grieved them, their wrath convulsing and leaping, they do not cease until they
either give some evil to the one who provoked them, or perhaps also take some
in addition, if it so happen; as often things that break down violently have
suffered something more than they have done, shattered against what resists
them.

Who could reach the evil? how those quick to wrath, it being kindled by any
chance pretext, shouting and growing savage, and rushing more shamelessly than
any venomous creatures, do not stand still until, through some great and
incurable evil, their anger having burst upon them like a bubble, the
inflammation is blown out. For neither the edge of a sword, nor fire, nor any
other of the fearful things, is sufficient to hold back a soul driven mad by
anger; no more than those possessed by demons, from whom those who are angry
differ in nothing, neither in form nor in the disposition of the soul. For as
they reach after returning grief for grief, the blood boils around the heart,
as if stirred and bubbling by the force of fire; and having bloomed out to the
surface, it has shown the angry man in another form, exchanging the one
customary and known to all, as some mask upon a stage. For their own familiar
and accustomed eyes are unrecognized; the glance is wandering, and already it
looks fire. And he whets the tooth, after the manner of swine that charge at
close quarters. The face livid and bloodshot; the bulk of the body swelling;
the veins bursting, the breath being shaken by the storm within. =31.357=
Harsh voice, and overstrained, and the speech inarticulate and falling out at
random, not proceeding by parts, nor in order, nor with clear meaning. But
when it has been kindled to the incurable, as a flame by abundance of fuel, by
the things that provoke, then, then indeed, there are spectacles neither
speakable in word nor bearable in deed to look upon: hands raised against
those of the same race, and brought down upon all parts of the body, and feet
leaping unsparingly upon the most vital parts, and everything that appears
becoming a weapon to the madness. And if they also find from the opposite side
an equal evil campaigning against them, another anger and a madness of like
honor; so then, having fallen together, they have done to one another and
suffered as many things as it is likely that those marshaled under such a
demon should suffer. For maimings of limbs, or even deaths, the fighters have
often carried off as prizes of anger. One began unjust hands, and the other
defended himself; and the one brought a counter-blow, and the other does not
yield. And the body is cut apart by the blows, but the wrath takes away the
perception of the pain. For they have no leisure for the perception of what
they have suffered, the whole of their soul having been moved toward revenge
on the one who grieved them.

Do not then heal evil with evil, nor attempt to surpass one another in
disasters. For in wicked contests the one who has won is more wretched,
because he goes away having the greater share of the sin. Do not then become
the fulfiller of an evil contribution, nor a more wicked payer of a wicked
loan. Did he insult in anger? Stop the evil by silence. But you, having
received that man's anger into your own heart as a stream, imitate the winds,
which by a contrary blast give back what was brought. Do not use the enemy as
a teacher, nor be zealous for that which you hate. Nor become as a mirror of
the irascible man, showing his form in yourself.
He is red; and you have not been reddened? Eyes bloodshot; and yours, tell me,
look with calm? Voice harsh; and yours gentle? Not even the echo in the
wildernesses has so been thrown back unimpaired toward the one who uttered, as
the insults turn back upon the reviler. Rather, the sound was given back the
same; but the reviling returns with an addition. For what sort of things do
they answer, insulting one another? The one said "obscure, and of the
obscure"; and the other called him a house-slave, son of house-slaves; this
one "poor," that one "vagrant"; this one "unlearned," that one "mad"; until
the insults, like arrows, fail them. Then, when they have slung out every
reviling through the tongue, so then they proceed to revenge through deeds.
For "wrath raises a fight," =31.360= and fight begets revilings, and revilings
blows, and blows wounds, and from wounds often deaths. Let us check the evil
from the first beginning, removing anger from the souls by every device. For
thus we would be able to cut out together with this passion, as with some root
and beginning, the greater part of evils. Did he revile? But you bless.
Did he strike? But you endure. Does he spit and count you as nothing? But you
take thought of yourself, that you have been constituted from earth, and you
will be dissolved into earth again. For he who has preoccupied himself with
these words will find every dishonor less than the truth. For thus you will
both make revenge impossible for the enemy, showing yourself unwounded by the
revilings; and you will procure for yourself a great crown of endurance,
making the madness of the other an occasion of your own philosophy. So that,
if you obey me, you will even be lavish of the insults. Did he say you are
obscure, and without glory, and a nobody from nowhere? But you say that you
yourself are earth and ashes. You are not more august than our father Abraham,
who called himself these things. Unlearned and poor and worth nothing? But you
call yourself a worm, and as having your origin from a dunghill, speaking the
words of David. Add to these also the noble thing of Moses. He, having been
reviled by Aaron and Miriam, did not plead against them to God, but prayed for
them.

Of whom do you wish rather to be a disciple, of the men beloved of God and
blessed, or of those filled with the spirit of wickedness? When a temptation
of reviling is moved against you, consider that you are judging yourself,
whether through long-suffering you draw near to God, or through anger you run
off toward the adversary. Give time to your reasonings to choose the good
portion. For either you have also benefited that man by the example of
meekness, or you have more severely avenged yourself through disdain. For what
could be more painful to the enemy, than to see his own enemy higher than
insults? Do not cast down your own mind, nor endure to become reachable to
those who insult. Let him bark against you to no purpose; let him burst upon
himself. For as he who strikes one who does not feel pain punishes himself
(for he has neither avenged himself on the enemy, nor stopped the wrath); so
he who reproaches the one who is not to be reviled cannot find consolation for
the passion.
On the contrary rather, as I said, he is even sawn asunder. For immediately
from the things present, as what is each of you addressed? The one a reviler,
but you magnanimous; the one irascible and harsh, but you long-suffering and
meek; the one will repent of what he uttered, but you will never repent of the
virtue. What need is there to say many things? Reviling has shut the kingdom
of the heavens against the one; for "Revilers =31.361= shall not inherit the
kingdom of God"; but silence has prepared the kingdom for you. For "he who has
endured to the end, this one shall be saved." But if you avenge yourself and
set yourself in equal array against the reviler, what will you even plead in
defense? That he provoked by beginning? And of what sort of pardon is this
worthy? For neither is the fornicator, transferring the cause to the harlot as
having stirred him toward the sin, accounted less worthy of condemnation.
Neither are there crowns without antagonists, nor falls without opponents.
Hear David saying: "When the sinner stood up against me, I was not provoked,
nor did I avenge myself, but I was made dumb and I was humbled, and I was
silent from good things." But you are provoked as at reviling that is base;
yet you imitate it again as a good. For see, you have suffered the things you
accuse. Or do you carefully look down upon the other's evil, and nowhere set
your own shame? Is insult a wicked thing? Flee the imitation. For the other's
having begun is not sufficient for an excuse. It is more just then, as I
persuade myself, even to increase the indignation, because he did not have the
example that brings one to sense; but you, seeing the angry man behaving
unseemly, did not guard yourself from his likeness, but you are indignant and
grow harsh and are angry in return; and your passion becomes an apology for
the one who went before. For by the very things you do, you both release him
from blame, and condemn yourself. For if wrath is a wicked thing, why did you
not turn aside from the evil? If it is worthy of pardon, why do you grow harsh
toward the one in wrath? So that, if you came second to the exchange, this
will profit you nothing. For neither in the contests for crowns is the one who
began the wrestlings crowned, but the one who prevails. And therefore not only
the one who led the way in the terrible thing is condemned, but also the one
who followed a wicked leader into the sin.

If he addresses you as poor, if he speaks truly, accept the truth; if he lies,
what is the thing said to you? Neither be puffed up toward praises beyond the
truth; nor grow savage toward insults that do not touch you. Do you not see
the arrows, how they are wont to fall away from things that are firm and
resistant, but in things soft and yielding to spend their force? Consider that
something of this sort is the matter of reviling also. The one who resists
receives it into himself; but the one who gives way and yields has dissolved,
in the softness of his manner, the wickedness borne against him. And why does
the address "poor" trouble you? Remember your own nature; that you entered the
world naked, and naked you will also depart.
And what is poorer than a naked man? You have heard nothing terrible, unless
you appropriate to yourself =31.364= the things said. Who was ever led away to
prison because of poverty? It is not being poor that is shameful, but not
bearing poverty nobly. Remember the Master; that "being rich, he became poor
for us." If he addresses you as foolish and unlearned, be reminded of the
Jewish insults, with which they reviled the true Wisdom: "You are a Samaritan,
and you have a demon." If then you grow angry, you have confirmed the
reproaches; for what is more foolish than anger? but if you remain without
anger, you have put the insulter to shame, having shown self-control in deed.
Were you slapped? For so also was the Lord. Were you spat upon? For so also
was our Master. For he did not turn away his face from the shame of spittings.
Were you slandered? For so also was the Judge.
Did they tear your little tunic? They stripped my Lord also, and they divided
his garments among themselves. You have not yet been condemned, you have not
yet been crucified. Many things are still lacking to you, that you may arrive
at imitation of him. Let each of these run under your thought, and let it
settle the inflammation. For such preparations and dispositions, taking away
as it were the leapings of the heart and the pulses, bring the reasonings back
to steadiness and calm. And this then is what was said by David: that "I
prepared myself, and I was not troubled." One must therefore settle the mad
and stricken movement of the soul by the memory of the examples of the blessed
men; how the great David meekly endured the drunken outrage of Shimei. For he
did not give occasion for anger to be moved, transferring the thought toward
God; that "The Lord said," he says, "to Shimei to curse David."
Therefore hearing "man of bloods, and man of lawlessness," he was not harsh
toward that man, but humbled himself, as though the insult were meeting him
according to desert. Remove these two things from yourself: neither judge
yourself worthy of great things, nor think that any man is far inferior to you
in worth. For thus wrath will never rise up in the dishonors brought upon us.
It is a terrible thing that one who has been benefited, and is indebted for
the greatest favors, should, in addition to being ungrateful, also begin
insult and dishonor. Terrible indeed, but it is a greater evil for the one
doing it than for the one suffering it. Let that man insult; but do you not be
insulted. Let the =31.365= words be a gymnasium for you toward philosophy. If
you are not bitten, you are unwounded. And if you do suffer something in the
soul, keep the grievous thing in yourself. For "in me," he says, "my heart was
troubled"; that is, the passion was not distributed toward the outside, but as
some wave broken within the shores was laid to rest.

Quiet for me the heart that barks and grows savage. Let the passions stand in
awe of the appearing of the reason in you, as the disorder among children
stands in awe of the presence of a venerable man. How then might we escape the
harm that comes from being angry? If we persuade wrath not to anticipate the
reasonings, but first take care of this, that it may never run out ahead of
the mind; and let us have it as a horse yoked under us, and obedient to reason
as to some bridle, nowhere stepping out of its own rank, but led by reason
wherever it may guide. For still the spirited part of our soul is serviceable
toward many of the works of virtue, when, as some soldier, having laid his
arms beside the general, it readily furnishes its helps toward the things
commanded, and is an ally to reason against sin. For wrath is a sinew of the
soul, producing in it tension toward standing fast in the good things.
For if ever it should find it relaxed by pleasure, as if tempering iron by a
dipping, it has made it austere and manly from being too soft and slack. For
unless you are wrathful against the wicked one, it is not possible for you to
hate him as much as is worthy. For one must, I think, have equal zeal both
concerning the love of virtue and concerning the hatred of sin. Toward which
wrath is especially useful; when, as a dog to a shepherd, so wrath following
upon the reasoning remains meek and manageable to those who benefit, and
easily called back by the reasoning, growing savage toward the alien both
voice and sight, even if it seem to have a show of kindness, but cowering when
the one familiar and dear to it has called out. This is the best and measured
cooperation of the spirited part with the prudent part of the soul.
For such a one will be irreconcilable and without truce toward those who plot;
never admitting friendship toward what harms, but always barking at and
tearing the plotting pleasure as some wolf. Such then is the benefit from
wrath for those who know how to handle it. Since each of the other powers
also, according to the manner of its use, becomes either an evil or a good to
the one who possesses it. For example, of the desiring part of the soul, the
one who has used it up for enjoyment of flesh and of the unclean pleasures is
abominable and unrestrained, but the one who has turned it toward the love of
God, and the desire of the =31.368= eternal good things, is enviable and
blessed. And again of the reasoning part, the one who handles it well is
prudent and intelligent; but the one who has sharpened the mind toward harm of
the neighbors is malicious and a worker of evil. Let us not then make the
things given us by the Creator toward salvation an occasion of sin for
ourselves. So then wrath also, when it is moved when it ought, and as it
ought, produces courage and endurance and self-control; but when it operates
contrary to right reason, it becomes madness. For this reason the Psalm also
admonishes us: "Be angry, and do not sin."

And the Lord holds out the judgment against the one who is angry without
cause; but he does not refuse the using of anger toward the things one ought,
as in the form of a medicine. For the "I will put enmity between you and the
serpent"; and the "Be at enmity with the Midianites," belongs to one teaching
us to use wrath as a weapon. For this reason Moses, the meekest of all men,
avenging idolatry, armed the hands of the Levites toward the slaughter of the
brothers. "Let each," he says, "put his sword upon his thigh, and pass through
from gate to gate, and turn back through the camp; and let each kill his
brother, and each his neighbor, and each the one nearest him." And a little
after: "And Moses said," he says, "You have filled your hands today to the
Lord, each in his son, and in his brother, that a blessing may be given upon
you." And what justified Phinehas? Was it not the just anger against those who
had committed fornication? who, being for the most part reasonable and meek,
when he saw the fornication of Zimri and the Midianitess taking place openly
and without shame, they not even covering the unseemly sight of the shame, not
enduring it, used wrath as was needed, driving the spear through both.
And Samuel, bringing into the midst Agag the king of Amalek, who had been
saved by Saul contrary to the command of God, did he not slay him under just
anger? Thus often wrath becomes a servant of good actions. And Elijah the
zealot, with a calculated and temperate wrath, for the benefit of all Israel,
put to death four hundred and fifty men, priests of the shame, and four
hundred men, priests of the groves, who ate at the table of Jezebel. But you
are angry with your brother without cause. For how is it not without cause,
when another is at work, yourself growing harsh toward another? And you do the
thing =31.369= of the dogs; who bite the stones, not touching the one who
throws. The one being worked upon is pitiable; but the one working is hateful.
Transfer the wrath there upon the murderer, the father of the lie, the worker
of sin; but also have compassion on the brother, because, if he remains in the
sin, he will be delivered with the devil to the eternal fire. And as the names
of wrath and of anger are different, so also the things signified by them
differ very much from one another. For wrath is as it were some kindling and a
sharp fuming of the passion; but anger is an abiding grief and a lasting
impulse toward repayment of those who have done injustice, as though the soul
were swelling toward revenge.

One must know then that men go wrong according to both dispositions; either
being moved madly and stricken against those who provoke, or lying in wait
deceitfully and with plots for those who have grieved them; both of which we
must guard against. How then might the passion not be moved toward the things
it ought not? How? If you have been trained beforehand in the humility which
the Lord both ordained in word and showed in deed; now saying: "He who wishes
to be first among you, let him be last of all"; and now enduring the one who
strikes, meekly and unmoved. For the maker and master of heaven and earth, who
is worshipped by all the intelligible and sensible creation, who "bears all
things by the word of his power," did not send him living into Hades, the
earth having been broken open under the impious man, but admonishes and
teaches: "If I have spoken evil, bear witness concerning the evil; but if
well, why do you beat me?" For if you have been accustomed to be last of all
according to the commandment of the Lord, when will you be indignant as having
been insulted contrary to your worth? When an infant child reviles you, you
make the revilings an occasion of laughter; and when one out of his mind from
phrenitis utters words of dishonor, you consider him pitiable rather than
worthy of hatred. It is not then the words that are wont to move the griefs,
but our disdain toward the one who has reviled us, and each man's imagination
concerning himself. So that if you take away both of these from your own mind,
the things borne are a noise sounding otherwise in vain.
Cease then from anger, and forsake wrath, that you may flee the trial of the
wrath which is revealed from heaven upon all ungodliness and unrighteousness
of men. For if by a temperate reasoning you are able to cut out the bitter
root of wrath, you will destroy together with this beginning many of the
passions. For the deceitful and suspicious, and untrusting and malicious, and
plotting and rash, and all the swarm of such =31.372= wicked things, are
offshoots of this vice. Let us not then introduce so great an evil into
ourselves; a sickness of soul, a darkening of reasonings, an alienation from
God, an ignorance of kinship, a beginning of war, a fullness of disasters, a
wicked demon being born in our very souls, and as some shameless inmate
occupying our inward parts beforehand, and shutting the entrance against the
Holy Spirit. For where there are enmities, strifes, wraths, factions,
contentions, producing unsilenced tumults in the souls; there the spirit of
meekness does not rest.
But having been persuaded by the exhortation of the blessed Paul, let us take
away all anger and wrath and clamor from us with all malice, and let us become
kind to one another and tenderhearted, awaiting the blessed hope that has been
promised to the meek (for "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the
earth"), in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the glory and the might unto the
ages. Amen.
