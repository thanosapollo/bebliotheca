"Terrible, friends, terrible and worthy of great caution is it that every
opinion whatsoever should be freely granted to those nearby, even if some
otherwise seem to be among the most suitable. For honors beyond desert do not
so much lead the many into goodwill toward those who honor them as they stir
them up to boldness against them. And these things still, though they are such
and of a kind not to be borne, I nevertheless knew I must bear. For that
Arius, being utterly wicked, still seemed to leave himself some room for
further advance in wickedness, prepared me to think it bearable, inasmuch as
he had not yet come to the very peak of his own wickedness. But he shall not
go unpunished, let him know this clearly, nor shall Arius rejoice in being so
bold against me. For he shall know that even old men have sinew for the
execution of justice. Therefore let him be banished by proclamation from us
all, and let a common anathema and an abomination be laid, both Arius himself
and all those whom the common demon has made to revolt with him, driving them
out of this holy church."

When the bishops were then divided among many opinions, it seemed good to the
emperor Constantine to hold a synod of all the bishops at Nicaea and to put a
stop to their contention with one another.

1.9a on l. 6 ff. Vita Constantini Cod. Angelic. A f. 17r immediately after
καταπαῦσαι above, end of 7a:

And when the appointed day came and they assembled, each declaring his own
judgment, the emperor was in their midst, waiting nevertheless for what the
common body would decide. But those around Hosius of Corduba and Alexander had
prepared the book ready, which all were required to subscribe. And its
composition in the very words is as follows:

"At Nicaea, metropolis of Bithynia, it seemed good to the victorious
Constantine Augustus to hold a synod of the holy bishops of the holy catholic
and apostolic church. We believe in one God, the Father almighty, maker of
heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible" and so on. "But those
who say, There was when he was not, and Before he was begotten he was not, and
that he came to be from things that were not, or who assert that he is of
another hypostasis or substance, or that the Son of God is created or
changeable or alterable, the catholic and apostolic church anathematizes."

Those men then having brought this book into the common assembly and requiring
all to vote in favor of the judgment, they also confirmed it, each ratifying
it by his own subscription. But those around Arius were present there
themselves as well, all of them. Yet they did not accept the pious faith.
Wherefore the emperor put forth a most pious judgment of this kind: that all
who would not consent to the common vote of the bishops, whether they were
presbyters, or deacons, or any others of the clergy, should be punished with
exile. And Philumenus was the one entrusted to put this into effect, holding
from the emperor the honor which the Romans are accustomed to call magister.
He therefore, bringing the book to Arius and those with him, ordered them to
choose whichever they wished, either to subscribe and have immunity, or,
refusing, to go into exile. But they chose to flee and to sink into the depth
of perdition, as was fitting for them.

2.1a on p. 12-13 Vita Constantini Cod. Angelic. A f. 22r Franchi de' Can. 94:

And Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, being greatly overpowered by the heresy of
the heteroousiasts, coming to Chalcedon together with Theognis, they met in
common to take counsel about what should be done; and they also join Maris.
And the three, already sitting together with one another in a certain chamber
of the church, held discourse about the matters at hand, and babbled about the
things concerning their heresy. When their judgment was therefore divided,
suddenly a great earthquake occurred around them and them alone; and a deep
gloom covered them about the third hour of the day; and there was an
unspeakable panic. But not even so were the thrice-accursed willing to take
warning, nor did they rise up, though the divine, being angry and displeased
at the things they were wickedly and godlessly plotting, was scourging the
lifeless earth, by the blow upon it refuting and overthrowing their daring and
madness, even if they did not even so choose to come to their senses. For that
the disaster happened to none of the others, but enclosed them alone whom the
practice of blasphemy held, how does it not furnish a clear proof of the
divine wrath stirred up against them? Alas for the hardening of Satan by which
the whole company of the heretics was held fast!

2.4a Vita Constantini Cod. Angelic. A f. 23 Franchi de' Cav. 95; see above p.
13, 31:

And Crispus, the eldest of Constantine's sons, born to him from the daughter
of Maxentius, serving as junior emperor to his father (for he held the honor
of Caesar) and displaying splendid deeds against the enemy, in the very flower
of his age, in the year following the synod held at Nicaea, died, to speak
summarily, all at once. But those filled with heresy and impiety say that he
found the end of his life at his father's hands, whose words, as being
exceedingly false, it would in truth have been just to pass over; but since I
have set myself with all zeal to record all the opinions rumored of old, it is
good to include this too in the present account, not so much for confirmation
as for the refutation of those who choose to speak falsehoods. For they say
that his stepmother Fausta furnished the cause of Crispus' murder through
erotic madness. For she, having fallen in love with the young man and already
being overcome by the passion, first wheedled him with varied words, drawing
him toward intercourse; but when he was altogether averse, not even enduring
the mere mention, and it was clear he would in no way be persuaded, then the
woman, already consumed with desire and not knowing what to do with herself,
turned to a plot against the one she desired, turning her love into hatred.
For, angered at the unfulfilled desire, she made the slaughter of him who was
no longer desired the remedy of her passion, eager to sate the longing with
murder. And
she persuades her own husband to kill his own son, making her own disease his,
and loading him with all manner of false words. And he, his reasoning
overtaken by anger at what he heard from his own wife, and unwilling to put
off anything for examination of the matter, becomes a Theseus to his son, and
in the same manner in which that man, by Phaedra's slander, killed Hippolytus,
who was born to him from Hippolyta the Amazon, in this manner he too
slaughtered Crispus by the false accusation of his own wife, his soul's
judicial faculty being thrown into confusion, and not even granting him any
speech. But justice did not fall asleep at the passion; for a fitting
punishment came around upon the stepmother. For she, having come again into
desire for one of the emperor's forerunners, whom the Romans are accustomed to
call cursores, and being caught in the act, is destroyed by her own husband,
who secretly ordered her eunuchs, whenever she should come into the bath, in
attending her, even though she refused, to drag out more time within,
relieving one another in succession, until the woman should faint from the
choking heat; and then to bring her out already drawing her last breaths,
beating their breasts as if she had left life not by a plot so much as by a
mishap of the bath; in order that both the work of the punishment might be
accomplished and the manner of it might hide the infamy of the cause. She then
brought this end of her injustice concerning the young man; but I do not know
whether Philostorgius, that most avid lover of falsehood, has spoken these
things truly against Constantine the victorious and pious, of whom it is not
easy to say how many virtues of body and of soul shone forth; for the man not
only had the glory from deeds of war, but was also adorned with the beauties
of the soul. This Constantine etc. † below 9a p. 20, 19

Ibid. f. 25v:

Constantine then, having reached Thrace, which was at that time especially
flourishing, and having learned that Byzantium was most excellently situated
both by land and by sea, settled there; and taking in the neck of the
peninsula (for the place is a peninsula), he built a wall from sea to sea,
enclosing both the suburbs and the nearby hills, so that the ancient city was
a small portion of the city being founded, by reason of the breadth of the
circuit. For where now the great porphyry column stands, the one that bears
his colossus, making his beginning from there, he accomplished all the rest
that spread toward either sea and the inland as a work of his own palm and
power. And I myself have heard this also from the oldest and most notable men
relating it, that Constantine, when he was marking out the gates of the wall
and leading those who followed from the measure at which the circuit was to
take its work, went forward, advancing on foot and carrying the spear in his
hand. And when, having first crossed one hill, he went toward a second, and
having passed this too he still advanced farther, measuring out a greater
length than seemed moderate to those following, one of those who had freedom
of speech with him came forward and asked: "How far, master?" And he answering
expressly: "Until," he said, "he who is before me stands still," so that it
became clear that one of the angels seemed to go before, handing over the
measures, inasmuch as this city was being founded very much as well-pleasing
to God, no less than Jerusalem of old; for indeed he was going to establish
here too a common council-house of piety. Until, then, wherever the appearing
form, going forward, then stood still and, dissolving, was invisible, there
Constantine too arriving planted the spear and said expressly: "As far as
here," where even now are the great gates of the city. And having founded the
city he called it Rome the Glorious, thus giving it the designation, according
to the Italian tongue, "Alma Roma." And he established a senatorial council in
it and assigned a most lavish grain-dole expenditure to the inhabitants, and
set in order the rest of the civic adornment in it, so as to suffice for a
rival glory to the former Rome.

2.11a on l. 7-10 Vita Constantini Cod. Angelic. A f. 36v:

And when the great Athanasius had been ordained, the great emperor
Constantine, receiving the vote of the city, was pleased and writes to the
Alexandrians this import:

"Having read the decree sent by you concerning the advancement of the bishop,
and having learned that the matter has gone according to the pleasure of you
all, and that the man is thus loved in harmony by all of you, I myself also am
pleased (for why should I not be?) and I judge that these things remain valid
and firm, which you yourselves have chosen in common by your vote."
