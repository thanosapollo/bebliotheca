OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS GREGORY, BISHOP OF NYSSA, FIVE
HOMILIES ON THE PRAYER.
HOMILY 1.
The divine Word leads us toward the teaching of prayer, through which
he sets down for his worthy disciples, who seek with zeal the knowledge
of prayer, how it is fitting to make the divine ear their own through
the words of prayer. But I, having dared a little, would add something
to what is written: that the present gathering must be taught not how
one ought to pray, but that one must pray at all, which perhaps the
hearing of many has not yet received. For this holy and inspired work,
prayer, has been neglected in life and overlooked by the many. About
this, then, it seems to me well first, as far as speech can, to bear
witness that one must at all events persist in prayer, as the Apostle
says, and then so to hear the divine voice that sets before us the
manner in which one must bring the petition to the Lord. For I see that
in the present life everything else is pursued with zeal, each soul
turned toward some other thing, while the 204 good of prayer is not
among the things men pursue with zeal. The huckster rises early for
trade, striving to display his own wares to the buyers before his
fellow craftsmen, so that he may anticipate the need of the one in
want, outstripping the others, and sell off his own. Likewise the buyer
also, having before his eyes that he may not miss what he needs by
being anticipated by another, runs not to the house of prayer but to
the market, and since all have the same desire for gain and strive to
get ahead of their neighbor, the hour of prayer is stolen away through
the things pursued and transferred to commerce. So the artisan, so the
one occupied with words, so the litigant, so the one allotted to judge:
each, wholly bent in zeal upon what is in his hands, has forgotten the
work of prayer, judging occupation with God a loss to himself in
respect of the matter at hand. For the one who follows a craft thinks
the divine alliance for the matter at hand to be some idle and
unworking thing; therefore, leaving prayer, he sets his hopes in his
hands, forgetting the one who gave the hands. Likewise also the one who
corrects speech in himself by care does not reckon the one who gave
speech, but, as if he had brought himself into this nature, he looks to
himself and attends to the zeal of studies, thinking that none of the
goods will come to him from the cooperation of God, and he makes zeal
more honorable than prayer. In the same way the remaining pursuits also
drive out the soul's occupation with the greater and heavenly things by
care for the bodily and earthly. For this reason sin is abundant in
life, always increasing toward the greater by additions, entangled with
all human pursuits, because forgetfulness of God prevails over all, and
the good of prayer is not joined to the things men pursue. With
commerce enters greed; and greed 206 is idolatry. So the farmer does
not measure his tillage to necessary needs, but, always stretching his
zeal toward more, he gives sin a wide entrance against the pursuit,
widening himself into the boundaries of others. Hence arise the
hard-to-heal rivalries, as those mastered by the same disease of greed
rise up against one another over the boundaries of the earth. From this
come the rages and the impulses toward evil and the


designs against one another, which are often settled in blood and
murder. Likewise the zeal about the law courts serves sins of many
kinds, finding countless advocacies of injustice. The judge has either
willingly bent the scale of justice toward a bribe, or, unwillingly
misled by the officiousness of those who confuse the truth, has
ratified injustice.
And why should one speak of the particulars through which sin is mixed
into human life in many branches and many ways, of which the cause is
nothing other than that men do not take along the alliance of God
toward the things pursued in their hands? If prayer goes before the
zeal, sin will not find a passage against the soul; for when the memory
of God is seated in the heart, the devices of the adversary remain
without effect, justice mediating everywhere in the matters under
dispute. Prayer also holds the farmer back from sin, multiplying the
fruits of the earth with little toil, so that sin no longer enters
together with the desire of more. So the traveler, so the one setting
out for campaign or marriage, so whoever it may be of those who have an
impulse toward something: if he should do each thing with prayer, he
will be turned away from sinning by the good speed toward what is
pursued, nothing contrary dragging the soul down toward passion. But if,
standing off from God, he should become wholly of the zeal, it is
altogether necessary that the one who is outside 208 God be altogether
in the contrary. And the one who does not join himself to God through
prayer is separated from God. Therefore this we must first be taught by
the Word: that one must always pray, and not lose heart. For from
praying comes being with God; and the one who is with God is separated
from the adversary. Prayer is a safeguard of self-control, a schooling
of rage, a settling of conceit, a cleansing of resentment, a taking
down of envy, an abolition of injustice, a correction of impiety.
Prayer is strength of bodies, plenty of a house, good order of a city,
might of a kingdom, a trophy of war, security of peace, a gathering of
those divided, a continuance of those standing together. Prayer is a
seal of virginity, faith of marriage, a weapon for travelers, a guard
of those asleep, courage of those awake, fruitfulness of farmers, safety
of those at sea. Prayer is an advocate of those on trial, release of
those bound, rest of those worn out, consolation of those in grief,
cheer of those who rejoice, comfort of those who mourn, a crown of
those marrying, a feast of birthdays, a winding-sheet of those dying.
Prayer is converse with God, a vision of the unseen, a full assurance
of the things desired, equal honor with the angels, advance of the
good, turning away of the evil, correction of things sinned, enjoyment
of the present, substance of the things hoped for.
Prayer made the sea-monster a house for Jonah, and brought Hezekiah
back from the gates of death toward life, and turned the flame for the
three young men into a dewy spirit, and raised a trophy for the
Israelites against the Amalekites, and laid low the hundred and eighty
and five thousands of the Assyrians in one night with the unseen sword.
And countless examples besides these can be found from things already
done, through which it becomes clear that nothing of the things honored
in life is higher than prayer. But it would be time already to occupy
ourselves with the prayer itself; or rather let us still add a little
to the 210 Word, that of the many and manifold goods that have come to
us from the divine grace this one thing we have toward a return for
what we have received: to repay the benefactor through prayer and
thanksgiving. I reckon, then, that even if we stretch converse with God
alongside the whole of life, giving thanks and praying, we fall short
of the worth of the return by as much as if we had not been willing
from the start to give a return to the benefactor. The span of time is
measured in three sections, the past, the present, and the future. In
these three the


benefaction of the Lord is grasped. If you reckon the present, in it
you live; if the future, he is for you the hope of the things expected;
if the past, you would not have been before you came to be from him.
You were benefited in receiving from him the very fact of coming to be,
and having come to be you were benefited in living and moving in him,
as the Apostle says. The hopes of things to come hang from the same
benefaction. But you are master of the present only. So that even if
you should not cease through all giving thanks to God, you will scarcely
fulfill the grace of the present, finding no device either of the
future or of the past toward a return of what is owed. And we, falling
so far short of thanksgiving according to our power, are not even
well-disposed about what is possible, not, I say, allotting every day,
but not even a fraction of the day, to leisure according to God. Who
spread the earth beneath me? Who by design made the wet nature passable?
Who fixed the heaven for me as a vault? Who torches for me the lamp of
the sun? Who sends springs in ravines? Who prepared the courses for the
rivers? Who yoked under me the service of the irrational animals? Who
made me, being lifeless dust, to share in life and 212 thought? Who
formed this clay after the image of the divine imprint? Who, when the
divine image in me was confused through sin, brought it back again to
the ancient grace? Who, when I had been made to dwell outside paradise,
and had come to be outside the tree of life, and had been covered over
with the pit of material life, draws me toward the first blessedness?
There is none that understands, says the Scripture. For otherwise,
looking toward these things, we would have fulfilled thanksgiving
unending and unceasing in the whole span of life. But now almost the
whole of human nature is awake toward the material only; toward this is
the zeal, in this the eagerness, about these things both memory and
hope are spent. Human nature is sleepless and unslumbering toward the
desire of more in every matter in which it is possible to find the
more, whether according to honor and glory, or according to abundance
of money, or according to the disease of rage: everywhere nature looks
toward the more in these. But of the true goods of God there is no
account, neither of those that appear nor of those promised. But it
would be time also to look down, as far as possible, upon the mind of
the words of prayer; for it is clear that obtaining what we wish comes
through learning how it is fitting to make the request.
What, then, is the teaching? When you pray, he says, do not babble, as
the Gentiles do; for they think that in their much speaking they will
be heard. Perhaps, then, the mind of the teaching has its clarity from
itself, set out more nakedly for us, and needing nothing of the finer
consideration; yet it is worth examining what the word of babbling
signifies, so that, having learned the mind, we may come to be outside
the thing forbidden. It seems to me, then, to chasten and contract the
flabbiness of thought of those who sink deep in vain desires, and for
this reason to have invented this strange 214 novelty of diction for
the exposure of the folly of those who are poured out in desires about
the unprofitable and vain. For the speech that is sensible and
understanding and looks toward the useful is properly called speech,
but that which is poured upon nonexistent desires through insubstantial
pleasure is not speech, but babble, as one interpreting the sense more
in the Greek manner might say: chatter, and nonsense, and twaddle, and
whatever else is of such a signification. What, then, does the Word
counsel us? Not to suffer in the time of prayers the same passion as
arises in the thought of infants. For just as those incomplete in
thought do not devise how any of the things according to their mind
might come to be, but at will they fashion for themselves some strokes
of luck, setting down treasures, and marriages, and kingdoms, and great
cities named with their own titles, and they


fancy in thought that they are that which the vanity of their
reasonings may sketch; and there are some who take hold of this vanity
even more youthfully, and, overstepping the measures of nature, either
become winged, or shine after the stars, or carry mountains by hand, or
make the heaven a road for themselves, or last to the ten-thousandth
year, becoming young from old, or whatever other such bubble-like and
hollow poems the heart breeds for the more infantile. As, then, in
affairs the one who does not reason such things through which some good
might come to be for the one deliberating, but idles in nonexistent
desires, is some mindless and wretched person, spending the time for
deliberating to do something profitable on these dreams, so the one who
in the time of prayer is not stretched toward the things profitable for
the soul, but thinks it right that God be disposed together with the
passionate movements of thought, is truly some nonsense and a babbler,
praying that God become a fellow-worker and servant of his own 216
vanities. I mean something of this sort: someone approaches God through
prayer, and, not considering in thought the height of the power to
which he comes, unnoticed he insults the greatness with shameful and
low requests. As if someone, thinking the vessels of clay precious
through excess of poverty or rusticity, then, having approached a king
who chooses to distribute whole riches and dignities, leaving the royal
requests thinks it right that one so great in dignity shape clay and
make some of the things that man longs for, so also the one who uses
prayer without education does not raise himself toward the height of
the giver, but longs for the divine power to come down toward the low
and earthly of his own desire, and for this reason he holds out the
passionate impulses to the one who sees the hearts, not that he might
heal the unfitting movements of thought, but that they might become
worse, the wicked impulse going forward into deed through the
cooperation of God.
For since so-and-so grieves me, and my heart is disposed toward him
with ill will, Strike him, he says to God, all but crying that: Let my
passion come to be in you, and let my wickedness pass over upon you.
For as in human fights it is not possible for someone to fight as ally
with the one side unless he has been provoked together with the one
angry against the opponent, so it is clear that the one who moves God
against the enemy invites him to be angry together and to become a
partner of the rage. And this is for God to fall into passion, and to
be disposed according to a human being, and to be recast from the good
nature into a beast-like harshness. So the one mad for glory, so the
one desiring to have the more in pride, the one hastening toward
victory in a lawsuit, the one pressing toward the crown in the gymnastic
contests, the one claiming the applause in theaters, and often even the
one melted toward the rabid passion of youth: all these do not bring
218 their petitions to God that they may come to be outside the
prevailing sickness, but that the disease may come to an end for them,
and, judging the missing of these things as a disaster, they truly
babble, begging God to become a fellow-worker of the sickness according
to their mind. And the hardest of all is that they desire the divine to
be moved toward contrary impulses, splitting the energy of God into
wildness and philanthropy; for the one they seek to be gracious and
gentle to themselves, this one they invite to be shown bitter and harsh
to their enemies. O the folly of those who babble! For if God is harsh
to those, he is not altogether tame to you either. But if in your case
he leans toward mercy, according at least to your hope, how could he
change toward the contrary, recasting the mercy into bitterness? But
the counter-position of the contentious toward such a thing is ready.
For at once they set beside them, in advocacy of their own bitterness,
the voices from prophecy: David longing that the sinners fail, and
praying against shame and confusion of the enemies; Jeremiah to see the
vengeance from God from the adversaries


desiring, Hosea asking that a barren womb and dry breasts be given to
the enemies; and they gather many such things lying scattered in the
holy Scriptures, establishing that one must pray against adversaries,
and make the goodness of God a fellow-worker of their own bitterness.
But we, so that we may stop those led toward the contrary from such a
starting-point from babbling, as in passing, will set this beside each
of the things mentioned. None of the truly holy, of those borne by the
Holy Spirit, whose sayings were written down according to divine
economy for the admonition of those after, will be shown to have his
zeal upon some evil, but the whole aim of their words looks toward the
correction of the wickedness that dwells in the nature.
As, then, the one who prays that there be no sick, that there be no
poor, desires not the destruction of the human beings but the
vanishing 220 of the disease and of the poverty, so also each of the
saints, having through prayer that the thing hostile and at war with
the nature come to vanishing, give a suspicion to the more uneducated
that they are bitter and harsh against human beings. For the psalmist,
having said that sinners should fail from the earth and the lawless, so
that they may not exist, prays that sin fail and lawlessness; for a
human being is not an enemy to a human being, but the movement of
choice according to wickedness has set what is joined to the nature
into the rank of an enemy. He prays, then, that the evil fail. But the
human being is not evil; for how would the likeness of the good be
evil? So, even if he prays against shame and confusion of the enemies,
he shows you the troop of the adversaries who from the unseen enemy
make war upon human life, about whom Paul also goes through more
nakedly, saying that our wrestling is against the principalities and
powers and the world-rulers of this world, against the spiritual things
of wickedness in the heavenlies. For the demonic plots, through which
the wicked falls toward sin are brought upon human beings (raging
encounters, starting-points of desires, occasions of envy and hatred
and pride and such evils), these the great prophet, seeing them running
round each soul by plot, prays to come to shame, the one praying
against these enemies. And this is for him to be saved; for it follows
by nature for the one defeated in the wrestling to be ashamed at his
fall, as for the one who has prevailed to exult at the victory. And
that these things are so, the form of the prayer makes clear. For let
them be ashamed, he says, and let them be confounded, those who seek my
soul. For he does not pray against those who plot toward a loss of
money, or dispute about boundaries of land, or show some wickedness
against his body, but against those who plot toward the soul. And what
222 plot against a soul is there, what other, if not alienation from
God? And a human soul is alienated from God not otherwise than through
the passionate disposition. Since, then, the divine is without passion,
everything that comes to be in passion is fenced off from conjunction
with the divine. So that he may not suffer this, he prays for shame of
those wrestling against him. And this is nothing other than to pray for
victory for himself against the enemies. And the enemies are the
passions. So Jeremiah, having zeal of piety, when the king was then mad
for idols, and those under his hand were twisted together with him,
does not tend some passion of his own, but brings the petition on
behalf of the common of human beings, thinking it right that the whole
human race be chastened by the impulse against those who were impious.
Likewise also the prophet Hosea, seeing wickedness then much-bearing
among the Israelites, condemns it to childlessness, and wishes the
bitter teats of sin to be dried, so that the evil may neither be born
among human beings nor be nursed. For this reason the prophet says,
Give them, Lord, a barren womb and dry breasts. And if any other such
word should be found among the saints, having some accusation and
impress of rage,


it looks altogether toward such a mind, which drives out the evil, it
does not wear down the human being. God did not make death (do you hear
the sentence?). How, then, was he going to invite God, who is alien to
the energy of death, toward death against his own enemies? He does not
delight in the destruction of the living. But the babbler, moving the
philanthropy of God against his own enemies, commands him to be
delighted at human disasters. But already some, he says, have been
thought worthy even of offices and honors and wealth, having used
prayer toward this, and have been supposed to be dear to God through
such good fortune. How, then, do you shut us out, someone might say,
from bringing such 224 petitions to God? But that all things hang from
the divine will and that life here is managed from above is clear to
everyone, and no one would speak against the word; yet of such
successes of prayer we have learned other causes, not as if God
altogether distributes these things as goods to those who ask, but so
that through these the faith toward God may be confirmed for the more
superficial, and, learning by experience little by little in the
smaller requests that God hears the supplications, we may someday
ascend toward the desire of the high and God-befitting gifts.
Just as we see in the case of our children, who for a while cling to
the maternal teat, seeking from the one who bore them as much as the
nature holds, but if the infant should grow a little and take on some
power of uttering, he despises the teat, and seeks some such thing, an
ornament, or a garment, or some such things with which the eye of
infants is delighted, and when he comes to age and thought grows
together with the body, then, leaving all the childish desires, he will
ask from the parents the things that befit the perfect life, so also
God, accustoming the human being through all things to look toward him,
for this reason often is not even unhearing of the smaller requests, so
that through the benefaction in the small he may call the one who has
obtained the grace toward the desire of the higher. And you, then, if
so-and-so has become by divine providence known and conspicuous from
more obscure things, or has acquired some other of the things sought in
this life, office or wealth or distinction, understand the aim: that
the philanthropy about these things becomes for you a demonstration of
the power of God in the great, so that through obtaining the childish
playthings you may bring to the Father the requests about the greater
and more perfect. And these are all the things that bring the gain
toward the soul. For it would be of the most irrational, having
approached God, to seek from the eternal the temporary, from the 226
heavenly the earthly, from the high the low-lying, from the one who
grants the kingdom of the heavens this earthly and low good fortune,
from the one who freely gives the things that cannot be taken away the
use in a little of things belonging to others, of which the taking away
is necessary, the enjoyment temporary, and the management dangerous.
And he well sets beside the absurdity by the addition, saying, As the
Gentiles do; for to have the zeal about the things that appear is
proper to those who set before themselves no hope of the age to come,
no fear of judgment, no threat of Gehenna, no expectation of goods, nor
any other of the things hoped for according to the resurrection, who,
looking toward the present life after the manner of cattle, judge
whatever they grant to throat and belly and the remaining sensualities
of the body to be in the portion of goods, or to be first among some,
and to be thought above the rest, or to sleep upon many talents, or
whatever else of the deception of life there is, to whom if someone
should speak about the hope to come, it seems to be sheer nonsense,
going through paradise and a kingdom and a way of life of the heavens
and such things. Since, then, it is proper to those who have no hope to
be glued to the present life, well does the Word say that the excess
and vain things of desire, which the lovers of pleasure think to
accomplish for themselves through prayer, are


of the Gentiles, of those who think that from importuning about the
unfitting they will have the divine as a fellow-worker toward the
things that ought not to be; for they think, he says, that in their
much speaking they will be heard. But these things, which one must not
ask, we have been taught through what we examined. And what sort of
petition it is fitting to bring to God, let us hear in what follows, by
the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the might
unto the ages of the ages. Amen. 228

1 {1Our Father who art in the heavens.}1 When the great Moses was
leading the Israelite people toward the mystic initiation on the
mountain, he did not think them worthy of the theophany before he
legislated for the people the cleansing by purity and sprinkling. And
not even so did they dare the manifestation of the divine power, but
they were struck with panic at everything that appeared, the fire, the
gloom, the smoke, the trumpets; and, turning back toward themselves,
they thought it right that the lawgiver become a mediator for them of
the divine will, as if their power were not sufficient to draw near to
God and to receive a divine manifestation. But our lawgiver, our Lord
Jesus Christ, being about to lead us toward the divine grace, does not
show us beforehand in the Word a Sinai mountain covered with gloom and
smoking with fire, nor voices of trumpets sounding out something
inarticulate and striking, nor does he cleanse the soul with three-day
purities and water that washes off filth, nor, leaving the whole church
at the foot of the mountain, does he grant the ascent upon the peak of
the mountain to one only, the peak covered with the gloom that hides
the glory of God, but first, instead of the mountain, he leads up upon
the heaven itself, having made it passable for human beings through
virtue, and then he makes them not spectators only of the divine power
but also partners, and leads those who approach into a kinship, in a
manner, of the nature that lies above, nor does he hide the
surpassing glory with gloom, so that it is hard to see for those who
seek it, but, lighting the gloom with the far-shining light of the
teaching, he made the unspeakable glory be seen in bright open air by
those pure in heart. And he grants water to sprinkle not from alien
streams, but that which wells up in ourselves, whether one should say
the springs of the eyes, or the pure conscience of the heart, bringing
on no mud from wickedness. And having legislated purity 230 not only
from the lawful intercourse of wives, but from every material and
passionate disposition, so he leads toward God through the prayer.
For this is the power of the words, through which we do not learn some
voices uttered in syllables through the Word, but a conception of the
ascent toward God accomplished through a high way of life. And it is
possible through the very words of the prayer to consider the divine
mystic initiation. When you pray, he says: he did not say, When you
vow, but, When you pray, as if what is according to the vow already
ought to have been accomplished beforehand, before drawing near to God
through the prayer. And what is the difference of these names according
to the signification? That a vow is a promise of some of the things
consecrated according to piety, but a prayer is a request of goods
brought to God with a supplication. Since, then, we have need of
boldness when we approach God making the supplications on behalf of the
things that profit, necessarily what is according to the vow will go
before, so that, having accomplished what is from ourselves, we may so
with courage think it right to receive in return what is from God.
Therefore the prophet says that, I will pay my vows to you, which my
lips distinguished, and, Vow and pay to the Lord our God. And in many
places of Scripture it is possible to find such a signification of
vow,


so that we may know that a vow is, as has been said, a promise of a
thank-offering gift-bearing; but the prayer interprets the approach to
God that comes after the fulfillment of the promise. The Word,
therefore, teaches us not to request something from God first, before
we gift-bear to him something of the things that please him; for one
must vow first, then pray, as if one should say that the sowing goes
before the harvest. Therefore we must first cast down the seeds of the
vow, and so reap the casting of the seeds when it has grown, receiving
the grace in return through the prayer. As, then, the entreaty will not
be in boldness unless the approach should come upon a 232 vow and
gift-bearing taken beforehand, necessarily the vow will go before the
prayer. As, then, this already having been accomplished, the Lord says
to the disciples, When you pray, say, Our Father who art in the
heavens. Who will give me wings as of a dove, says the great David
somewhere in the psalmody; and I too would say, having dared the same
voice, Who will give me those wings toward being able to fly up together
in thought with the height of the magnificence of the words, so as to
leave all the earth, and to cross all the air poured in the middle, and
to grasp the ethereal beauty, and to reach the stars, and to look down
upon all the ordering in them, and not even to stand in these, but to
go through even through these, and to come to be outside all things
that are moved and shifted, and to grasp the standing nature, the
unmoved power, that which is seated upon itself, that which leads and
bears all things that are in being, all things hung from the unspeakable
will of the divine wisdom, so that, having come in thought far from all
things that are altered and shifted, in an unturned and unleaning
condition of the soul, I may first make my own through the judgment the
unturned and unalterable, and then so call upon him with the most
proper title and say, Father? For of what sort of soul is there need
for the one who says it, of how much boldness, of what sort of
conscience, so that, having thought God, as far as possible, being led
by the hand from the titles conceived for him toward the understanding
of the unspeakable glory, and having learned that the divine nature,
whatever it is, is goodness itself, sanctification, gladness, power,
glory, purity, eternity, always holding according to the same things
and in the same way, and whatever such things are thought about the
divine nature, having considered them both through the divine Scripture
and through his own reasonings, he will then dare to send forth such a
voice, and to name such a one his own father? For it is clear that, if
he should share in some thought, he would not dare, unless he sees the
same things also in himself, to send forth 234 that voice toward God,
and to say, Father. For the one good in essence does not have it in
nature to become a father of the wicked choice, nor the holy of the one
profaned according to life, nor the unalterable of the one turned
about, nor the Father of life of the one deadened from sin, nor the
pure and undefiled of those who behave unseemly in the passions of
dishonor, nor the benefactor of the greedy, nor, in a word, the one
beheld in every good of those found in some evil. For if someone,
looking toward himself still in need of cleansing, and recognizing his
own miserable conscience full of stains and wicked brands, before being
cleansed of such evils should adopt himself into the kinship of God and
say, Father, the unjust to the just, and the unclean to the pure, it
would be sheer insult and the words would be of abuse, if he should
name God father of his own misery; for the voice of father signifies
the cause of what has come to subsist from him. Therefore the one
miserable in conscience, if he should say God is his father, will
accuse him of nothing other than being originator and cause of his own
evils. But there is no communion of light with darkness, says the

not know that the occupation about the throat is almost a root of the

Something of this sort the Lord seems to me also to teach in another

Why, then, do you boil so much against him with the rage, when someone
has been lazy, or has stood off, or perhaps has shown contempt to you

coming-to-be, and made you a partner of the wonders in the world, who
set forth the sun for enjoyment, and granted all the starting-points

distinguishes the beautiful and the worse? How, then, are you toward

toward the sin? did you not exchange the wicked mastery? did you not,

overlook through long-suffering some of the things offended toward you?
If, then, we are about to bring to God the plea on behalf of the mercy
and the pardon, let us prepare the boldness for the conscience, so as
to set the life forward as an advocate of this voice, and to say truly
that we also have forgiven those who owe us. 312 And what does what
lies next, attached to the things said, wish? I think it necessary not
to run past this either uncontemplated, so that, knowing to whom we
pray, we may bring the supplication with soul and not with body. Lead
us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil. What, brothers, is
the power of the things said? It seems to me that the Lord names the
evil in many-turned and different ways, calling him with many names
according to the differences of the wicked energies, devil, Beelzebul,
mammon, ruler of the world, man-slayer, wicked one, father of the lie,
and other such things. Perhaps, then, temptation also is one of the
names thought about him, and the joining of the things said confirms
such a suspicion for us; for having said, Lead us not into temptation,
he brought on the being delivered from the wicked one, as of the same
being signified through either of the names. For if the one who has not
entered into temptation is altogether outside the wicked one, and the
one who has come to be in the temptation of necessity comes to be in
the wicked one, then the temptation and the wicked one are some one
thing according to the signification. What, then, does such a teaching
of the prayer command us? To come to be outside of the things beheld
according to this world, as he says elsewhere to the disciples that the
whole world lies in the wicked one. Therefore the one who wishes to
come to be outside the wicked one will of necessity colonize himself
from the world; for the temptation does not have a place to touch the
soul, unless, having put around this cosmic occupation as some bait
upon the wicked hook, he holds it out to the more greedy. Or rather the
thought might become clearer for us through other examples. The sea is
often hard from a triple wave, but not also to those colonized far from
it. Fire is destructive, but of the matter that has fallen under it.
War is terrible, but only to those who share the array. And as the one
who flees the disasters of the evils from the war 314 prays not to fall
into war, and the one who fears the fire not to come to be in it, and
the one who shudders at the sea not to come into a necessity of
sailing, so also the one who fears the assault of the wicked one let
him pray not to come to be in him. And since, as


we have said before, the Word says that the world lies in the wicked
one, and in the cosmic affairs are the starting-points of the
temptations, well and fittingly the one who prays to be delivered from
the wicked one invites to come to be outside the temptations. For no
one would swallow the hook, unless he had pulled down the bait in
greediness. But let us also, having stood up, say to God that, Lead us
not into temptation, that is, into the evils of the life, but deliver
us from the wicked one, who has acquired the strength in this world,
from whom may we be delivered by the grace of Christ our God, because
to him is the glory and the might unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
