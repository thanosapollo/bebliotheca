GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA TO SIMPLICIUS ON THE FAITH
Gregory to Simplicius the tribune, concerning the Father and the Son
and the Holy Spirit.
God commands through the prophet that no one should think there is a
recent god, nor worship a strange god. It is therefore clear that
"recent" is said of what is not from eternity. And conversely again,
"eternal" is said of what is not recent. He, then, who does not believe
that the Only-begotten God is from the Father from eternity does not
deny that he is recent; for what is not eternal is altogether recent.
But everything recent is not God, as the Scripture said: "There shall
be in you no recent god." Therefore he who says that there was a time
when the Son was not denies his divinity. Again, he who says "You shall
not worship a strange god" forbids the worship of a strange god. The
strange is considered in contrast to our own God. Who then is our own
God? Clearly the true God. And who is the strange one? Surely he who
stands alien to the nature of the true 3,=1.62= God. If then our own
God is the true God, if the Only-begotten God is not of the nature of
the True, as the heretics say, he is strange and not ours. And the
Gospel says that the sheep will by no means obey a stranger. He who
says that he is created constructs him as alien to the nature of the
true God. What then will those who say that he is created do? Do they
worship their created god, or not? If they do not worship, they judaize,
denying the worship of Christ; if they worship, they commit idolatry,
for they worship one alien to the true God. But it is equally impious
both not to worship the Son and to worship a strange god. One must
therefore call the Son true of the true Father, so that we both worship
him and are not condemned as worshipping a strange god.
Against those who cite from Proverbs, "The Lord created me," and on
this account think they say something strong about the Creator and
Fashioner of all being created, it is fitting to say this: that the
Only-begotten God became many things for our sake. For being Word he
became flesh, and being God he became man, and being bodiless he became
body, and besides these he became sin and curse and stone and axe and
bread and sheep and way and door and rock and many such things, being
none of these by nature, 3,=1.63= but becoming them for our sake
according to the economy. Just as, then, being Word he became flesh for
our sake, and being God he became man, so also, being Creator, he
became a creature for our sake; for the flesh is created. As he said
through the prophet, "Thus says the Lord who formed me from the womb as
his servant," so he also said through Solomon, "The Lord created me the
beginning of his ways for his works"; for all creation serves, as the
Apostle says. Therefore also he who was formed in the womb of the
Virgin according to the word of the prophet is the servant, not the
Lord, that is, the man according to the flesh, in whom God was
manifested; and here too he who was created as the beginning of his
ways is not God but the man, in whom God was manifested to us in order
to renew again the ruined way of human salvation. So since we know two
things concerning Christ, the divine and the human (the divine in the
nature, the human in the economy), we accordingly attest eternity to
the divinity, and we reckon the created to the human nature. For as
according to the prophet a servant was formed in the womb, so also
according to Solomon he was manifested in the flesh through this
servile creation. When they say that if he was,

he was not begotten, and if he was begotten, he was not, let them be
taught that one must not apply the properties of fleshly generation to
the divine nature. For bodies that do not yet exist are begotten, but
God makes the things that are not to be; he himself does not come to be
from non-being. Therefore Paul also names him radiance of glory, so
that we may be taught that, just as the light from 3,=1.64= the lamp
both is from the nature of that which radiates and is with it (for at
the same time the lamp appeared and the light from it shone forth
together), so here too the Apostle commands us to understand that the
Son is from the Father and the Father is never without the Son; for it
is not possible for the glory to be without radiance, as it is not
possible for the lamp to be without its shining. And it is clear that,
just as being radiance is a testimony that the glory also is (for if
the glory were not, there would not be what is radiated from it), so to
say that the radiance was not at some time is a proof that the glory
also was not, when the radiance was not; for it is impossible for the
glory to be without radiance. Therefore, just as it is not possible to
say of the radiance that if it was, it did not come to be, and if it
came to be, it was not, so it is vain to say these things about the
Son, because the Son is the radiance. Those who speak of the lesser and
the greater concerning the Son and the Father, let them be taught by
Paul not to measure the immeasurable; for the Apostle calls the Son the
impress of the Father's hypostasis. It is clear then that however great
the hypostasis of the Father is, so great also is the impress of the
hypostasis; for it is not possible for the impress to be lesser than
the hypostasis contemplated in it. And the great John also teaches the
same things, saying, "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was
with God." For by saying that he was in the beginning and not after the
beginning, he showed that the beginning was never 3,=1.65= without Word.
And by showing that the Word also was with God, he indicated the Son's
lack of deficiency in relation to the Father; for the whole Word is
contemplated together with the whole God. For if the Word were deficient
in his own magnitude, so as not to be able to be toward the whole God,
it would be altogether necessary to think that what of God exceeds the
Word is without Word. But in fact the majesty of the Word is
contemplated together with all the majesty of God. Therefore there is
no place in the divine doctrines for speaking of greater and lesser.
Those who say that the begotten is unlike the unbegotten in nature, let
them be taught not to be foolish from the example of Abel and Adam. For
Adam, according to the natural generation of human beings, was not
begotten, but Abel was begotten from Adam. Yet he who was not begotten
is called unbegotten, and he who was begotten is called begotten; and
Adam's not having been begotten in no way prevented him from being a
human being. Nor did generation make Abel something other than human
nature, but this one is a human being and that one too, even if one was
begotten and the other without generation. Therefore also in the divine
doctrines, not being begotten and being begotten do not produce a
difference of nature, but just as in Adam and Abel there is one
humanity, so also in the Father and the Son there is one divinity. And
those who blaspheme concerning the Holy Spirit say the same thing they
say concerning the Lord, that this too is created; and the Church
believes equally, as concerning the Son, so also 3,=1.66= concerning
the Holy Spirit, that he is uncreated; because all creation becomes
good from participation in the transcendent Good, but the Holy Spirit
has no need of one who makes him good (for he is good by nature, as
Scripture testifies); and because creation is led by the Spirit, but
the Spirit grants the leading; creation is governed, but the Spirit
governs; creation is comforted, but the Spirit comforts; creation
serves, but the Spirit sets free; creation is made wise, but the Spirit
gives the grace of wisdom; creation partakes of the gifts, but the
Spirit grants them by authority.

For all these things the one and the same Spirit works, distributing
to each as he wills. And one can find countless other proofs from the
Scriptures, that all the lofty and God-befitting names which Scripture
applies to the Father and the Son are also contemplated concerning the
Holy Spirit: incorruption, blessedness, the good, the wise, the
powerful, the just, holiness; every honorable name is said of the Holy
Spirit as it is said of the Father and of the Son, except those by
which the hypostases are clearly and unconfusedly distinguished from
one another, I mean that the Holy Spirit is called neither Father nor
Son; but the rest, whatever the Father and the Son are named, these
Scripture also applies to the Holy Spirit. For this reason then we
grasp that the Holy Spirit is above creation. Therefore where the
Father and where the Son are understood, there also the Holy Spirit is
understood; for the Father and the Son are above 3,=1.67= creation,
which the sequence of the argument has also attested for the Holy
Spirit. He, then, who places the Holy Spirit above creation has
accordingly received the right and sound doctrine; for he will confess
one uncreated nature, contemplated in Father and Son and Holy Spirit.
But since, as a proof, as they think, that the Holy Spirit is created,
they bring forward to us the prophet's voice that says, "He who
establishes thunder and creates spirit and announces his Christ to
men," it is fitting to understand this, that the prophet speaks of
another spirit being created in the establishing of the thunder, and
not the Holy Spirit; for the mystical word names the Gospel thunder.
Those in whom faith in the Gospel becomes firm and unchangeable, these
through faith pass from being flesh to becoming spirit, as the Lord
says: "That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born
of the Spirit is spirit." God then is he who, by making the evangelical
thunder firm, makes the believer spirit for those who believe; and he
who has been born of the Spirit and become spirit through such thunder
announces Christ, as the Apostle says: "No one can say Lord Jesus
Christ except in the Holy Spirit."
