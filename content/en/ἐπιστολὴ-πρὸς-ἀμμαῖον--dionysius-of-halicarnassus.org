* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Dionysius to Ammaeus his dearest, very many greetings

Among many other strange and paradoxical reports which our time has brought
forth, this too appeared to me when I first heard it from you, that one of the
philosophers from the Peripatos, wishing to grant everything to Aristotle who
founded this philosophy, undertook to make this also clear, that Demosthenes,
having learned the rhetorical arts from him, transferred them into his own
speeches and, being adorned according to those precepts, became the strongest
of all orators. At first, then, I supposed the man who attempted to say these
things was one of the many, and I advised you not to attend to every paradox.
But when I also learned the name of the man, whom I accept both for his
character and for his writings, I was astonished, and becoming much occupied
in myself I thought the matter needed more careful examination, lest perhaps
the truth had escaped me as being so and nothing had been said at random by
the man, in order that I might either give up the opinion which I myself
formerly held, having learned securely that the arts of Aristotle precede the
speeches of Demosthenes, or that I might persuade the man who has so judged
and is prepared even to write, before publishing the treatise to the crowd, to
change his opinion.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

And you too furnished me no small impulse toward examining the truth not as a
side-task, asking me to make clear the arguments by which I have convinced
myself that, when Demosthenes was already in his prime and had delivered the
most distinguished contests, then the rhetorical arts were written by
Aristotle. And you seemed to me to counsel this also rightly, not to confirm
the matter by signs nor by probabilities nor by foreign testimonies, since
none of these proofs is gathered through necessary premises, but to produce
Aristotle himself through his own arts confessing that the truth is so. This
then I have done, most excellent Ammaeus, taking thought both for the truth,
which I think must be examined in every matter, and for the gratification of
all who have been earnest about political speeches; in order that they may not
suppose this, that the Peripatetic philosophy has embraced all the rhetorical
precepts, and that neither the circle of Theodorus and Thrasymachus and
Antiphon found anything worthy of zeal, nor Isocrates and Anaximenes and
Alcidamas, nor those who lived with these men as writers of technical precepts
and contestants of rhetorical speeches, the circle of Theodectes and Philiscus
and Isaeus and Cephisodorus, and Hyperides and Lycurgus and Aeschines, nor
that Demosthenes himself, who surpassed all both those before him and those in
his own time and left no excess even for those being born, would have become
so great, being adorned with the precepts of Isocrates and Isaeus, if he had
not thoroughly learned the arts of Aristotle.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

/This story is not true,/ dear Ammaeus, nor were the speeches of Demosthenes
composed from the arts of Aristotle that were brought out later, but according
to certain other introductions; concerning which I will make clear my views in
a separate writing; for the argument about them is large, which it would not
have been well to make a side-task of another writing. But in the present I
will try to make this clear, that when Demosthenes was already in his prime in
public life and had delivered the most distinguished contests, both the
forensic and the deliberative, and was admired throughout all Greece for force
of speeches, then the philosopher wrote the rhetorical arts. And perhaps it is
necessary first to premise, as I received from the common histories, the
things which those who compiled the lives of the men have left us. And I will
make the beginning from Demosthenes.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

This man was born in the year before the hundredth Olympiad; and in the
archonship of Timocrates. He had entered upon his seventeenth year  [lost]
and he began to write public speeches in the archonship of Callistratus, being
in his twenty-fifth year. And the first of his contests prepared in a
law-court is the one against Androtion, which he wrote for Diodorus who was
judging the decree as contrary to law, and at the same time another in the
archonship of Callistratus, the one concerning the exemptions, which he
delivered himself, the most graceful of all the speeches and the most written.
And under Diotimus who came after Callistratus he spoke among the Athenians
his first public oration, which those who compile the rhetorical tables
entitle /On the Symmories/; in which he exhorted the Athenians not to dissolve
the peace that had come about with the King nor to begin the war first, unless
they should prepare the naval force, in which they had the most strength, and
he himself proposes the manner of the preparation. And under Thudemus who held
office after Diotimus he both wrote the speech against Timocrates for Diodorus
who was judging Timocrates as contrary to law, and he himself delivered the
deliberative speech concerning the aid of the Megalopolitans. And after
Thudemus there is Aristodemus as archon, under whom he began the public
orations against Philip, and he delivered a speech in the assembly concerning
the sending of the mercenary force and of the ten flying triremes to
Macedonia. In this time he also wrote the speech against Aristocrates for
Euthycles who was prosecuting the decree as contrary to law. And under
Theellus who came after Aristodemus he delivered the public oration concerning
the Rhodians, in which he persuades the Athenians to dissolve their oligarchy
and set the people free. And under Callimachus, the third who held office
after Theellus, he delivered three public orations exhorting the Athenians to
send aid to the Olynthians who were being warred upon by Philip, the first of
which the beginning is /On many things one might see, men of Athens, it seems
to me/; and the second /Not the same things present themselves to me to judge,
men of Athens/; and the third /In place of many things, men of Athens, of
money/. Under this archon has also been written the speech against Meidias,
which he composed after the vote which the people voted against him. Up to
this point, twelve public speeches being found, of which I have spoken, among
which there are seven deliberative and five forensic, all came about earlier
than the arts of Aristotle, as I will show both from the things related about
the man and from the things written by him, beginning from here.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Aristotle was of a father Nicomachus, tracing his family and his art back to
Machaon the son of Asclepius, and of a mother Phaestis, a descendant of one of
those who led the colony from Chalcis to Stageira; and he was born in the
ninety-ninth Olympiad, when Diotrephes was archon at Athens, older than
Demosthenes by three years. And in the archonship of Polyzelus, his father
having died, being in his eighteenth year he came to Athens, and having been
introduced to Plato he spent a twenty-year time with him. And when Plato died
in the archonship of Theophilus he departed to Hermias the tyrant of Atarneus
and, having spent a three-year time with him, in the archonship of Eubulus he
withdrew to Mytilene; and from there he went to Philip in the archonship of
Pythodotus, and he spent an eight-year time with him as tutor of Alexander;
and after the death of Philip, in the archonship of Evaenetus, having arrived
at Athens he taught in the Lyceum a time of twelve years. And in the
thirteenth, after the death of Alexander, in the archonship of Cephisodorus,
having set out to Chalcis he dies of disease, having lived three years in
addition to sixty.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

These then are the things which those who have recorded the life of the man
have handed down to us. And the things which the philosopher himself writes
about himself, taking away every attempt of those who wish to grant him things
that do not belong, among many others of which I have no need to make mention
for the present, the things he has set in the first book of this treatise,
that he was not a youth when he was composing the rhetorical arts, but had
come to be in his strongest prime and had already published beforehand both
the topical compositions and the analytical and the methodical, are the
strongest evidences. For beginning to exhibit the benefits which rhetorical
speech has embraced, he writes these things word for word: /And rhetoric is
useful because of this at least, that the true and the just are by nature
stronger than their opposites; so that if the judgments do not come about
according to what is fitting, it is necessary that he be defeated through
himself; and this is worthy of censure. And further toward some, not even if
we should have the most exact science is it easy to persuade speaking from
that; for the speech according to the science is teaching, and this is
impossible; but it is necessary to make the proofs and the speeches through
the common things, just as we also say in the Topics concerning the encounter
toward the many./

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

And having chosen to speak about examples and enthymemes, that these have the
same force as inductions and syllogisms, he sets these things about the
analytical and methodical treatise: /And of the things through being shown or
appearing to be shown, just as also in the Analytics one is induction, another
syllogism, and another apparent syllogism, so also here likewise; for the
example is induction, and the enthymeme syllogism, and the apparent an
apparent syllogism; for I call enthymeme a rhetorical syllogism, and example a
rhetorical induction; and all make the proofs through showing anything
whatever, and this is clear to us from the Analytics, so it is necessary that
each of them be the same as each of these. And what the difference of example
and enthymeme is, is clear from the Topics; for there it has been said earlier
about syllogism and induction, that to be shown upon many and similar things
that it is so is there induction, and here example; and that, certain things
being, something else comes about through these besides their being so, either
universally or as for the most part, is there called syllogism, and here
enthymeme. And it is clear also that each kind of rhetoric has a good; for
just as it has been said also in the Methodics, so it is likewise in these
too./ As many things then as Aristotle has written about himself, witnessing
expressly that he composed the rhetorical arts being already older and having
published beforehand the strongest compositions, these are they; from which
what I chose to make clear, that the contests of the orator precede the arts
of the philosopher, I think has been sufficiently demonstrated; if at least
the one, being in his twenty-fifth year, began to take part in public life and
to speak in the assembly and to write speeches for law-courts, while the other
at the same times was still with Plato and spent until seven and thirty years
neither heading a school nor having made a choice of his own.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And if someone will be so contentious as to contradict even these things,
agreeing that it is true that the rhetorical arts were written later than the
analytical and the methodical and the topical, but saying that nothing
prevents the philosopher from having constructed all these treatises while
still being educated under Plato, introducing a cold and unpersuasive attempt,
and forcing the most malicious of the attempts to make more persuasive, that
even the improbable sometimes becomes probable, having let go the things I had
to say against these I will turn to the testimonies of the philosopher
himself, which he has set in the third book of the arts concerning metaphor,
writing word for word thus: /And of the metaphors being four, those according
to analogy are most in repute; as Pericles said that the youth that perished
in the war had so vanished from the city, as if someone should take the spring
out of the year; and Cephisodotus, when Chares was eager to give accounts of
the things about the Olynthian war, was indignant, saying that he, having
brought the people into a choking, was trying to give the accounts./

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

In this way then the philosopher himself clearly demonstrates that the arts
were written by him after the Olynthian war. And this happened in the
archonship of Callimachus, as Philochorus makes clear in the sixth book of the
Atthis, writing word for word thus: /Callimachus of Pergase; under this man,
the Olynthians being warred upon by Philip and having sent ambassadors to
Athens, the Athenians both made an alliance and sent aid, two thousand
peltasts, and thirty triremes, those with Chares and eight which they manned
in addition./ Then, having gone through a few of the things that happened in
between, he sets these: /And about the same time, the Chalcidians in Thrace
being hard pressed by the war and having sent an embassy to Athens, the
Athenians sent them Charidemus the general in the Hellespont; who, having
eighteen triremes and four thousand peltasts, and a hundred and fifty
horsemen, came into Pallene and Bottiaea with the Olynthians and ravaged the
country./ Then concerning the third alliance he says these: /And again the
Olynthians having sent ambassadors to Athens and begging them not to look on
while they were warred down, but besides the existing forces to send aid not
mercenary but of the Athenians themselves, the people sent them seventeen
other triremes and two thousand hoplites of the citizens and three hundred
horsemen in horse-transports, and Chares as general of the whole fleet./

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

These things too, then, being said, suffice to make clear the ambition of
those who claim that Demosthenes has emulated the arts of Aristotle, who had
already happened to have delivered four Philippics, and three Hellenic public
orations, and to have written five public speeches for law-courts, which no
one could slander as cheap and paltry and showing nothing technical, since
they were composed before the arts of Aristotle. Yet I at least will not,
having gone forward to this point, stop, but I will also show that his other
speeches that are most in repute, both the deliberative and the forensic, were
delivered earlier than the publication of these arts, using again Aristotle
himself as witness. For after the archon Callimachus, under whom the Athenians
sent the aids to Olynthus, having been persuaded by Demosthenes, there is
Theophilus as archon, under whom Philip mastered the city of the Olynthians.
Then Themistocles, under whom Demosthenes delivered the fifth of the public
orations against Philip concerning the guard of the islanders and of the
cities in the Hellespont, of which the beginning is: /What we, men of Athens,
have been able to find, these are./ And after Themistocles, Archias, under
whom he advises the Athenians not to hinder Philip from taking part in the
Amphictyony nor to give a starting-point of war, having newly made the peace
toward him; and the beginning of this public oration is this: /I see, men of
Athens, the present affairs./ And after Archias there is Eubulus; then
Lyciscus, under whom he delivered the seventh of the Philippics toward the
embassies from the Peloponnese, making this beginning: /Whenever, men of
Athens, speeches come about./ After Lyciscus there is Pythodotus as archon,
under whom he delivered the eighth of the Philippics toward the ambassadors of
Philip, of which the beginning is /Men of Athens, it is not possible that the
accusations,/ and he composed the speech against Aeschines, when he was giving
the accounts of the second embassy, the one upon the oaths. After Pythodotus
there is Sosigenes, under whom he went through the ninth public oration
against Philip concerning the soldiers in the Chersonese, in order that the
mercenary force with Diopeithes might not be dissolved, having this beginning:
/It was necessary, men of Athens, that those speaking all/; and under the same
archon the tenth, in which he tries to teach that Philip is dissolving the
peace and is first in bringing out the war, of which the beginning is: /Many
speeches, men of Athens, coming about./ After Sosigenes there is Nicomachus as
archon, under whom he went through the eleventh public oration concerning
Philip having dissolved the peace, and he persuades the Athenians to send aid
to the Byzantines, of which the beginning is: /And considering them serious,
men of Athens./ Nicomachus is followed by Theophrastus as archon, under whom
he persuades the Athenians to endure the war nobly, as Philip having already
declared it; and this is the last of the public orations against Philip,
having this beginning: /That Philip, men of Athens, did not make the peace
toward you, but postponed the war./

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

And that Demosthenes delivered all these speeches also which I have numbered
before the publication of the arts of Aristotle, I will produce Aristotle
himself as witnessing. For beginning in the second book of the arts to define
the topics from which the enthymemes are brought, he takes up also the one
from time, setting beside it the examples. And I will set the very wording of
the philosopher: /Another looks to the time; as Iphicrates in the one toward
Harmodius, that if before doing it he claimed to obtain the statue if he
should do it, you would have given it; and having done it will you not give
it? Do not then, while about to, promise, and having suffered, take away. And
again toward Philip passing through the Thebans into Attica, that if before
helping toward the Phocians he claimed it, they would have promised; it is
strange then if, because he let it go and trusted, they will not let him
through./ And this time, in which Philip claimed that the Thebans give him a
passage upon Attica, reminding them of the aid that came about in the war
toward the Phocians, becomes clear from the common history. For it was thus:
after the capture of the Olynthians, in the archonship of Themistocles,
covenants came about for Philip toward the Athenians concerning friendship and
alliance; these remained a seven-year time until Nicomachus; and under
Theophrastus who held office after Nicomachus they were dissolved, the
Athenians accusing Philip of beginning the war, and Philip charging the
Athenians. And the causes through which they came into the war, both saying
they were being wronged, and the time in which they dissolved the peace,
Philochorus makes clear exactly in the sixth book of the Atthis. And I will
set from it the most necessary things: /Theophrastus of Halae; under this man
Philip first sailing up attacked Perinthus, and failing from there was
besieging Byzantium and was bringing engines./ Then, having gone through as
many things as Philip charged the Athenians with through the letter, he adds
these again word for word: /And the people, having heard the letter, and
Demosthenes exhorting it toward the war and writing the decrees, voted to take
down the stele that had been set up concerning the peace and alliance toward
Philip, and to man ships and to set the other things of the war in motion./
Having written that these things came about under the archon Theophrastus, in
the year after that he goes through the things done after the dissolution of
the peace under the archon Lysimachides. And I will set of these too the most
necessary things themselves: /Lysimachides of Acharnae; under this man they
postponed the works about the shipsheds and the equipment-store because of the
war toward Philip; and they voted that all the money be military, Demosthenes
writing it. And Philip having seized Elateia and Cytinium and having sent
ambassadors to Thebes of Thessalians, Aenianes, Aetolians, Dolopes,
Phthiotians, and the Athenians at the same time having sent out the
ambassadors about Demosthenes, they voted to be allies with these./ The time
then having become clear, under which both the ambassadors of the Athenians
about Demosthenes and those from Philip entered Thebes, that it falls under
Lysimachides as archon, both already having prepared the things toward the
war, Demosthenes himself will make clear in the speech concerning the crown
what the claims from both embassies were; and I will set, taking from his very
wording, the things that bear upon the matter: /Having so disposed the cities
toward one another through these, and having been lifted by these decrees and
the answers, he came having the force and seized Elateia, as not even if
anything should come about would we and the Thebans still blow together./
Moreover, having gone through the things that then happened, and having gone
through the speeches spoken by himself before the assembly, and how he was
sent as ambassador by the Athenians to Thebes, he adds these word for word:
/And when we arrived at Thebes, we found ambassadors of Philip and of
Thessalians and of the other allies present, and our friends in fear, and
those of that man bold./ Then, having ordered a certain letter to be read, he
adds these: /When then they made the assembly, they brought those men on
because they had the rank of the allies. And coming forward they spoke in
public, praising Philip in many things, and charging you in many, reminding
them of all as many things as you ever did contrary to the Thebans. The sum
then: they claimed that they give back gratitude for the things they had been
well treated by Philip, and take justice for the things they had been wronged
by you, in whichever of two ways they wish, either letting them through upon
you or joining in invading Attica./ If then under Lysimachides as archon, who
came after Theophrastus, the peace already having been dissolved, the
ambassadors from Philip were sent to Thebes, exhorting them most of all to
join in invading Attica, and if not, to furnish Philip a passage, remembering
his benefactions about the Phocian war, and Aristotle makes mention of this
embassy, as I showed a little earlier when I produced his wordings, it has
surely been demonstrated by unambiguous evidences that all the contests of
Demosthenes that came about before the archonship of Lysimachides in
assemblies and in law-courts are earlier than the arts of Aristotle  [lost]

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

I will add another testimony, taking it from the philosopher, from which it
will be still more clear that after the war that happened for the Athenians
toward Philip the rhetorical arts were composed by him, Demosthenes being
already in his prime in public life and having delivered all both the
deliberative and the forensic speeches, of which I made mention a little
earlier. For going through the topics of the enthymemes the philosopher sets
also the one from the cause; and I will produce his wording: /Another from the
non-cause as cause, as by having come about at the same time or after this.
For they take the after this as through this, and especially in public lives;
as Demades the policy of Demosthenes the cause of all the evils; for after
that  /.  What contests then did Demosthenes prepare, using the Aristotelian
arts as guides, if all the public speeches through which he is praised and
admired came about before the war, as I showed earlier, except one, the one
concerning the crown? For this alone has come into a law-court after the war,
in the archonship of Aristophon, in the eighth year after the battle at
Chaeroneia, and the sixth after the death of Philip, at which time Alexander
was winning the battle at Arbela.  And if someone of those who are contentious
toward all things will say that perhaps he wrote this speech, the strongest of
all the speeches, having come upon the arts of Aristotle, having many things
to say toward him, in order that my argument may not become longer than it
ought, I promise to show that this contest too was completed before the arts
of Aristotle, using the philosopher himself as witness. For having set forth
as a topic of enthymemes the one from relatives, he writes these word for
word: /Another from relatives; for if to the one there belongs to have done
well or justly, to the other there belongs to have suffered, and if to have
commanded, also to have done; as the tax-farmer Diomedon; for if it is not
even shameful for you to sell, neither is it for us to buy. And if to the one
who has suffered there belongs the well and justly, it will belong to the
thing done and to the one who has done or is doing. And it is possible to
reason falsely in this; for it is not the case that if he suffered justly, he
has at the same time also suffered justly from this man. Therefore one must
examine separately whether the one who suffered is worthy to suffer and the
one who did to do, then use it in whichever way it may fit. For sometimes such
a thing disagrees, as in the Alcmaeon of Theodectes, and as the trial about
Demosthenes and those who killed Nicanor./ What then is the trial of
Demosthenes and of those who killed Nicanor, about which the philosopher has
written, in which the most authoritative head of the dispute was from the
topic of relatives? The one toward Aeschines on behalf of Ctesiphon who
furnished Demosthenes the decree concerning the crown and is defending the
indictment for illegal proposals; for in this the thing sought was not the
common one, whether Demosthenes was worthy of honors and crowns, having
contributed from his own possessions the expense toward the walls, but whether
in the time in which he was accountable, the law hindering the crowning of
those accountable. For this is the from-relatives, whether just as it was
possible for the people to give, so also for the accountable man to receive
the crown. I at least think Aristotle makes mention of this trial. And if
someone will say that it is about the one of the gifts, which he defended in
the archonship of Anticles about the death of Alexander, he will make the arts
of Aristotle still much newer than the contests of Demosthenes. But that the
orator did not take over from the philosopher the arts with which he prepared
those wonderful speeches, but on the contrary Aristotle, having set beside him
the works of Demosthenes and of the other orators, wrote these arts, I think
has been sufficiently demonstrated.
