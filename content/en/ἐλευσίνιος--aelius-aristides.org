O Eleusis, once sweeter for me to sing, what Orpheus or Thamyris, or what
Eleusinian dweller Musaeus, will suffice for so great a matter? With what
citharas or lyres shall the common fall, the common fall of the earth, be
lamented? What is this theme you have set forth, O Zeus? Even as I bring
myself to the speech I grow numb and turn back, and I am forced to say this
one thing, that I cannot be silent. For who among the Greeks or who among the
barbarians is so dull or so deaf, or who so utterly beyond earth or the gods,
or, to speak in a word, so insensible of beautiful things, except those who
most miserably deserve to perish, who did these things, as not to have
regarded Eleusis as a common precinct of the earth, and of all things that are
divine for men, at once the most awe-inspiring and the most radiant? And for
what other place did the reports of myths hymn more wondrous things, or did
the rites hold a greater astonishment, or did the things seen more come into
rivalry

with the things heard? Countless generations of blessed men and women saw, in
the unspeakable visions, all that belonged to the spectacle; but what poets
and storytellers and all writers hymn in public is this: that Demeter's
daughter vanished for a time, and that Demeter went over all earth and sea
seeking her daughter, and that for a while she could not find her, but coming
to Eleusis she both gave the name to the place and, having found the maiden,
established the mysteries; and that grain came from the two goddesses to the
Athenians, and from the Athenians to both Greeks and barbarians (and to these
Celeus and Metaneira and Triptolemus are attached in the tale, and chariots of
winged serpents borne over all earth and sea); and that of foreigners Heracles
and the Dioscuri were first initiated, and that a gymnastic contest first
arose at Eleusis in Attica, and that the prize was of the fruit that had
appeared, as men tried how much they had advanced in strength from cultivated
food; and that the Greeks also brought first-fruits of the crops each time to
Athens, as to the mother-city of themselves and of the fruits, to the city of
the Athenians. And the Eumolpidae and the Ceryces, tracing themselves to
Poseidon and Hermes, furnished, the one family, hierophants, the other,
torch-bearers.

And such are the things that belong to myths. But later, when the Heraclidae
had come down into the Peloponnese and time had passed, the Dorians marched
against Athens. And when they reached Eleusis, ashamed, or if one must even
say fearing, they went away by the same road. And that movement of theirs then
settled Ionia among us. And when the Median expedition had come about, and the
greatest affairs and dangers had seized not only Greece but also all that was
outside the

Persian empire, many of the sanctuaries among the Greeks were burned, and
besides that the crown of Greece, the city of the Athenians; but Eleusis so
far excelled that she not only remained, so to speak, unsacked, but also, when
the sea-battle was joining, Iacchus went forth to fight alongside, and a cloud
setting out from Eleusis and raised over the ships struck down into the ships
of the barbarians together with the mystic song. And Xerxes, struck with
amazement, fled, and the fortunes of the Medes were destroyed. And when a
great war arose among the Greeks against one another and all things were
turned upside down, Eleusis alone in a manner of speaking remained at rest;
and neither the horse of the Boeotians nor the invasions of the Lacedaemonians
and Peloponnesians touched the enclosure, nor did they see the temple with
eyes other than those that were fitting. Then later, when Sphodrias set out
from Thebes, the torches that appeared sufficed to quench the daring. Then the
other truces were violated: during the Pythia the Cadmea was seized, and at
the Isthmia Argives led the same sacred embassy and Corinthians led it, having
overcome one another with arms. And the battle at the Alpheus I pass over in
silence, except that even there Zeus set no mean sign, the daring and victory
of those who were being robbed. But the mystic truces alone saved the name,
and Greece was sound for the Eleusinians alone. And most clearly this festival
was a purification both of mad outbreaks and of every unseemly disaster. And
why must one go through each thing? But Philips and Alexanders and Antipaters
and this whole catalogue of the later dynasts, though they moved countless
things among the Greeks, considered Eleusis alone truly inviolable and greater
than themselves. And I pass over in silence the Celts who last burst in revel
upon Greece, and whatever such things one might add: in all of them the
sanctuary escaped unharmed.

This then alone was left as a reminder of the ancient prosperity and dignity,
both to the city and to Greece. For sea-battles and land-battles and laws and
constitutions and high thoughts and voices and all things, so to speak,
failed, but the mysteries held out. Then the other festivals are completed
every fifth and third year, but the festival of the mysteries alone succeeded
in being held every year. And the greatest and most divine thing: this
festival alone among festivals was gathered and held in a single hall, and the
filling of the city and of the Eleusinion were one and the same. Then
sculptures and paintings and this surrounding adornment: who would not have
been gladdened even seeing them at the crossroads, let alone if he saw them as
an addition to the more solemn things? But indeed the gain of the festival was
not only the present cheer, nor the releases and deliverances from the
hardships of former time, but also to have sweeter hopes about the end, that
they would fare better, and would not lie in darkness and mire, which they say
await the uninitiated. These things, then, up to the wretched day; but what
the divinity has now given over to see and to hymn, what Argive lament, what
songs of Egyptians or Phrygians will measure themselves against? What
Eleusinian Aeschylus will sing to a chorus? What fire-set snares of Nauplius,
as Sophocles said, is it worthy to compare to this pyre? O torches, by what
sort of men you were quenched. O terrible and lightless day, that took away
the light-bearing nights. O fire, what a fire you appeared at Eleusis, and in
place of what a fire! O cloud and darkness, that now hold Greece. O Demeter,
who of old found the maiden there, but now the temple is left for you to seek.
And now the mysteries draw near, O earth and gods. And this Boedromion now
needs another cry, not such as when Ion came to the aid of Athens. O
proclamation, O catalogue of the sacred days and nights,

into what sort of day you have ended. For which is it even more worth
groaning? For the uninitiated, or for the initiated? For the initiated have
been robbed of the fairest things they saw, the uninitiated of those they
might have seen. O you who have wickedly danced out the mysteries. O you who
have shown the unshown things, common enemies of the gods under the earth and
above the earth. O Greeks, of old and now truly children, who looked on while
so great an evil was approaching. Will you not, O marvelous ones, now at least
prove yourselves to be something? Will you not at least save Athens herself?
