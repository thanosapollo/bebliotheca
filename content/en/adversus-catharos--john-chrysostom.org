Homily 6.
=63.491=
A homily delivered in the Church of the Apostles on the day of
Emperor Theodosius, against those who call themselves Cathari, after
two other bishops had spoken.
1. How fair is the pair of those who have spoken: one drawing the
ark with oxen, the other offering the first-fruits of words. For even
if their age is different, the pattern of their husbandry is one; or
rather, even their age is not different. For the young man has the
steadiness of the aged, and the aged man has the vigor and prime of
the young. So one would not err in calling both of them both elders
and youths, not according to the condition of their years, but
according to the disposition of their mind. Come then, let us too
contribute what is ours. For I see that your hearing is insatiable,
and we owe no ordinary debt to the blessed Theodosius, not because he
was emperor, but because he was pious; not because he was clothed in
purple, but because he had put on Christ, a garment that never grows
old; and he had put on the breastplate of righteousness, and the
shoes of the gospel of peace, and the sword of the Spirit, and the
shield of faith, and the helmet of salvation. With these weapons he
overthrew the tyrants, both the former and the last. For the first he
took without toil and without bloodshed, and set up the trophy,
without losing even a small part of his army; but the latter, when
battle was joined, he overthrew alone. For when the armies were drawn
up on either side, and clouds of arrows were launched, and a rout of
his own men was taking place as the enemy pressed hard, he leapt from
his horse, set his shield on the ground, bent his knees, and called
for alliance from heaven, and made the place of battle a place of
church, fighting not with bows and arrows, nor with spears, but with
tears and prayers. And so, when a sudden blast of wind fell upon
them, the arrows of the enemy were carried against those who shot
them; and the enemies who were breathing rage and slaughter, seeing
this, suddenly changing, proclaimed this man emperor, and handed over
their own, binding their hands behind them. And the blessed
Theodosius returned, made illustrious not by the victory alone, but
also by the manner of the victory. For it is not as with other
emperors, that the soldiers share the trophy with him, but the whole
belonged to him alone and to his faith. For this reason, then, we
call him blessed, and we do not even say that he has died. "For
everyone who believes in me," he says, "even if he dies, shall live,
and everyone who lives and believes in me shall never die." These
things Christ has spoken; these things shine forth through the deeds.
What then? Did he not die? someone says. By no means: for I would not
call this death, but a kind of sleep and a going abroad. For just as
many of the living have died, having buried the soul in the body as
in a tomb, so many of those who have died=63.492= live, shining with
righteousness; just as this blessed man also. For the most grievous
death, and death in truth, is that which is born of sin, which he
says those who believe in him do not die. "For he who lives," he
says, "and believes in me, shall never die....."
2. Did he who ran over land and sea as if winged, who brought
countless nations to Christ, who shared in the ineffable mysteries,
who was caught up to the third heaven, dare to say any such thing of
himself? By no means, but quite the contrary: he called himself an
untimely birth, and last of the apostles, and did not even think
himself worthy of the name. "For I am not sufficient," he says, "to
be called an apostle." What then is this madness? What is this
boastfulness? What is this frenzy? Being a man, you call yourself
pure, and you are persuaded that you are pure: and of how great
folly is this? For in calling yourself pure, you do something like a
man who would say that the sea is free of waves. As, then, that never
fails of waves, so neither do we fail of sins. Or do you not know
what we are: rejoicing, grieving, growing rich, being poor, being
insulted, being praised, being driven and warred against, enjoying
security, hungering, being filled? Countless passions surround the
soul, countless circumstances of affairs, countless diseases of the
body, countless irregularities of things, and you dare to say that in
a strait of so many surgings you are pure? And what could be more
filthy than one so disposed? And why do I speak of the whole of life?
Can anyone say of a single day, tell me, that he is pure? For even if
he does not fornicate, nor commit adultery, nor sin those forbidden
things, will he be able to boast that he was not vainglorious, that
he did not grow arrogant, that he did not look with unchaste eyes,
that he did not covet what belongs to his neighbor, that he did not
lie, that he did not stitch together deceit, that he did not curse
his enemies, that he did not envy his friend? And if he who loves the
one who loves him will be no less than the tax-collector in this
respect, of what pardon will he be worthy who even envies his
friend? Surrounded by so many evils, do you dare to call yourself
pure? But their madness will be shown not from this alone, but from
elsewhere also. I entreat you, and your love, considering all these
things, to be delivered from their boastfulness and to be outside of
their madness, and to try with all zeal both to wipe away the sins
that are present and to beat off those that approach. For even if
countless evils hem us round, if we are sober and watchful, we shall
be able to obtain much defense, much pardon, and to wash away what we
have done amiss. =63.493= And how this will be, hear: if we come to
church, if we groan over what we have done amiss, if we confess the
sins we have committed, if we give alms, if we offer prayers, if we
help the wronged, if we forgive our enemies their sins, if we weep
over what we have sinned; for all these are medicines of sins. These
things, then, I entreat, let us furnish, washing ourselves each day,
wiping ourselves clean; and with all these things let us count
ourselves wretched, saying that we are unprofitable servants; for
this too is no small kind of the spending of =63.494= sins, that even
when we succeed we imagine nothing great of ourselves, lest we suffer
what the Pharisee suffered. If we so manage our own affairs, we shall
be able to obtain loving-kindness and pardon on that fearful day, and
to be counted worthy of the promised goods; of which may it be that
we all enjoy, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus
Christ, through whom, and with whom, to the Father be glory, together
with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of ages.
Amen.
