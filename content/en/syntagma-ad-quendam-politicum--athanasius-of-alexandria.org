OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS ATHANASIUS
To a certain man of public life, a compendium very profitable, and exceedingly
beneficial.

Since you, a man ready-handed and of high-minded nature, together with
experience and learning of military matters, and moreover with an exact
grasping
of civil affairs, have not passed over carelessly even the sayings of the
priests, but have labored with love over these things also to such a degree
that, often feeding the body with foods, you do not deprive the soul of its
feeding from the words; but, as with some divine salt, you season your
table-conversations with the sayings of the Fathers; behold, I also, having
abridged in small compass some few of the useful and profitable things,
readable
by every Christian, but more by those who have risen above in height of life
and
word and nobility and rank, having improvised as it were a gift beseeming most
a
man loving letters and loving God, O best of friends, I have offered to you
the
present compendium, gathered like a nosegay from various Fathers and from
discourses heard, yet making the knowledge of those who pursue it not
contemptible; for it contains at once a discrimination and a division of the
three-part soul that is in us, as all we men are moved, and how we are thrust
along toward salvation or loss. And it defines the thoughts through which
every
sin is accomplished. And it divides also the passions in us, which are of the
soul and which of the body. Next it sets forth also the modes of the
providence
of God. Then it treats also of the free choice of man, and of our misuse of
things. And it abridges also the faith in the holy and blessed Trinity.
Further
it teaches also how one must believe concerning the incarnation of the Lord,
and
concerning the difference of virtue, and the discrimination of the nature of
things. And in general it is a kind of epitome of the knowledge of us and of
them, and of the nature and faith of the things concerning us. Which, carrying
it in your hands and pursuing it more carefully, and reaping the profit from
it,
you will not forget, I know well, him who both wrote it with his own hand and
gave it. =28.1397=

A discrimination of the three parts of the soul. That every rational soul
possesses three parts: reason, spirit, and desire. If in the spirit there is
love and philanthropy, and in the desire purity and sobriety, the reason is
enlightened; but if in the spirit there is misanthropy, and in the desire
licentiousness, the reason is darkened. The reason then is healthy and sober
when it has the passions in subjection, and beholds spiritually the principles
of the creatures of God, and leads them toward the holy and blessed Trinity.
And
it again is then moved according to nature when it loves all men, and holds
toward none of them grief or rancor. And the desire, when by continence and
humility and non-possession it mortifies the passions, that is, is no longer a
slave of the flesh, nor conformed to the reaching out after glory offered, but
has turned toward the divine and immortal longing. For the desire too has its
motion toward three things: either toward pleasure of the flesh, or toward
vain
glory, or toward the deceit of riches; and by this irrational reaching-out it
despises God and the things of God, and forgets its own nobility, and is made
a
beast toward its neighbor, and darkens the reason, and does not allow it to
look
up toward the truth. Whosoever has gained the higher mind of these, from
henceforth enjoys the kingdom of the heavens, and lives a blessed life,
awaiting
the blessedness laid up for those who love the Lord.

A division and minute subdivision of the soul. The soul is divided into the
rational, the spirited, and the desiring. Of the rational the sins are these:
unbelief, heresy, folly, indiscretion, blasphemy, the consents to the sins
that
come from the passible part. And the cure and healing of these is the
undoubting
faith toward God and the true dogmas, the continual meditation of the oracles
of
the Spirit, pure prayer, and unceasing thanksgiving to God. Of the spirited
the
sins are mercilessness, hatred, want of sympathy, rancor, envy, murder, and
continual meditation on such things. The cure and healing of these:
philanthropy, love, brotherly love, compassion, forbearance, kindness. Of the
desiring the sins are gluttony, fornication, uncleanness, licentiousness, love
of money, desire of glory. The cure and healing of these: continence, fasting,
hardship, non-possession, the scattering of riches to the poor, the reaching
out
after those immortal things, the appetite for the kingdom of God, and the
desire
of adoption.

A discrimination of the thoughts through which every sin is accomplished.
Eight in all are the comprehensive thoughts. First, that of gluttony; second,
that of fornication; third, that of avarice; fourth, that of anger; fifth,
that
of grief; sixth, that of acedia; seventh, that of vainglory; eighth, that of
pride. That all these thoughts molest us or do not molest us =28.1400= is not
within our power; but to dwell with them, or to move passions or not to move
them, is likewise within our power. And assault is one thing, and combination
another, and passion another, and struggle another, and consent another, and
actualization another. And assault is the simply-happening reminder of the
enemy; as, "Do this or that;" as upon Christ our God: "Say that these stones
become loaves." This, as was said, is not within our power. And combination is
the acceptance of the thought injected by the enemy; as, with it, meditation
and
converse, according to our choice. And passion is the habit produced from the
combination, the enemy injecting a passion; and as it were the concurrence,
meditation, and fantasy. And struggle is the resistance, either for the
undoing
of the thought of the passion, or for consent, as the Apostle says: "The flesh
desires against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh. And these are
opposed to one another." And consent is the nodding toward the passion of the
thought. And actualization is the very doing. And gluttony is destroyed by
continence, and fornication by the divine longing and the reaching out toward
the things to come; and avarice by compassion toward the poor; and anger by
goodness and love toward all; and worldly grief by spiritual joy; and acedia
by
endurance and patience and thanksgiving to God; and vainglory by the hidden
working of the virtues and continual prayer; and pride by not judging or
despising anyone, as the boastful Pharisee, but counting oneself last of all.
The mind, then, freed from these said passions and lifted up toward God, lives
from thenceforth the blessed life, receiving the earnest of the Spirit; and
having journeyed from here with dispassion and true knowledge, it stands
before
the light of the holy and blessed Trinity unto endless ages, being illumined
with the holy angels.

Disposition of the passions. Of the passions some are bodily, some psychical.
And bodily we call gluttony, fornication, drunkenness, licentiousness; and
psychical, hatred toward the neighbor, envy, strife, vainglory, pride. And
these
work upon our soul when love and continence are absent; but when love and
continence are present, all these vanish away, some by spiritual love, some by
fasting and continence. For then the mind recovers its proper light and
beholds
God unhindered.

Concerning the providence of God. All things are from God, both the good and
the grievous and the unworthy; but some by good pleasure, some by economy,
some
by permission. And those by good pleasure, when we live according to virtue;
for
God wills us to live without sin, and to conduct ourselves with piety and
virtue. By economy, when we are chastened while erring and sinning. By
permission, when, being chastened, we do not turn again; =28.1401= for God
willed that man be saved, as also the angels cried crying: "Glory to God in
the
highest, and on earth peace, among men of good pleasure." And again he
chastens
us economically when we sin, "that we be not condemned with the world," as the
Apostle says: "Being judged by the Lord, we are chastened. There is not an
evil
in a city which the Lord has not made;" that is, plagues, famines, diseases,
and
slaughters of wars. For all these are destructive of sin. And he permits, and
even wholly abandons, those who are not willing either to live without sin,
or,
being chastened, to turn, but to abide still in wickedness; as it is written,
that "God has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart;" and "He gave them
over to a reprobate mind to do the things not fitting;" that is, he let them
go
because of their free choice; and "In hardening I will harden the heart of
Pharaoh;" that is, I will permit it to be hardened because of his
insensibility.

Concerning our free choice. God made man self-mastering, honored with reason
and wisdom, setting before his eyes life and death; that, if he will, by his
free choice he lay hold of life, he live unto the age; but if from an evil
choice he lay hold of the way of death, he be chastened everlastingly. For the
things of nature, being unchangeable, are worthy neither of punishments nor of
honor. For no one was ever blamed for being white or black, or for being tall
or
short; for these are not of our choice. But of choice are both the punishments
and the honors. There is need, then, of both: of our counsel and will, and of
the cooperation and upholding of God; for when one is lacking, the other also
is
idle. For God foreknew, and predestined, and called; but those called
according
to purpose, as the Apostle says, that is, according to will and counsel; for
those not willing he let go according to their own will. For he does not
compel,
nor does he violate our free choice; for it is of the inclination toward both,
virtue, I say, and vice, that we received our self-mastery. Nothing of the
things given us by God for use is evil; for all things whatsoever from God are
good, and exceedingly good; only the misuse is evil; and that is of our own
self-mastery. Wherefore not foods are evil, but gluttony; nor the begetting of
children, but fornication; nor the partaking of wine, but drunkenness; nor
riches, but avarice; nor glory, but vainglory. One must therefore do all
things
so as to flee the misuse, and to use rightly the things given by God; for so
doing, we shall become superior to wickedness, and be found within virtue.

An acquaintance clear and brief, and a discrimination of our faith in the holy
Trinity. We owe it to believe as we were baptized; and we were baptized in the
name of the Father, and of the Son, =28.1404= and of the Holy Spirit; into one
essence, namely, and Godhead, and one kingdom, and principle, and power; and
three hypostases, or persons, and characters, or, if you will to call them so,
properties. For hypostases are one thing, and essence another. For essence we
call the common thing of the three hypostases; for the three persons are of
one
nature and essence. And hypostasis we call a nature with characterizing
properties; for the Father is one, and the Son another, and the Holy Spirit
another; which divides the properties only, that is, the hypostases, but not
also the nature. But not "one thing and another," which divides the natures;
but
of the same nature and of the same essence. For at once Father and Son and
Holy
Spirit; but the Father also unbegotten, and the Son birth-fruit of the Father,
as light from the sun, and word from mind; and the Holy Spirit also procession
of the Father. Proper, then, to the Father is the unbegottenness and to be
called Father; proper to the Son, to be called Son and to be begotten of the
Father; proper to the Spirit, to be called Holy Spirit, and to proceed from
the
Father. And of the three persons, or rather hypostases, one nature, one will,
one actualization, one goodness, one power, one volition. Therefore we also
confess one God. For they are not separated from one another, as men also are,
nor are their judgments diverse; but at once Father, Son, and Holy Spirit
pervade one another without confusion and without division. And each person,
as
was said, keeps its own property: one the fatherhood, one the sonship, one the
procession. If, then, someone should ask you: What is the Father? Answer him:
What the Son is, and the Holy Spirit, remaining Father. And if he say: What is
the Son? Answer: What the Father is and the Spirit, remaining Son. Likewise
also
concerning the Holy Spirit; for this is what the Father and the Son are,
remaining Spirit. The property is without communion, as the great among
theologians and divine Gregory says. For how, he says, would a property
remain,
being moved and passing over? The Godhead, then, if one must say it briefly,
is
undivided in the divided, the division being of nature, not of hypostases; as
in
three suns cleaving to one another, one blending of the light. When,
therefore,
we look toward the Godhead that is in one nature, and the first cause, and the
monarchy, one is what is envisioned by us; but when toward the properties and
the persons in which is the Godhead, three are the things worshiped. Let
examples be added, a faint image declaring so great a thing, of the divine
nature, I say, according to the Theologian Gregory: conceive for me the seeing
of an eye, and the beginning of water, and the spring, and the river; for
these
have neither been separated in time, nor have they been thrust apart from one
another in their cohesion to each other, even if they seem to be divided by
their properties; conceive also the sun, and light, and ray; and mind, and
word,
and spirit going forth with the word. Yet according to him who said: "Let
faith
lead, and not demonstration," and every man must, through pure and
God-illumined action, using the Spirit as guide, that light be apprehended by
light, believe in Father, =28.1405= and Son, and Holy Spirit, the one Godhead
and power known and worshiped in three hypostases.

How one must believe concerning the incarnation of the Lord. That the very
Word of God, the uncircumscribed, the bodiless, the only-begotten Son of the
Father, the light from the light, the fountain of life and of immortality, the
effulgence of the glory, the character of the hypostasis, by the counsel of
his
Father that is before-origin and co-eternal, and by the cooperation of the
Holy
Spirit, from the virginal bloods of the Theotokos and ever-virgin Mary, in his
own hypostasis riveted to himself flesh ensouled and rational, and this
fashioned
through the nine-months' time from her; he was born God man-formed, that is,
God
and man in one hypostasis bearing two natures, undividedly and without
confusion, and two wills and actualizations, divine together with human; for
he
willed as God and worked, and willed as man and worked; but a natural will and
actualization, not a sinful one. For the human nature was taken up without
sin;
and from the union itself they were united in will. What the divine will
willed, this also the will of the assumption. But we call natural both will
and
actualization; as, he willed to eat, he willed to drink, he willed to walk
about; and these he worked, and willed. But he did these things with the
divine
nature conceding, yet not of necessity, as we; but also the toiling, and the
agonizing, and the asking the Father that the cup pass by, and this of the
natural will. But also after the resurrection and the session at the right
hand
of the Father we confess two natures in Christ, volitional and energizing. For
where there is a rational nature, there also is will and actualization. But
two
natural wills, and one gnomic will; for the Godhead dispensing, and the
humanity
following, and conceding to it to work both.

Concerning the difference of virtues. We received commandments from Christ our
God, which work things of God from henceforth, and make us live without sin,
and
converse as angels among men; such as repentance, humility and continual
mourning, the love toward him springing from the whole heart, and the
disposition toward the neighbor, pure prayer, and self-denial, and to hold
before one's eyes throughout the death for his sake, and to walk in his light
unceasingly, and to hold oneself beneath every creature, and to beseech him
continually. For he who works these divine commandments with faith becomes a
son
of God, and son of light and heir of God, and co-heir of Christ, and conformed
to his image and of kindred nature; and, to say it briefly and simply, God by
position according to grace. But there are other commandments of Christ, which
procure forgiveness, yet do not also count worthy of perfection; such as
=28.1408= to confess and openly declare one's own sins, not to judge, not to
meddle with the things of others, to show mercy from one's possessions, to
bear
bravely and with thanksgiving the grievous things that come upon one, to
confess
that one is saved by gift and grace. If, then, someone works the first and
God-working commandments, from thenceforth he receives the earnest of the Holy
Spirit; but if not, even if he works the second with diligence, that he may
obtain forgiveness of the things wherein he offended. But if he fall away from
both, this man is useless and wretched and miserable; and let him await the
punishment laid up for the devil and his angels.

Discrimination of the nature of things. Of things some are properly good, some
properly evil, and some middling; and properly good: prudence, courage,
sobriety, justice; properly evil: folly, cowardice, licentiousness, injustice;
and middling: wealth, poverty, glory, ingloriousness, health, sickness, much
learning, ignorance. And they are called middling because in themselves they
are
neither properly good nor properly evil, but become the one or the other
according to the use of those who use them; as wealth is good when given to
almsgiving, but evil when spent on pleasures. Likewise poverty also is good,
teaching us endurance and thanksgiving and freedom of speech; but evil,
bearing
blasphemy and want of freedom from our not enduring. And glory is good, when
it
teaches us humility and a reaching out toward the glory to come; but evil,
when
it persuades us to servility and to do all things through fear of man.
Likewise
health is good, when we use it for the pleasing of God and the working of his
commandments; but evil, when toward licentiousness and injustice. Similarly
sickness, when we give thanks and use it as a fellow-worker, is good; but
evil,
when it drives us toward ingratitude and blasphemy and anger. And much
learning
is good, when we use it for teaching and for the help of those wronged; but
evil, when toward vainglory and advocacy of injustice. And ignorance is good,
when it prepares us to be subject to those who know, and to derive profit from
them; but evil, when it persuades us to follow our own ignorance, and not to
follow the better. Likewise also all the rest, such as nobility of birth and
low
birth, comeliness and uncomeliness, a good voice and a bad, strength and
weakness, swiftness and slowness, skill and unskillfulness, and fitness and
unfitness for ministries. All these, as was said, are of the middling; and he
who uses them well will suffer no harm from them, as the blessed Job. For,
being
examined in both, he appeared approved and unbeaten, and in all things pleased
God. So that one must not blame the things; for nothing of the things given by
God is evil, but it is from our erring and self-enslaved choice, and from the
self-blinded discrimination of our soul concerning these things, which of its
own free will slides away toward the misuse of them, out of its own love of
pleasure, and evil-thinks of God and of his kingdom.
