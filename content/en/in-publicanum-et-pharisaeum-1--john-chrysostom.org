On the Publican, and on the Pharisee.

=62.723= The passion of love of money desires greed; but it is healed through
almsgiving, when it runs to repentance. The manner of fornication pursues sin;
but the weeping of repentance heals this, and shows the soul cleaner than the
sun, flashing with the virtues of temperance. And the inhumanity of robbery,
when it changes the deeds of bloodshed and cries to Christ, "Do not deprive
me, Christ, of your kingdom," his soul enters into paradise. The error of
idolatry itself also has a medicine of healing, when it faithfully receives
the Christ who is proclaimed, and renounces the error of lawlessness, and
clothes itself in the grace of piety, through baptism, which by three
invocations of God signifies the eternal Trinity.

The mire of hatred of the brethren is itself washed away, when with a grateful
mind they receive that Gospel saying: "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they
shall be called sons of God." Who does not rejoice to be named a child of God
the Father? Because God fashioned man, and holds the world together by a nod
of loving-kindness, and all things yield to his will, and by excess of
goodness he brought the things that are seen from not being into being,
setting man as steward =62.724= of his creatures, that having become faithful
he may receive the wage of the five talents, and may rule the ten cities
written in the Gospels. But if someone is found lazy, entrusted with one
talent, and hides this, and does not bring to the master the double price,
that is, along with the sensible also the intelligible man, but begins to say,
"I was afraid because you are a stern man," and the rest of the words of
ingratitude and perversity, then even what he has shall be taken from him, and
he shall be cast out into the furnace of fire.

For this world was given as a training-ground, which at one time affords grief
to men, and at another joy. And he who has lived thankfully in both of these
shall be entrusted with the kingdom of the heavens. For just as the current of
rich waters, receiving what flows upon it, raises up bubbles, and some of them
burst as soon as they come to be, while others, swollen more, afterward burst
themselves as well: in the same way the sea of life covered those who appeared
for a little, and those who lasted longer it also drowned. Therefore let us
take up the word again: every occasion of sin has a swift grace of
forgiveness, when it has repentance running before it. The rot of pride is a
hard-to-heal disease, a pretext of hatred of men, a swaggering fall, =62.725=
a height casting itself down from itself, a shadowy fantasy, a reed shaken by
wind, smoke raised into air and dissolving into nothing.

What do you think, O braggart, that you possess more than the poor man?
Nothing. Do you see another heaven, while he is burned by the sun's ray? When
the air becomes freezing, are you warmed by the sun, while he is chilled by
the cold? Is the air not breathed alike? Is the same earth not walked? Is he
not clothed with the same body? Ought you not rather to give thanks to God who
entrusted to you so much as the man in want is able to measure? I tell you,
brother, this ought rather to urge you toward humility. For the rich man who
is moderate shows love of mankind; for the poor man has humility even when he
does not wish it. You then will be shown great when you pursue by purpose what
he pursues by necessity. But if you are not persuaded by these things, hear
Scripture: "The Lord resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble." And if
you despise these things too, you will be despised by God; then the conceit of
insensibility, wishing to lead you up to a lifted height, is about to raise
you greatly, that it may bring you down more greatly.

For what is wealth? Matter leaping from one to another; for when death comes
in, this departs from you, but the evils from it never depart from you. See
that you do not grow proud, being earth and ashes, and fall into the fall of
the Pharisee; know yourself; for this the Lord too enjoins, saying through the
parable: "Two men went up into the temple to pray, the one a Pharisee, and the
other a publican." Two men went up, but two men did not come down, traversing
the same road without going astray. And the Pharisee clearly missed this road.
They entered one place to pray, and they were not found going out into the one
place. The Pharisee was running confidently, but the publican was going in
fear; and the one who was running stayed behind, while the one behind
overtook. The Pharisee was running to proclaim his righteous deeds; the
publican too was hastening to announce his trespasses. They went up to pray;
and the one was seen puffed up, the other was shown praying.

For such things the Pharisee was laying down as law for himself, saying: I am
not a debtor of the commandment of the law; I am not liable for what the
prophets commanded; I do not need the teaching of Moses, from which I have
failed in none of these things; but I go up to pray, paying the debt of custom
which my forefathers used to perform. Why then, O Pharisee, do you reverence
the temple for the sake of the forefathers, but for the sake of prayer you do
not see the temple? Your forefathers faithfully performed the thanksgiving,
but you parade the thanksgiving, that your sacrifice may be glorified by men;
and upon you comes the saying of David: "Let his prayer become sin, and let
another take his office," that is, the publican. And what of the publican's?
He was going to the temple condemning himself, naming himself unworthy of
prayer. He called himself unworthy; therefore Christ also accepted his
purpose. He was saying he was in debt to sin, but Christ showed him bound to
righteousness; he granted him forgiveness of the things he had done amiss, and
bought the purpose in return; he freed him from sin, that he might enslave him
to righteousness. O slavery, through which we flee an evil slavery, and take
refuge in the freedom of Christ! O slavery, furnishing a light yoke, and after
this having and giving back eternal life! O slavery, which in this world is
called slavery, but in the one to come grants an unending kingdom!

Let us run to this slavery, brethren, that we may reap the freedom of our
souls. When someone becomes a good slave, then the Lord shows him forth as a
genuine son, then he fulfills upon him the saying through the Prophet: "I
said, You are gods, and all of you sons of the Most High." Blessed are those
counted worthy of such an honor! Blessed are those who have been eager to be
delivered from corruption! Blessed are those who through this slavery acquired
the freedom, and crying to the freedom with which Christ redeemed us, we stand
having the cross of Christ as a stay! Let us cry to Christ: Count us, O good
Shepherd, as one of the lost sheep; seek us =62.726= in the thoughts of our
hearts, and having found us wandering and about to be caught by the enemy,
gather us into your flock. Count us as one drachma, and lighting with the
fire of your divinity search,
and having found us, rejoice with the saints and with the angels who dwell
with your undefiled divinity; show us forth as the publican, that praying we
may embrace humility, and turn away from pride, and strip off high-mindedness
the mother of sin, and cling to temperance the mother of repentance.

Let us put off the old man with his deeds, and put on the new with his deeds
and his grace; let us groan from the depth of the heart as we pray, that we
may drive our enemy into the depth of the furnace of Gehenna. For when you
have groaned, he is struck; when you strike the breast, he is wounded; when
you receive the blows, he receives the slaughters. For the publican too,
striking the breast, struck the Pharisee in return, and convicted the thought
in him that was working the conceit of pride. For the Pharisee stood and
prayed by himself, saying: "God, I thank you that I am not as the rest of men,
extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as this publican; I fast twice in
the week, I tithe all that I acquire."

O Pharisee, you emptied your wage through boasting! Therefore you received a
vote of condemnation. You say: "I am not as the rest of men." He was a man
according to nature and God's fashioning, but irrational according to the
taking on of pride; a man as having that which is according to the image, but
a beast as possessing arrogance. For he set himself outside the nature of men,
he laid down another creation as law for his own body, he styled himself
higher than earthly things, he supposed himself greater than every prophet and
patriarch. Did you not remember, O Pharisee, Elijah's boast, boasting the same
things, though not from conceit but from divine zeal, when he said: "Lord,
they have killed your prophets, and dug down your altars, and I alone am
left"? And what was the oracle to him? "I have left to myself seven thousand
men who have not bent the knee to Baal."

What then? When all had transgressed, there were seven thousand who had the
divine zeal and were equaled with the prophet, and you cry, "I am not as the
rest of men, extortioners, unjust, adulterers"? Did no one under heaven escape
the defilement of greed? Does no one embrace temperance, Pharisee? You condemn
yourself, you who are condemned by all; you examine against yourself, you who
are rejected by God; you crown yourself through boasting, but you lost your
crown through condemnation; you praise yourself as sinless, but you were shown
liable to sin; you set yourself on a pillar as having fulfilled the law, but
having touched the law you insulted the lawgiver. You cried your achievements
before all; you turned them into trespasses. The publican, crying out by
silence, prevailed upon the God Word, whom you, crying against yourself,
provoked. You were not ashamed to say: "I am not as the rest of men,
extortioners, unjust, adulterers, or even as the publican," stained with sins,
mixed up in profligacies, who made the holy place common by his entrance, who
prays with me unworthily, the worker of all lawlessness. And the name itself
is enough as witness of his own condition; for he is a publican, paying to sin
the works of wickedness.

Even if these things are not written in these very words in the undefiled
Gospels, still the aim of pride preserves this manner. O Pharisee, you
disapproved the things of the publican, but Christ was well pleased in the
publican; you set him at naught against yourself, but Christ crowned him
before all. Christ was pleased, seeing together with the publican's repentance
a shame intertwined in him, and a groan from the depth of the heart sent up at
once to heaven, and tears making their way; Christ was pleased, or rather he
felt reverence for the publican, who by shame shook off the shame. The
publican fitted well to himself the saying of Wisdom: =62.727= "There is a
shame that brings on sin." This he went after in the life of ingratitude.

And there is a shame that is glory and grace. This he came upon through the
manner of repentance, healing contraries from contraries through faith.
Imitating a physician, he places a plaster against the body, which eats away
the flesh but cleanses the wound, so that when the living body is uncovered,
he may recognize the device of the healing. The channels of the pleasures,
using the groan as if an iron cautery, he stopped up, and the swelling wound
of the passion he soothed with tears and healed, and packing the opening of
the wound with lint through the striking of the hands he closed it, and the
flux flowing upon the soul from the body he dried up, and through repentance
he brought it clean to the Fashioner.

But let us hear also the rest of the Pharisee's words: "I fast twice in the
week." It indicates the abstinence of the fourth day and of Preparation. And
the Sabbaths are rest for the Hebrews. "I abstain from the various foods; I do
not soil my lips with unclean taste, I am wholly clothed in abstinence, I am
wholly clad in holiness; and besides I also bring to God the tenths of what I
acquire." O conceit breathing insensibility! "I fast twice in the week." Is he
not disposed as one making a gift of his rations to God? "I fast twice in the
week." "Lord, does not fasting," he says, "furnish to God the commandments of
the law? and what men accomplished by toil, this I do by purpose?" What then
did the lover of mankind as judge, the generous president of the games, do for
the course of repentance? He both endured the Pharisee vaunting, and delighted
in the publican repenting. But since the one by his words, as by violent
winds, shook off the fruit of the soul, while the other, having crushed
himself, did not wait for another accuser, he assigned to each what was toward
his desert. =62.728= For the publican who had become his own accuser he left
free of the condemnation; but the Pharisee who set his achievements in the
midst he declared poorer than the publican. The manner for both: for the one,
wealth was the occasion of poverty; for the other, a slip from wealth into
poverty.

For what does the Master say? "Amen I say to you, this man went down to his
house justified rather than that one." O how evil is the disease of pride! The
Pharisee went up thick-crowned, crowned as with flowers by his achievements;
the publican journeyed with him naked of every defense. The one, having
readily humbled himself, was exalted; the other, having hurled himself toward
untimely height, endured a ridiculous stripping. Was it not better to use
lowered voices? Was it not better to hear the prophetic voice saying: "Humble
yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you"? Was it not more profitable
to hold the tongue, and to keep the wealth which he had acquired with many
labors? For the virtues he gathered in time he scattered by the voice. Perhaps
the Pharisee did not hear what is written, that "Every one high-hearted is
unclean before the Lord"; was he not taught what is well said: "What your
right hand does, let your left not know"? But perhaps he knew both, yet the
strength of vainglory conquered.

Let us then acquire the publican's humility, and thrust away the weight of
sins; and let us hate pride, through which the Pharisee lost the wealth of the
virtues; for "The Lord resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble." To
him be the glory and the might, now and always, and unto the ages of ages.
Amen.
