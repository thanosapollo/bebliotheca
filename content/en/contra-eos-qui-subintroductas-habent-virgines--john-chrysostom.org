INSTRUCTION AND REFUTATION AGAINST THOSE WHO HAVE SUBINTRODUCED VIRGINS
Among our ancestors there were two pretexts that settled women with men: one was
that of marriage, ancient and just and reasonable, since it has God as its
lawgiver: "For this cause a man shall leave his father and his mother, and
shall be joined to his wife, and the two shall be one flesh." The other was
that of fornication, both later than this and unjust and lawless, since it was
brought in by the wicked demons. But in our own generation a third way as well
has been invented, some new and strange one, and one that has much perplexity
for those who wish to find the cause. For there are some who, apart from
marriage and intercourse, taking unwedded girls, seat them at home
continually, and shut themselves in with them even to extreme old age, neither
for the begetting of children, for they say they do not come together with
them, nor from wantonness, for they say they keep them unstained; but if
anyone asks the cause, they have many and have rehearsed them, yet a
reasonable one, as I at least think, not even one, nor a decent one. But not
yet about these, nor let us for the present tell their pretexts, but the one
we ourselves most suspect to be the cause, let us bring that into the open.
What then is this? And if I should altogether miss the mark, it is permitted
to those of you who wish to take hold of it. What then is the pretext? It
seems to me that there is a certain pleasure in dwelling with women, not only
by the law of marriage, but even apart from marriage and intercourse. And if I
do not think rightly, I have nothing to say; for I am for the present telling
you my own opinion; and perhaps not mine only, but theirs as well; for that it
seems so to them also is clear from this. For they would not have despised so
much glory and so many scandals, unless the pleasure of this cohabitation were
some strong and tyrannical thing. And if some should be displeased when we say
these things, I will ask them to pardon us, and not be angry; for I did not
choose of my own will, nor simply, to take on this enmity; I am not so
wretched and miserable as to want to offend everyone for nothing; but I am in
great pain and I ache both when I see the glory of God being insulted, and the
salvation of the many draining away little by little because of this pleasure.
For that this is sweet, and has a keener love than dwelling by the law of
marriage, you are perhaps even now in an uproar as you hear it, but after the
demonstration you yourselves will also agree. For in the case of a lawful
wife, intercourse being unhindered has both soothed the longing and often
brought the man to satiety, and has dissolved the violent desire. And apart
from these things, labor-pains and births and childbearing and the rearing of
children, and the continual diseases that follow from these, besieging the
body and withering the bloom of youth, make the sting of pleasure weaker. But
in the case of the virgin none of these is present; for there is neither
intercourse able to loosen and bring down the goad of nature, nor labor-pains
and the rearing of children withering the flesh, but they preserve their prime
for a long time, remaining untouched.

Hence after births and the rearing of children the bodies of those who marry
become weaker, but even to the fortieth year the virgins who are kept in
chambers remain competing; whence double things of desire are raised in those
who dwell with them, both because the longing is not allowed to be soothed by
intercourse, and because the ground of the longing remains stronger for a long
time. This I suspect is the pretext of this sharing of a tent. But let us not
be angry with them nor be displeased; for no one wanting to raise the one who
is ill


does this with anger and rebuke, but applies the medicines with treatment and
exhortation. For if it were our task to punish, and we had the rank of judges,
we would have to be angry as well; but if, leaving that, we take the rank of
physicians and of those who wish to heal, we must exhort and beg, and, if need
be, even take hold of their very knees, so that what we are eager for may come
to pass. As therefore physicians, leading the sick away from things that harm
yet have pleasure, whether foods or drinks, persuade the patients of this
before the rest, that such a thing has much unpleasantness together with the
harm: so let us also do, showing them that this most pleasant cohabitation has
much ruin, and has been prepared no better than deadly drugs. And it seems to
have a certain cheer, but it pours out much sharpness and bitterness hidden
within upon the soul that is being pleased. Since this withdrawal too, when it
comes with persuasion, is safe; for the one who is parted from the beloved by
fear and by necessity becomes a more violent lover, and perhaps will even come
back again; but the one who has condemned the thing, and has fled it as
harmful and bitter, would not even turn back again, having the vote from his
own judgment stronger than every necessity. 2 How then shall we persuade them
that this is not only harmful, but also bitter? And how else than from the
very nature of the facts? Let us ask them then: if someone set a costly table,
full of many and most pleasant dishes, and with a violent threat forbade
touching any of the things set out, would anyone choose to sit at such a table
and punish himself? I at least do not think so; for he would not reap so much
pleasure from the sight as he would endure dejection from the prohibition. And
what if someone, showing a thirsty and burning man a spring both pure and
clear, allowed him not only not to taste of it, but not even to touch it with
the tips of his fingers, would anything have been more bitter than this
punishment? Not even here, I think, will anyone contradict. For so great an
evil is this, that even those of the outsiders who are especially skilled at
examining these things, I mean the nature of pleasure and of pain, when they
wished to make certain people punished violently, did it in no other way than
by this manner; for having fashioned a certain myth, they bring in the myth a
man who had to pay the last penalty, and setting nothing else before him but a
banquet, and showing water flowing by, they allowed him to enjoy none of
these, but as soon as he stretched out his hand all the things seen flew away,
and this happened continually. And this was the manner of the punishment
according to the myth of the outsiders. And a certain one of the philosophers,
seeing one of those with him kiss a handsome youth, wondered, saying that this
man would also readily dive into fire, who had dared by the kiss to kindle
such a furnace in himself. But I would not say so much as this, that they kiss
those who dwell with them or touch them; and if certain others should accuse
them of these things, I will try to show that, even if they go only so far,
they draw upon themselves again a torture more bitter than the former.

For if sight alone implants so much pain, when touch also is added, a much
coarser enjoyment than sight, it raises the flame greater, and makes the ache
sharper, and makes the beast more grievous. For by as much as we increase the
things of desire, and furnish it stronger food, by so much also the things of
pain are stretched for us. And just as the one sitting beside the table and
the spring is not so pained when he sees, as when, having been allowed to
touch with the hand, he is again forbidden to taste; so also those who are
allowed to touch virginal bodies are punished more grievously from the touch
than from the sight, drawing to themselves a more bitter pain from the
failure. And why must we philosophize these things from the affairs of the
outsiders? For the vote of God, which is stronger than all these, this shows
that this is such


a thing. For wishing to punish Adam, he did not settle him far from paradise,
but near it, so that he might have a continual punishment, the sight of the
longed-for place, seeing it always, but not being allowed to enjoy it. But
perhaps someone would say, And how, if this is by nature so bitter, do the
many pursue it with so much eagerness? But I would say to this that this very
thing is the greatest proof of their extreme sickness. For this is the custom
of those who are ill, to reap a certain cold and short-lived pleasure, and
from it to prepare a long punishment for themselves. This one may see also in
those with fever. Those who are not willing to endure a little, laying hold,
for a short-lived amusement, of drinks and foods that have been forbidden,
work for themselves a long and most grievous disease. And the healthy must not
take their votes about things from the sick; since, if we follow these, we
shall condemn both medicine and philosophy. For they suffer this not only in
fevers, nor only in the case of women, but also in the case of money and in
all the rest, those who are sick with such things. For those who are mad about
money, knowing that if they give up these small things here to those in need
they will receive countless good things there, nevertheless hold them and bury
them, and choose to fall from all the good things rather, because of this cold
and brief pleasure, than to be delivered from eternal punishment and to reap
immortal life through a timely contempt of these things. This then these men
also suffer: not enduring to stand a little away from this small desire that
comes through the eyes, they gather an unendurable fire for themselves; and by
as much as they think they are pleased, by so much more they are entangled in
the evil, the devil having devised this for the remaining and the increase of
the flame, whence burning they both are pleased and are in pain, planting a
certain absurd mixture in their own souls.

3 And if anyone from these words should condemn us of incontinence, for they
say that noble men, even when they dwell with women, suffer nothing terrible,
I count such men blessed, and I would wish myself also to receive so great a
strength; and I myself am perhaps persuaded that it is possible for there to
be some such men; but I would have wished that those who accuse us could also
be persuaded of this, that a young man in his vigor, dwelling with a virgin
girl and sitting with her and dining with her and conversing through the day,
for I add none of the other things, the untimely laughter and the relaxations
and the soft words, and the rest, which perhaps it is not even good to speak;
but that, having the same house, and sharing table and salt, and with much
freedom both taking many things and giving many, he is caught by none of the
human things, but remains pure both of wicked desire and of pleasure: I would
have wished to be able to persuade those who accuse us of these things, but
they are not willing to be persuaded, but they even cry out against us who
make this defense as shameless, and as sick with the same things as they, and
as wrapping up our own vice. And what is this to us? he says; for we are not
liable for the folly of others, nor, if someone should be scandalized
unreasonably, am I worthy to pay a penalty because of that man's folly. But
Paul did not say this, but even if someone is scandalized unjustly, and
through weakness, he commanded us to help. For only then are we delivered from
the punishment laid down for those who cause scandal, when from the scandal
another gain is born greater than the harm from the scandal; but if this is
not so, and only this one thing happens, that others are scandalized, whether
they are scandalized reasonably or unreasonably, whether as weak, their blood
is upon our head, and from our hands God requires such souls. For this reason
then, so that we may neither always take thought nor always despise those who
are scandalized, Christ has set such bounds and canons for us, and


doing this and that at the fitting time. For when he was speaking about the
nature of foods, and was showing it to be clean, and was releasing from Jewish
observation, when Peter came in and said that They were scandalized, "Leave
them," he says. And he not only despised them, but also accusing them, "For
every plant which my heavenly Father has not planted shall be rooted up"; so
he made void the law concerning foods. But when those who demanded the
didrachma came to Peter and said, "Does your teacher not pay the didrachma?",
he no longer did the same as in that case, but he takes thought for the
scandal and says, "But that we may not scandalize them, cast a hook into the
sea, and take the first fish that comes up, and you will find a stater in its
mouth; take this and give it for me and you." Do you see him both taking
thought and not taking thought? For here it was not urgent that the glory of
the Only Begotten should be unveiled; how could it be, where he even took care
to overshadow it, and commanded many not to say that he was the Christ? And
for this reason from laying down the drachma there was no harm, but from not
laying it down great evils were born; for as a tyrant, and one rising up, and
an enemy of the whole city, and one casting them into the last dangers, so
they would have turned away. For this reason then even when they wished to
seize him and make him king, he fled far off, thrusting away this suspicion
from much that remained. But there what was being accomplished was necessary;
therefore usefully and in season and upon a greater achievement he despised
the scandal. For it was now timely not to hinder those who were about to
ascend to the highest philosophy through sparing the Jews, but to send them
forth, now that the time was calling, toward the purity of the soul, and not
to hold them in bodily observation, but to allow them to be delivered from
that lowliness. So Paul also both despises those who are scandalized, and does
not despise them, following the teacher, and says, "I please all men in all
things, not seeking my own profit, but that of the many, that they may be
saved." And if Paul overlooks his own profit, that he may find that of the
brethren, of how great a punishment would we not be worthy, who do not even
choose to stand away from our own harm for the sake of finding the profit of
others, but gladly destroy others together with ourselves, when it is possible
to save both ourselves and others? For this reason then, when he sees the gain
to be much, and greater than the harm of the scandal, he despises those who
are scandalized; but when there is no gain, and only this comes out, that they
are scandalized, he chooses both to do and to suffer all things, so that this
may not happen. And he does not philosophize such things as you do, nor does
he say, Why then are they weak? why then do they act unreasonably? but for
this very reason especially he spares them, because they suffer unreasonably,
because they are weak.

4 For tell me, what reasonable pretext could the one scandalized have to speak
against the man who eats meat and drinks wine? For God set this law for us
from above. Yet even so, if someone is scandalized even at these things, Paul
abstains from these as well. "For I will not eat flesh," he says, "nor drink
wine, that I may not scandalize my brother." And he did not say the things you
say, that Am I going to be liable for the unreason of others? and if it should
seem good to someone to be scandalized simply, am I worthy to pay a penalty?
And yet if he had even said it, he would have said it far more justly than
you. For the one scandalized at those things is irrational and exceedingly
foolish; but the one scandalized at these would have many just and reasonable
causes to speak; yet even so Paul, who would have spoken more justly than you,
if he had wished to speak, did not speak, but looked to one thing only, the
salvation of the neighbor. And see the excess; for he did not say, "Once, or
twice, or for so much and so much time," but "Forever," he says, "I will not
eat," if the other should be scandalized. And lest you think that he stood
only at these things, he also added something else more than this:


for after saying "It is good not to eat flesh, nor to drink wine," he joined
that also: "nor to do anything in which your brother stumbles, or is
scandalized, or is weak." And again consider with me the wisdom of the
teacher; for leaving the one who is weak, he corrects the strong before that
one; for this man is the cause of that one's being weak, being the master able
to correct the sickness, and not doing it. And why do I speak about the weak
brethren? For even to Jews and to Greeks he commands that this care be given.
"Give no offense," he says, "either to Jews or to Greeks or to the church of
Christ." So that by as much as you say you are stronger, and are harmed in
nothing from this sharing of a tent, by so much more you make yourself liable
for tearing apart the bond. For by as much as you are stronger, by so much
more justly would you bear up the weaker. If then you are weak, stand away for
yourself; but if stronger, for the one who is weak. For the one who has
strength must be strong not for himself only, but also for others. And if,
saying that you are strong, you overlook that man's weakness, you will pay a
double penalty, both because you did not spare, and because you had much power
to spare. For each of us is liable for the salvation of the neighbor. For this
reason we were commanded not to look to our own things, but to those of the
neighbor. For we were bought with a price; and the one who bought us commanded
this for the common profit of us all. For not only this gain comes about, that
we save our own members, but that we also set ourselves in greater safety. For
even if you philosophize ten thousand times, you are convicted by the facts
themselves, receiving no ordinary harm from this cohabitation. For when I see
you hard to tear away, and despising thousands who are harmed, and not even
turning when many accuse, but even trampling your own glory, and rubbing much
accusation upon the common body of the Church, and opening the mouths of the
unbelievers, and casting all under an evil opinion, and so many evils coming
from the dwelling together, and no good coming to you, but from the
separation, the taking away of all these evils, and the gaining of many other
good things, and then you do not endure to leap away, how shall I be able to
persuade those who accuse, that you have been delivered from all sympathy, and
are pure of wicked desire? Rather I do not even contend about these things;
but let it be that you are even pure while dwelling together; and yet the
blessed Job did not dare to bear witness to himself of so great a power and
philosophy, but the man who went through every virtue, and was carried above
all the nets of the devil, and first and alone showed so great an endurance,
and surpassed all iron and adamant by the self-control of his soul, and cut
down the strength of the devil, so feared such a wrestling, and thought it
impossible for one dwelling with a virgin to remain unhurt and pure, that he
not only set himself far and long away from this cohabitation, but even from
the sight that comes simply and by chance, and he made a law for his own eyes,
not even simply to look upon a virgin. For he knew, he knew clearly, that not
only the one who dwells with her, but even the one who looks curiously into a
virginal face, it is hard, and perhaps even impossible, to escape the harm
from there; therefore he also said, "And I will not understand concerning a
virgin."

5 And if Job is small for you as a rival, and yet we are not even worthy of
that man's dunghill, still, if you think the example to be less than your
greatness of soul, consider Paul, the most loud-voiced herald of the truth,
who, having gone around the whole world, and having been able to speak those
words full of much philosophy, that it was no longer he who lived, but Christ
in him, and that he had been crucified to the world, and the world to him, and
that he dies daily, after so great a grace of the Spirit and so great a
display of contests, after the unspeakable dangers, after the exact
philosophy, showing us and


hinting that as long as we breathe and are clothed with this flesh, we have
need of wrestlings and toils, and it is never possible to accomplish
temperance without trouble, but we need many sweats and labors toward this
trophy, so he said: "I buffet my body, and bring it into subjection, lest
after preaching to others I myself should become a castaway." And he said
these things making clear the uprising of the flesh and the madness from
desire, and the continual war, and his life in the contest. For this reason
Christ also, making clear the difficulty of the thing, did not even allow
simply looking into the faces of women, but even threatened to punish those
who look in this way with the punishment of adulterers. And when Peter said
that "It is not expedient to marry," he did not make a law not to marry, but
hinting at the bulk of the thing he said, "He that is able to receive it, let
him receive it." And we hear also in our own generation that many, having
bound their whole body with iron, and having wrapped themselves in sackcloth,
and having run up to the peaks of the mountains, and living with continual
fasting and all-night watches and sleeplessness, and showing every hardship,
and having forbidden all women to set foot in their room and their hut, and
training themselves in this way, scarcely get the better of the madness
according to desire. But you say that, even if you see a man dwelling with a
virgin and bound to her, and living in luxury, and giving up his soul rather
than the woman who dwells with him, and choosing both to suffer and to do all
things rather than be parted from the one longed for, you should not believe
anything wicked, nor think the thing to be of desire, but of piety. But it is
not about those who have bodies, O admirable man, that one must be so
disposed, but about those who dwell with stones. And yet many, and you perhaps
disbelieve because of your great temperance; but I have heard certain men
relating that many have even suffered something toward statues and stones. And
if where there is hardness and resistance the shaping alone had so much power,
when together with the shaping a soft body also lies underneath, what madness
will it not work? And how will those who accuse not seem to speak more
reasonably than we who make defense? For what is reasonable, tell me, that a
man should desire a woman, or that he should not desire? We would altogether
say, to desire. Again, the man who, though ten thousand reasonable pretexts
push him out, does not endure to go out, but pushes himself in upon no
achievement, but upon countless reproaches and harms of his own people and of
strangers, what is reasonable? that he suffers this from a good choice, or
from a wicked one? From a wicked one, perhaps someone would rather say. Still,
let us not be exact about these very things, but let it be laid down that
those who are scandalized are not even scandalized according to reason, nor
justly, but at random and in vain; why do you dwell, tell me, with the virgin?
For this cohabitation has been ordained for no reason, except for love and
longing; since take this away, and the need of the thing is destroyed. For who
would choose, being a man, apart from this necessity, to put up with feminine
luxury and insolence and the other shortcomings of that sex? For this reason
God also from the beginning armed the woman with this strength, knowing that
she would be exceedingly contemptible if she did not bring this rule with her,
and that no one would choose to dwell with her while remaining pure of desire.
For if even now, when so great a necessity lies upon them and much other need,
for they also bear children, and keep the house, and serve in other things
still more than these; if then even now, performing so many services for the
men, they have often become contemptible, and have been thrown out of the
house, how, apart from desire, would they have been longed for by you, and
that when they wrap you in so many reproaches? Either then tell the cause of
the cohabitation, or it is necessary to suspect no other, but a wicked desire
and a disgraceful pleasure.

6 What then, if we are able, he says, to speak a reasonable and 6 just cause,
will you have said these things about us in vain? Most of all you will name no
such cause; still


even so I wished to learn whether you have even a shadow of a reasonable
pretext to speak. The virgin, he says, is without a protector, having neither
a husband nor a kinsman by marriage, and often not even a father, not a
brother, and she needs one who will stretch out a hand, and who will comfort
the desolation, and who will be set forward everywhere and will establish her
in much safety and in a harbor. In what safety, tell me? in what harbor? For I
see the breakwater not keeping off the waves, but raising them, and the harbor
not checking the storm, but even giving birth to the great waves that were not
there. Then are you not ashamed, do you not hide your faces, making such a
defense? For if from this service no evil-speaking were born, nor harm, nor
scandal, but it were possible to do this very thing with good report, than
whom would you not be more pitiable, increasing her wealth, and training her
toward love of money, and casting her into affairs, and exercising her toward
worldly cares, exchanging the rank of stewards and guardians and of certain
men of the marketplace? And very much so. For you will not be able to speak
about having no possessions, and to persuade them to despise what they have,
you who do all things so that what they have may remain and increase, and so
that revenues may be added to revenues, becoming certain hucksters and
retailers because of them. Good, are they not, and noble, your hopes, you who,
commanded to take up the cross and follow Christ, having thrown down the
cross, like soft soldiers their shield, sit beside the distaff and the little
basket, having opened the door into the present life by another more shameful
entrance. For it is not so shameful for those who have married to manage these
things, as for you who pretend to have leaped away from the present things,
and through another person are slipping into them again. For this reason we
everywhere get the reputation of gluttons and parasites and flatterers and
slaves of women, because, having thrown to the ground all the nobility given
us from above, we exchange it for the slavishness and cheapness that come from
the earth. And those noble men then did not choose to distribute money to
widows, and that when so much blasphemy was arising because there was no one
to manage, but thinking this to be less than their own philosophy, they
entrusted it to others; but we are not ashamed, increasing another's wealth to
the ruin of those who possess it, being no better disposed than the eunuchs
who have spent their time on such things, we who are commanded each day to
carry the blood and the souls upon our hands instead of great weapons. What
then? Must we look on while all the virgin's things are seized and led and
carried about by kinsmen, by household slaves, and by strangers or even by her
own people? A fine return, is it not, we shall give the virgin, in return for
her not marrying, nor loving the present world, but choosing Christ instead of
all, if we leave her exposed to those who wish to rob her of what she has. And
how much better it was to come together in marriage, and to use the one who
dwells with her for the management of these affairs .... or, remaining
unmarried, to trample the covenants toward God, and to insult a thing so
reverend and fearful, and to drag others also down to the shipwrecks of her
own evils? And how do you say that she has chosen Christ instead of all, when
Christ cries, "You cannot serve God and mammon"? And how has she hated the
world and the present things, when you persuade her to be fluttered toward the
desires of the world? And to whom of those who have a husband will you ever be
able to advise to overlook money, when you are helping the virgin to build up
wealth? And when will you let this very woman be devoted to the Lord and
undistracted, spending all your life and eagerness upon her affairs? And when
will the virgin be able to philosophize, seeing you, the man, thinking it a
terrible thing if her money should be torn apart? And how will she know how to
despise loss, seeing you both doing and suffering all things so as to stretch
out for her the present wealth? God does not wish us to be freed from affairs


in this way, but in the despising of what we have, in being hung away from all
the things of this life. But you do not allow it, nor do you grant the law of
God to have force.

7 What then, if she needs the protection of others, he says, and suffers many
things unworthy of her? For are these things now not unworthy of the virgin?
Nothing is so unworthy of the virgin as to be rich and to be wrapped in a
multitude of affairs. And what then? If besides these she should also command
other things, such as to lend gold, and then, having called us into the
contracts, should not persuade us, and not having persuaded us should take
others, shall we be the cause? And what then? If she should set up certain
other illiberal and unseemly trades, and then, when we are unwilling to
cooperate, should need certain others, would we be worthy to be accused? By no
means, but even to be praised; for the contrary would have been a matter of
blame and accusation, to contribute to them toward such things and to
cooperate. Do you wish that her money not be carried about nor led away?
Advise her to lay it down where she will not even need a man for its guarding,
and it remains unconquered continually; but if she should wish to have
affairs, why play in things that are not for play? For for one who is a virgin
to do such things is to play a game that has not delight, but death. For when
she has made herself liable to such wrestlings, and does all things unworthy
of such a promise, the punishment is greater, the vengeance more grievous. Did
you not hear what law Paul set for her, or rather Christ speaking through him,
that the woman and the virgin are divided, and "The unmarried woman cares for
the things of the Lord, that she may be holy both in body and in spirit"? But
you do not allow it, obeying all their desires more readily than bought
slaves. Yes, he says; what then, if she wrestles with extreme poverty? For
concerning the well-off these things have been well said by you; but those who
lie in poverty and much neglect, what charge is it to set them upright? Would
that you were not throwing them down nor pushing them toward the very pits of
destruction, and it would have been enough; for if you do these things as one
obeying the one who commanded to stand before the poor, you have countless
brethren; display this good work there, where nothing lurks that can
scandalize, since here at least the almsgiving is worse than all cruelty and
inhumanity. For what is the profit, when you feed the body, but sink the soul?
when you give a garment, but make the reputation more shameful than that of
the naked? when, having become useful in the bodily things, you ruin all the
spiritual? when, having made the things on earth easy for her, you cast her
out of the heavenly? And what almsgiving is it, when the glory of God is
insulted? when there is reproach and shame and jests and insults both of the
woman herself who is pitied and of many others who are scandalized through
her? These things come not from pity but from an inhuman and harsh soul. For
if they were done from pity and love of mankind, they ought to have been shown
toward the men. But the women need more protection, he says, and the men have
many resources from nature. And yet among men also there are many more weakly
disposed than women, both through long old age, and through weakness, and
through maiming of the body, and through grievous diseases, and through
certain other such pretexts; still, since you care most of all for the race of
women, as being weaker, and are exceedingly merciful and sympathetic toward
them, not even here will we be at a loss for this pretext, but we will show
you subjects which are both delivered from all accusation, and will furnish
you a greater reward. For there are women, some relaxed by old age, some with
their hands cut off, some with their eyes maimed, some suffering other varied
and manifold passions and a poverty more grievous than the passions. For both
the poverty and the having nothing at all wears down the diseases of their
body; and the poverty itself again becomes more grievous and more unendurable
from these. Go out to the hunt of these, gather these; or rather you do not
even need labor for the gathering; so


they lie ready for all who wish to stretch out a hand. And if you have money,
spend it on these; and if you are strong, bring in here the service from the
body; you will see many affairs here too, needing both bodily service and
expenditure of money and running about. For it is a work to provide dwellings
for these, and to prepare medicines, and to buy a bed and garments, and
suitable foods, and to take care of all the rest, even if those who are sick
with such things are only ten; but now our city is filled with these, and you
will find a thousand and twice as many. These are the ones who need
protection, these the desolate, these who lie on the ground; this is
almsgiving, this is love of mankind, this passes over to the glory of God, and
prepares the benefit both of those who see and of those who suffer and of
those who do. For the weaker among the strong would more justly be helped, and
the aged among the young, and those who do not even abound in necessary food
among those who possess even moderate things, and those who are abominable to
many because of the unbearableness of the passions among those who are desired
among many, and those who can both wipe away the insult that has come about
and add good report among those who rub insult upon us. Show then that you do
this because of God, and take hold of these. But if you are not willing even
to see these in a dream, but go about hunting good-looking and young women,
and for this illiberal hunt having another cause that is not endurable, but
putting forward another that seems to be good-looking, that of protection,
even if you cheat men, you will not deceive the unbribed court, doing these
things for one pretext, but putting forward another.

8 For you say that you do all things because of God, but you do the things
that belong to the enemies of God; for to make his name blasphemed and
slandered belongs to the enemies of God. And I make yet another excess. For
let us grant that the one who says these things speaks truly, and is pure of
all desire, and takes on this protection from nothing else, but from piety
alone; not even so shall we find him delivered from punishment. For if he were
at a loss for other pretexts, in which he had both to display the piety and to
do this apart from souls being scandalized; most of all even so these things
ought not to be transacted where the loss is greater than the gain. For tell
me, where is it equal, that one who stands before one virgin or two for the
sake of bodily affairs should scandalize infinite multitudes in the soul?
Still, the accusation is not so grievous in that way; but when you find ten
thousand roads both delivered from accusation, and free of scandals, and
having greater abundance, why do you weave affairs for yourself at random and
in vain, and undermine the gain, and build unsafely and disgracefully, when it
is possible with safety and much glory? Do you not know that the life of the
Christian must shine brightly everywhere, and that the one who has shamed his
own glory will thereafter be useless everywhere, and will be able to gain
nothing great, even if he happens to accomplish great things? "For if the salt
has lost its savor," he says, "with what shall it be strong thereafter?" For
God wishes us to be salt and light and leaven, so as to be able for others
also to partake of the benefit from us. For if men living blamelessly could
scarcely turn those who have been neglected, if we should also give them a
handle, how shall we not be liable from every side for their destruction? For
just as it is not possible for one who has a corrupted life ever to be saved,
so neither is it possible for one who has put an evil opinion around himself
to escape the punishment. But if one must say something wonderful, even if
someone should sin great things, but do this in secret and scandalize no one,
he will pay a lesser penalty than the one who has sinned lesser things, but
with openness and with scandalizing many. And lest you wonder at what has been
said, and condemn the word of excess, we will bring you this vote from heaven,
and we will read this law from there. For the blessed Moses,


the meekest of all men upon the earth, and a friend to God, and greater than
prophets, for to the others he spoke through riddles, but to this man as a
friend toward a friend; this man then, such as he was, who suffered countless
hardships in the desert for so many years, who often ran risks concerning the
last things, both among Egyptians on behalf of Jews, and among Jews again on
behalf of those ungrateful men themselves, nothing else after those many
hardships and the countless achievements hindered him from obtaining the
promise, except scandalizing those with him at the water. And God, hinting at
this, said, "Because you did not believe me, to sanctify me in the eyes of the
children of Israel, therefore you shall not bring this congregation into the
land which I have given them." And yet even before this he disobeyed in
something, for he spoke against God both once and twice when he was being sent
into Egypt, and after these things in the desert he disbelieved, saying, "Six
hundred thousand on foot are there, and you have said, I will give them flesh,
and they shall eat a month of days. Shall sheep and oxen be slain, or shall
all the fish of the sea be gathered together, and shall it suffice them?" and
after these things again he lost heart, and declined the protection of the
people; yet none of these things had power to deprive him of the prizes set
before him, but only what happened at the water; for by the nature of those
things it was less, but by its coming about with the harm of others it became
much greater. For those things happened privately and in secret, but this was
being sinned openly and before the whole people. For this reason God also,
accusing, hinted at this, saying, "Because you did not sanctify me in the eyes
of the children of Israel," uncovering the nature of their sin, and hinting
whence it became unpardonable. And if this made so great a man offend, how
will this not sink and destroy us who are worms and nothing? For nothing so
provokes God as when his name is blasphemed. And to the Jews also he continued
accusing this up and down, that "My name is profaned"; and again, "You profane
it"; and "Through you my name is blasphemed among the nations"; and so great a
care came to him that this should not happen, that he even often saved the
unworthy, so that this might not come about. "For I did it," he says, "that my
name might not be profaned"; and "Not for your sake do I do this, house of
Israel, but that my name may not be profaned." And Paul also prayed to be
anathema for the glory of God; and Moses himself begged to be blotted out of
the book for the glory of the name of God; but you not only choose to suffer
nothing, so as to thrust away this blasphemy, but you even do all things
through which you both increase it and stretch it each day. Who then will
excuse us, and who will pardon? There is no one. And God makes so great a
care, and the saints also, that his name not be blasphemed, not as though God
needed the glory from us, for he is without need and perfect, but as though
much harm comes to men from the thing. For when the name of God and the glory
are slandered toward them, nothing benefits them thereafter; and if God when
slandered benefits nothing, much more we.

9 Let us then do all things, so that no pretext of scandal may come about; but
even if they accuse unjustly, let us persuade, and dissolve the charges, and
let us imitate the saints, who made so much eagerness both for the glory of
God and for their own, because of God. Let us not then, having thrown all
things down simply and trampled them, think it enough for our defense if we
say that we bought a garment for a virgin and shoes, and arranged well the
other things according to bodily rest. And who will manage, he says, the
things according to our house? and who will oversee what we have, and who will
stand before it, both when we are spending time outside and when there is no
wife inside? For they say these things also, setting them against the former
and more shameful, and none


of these things concerns them, nor are they ashamed, like those who are drunk,
of uttering whatever comes upon them. Therefore let us not grow weary either,
even if the things said are such as not even to be thought worthy of some
answer; let us not grow weary both answering, and speaking with them gently,
until we deliver them from this drunkenness, as far as it lies with us. For I
am ashamed and I blush attempting to overturn these things, which they are not
ashamed to speak against; but the shame must nevertheless be borne because of
the profit of those who are not ashamed. For it is absurd that we, accusing
them of contempt for the brethren who are scandalized, should ourselves
through shame despise their healing. For what indoor things, tell me, do they
say, of which they think the need for the protection of the virgin to be
necessary? Have you crowds of barbarian and newly bought girls, and must these
be trained both toward wool-work and toward the other service? or a store of
much money and costly garments, and must a guard sit inside continually, and
must the eyes of the virgin be walled against the wickedness of the household
slaves? or do you continually celebrate dinners and drinking parties, and must
the house be adorned, and cooks and table-setters enjoy the providence of the
virgin? or are there many-formed and continual expenses, and must someone
stand over them continually, so that some things may be guarded with
exactness, and others may not flow out of the house in vain? None of these, he
says; but so that she may look over the chest itself and the garment and the
rest of the poverty, so that she may prepare the table, and spread the bed,
and kindle a fire, and wash feet all around, and furnish all the other rest.
Then in return for this small and cold rest shall we bear so much
evil-speaking, shall we undergo so many reproaches? And how much better and
more easily will a brother serve these things? For by nature the man is
stronger than a woman, and by need more proper to us, and not so expensive.
For the woman, being softer, needs both a softer mattress, and a finer
garment, and perhaps again a girl to serve her, and she does not furnish us so
much service as she needs from us; but the brother has been delivered from all
these; and if he should need something, he will need the same things as we.
And this is no small thing toward ease, that those who dwell together should
not stand in need of different things, but of the same; which in the case of
the virgin is not present. For first, whether she needs to bathe, or the body
is ill, neither will the brother serve her toward these things, even if he
should greatly choose to act unseemly, nor will she be able to suffice for
herself; but if those who dwell together are brothers, they will give this
service to one another in return. Again when there is need to sleep, with a
virgin sitting inside, there must also be two couches, and double bedding and
coverlets, and if they are of sound mind, dwellings too; but when they are
brothers, again the things of need are contracted, since it is more proper.
For one house, and one pillow, and one couch, and the same coverlets will
suffice for both; and in general if someone should go through all the service,
he will find much ease here, but difficulty there. And I pass by the
unseemliness of the house; for what sort of thing it is, having gone up into
the house of a man who lives alone, to see women's shoes hanging, and girdles,
and headbands, and little baskets, and a distaff, and shuttles, and combs, and
a loom-foot, and the other things which it is not possible to speak all in
part. And if you examine these things also in the case of the well-off woman,
the laughter is more. For first he turns alone in the middle of a herd of so
many maidservants, like a dancer on the orchestra in the chorus of women, the
one who sings along for those who dance; than which what could be more
shameful and more dishonorable? Then he is torn the whole day, being angry at
household slaves over the affairs that belong to the woman. For either he must
be silent, and, neglecting all things, be rebuked by her, or speaking and
rebuking he must act unseemly; and see what happens. For the one commanded not
even to come near worldly affairs, not simply worldly, but even of the women's
things


he comes further inside; for they will not decline to give out women's
vessels, but they will stand over the silversmiths continually asking whether
the mistress's mirror has been made, whether they have finished the pail,
whether they have given back the little oil-flask. For all things have come to
this corruption, that most of the virgins use these vessels more than the more
worldly women.

10 From there again he has run to the perfume-seller to speak about the
mistress's spices; and often for the sake of more eagerness he will not
decline even to insult the poor man, for virgins also use varied and costly
ointments; then from the perfume-seller to the one who sells linens, and from
that man again to the tentmaker. For they are not even ashamed to command
these small things, since they see them obeying exceedingly and being grateful
to them when they command, rather than when they serve others. From here
again, when something of that portable tent needs to be repaired, they even
continue until evening itself without food, nailed to the workshops. And this
is not the wonder, but that they are also burdensome to the wretched household
slaves, and there is much insolence against them and anger and shouting. And
consider how many the evil-speakings from these things; for a household slave
who has been insulted, and over such affairs, since he has no way to defend
himself against the one who has insulted him, does this through the tongue and
through secret accusation, sparing none of the things able to fill his wrath,
but using this defense with so much excess as it is likely a household slave
would, both having been so insulted, and having this only as a comfort of his
own evils against the one who has pained him. But the one who lives with the
poor woman will not speak with silversmiths, for poverty, he says, does not
allow it, nor will he sit beside perfume-sellers, but he will often trouble
shoemakers and weavers and embroiderers and dyers. And why must one catalogue
all the unseemliness, such as when they go into rooms selling warp and woof,
when they go about the marketplace seeking these same things again? And these
things in the houses and in the marketplaces, and they undergo things much
more ridiculous than these. But at the church it is not even possible to speak
the shame. For as though it were necessary that no place should be ignorant of
their insolence and this illiberal slavery, so even in this holy and most
fearful place they proclaim to all their incontinence, and what is more
grievous, that they even adorn themselves over the things of which they ought
to be ashamed. For receiving them from the doors outside, and becoming instead
of eunuchs they bustle, and going before they think greatly, while all are
watching, and they do not sink, but even exult; and even in the most fearful
time of the mysteries themselves they serve much toward those women's
pleasure, furnishing handles to many of those who see. And the wretched and
miserable women, when they ought to keep them off from such favor, even pride
themselves and think greatly. And yet who, whether for these or for those, if
he were going to utter some curse, would have found one more grievous than
this, or that they should have countless witnesses of their wantonness, and
act unseemly in the eyes of all? Why must one say how many things are
overturned in the churches themselves because of the favor of these, how many
of the commands of God are neglected by many, so that these women may not be
provoked? and why do I say, not be provoked? if only someone should look at
them with a gloomy and unpleasant look, they choose to endure all things
rather than to look on while that woman suffers this. But until when do we
also act unseemly, relating all their things? For we did not choose this,
since we would have needed many and long words going through all; or rather
all things it was not possible to say even if we wished, but putting together
a few from many, even so it would have been enough to work the length; but we
have not come to this, but we have remembered even these unwillingly, so as to
sting a little those of the hearers who have sense; and for the rest we must
exhort and entreat. I beg then and I beseech, and


I roll at your knees, and I set a supplication before all, be persuaded, and
let us recover from this drunkenness, and let us become our own, and let us
recognize the honor which God has given us, and let us hear Paul crying, "Be
not servants of men"; and let us cease serving women to the common ruin of us
all. Christ wishes us to be noble soldiers and athletes. He did not arm us
with the spiritual weapons for this, that we should take on the service of
three-obol girls, that we should turn about wool and looms and such services,
that we should sit beside women spinning and weaving, that we should continue
every day imprinting women's manners and words upon our own soul; but that we
should strike the invisible powers that oppose us, that we should wound the
devil who commands them, that we should drive out the wild ranks of the
demons, that we should tear down their strongholds, that we should bind the
authorities of the world-ruler of darkness and lead them captive, that we
should put to flight the spiritual things of wickedness, that we should
breathe fire, that we should be ready and prepared toward daily deaths. For
this he clothed us with the breastplate of righteousness, for this he girded
the belt of truth, for this he set around the helmet of salvation, for this he
shod us with the preparation of the gospel of peace, and put the sword of the
spirit into our hands, for this he sent fire into our souls.

11 If then, tell me, you saw one of the soldiers, after putting on the helmet,
the greaves, the breastplate, after taking the sword, the shield, the spear,
the bows, the arrows, the quiver, when the trumpet was sounding brightly and
calling all outside, and when the enemies were breathing violently and
prepared to tear the city up from the foundations, not running out to the
battle-line, but going into a house and sitting beside a woman with those
weapons: would you not, if it were allowed, drive the sword through the
middle, not even giving a share of speech? And if you would have been filled
with so much anger, how do you think God will be disposed toward things much
more absurd than these? For these things are more shameful than those and more
absurd by as much as the war is more grievous, and the enemies more violent,
and the prizes about which the war is greater, and all things simply have so
much difference as the middle between truth and a shadow. Let us not then
soften our strength, nor cut out the sinews with these associations; for
unspeakable and much vice flows into our souls from here. For what if we do
not even perceive it, being drunk with the friendship? For this itself is also
the most terrible of all, that we do not even know how we are unnerved and
become softer than all wax. For just as someone taking a proud lion looking
grim, and then shearing the mane, and taking out the teeth and stripping off
the claws, makes shameful and ridiculous and easy for children to overcome the
fearful and unendurable one who shakes all things from the roar alone; so also
these women make all whom they take easy for the devil to handle, softer,
hotter, shameless, foolish, quick to anger, bold, untimely, ignoble, harsh,
slavish, illiberal, rash, talkative, and in a word, carrying all the corrupted
women's manners they imprint them upon these men's souls. For it is impossible
that the one who dwells with women with so much sympathy, and is nourished in
their associations, should not be some vagabond and a man of the marketplace
and of the rabble. For even if he should utter something, he will utter all
things from the looms and the wool, his tongue having been dyed with the
quality of women's words; and if he should do something, he will work this
with much slavishness, having settled himself far from the freedom that befits
Christians and becoming useful toward none of the great achievements. For if
such a man is useless toward the worldly and civic affairs, much


more toward the greatness of the spiritual things, which need so much nobler
men that those who are going to pursue them cannot even touch them, unless
they become angels from men. And they not only receive so great a vice
themselves, but they also become causes of corrupted manners to those women.
For just as these men, wishing exceedingly to please them, go out of the
fitting way of life, so also these women fall from the shame that belongs to
them because of those men, furnishing them this wicked and destructive return.
For they both adorn themselves more curiously, and make much provision of a
broken figure and gait, and babble through the whole day things that do not
belong; for since they see them delighted with these wanton manners and words,
they practice all things through which they will be able to hold them captive.
But if we are willing, recovering a little, to become our own, we shall gain
both those women and ourselves and all the others; and just as now we have
been made liable for the destruction of the many, so then we shall receive the
reward of the salvation of all, and the things of which we now enjoy
shamefully, then we shall enjoy with much honor. For tell me, do you wish to
be honored by women? Most of all this is unworthy of a spiritual man, to love
such honor; still this too will be present then, when we do not seek it. For
man is by nature accustomed to despise those who court him, and to wonder at
those who do not flatter; and the female nature is accustomed to suffer this
passion still more. For she is unendurable when flattered, and she wonders
most of all at those who do not endure to yield and to lie down under her
untimely desires; and you will bear me witness of this. For now not only the
outsiders, but even the women who dwell with you themselves laugh at you, even
if not openly, yet in their conscience, and they pride themselves in this
grievous tyranny; but then they will altogether wonder at you, and will be
struck by your freedom. And if you disbelieve our words, ask those women
themselves whom they praise and accept more, those who are enslaved or those
who rule them, those who are subject and do and suffer all things for favor
toward them, or those who endure none of these things, but are even ashamed of
their wicked commands; and if they are willing to speak the truth, they will
altogether name those; or rather there is not even need of an answer, the
facts themselves crying this.

12 But for the sake of pleasure the one who dwells with them has the
advantage, feasting his eyes on the faces of the virgins. Most of all even if
this were so, for this very reason one ought to flee; but now it has been
sufficiently shown to us that such a thing is not pleasant, but the contrary,
not to enjoy this spectacle; and do you add for me also the cheer from the
conscience. For nothing is accustomed to cheer us so as a good conscience, and
good hopes. But do you seek this for the sake of rest? But this too has been
shown to be easier when a brother dwells with you. For now you have differed
in nothing from a slave, and seeking rest you have found a most grievous
slavery; but then you both stand outside this service, and you will be of
those who command, not of those who are commanded. When then there, instead of
pleasure, pain, and instead of glory, shame, and instead of freedom, slavery,
and toil instead of rest, and there is added also that God is blasphemed, and
so great a destruction, and scandals, and immortal punishment, and a falling
from countless good things, but here all the contrary, glory, honor, pleasure,
freedom of speech, liberty, salvation of souls, inheritance of the kingdom,
deliverance from punishment, why shall we not exchange these for those? I at
least do not know, unless someone should simply desire to wash himself away;
for there will no longer be a defense for us nor pardon. For if even when none
of these things was present, we ought to endure all things for the glory of
God, when it is possible both to reap the things here, and to obtain the good
things to come, and we, in addition to the glory of God being blasphemed,


also destroy ourselves besides, who will snatch us out and deliver us from the
vengeance laid down upon these things? There is no one. Gathering all these
things then, let us be reconciled to ourselves at last, at least sometime, for
the salvation of our souls. And if the tearing apart of a long habit seems to
have some difficulty, having entrusted the whole after the grace of God to the
power of reason, and having persuaded ourselves of that too, that if we only
set a beginning to the thing, we shall no longer see it hard, so let us dare
against the habit. For if you stand yourself away for ten days, you will bear
the twenty more easily, and twice as many again; then going forward by the
road you will no longer even take a perception of the difficulty at the
beginning, but you will see the thing most hard to overcome becoming most
easy, and you will set yourself again in another habit, and you will find the
change to be easy not through habit only, but also through the good hopes. So
those women also will wonder at you more, and God will accept you before them,
and all men will crown you, and you will live a life having much freedom, and
much pleasure. For what could be sweeter than to be delivered from an evil
conscience, and to dissolve the continual war of desire, and with much ease to
weave the good crown of temperance, and to look toward heaven with free eyes,
and with a pure voice and heart to call the Master of all? No one so, having
been delivered from a chain as a prisoner and from squalor and from the other
misery in the prison, or rather no one so maimed, having looked up again and
seen this sweet light, exults and is glad and leaps, as the one who has been
able to be freed from this slavery. For the deliverance from that tyranny is
much sweeter than this light, and the misery of that slavery and of the bonds
is more grievous than all gloom. But why must one stretch these things out,
pointing to each of the two lives, of the one the illiberality and the
dejection and the harm and the much wasting, of the other the freedom and the
pleasure and the benefit and the much temperance? For no word will be able to
set them forth, but only the trial of the facts. And then you will know well
from what evils you have been delivered, and what sort of life you have
obtained, when you are willing to be persuaded by us through the facts. Be
persuaded then, that through the works themselves you may learn the things
that have been said. And if you still take it hard and disbelieve our words,
ask someone of those who were once enslaved to this slavery, then were
delivered all at once, and ran up to the good freedom; and then you will know
the gain of this exhortation. Since Solomon also, when he was held by the
desire of the worldly things, both thought them to be great and wonderful, and
displayed much love of labor about them, building splendid houses, and
gathering infinite gold, and collecting from everywhere choruses of musicians
and all kinds of table-setters and cooks, and preparing for the desire of the
soul both the grace from the gardens and the pleasure from splendid bodies
abundantly, and cutting, so to speak, every road of amusement and delight for
himself; but when he recovered a little from there, and like one from some
gloomy abyss was able to look up toward the light of philosophy, then he sent
forth that high voice worthy of the heavens, saying, "Vanity of vanities, all
is vanity."

13 This vote you also, and one higher than this, if you wish, will bring
concerning this untimely pleasure, if you stand yourselves a little away from
the wicked habit. And yet Solomon, having come to be in the times above, was
not even required much exactness of philosophy; for neither did the old law
forbid living in luxury, nor did it say that to enjoy the other enjoyments was
superfluous and vain; yet even so, the facts being thus, he was able to see
the uselessness in them, and to condemn much of their


vanity. But we are called to a greater way of life, and we ascend to a higher
peak, and we have stripped for greater pits of contest. And what else but to
live according to those intellectual and bodiless powers above are we
commanded? How then is it not shameful and worthy of much punishment to appear
far less than that man, and not only not to become higher than the things
conceded, as he did, but even to touch the things forbidden and having an
unendurable vengeance? For to nourish a wicked love in the soul, and to look
toward a woman for desire, and to study another's beauty, and to shame
oneself, and to harm the weaker, and to furnish many handles to Greeks, and
many to Jews, and to trip up both one's own people and strangers, and to
scatter much blasphemy upon the glory of God, and to take on slavish services,
and to push ourselves into the tumult of worldly affairs, and to drink away to
the devil the freedom given us, and to exchange for it a most grievous
tyranny, and to be ridiculous to friends, and disgraceful to enemies, and to
rub an evil opinion upon the common body of the Church, and to shame the
reverend dignity of virginity, and to furnish many pretexts to those who wish
to be wanton, and to prepare other terrible things more than these, for it is
not possible either to see all or to set them forth in a word, as many as they
undergo through the facts themselves, is both among the things exceedingly
forbidden, and among those that have an unendurable punishment. So that even
if there is some small pleasure, setting all these against that, the laughter,
the shame, the suspicion of the many, the accusations, the jests, the
reproaches, the worm that does not end, the outer darkness, the unquenchable
fire, the affliction, the distress, the gnashing of teeth, the bonds that
cannot be loosed, and laying them down as in a balance and setting them

and for your glory we both despised habit, and mastered pleasure, and
afflicted our soul, and having cast out all friendship and prejudice, we have
preferred you and the longing toward you before all things. For so we shall
gain ourselves, and we shall gain those wretched women, and we shall gain
those who are scandalized, and we shall stand beside the martyrs themselves,
and we shall receive the first rank. For I do not set as less than those who
contended that greatest contest, and who bore the enduring pains, a man held
by an old desire, and preoccupied in some most pleasant and ancient habit,
then because of the fear of God cutting through the bonds, and running up
toward what seems good to God. For it is among the things most exceedingly
hard, both to cast out an ancient sympathy and affection, and to cut through
the many-folded handles, and to grow wings, and to run up toward the heavenly
vaults. For just as for those the toil is sharp, so also for these the pain is
longer. Therefore the crowns again are equal, since their wrestlings also lie
parallel. For if the one who has brought out a precious man from an unworthy
one shall be as the mouth of God, the one who has both freed himself and
delivered countless others from accusation, consider how great a reward he
will receive, and lightened by the hope of the prizes despise a wicked habit,
so that having run through the present life according to what seems good to
God you may both see her there with a pure conscience, and may enjoy her most
holy association. For when the bodily passions have thereafter been loosed,
and the tyrannical desire quenched, there is no hindrance to men and women
being there together, all wicked suspicion being out of the way, and all who
are brought into the kingdom of the heavens being able to preserve the way of
life of the angels and of those intellectual powers.
