* Judgment on Heliodorus and Achilles Tatius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

What is the judgment of the writings, of which Charicleia is the subject of
the one, and Leucippe of the other?
I know that many even among the highly educated dispute about these two
erotic writings, of which Charicleia is the subject of the one and Leucippe
of the other, maidens elegant in form and far superior in character. For
some say that the writing of Charicleia defeats the erotic construction of
Leucippe by a full vote, while others, diametrically opposed, hold that the
former is rather defeated by the latter. I, having consorted with both
books and having examined closely both the diction of each and their
thoughts, attach myself to none of those who have so distinguished them and
have pronounced sharply against the other; rather I have judged that each
of the writings both is defeated by the other and in turn defeats it; yet
that the work of Charicleia prevails in the greater part.
For the beauty of the discourse concerning her is neither altogether
cosmetic and theatrical, nor yet Attic and haughty, but distinguished by
magnificence. Nor is it deprived of the delightful and of what tends to
pleasure, but it is flowered round with delicate and fair names, and by
variations of figures and by the novelty of the phrasing it has been
compacted toward the more sublime. It has been fitted together most
gracefully, and it is animated by thoughts both paradoxical and concise,
and it has been arranged according to the arts of Isocrates and
Demosthenes. For from afar what comes running underneath is seen to be
managed, and what opposes is at once given back toward this. The one who
first reads, thinking that much is set down in superfluity, as the
discourse proceeds will marvel at the arrangement of the writer; and the
very beginning of the writing resembles coiled snakes: for these, having
hidden the head inside the coil, put forward the rest of the body, and the
book, having let the entrance of the plot slip into the middle, as if it
had drawn lots, has made the middle its beginning. The discourse luxuriates
in the flowers of every grace. It is sweet in diction and in the fair
speech of the rhetor, and beautiful in loftiness of utterance and in
seeming for the most part to be composed to the measure of grand speaking.
It has also been adorned with episodic narratives that breathe, as one
might say, an Aphrodisian grace. And in it the fluent part that honeys the
greedy ear is poetically constructed without vulgarity, while the compact
and, as it were, heroic part is poured out with fine diction. What I know
most people find fault with, I mean the matter of Charicleia, that she does
not speak in a womanly or female manner for the rhetor, but contrary to the
art her tongue is raised toward the more sophistic: this I myself do not
know how I might praise sufficiently. For she has not been introduced by
the writer as a private, unlearned girl, but as initiated and from the
Pythian, and therefore most of her laments give oracles; she is possessed
like the frenzied prophetesses and is wholly of the tripod's cauldron.
The writer also assigns the probable very well to the other characters. The
unseemly among the plots, which one could not cover over, this man,
narrating becomingly, has shown them better in the telling than worse in
the doing. So too he removes the old man Calasiris from the blame of
procuring, a thing among those not much believed until this writer, by the
variety of his art, thrust away what seemed blameworthy. And what is more
marvelous still, that in a writing so fluid and poured out he kept the
compact and, as it were, stubborn quality of chastity, and having once
drawn Charicleia's soul down into love he has kept her from this common
Aphrodite, not even in the things in which she was overcome shaking off the
lawful. I see that the writing also touches on much learning: for physical
subjects are introduced in it <and> coined maxims and theologies and some
things even of the moving sphere, and it is not far from the generations
that come from this; and the {both} Cronian nods make this clear, which
Calasiris was eager to flee through certain secret discourses. For I think,
to speak in the Greek manner, that not all the configurations have come
together toward this one thing. Nor is it far from the public speeches of
Demosthenes, with the Bacchic thyrsus and the inspiration from there. It
also takes thought for the one who encounters it, giving rest by changes
and novel expressions and episodes and varied turnings. And it utters
maxims as much as anything else I have encountered; and, not to speak at
length, <thus> blended with bloom and grace, possessing the pleasant
together with the beautiful, one could meet with no {the} other.
The book concerning Leucippe I think was polished as an imitation of that
one, but the painter {discourse} did not transfer all the things in the
archetypal painting toward his own character; rather he has been left
behind in the rest, but is sweeter than that one in phrasing. For not
having taken thought for magnificence he has become clearer through the
falling-off, and sweeter through the diction, which is altogether most
popular and most theatrical. Most ears do not love writings because they
chatter, but if the writings melt them, they greet the pleasures of the
words to satiety. In some passages he wishes to stand upright, but he
resembles those who are diseased in the joints of the feet: for he quickly
forgets the upright law and clings to his customary habits. For these
reasons he also seems for the most part to speak as a private man and to
throw far from Attic correctness of speech. Nor is the book youthful toward
its plots, nor does it take risks in their introductions, but it uses the
facts according to nature and keeps chronological order and accepts
clearness, putting <the> tongue forward of the mind and the customary
rather than novelty. When he rises into public speeches he is much
convicted against the art, neglecting force and method, but taking thought
there for beauty and composition and not forgetting his customary habit. He
makes the actions paradoxical and novel together: for he cuts open the
charming girl and buries her and again brings her up from the recesses of
Hades, and such things he accomplishes in places. But these things have
been copied out from the archetypal tablet; yet he is indiscriminate in
erotic converse and has a tongue that accuses his character. Expanding what
according to art one ought to have said all at once, and as it were putting
under the senses things at which one who saw them by chance would shut his
eyes, he is ignorant of the precepts of the art and, because of the
pleasure of fine speaking, he endures defiling the mind. He also tears
apart the character of the erotic pairing and quickly changes the lover and
brings him back again, having this only well in mind, that time knows how
to wither even the peaks of loves.
And to sum the whole up, the book of Charicleia I have admired both for the
thoughts and for the corresponding diction, and I have judged it worthy of
praise throughout. But the one concerning Leucippe I think is enough for
the rhetor in place of some other episode, so that if he should wish to
flower round some parts of his own discourses with the graces from there,
he may take from near at hand whatever has been brought into it toward
cosmetic beauty. You have, O friend, the judgment of the books in headings
that can do much. For we have not touched the particulars, but we have made
for you summary epitomes of the styles.
