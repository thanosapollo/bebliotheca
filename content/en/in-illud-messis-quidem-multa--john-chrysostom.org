* On that saying, The harvest indeed is plentiful

HOMILY X.
=63.515=
A homily of the same, spoken in the church of the Apostle, a bishop having
given a short preface, on the text, The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers
are few.
αʹ. Did you see the old man and the young, old in body, but young in mind? Did
you see gray hair flourishing =63.516= and the vigor of old age in full
strength? For such are the things among us, contrary to those outside. For in
the affairs of those outside old age is useless; for example, the soldier,
having come to this age, cannot stretch a bow, cannot send forth an arrow, nor
shake a spear, nor mount a horse, =63.517= nor assault walls, nor can he
accomplish any other such thing; and the steersman too, when old age has
slackened for him the tension of the flesh, will not be able to stretch ropes,
nor spread sails, nor handle an oar, nor shift the tillers, nor fight with
waves, nor any other of such things.
Again the husbandman, being at this age, will not have strength to yoke oxen,
nor to draw a plow, nor to break up and cut furrows, nor to tend a tree, nor
to set himself in the company of reapers, nor to do any other of such things;
but each of these sits at home, having the exemption that comes from age, and
bound by a great and inexorable necessity, old age. But the teacher of the
Church is not so; but then most of all he will contend, and take hold of
words, and set forth teaching, and try to train the people. For old age in the
other things is useless, but in the Church and in the labors of virtue it is
most useful. Since even the blessed Abraham then carried off the greatest
prize, when he was of this age; for then, then he overthrew the tyranny of
nature, and slew the child; for he slew him, if not in the attempt, yet in the
will; if not in the completion, yet in the purpose; if not in the deed, yet in
the mind; then he became priest of his own bowels, and having almost migrated
from nature itself, and having shifted his mooring toward heaven, he
accomplished that wondrous and strange work. And Paul, the teacher of the
world, in deepest gray hair stripped for those wondrous contests, and bonds
and chains and prisons he bore in an aged body with much vigor; and making
this clear he himself said: As Paul the aged, and now also a prisoner in
Christ Jesus.
And I say these things not dishonoring youth; since even the children who
trampled the furnace were very young and mere lads, and Jeremiah who received
the prophecy; and Daniel too, when he was bridling the lions, and through his
ineffable boldness was bringing those secret things of the king's dreams into
the midst and making them plain to the one who had seen them, was very young;
and youth became no hindrance to the virtue of the man. For in the things
outside, as I said before, both old age past its season and untimely youth
would be the greatest hindrances; but in the wrestlings of virtue no longer,
but there is need of one thing, of purpose only and of a soul made ready, and
neither is youth useless, nor old age unfit, but in either age one may see the
ripe fruit abounding, only, as I said before, if one should have a soul that
is sober and that knows how to philosophize, and that draws to itself the
goodwill of God through much virtue. Let no one then put forward either youth
or old age as an apology for sloth; since even now many young men among us
fill this spiritual


theater, while those who have grown old behave shamefully in the
horse-races; and others again, elders with gray hair [and] are adorned by the
hearing, while young men make their youth more irrational through the
spectacle there. For even if those present with us are many, still I have no
satiety; for I did not wish many only to be present, but all, and that no one
should be left behind from the church; so that, until even one appears
straying from the flock, I am torn and cut apart with pains.
Since =63.518= even that shepherd, leaving the ninety-nine sheep, pressed on
after the one that had wandered, and did not stand off before he had brought
it back to the flock, and he made much haste for it; for the number of the
hundred was lame, until it received the one, and the hundred then became a
hundred, when this too was saved. Do not then tell me that the brother who is
lost is one; but consider that he is a brother, the creature much sought after
by God, for whom so many things came to be, for whom the precious blood was
poured out and so great a price was paid, for whom heaven was stretched out,
and the sun was kindled, and the moon runs, and a varied chorus of stars
shines, and air was spread out, and the sea was poured forth, and earth was
founded, and springs gush, and rivers flow, and mountains have been fixed; for
whom meadows and paradises, for whom seeds and plants, for whom the varied
kinds of plants, for whom the things in mountains, the things in deserts, the
things in cities, the things in plains, the things in glens, the things in
hills; for whom the countless tribes of fishes, for whom the varied kinds of
four-footed things, for whom the manifold races of birds, for whom law, for
whom prophets, for whom apostles, for whom the countless wonders came to be;
what need to say the many things? for whom the only-begotten Son of God became
man, and having become man was slain.
βʹ. Consider how great a business God has undertaken for the salvation of men,
and do not despise any man, but when this spiritual theater has been
dissolved, seek out the one left behind, and do not stand off before you have
brought him back, and joined him to the mother, and led him away from the
wicked custom, and saved the captive and drawn up the one sunk, and freed the
one taken by beasts from the harsh beasts.
For what pardon would you have, what apology? We come to the tombs of
apostles; we see their wounds and their brands, the blood that flows more
precious than gold, the chains, the scourges, the daily deaths, which they
suffered for the Church; the disciple of Paul running about everywhere with
Paul, and being made equal to the teacher, and the calf matched with the bull;
the brother of the first of the apostles, the fisherman who spread the net and
fished men instead of fishes, the herald of the Gospel; and as in a meadow we
revel in their achievements. But those who have left us sit becoming
spectators of irrational horses, washing one another with revilings and
insults, kindling anger and a fight that has no pretext, rejoicing with a joy
more wretched than grief, over charioteers who win, over horses being broken;
than which what could be more irrational? For why do you rejoice, tell me? and
why do you skip and leap and come down from there with a bright face? And you,
for what cause do you ache and hide your face and hang your head and pain your
own soul, because so-and-so prevailed, and the other was overcome? and what
are these things to you? what account would this grief, or this pleasure,
have? and of what punishment would not each of you be worthy, making no
account of his own soul being swept away each day by the passions, but
rejoicing or grieving over irrational animals and other irrational men? But
lest again, having spent the whole discourse on these things, we should
consume the time, having entrusted the correction of those to your love, let
us take hold of the things today


read, and into =63.519= the meadow we shall try to lead you of the divine
Scriptures; for the things from there are like a meadow. For
just as in the meadow the flowers are various, but all in succession most
fair, each of the things seen drawing the spectator's sight to itself; so also
one may see in the divine Scriptures. For the blessed David draws our mind to
himself, and the apostolic saying spoken about Timothy; and still also the
daring Isaiah philosophizing about human nature, and the Master of these,
Jesus, discoursing with the disciples and saying: The harvest is plentiful,
but the laborers are few.
Come then, if it seems good, bringing this saying into the midst, let us
unfold its thoughts; for even if it is somewhat short in measure, yet it shows
us an unfathomable sea of thoughts. Wherefore we shall walk toward it with
much eagerness. For when he saw, it says, the crowds distressed, he says to
the disciples: The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few.
What harvest, tell me, and for what cause did he so name this teaching? And
yet the harvest is the end of the matter. For when the seed, having been cast
into the flanks of the earth, has enjoyed moisture, then having become soaked
and swollen it takes hold of the neighboring earth, and having been unfolded
into many fibers it sends the roots down to the depth, it first peeks out from
the surface, then little by little, being nourished by sun and rain and air,
it grows by the nature of the earth, and becomes first a green herb and puts
forth tender leaves, then it brings forth an unripe ear, and when spring has
come, again little by little ripening into stalk and beards, it yields the
full fruit. Then at last the husbandman, having sharpened the sickle, chooses
reapers, and having gathered the fruit stores it at home. Wherefore the
harvest is the end of every toil and of the husbandry. How then, tell me, does
he call it a harvest, when the affairs are still in their beginnings? For
impiety was prevailing everywhere in the world, and altars were being kindled,
and idols were being worshipped, and Jesus was being dishonored, and a deep
night held all things, and there was a harsh winter, the sea being tossed, the
wild winds cutting through human life, the passions playing the tyrant, and
all things becoming submerged.
For everywhere fornication and adultery and wantonness; everywhere greed and
robberies and wars; and bloods on land, and bloods in the sea, the pirates
bringing murders even there, and continuous wars and battles and civil
slaughters and murders more accursed than all murders; for even children were
being slain to the idols; and nature was being ignored and kinship was being
overturned, and everywhere cliffs and reefs and submerged rocks and heavy
ledges, and no man a steersman; or rather, few steersmen in the nation of the
Jews, but no one listening, the sailors being at faction among themselves, and
cutting one another down and sinking one another. For Scythians and Thracians
and Moors and Indians and Persians and Sauromatae, and those who inhabit
Greece and those who inhabit Epirus, and all, so to speak, that is under the
sun was being initiated to the demons, and by those avenging spirits both
country and city and desert, and land and sea, and barbarian and Hellas, and
mountains and glens and hills were being driven to frenzy; and only the nation
of the Jews, which seemed to be pious, had prophets =63.520= and small seeds
of the knowledge of God; but even these were being buried by time, and the
teachers of that race were becoming bitter accusers of the things being sinned
by them; and they were so far from teaching the others and leading them by the
hand toward piety, that they were then even becoming causes of scandals to the
others; and making this clear the prophet was saying: Because of you my name
is blasphemed among the nations.


γʹ. How then, when so many evils were holding the world, and the seeds not yet
having been cast (for everywhere thorns and thistles and wicked herbs), the
land not yet having been cleared, nor a plow having been drawn, nor a furrow
having been cut, does he discourse about a harvest and say: The harvest is
plentiful? and for what cause does he so call the Gospel? For what cause?
since the affairs were so disposed, and he was about to send them out
everywhere in the world, and they were likely to be thrown into tumult and to
be troubled, and to reason with themselves and to say: And how would it be
possible, being eleven (for the one had become a prey of beasts), unlearned,
illiterate, poor, obscure, naked, wearing a single tunic, unshod, carrying
neither belt nor staff nor an obol, to go through the whole world so disposed,
and to tear them from the old custom, and to bring them into the doctrines of
the new, and to sound some strange teaching into them? for when shall we pull
up the thorns? when shall we sow the seeds? when shall we till their mind?
when shall the sowing come up? and who will not tear us apart? and who will
not cast us down a cliff? how at all shall we be able to open a mouth, to
stand, to discourse, to appear to so many myriads? how shall we dissolve the
wrath of tyrants, the uprisings of peoples, the constructions of philosophers,
the cleverness of rhetors, the tyranny of prejudice, the strength of old
custom, the plots of demons, souls already seized by countless diseases? we
then the eleven, how shall we set right all those in the world, the unlearned
the wise, the naked the armed, the subjects the rulers, those possessing one
tongue discoursing toward countless voices, and barbarian and foreign-tongued
nations? and who will endure us, whose very voice he would not even be able to
hear? lest reasoning these things they should be thrown into tumult, he called
the Gospel a harvest, all but saying: All things have been made ready, all
things have been prepared; I send you to a collection of fruits that is ready;
on the same day you will be able both to sow and to reap. As then the
husbandman going out to the reaping rejoices and is bright and is glad, not
reckoning toils, nor any difficulty, but pressing on as to a ready income, and
running upon the yearly fruits, and nowhere any hindrance, no obstacle, no
uncertainty of the future, rains, hails and droughts, and the wicked army of
locusts; for he who takes hold of reaping suspects no such thing; for this
reason they even take hold of the toils dancing and skipping; so also you, and
much more, must go out into the world with much pleasure. For the matter is a
harvest, a harvest having much ease, a harvest furnishing you the standing
crops already prepared; one must only speak, not toil. Lend me, he says, the
tongue, and you will see the ripe fruit being brought into the royal
gra =63.521= naries.
For this reason also afterwards, sending them, he was saying: Behold, I am
with you all the days unto the end of the age. For he himself was the one
making the hard things easy, and what the prophet was saying, that I will go
before you and I will level mountains; these things were coming to pass
perceptibly in the case of the apostles; for Christ was making a way before
them and was making the road easy. This also the most loud-voiced Isaiah,
making it clear, was saying: Every valley shall be filled, and every mountain
and hill shall be brought low; not speaking about mountains and hills; for
this did not even come to pass; but calling those who have lost their senses
and are puffed up and high-minded by the name of mountains. But Christ brought
all low with a humility full of height, and made the mountains plains; and he
so calls those who have lost their senses, not because of the height of their
mind, but because of the fruitlessness of the arrogance, and the barrenness of
the soul, and the insensibility of the words. For just as the mountain would
not bear fruit, so neither would arrogance; or rather not so, but even much
more harshly. For the mountain


would not bear fruit, but arrogance not only does not give any fruit, but even
withers the fruit that is and dries up that which has sprouted; as then
humility not only warms what is, but is also accustomed to give birth to what
is not.
δʹ. And the greatest examples of the things said are both the Pharisee and the
publican. For the one, having gone up with arrogance, not only did not bear
any fruit, but even lost what was; but the publican, being barren and having
nothing good, makes an achievement for himself; for when he only groaned and
hanging his head called himself wretched, he went down abounding with much
righteousness and in this overtaking the Pharisee. But that you may see even
from the things themselves, and may learn in the case of definite persons
where Christ brought low mountain and hill, let the word bring Paul in to us
again. For this tentmaker (for I gladly keep turning his craft about, that you
may learn that the craft is not a reproach, but idleness is an accusation and
a charge); this Paul then, having come to be in Salamis, met with a certain
proconsul and a magician with him. Double was the cliff, both the arrogance
from the office, and the teaching from the magician. And when he was seeing
that polluted and shameless beast hindering the proconsul and cutting off his
teaching, hear what he says, filled with the Holy Spirit: O full of all deceit
and all mischief, son of the devil.
For to insult is not everywhere an evil, but to insult at random; for on this
Christ also set a punishment; as he who does this at the fitting season
imitates a physician cutting at the fitting season, and by the cut casting out
the putrefaction. This then Paul also does: Wilt thou not cease perverting the
straight ways of the Lord? But he does not endure your words, O Paul; bring on
some apostolic reasoning, turn back the tongue, stop up his mouth, stay the
wicked streams. What then does he do? Showing that the war is not for him
against men, he says: And now behold, the hand of the Lord is upon you. For he
himself who said, I am with you all the days, was present also with Paul then,
and having this promise he both calls him and =63.522= thinks it right that he
become an avenger, and take hold together of the things set forth, and pull up
the hindrances. And now behold, the hand of the Lord is upon you. How great
the condescension of Christ; how great the boldness of Paul; how in a sentence
he brings on the blow, and Christ immediately nods to what is said. What is
Hand of the Lord? A contrary striking power. For he was present with them
everywhere, readily obeying, and making the matter a harvest, not letting them
toil nor be distressed, but contributing the things from himself. And he stood
blind, not seeing until a time. Did you see even the punishment with
loving-kindness? for the sentence is admonition, not chastisement, correction,
not obliteration. For this reason he did not dry up a hand, he did not cut
out a tongue uttering the wicked words, he did not maim legs, but the eyes
themselves. For since he himself was healed from this and was freed from the
wandering road and from the harmful courses, he lays the same medicine on this
man too, all but saying: I too was running badly, and I was seeing deadly
things; for this reason I was maimed well, that I might look up better; this
medicine I lay on you too and I maim the outward eyes, that I may open the
inward eyes.
Then the proconsul, seeing what had happened, believed. Did you see the
mountain become level, and the hill brought low, and the harvest completed,
and the reaping prepared, and the ripe fruit being gathered, and Paul having
toiled nothing nor been distressed, but with a bare word having netted the
proconsul? For this reason he calls the Gospel a harvest, because of the
swiftness and the ease which he was furnishing them. So also that eunuch, the
barbarian and foreigner and established in great power, was being netted, nor
there any time or


toil having been spent; for at once he both stepped onto the chariot and began
to catechize; or rather, before the stepping on, marvel at that, how the
barbarian and foreigner and inflamed from the power (for he was not handling a
small office), the poor man, the beggar, the unknown, who had never come to
him either into sight or into company, then appearing first, this man he both
called and brought up and sat down with himself. Who accomplished these
things? That one who said, I am with you all the days unto the end of the age;
that one made the approach more tame, and prepared a barbarian to
philosophize, and brought the mountain low, and set the unknown more intimate
than every acquaintance. Do not then run past it simply, nor think the matter
a bare history, but consider the greatness of the achievement. For if the
rulers now, being both faithful and being trained to be humble-minded and
having nothing barbarian, would not choose, I do not say an unknown and a
stranger, but even some one of their acquaintances, to sit down with them
quickly in the marketplace; how did that man the stranger? for I shall not
cease saying these things; the stranger, the one who had never appeared,
the paltry, the one easily despised from his look, this man he both brought
up and seated. and to that man's tongue he gave over his own salvation, and he
endured to become a disciple and he exhorts and he begs and he supplicates,
saying, I beg you, about whom does the prophet say this? and he receives the
things said with much attention. And not this only, but having received he was
not careless, he did not put it off. he did not say, Let me return to my own
country, let me see my friends, my household and kinsmen, which many of the
Christians say now when they are called to baptism: Let me re =63.523= turn to
my country, let me see my wife, let me see the children with my kinsmen; those
being present and keeping festival with me, thus I shall enjoy the baptism,
thus I shall partake of the grace. But that barbarian then did not say these
words, and this though being a Jew and having been trained to make much
observation of places, and especially as it was continually sounding in him
that one must keep the guard of the place, and for this reason having made a
long journey abroad, that he might worship in the place which God commanded;
but having suddenly cast out all that custom and having stood off from such
observation; when the word had been completed, and he saw a spring by the
road, he says: Behold, water; what hinders me from being baptized?
Did you see the harvest again being completed? did you see no time spent, but
at once the earth receiving, and putting forth the ear in full plume, and the
cluster becoming ripe? Behold, water, he says. He did not seek walls, nor a
golden roof, nor an arrangement of garments, nor sandals; but since he had a
purpose made ready and was boiling with longing and was kindled with
eagerness, he ran at once to the grace, and he presses the teacher to lead him
by the hand to the mystagogy, and to impart those immortal and fearful
mysteries. Wherefore that man too immediately nodded, having accepted the
eagerness, and baptized him. For this reason he calls the Gospel a harvest,
because of the ease which he was furnishing them. For also elsewhere it says,
that they were attending to the things being said by Paul; for the Lord opened
their heart to attend to the things being spoken by him. But when you hear
that he brings mountains low, and works a harvest, and makes the fruit ripe,
and constructs much ease, do not de =63.524= prive those who believe of the
greatest achievements; for he was not forcing the freedom of the will, nor was
he spoiling the self-determining power, but he was working with them and
helping and stretching out a hand.
For if the whole were his, nothing was hindering all from being saved; but now
the one part was becoming his, and the other of those coming forward; of those
coming forward, the wishing and the choosing, and showing much eagerness and a
noble faith, and of him the furnishing the gifts, and making the word distinct
and the teaching clear, and casting the seeds, and helping toward the hearing
becoming ripe. These things


then all considering, let us also take him as ally and helper into the toil
for virtue, contributing all the things from ourselves. For if a matter having
so great difficulty he so easily accomplished, not in one and two and twenty
cities, but everywhere in the world, I mean the preaching and the teaching,
and into as much earth as the sun looks upon all this has been sown and
abounds with this fairest fruit; it is quite clear that he will also take hold
together with us in the sweats for virtue, only if we ourselves are not supine
and laid back, but contribute the things from ourselves, eagerness, haste,
will, purpose, watchfulness, care, the being freed from worldly affairs, the
seeking the things to come, the longing for them each day. For if we furnish
these things with exactness, the things from him too will all follow; and when
those have come to be we shall obtain the heavenly goods; of which may it be
that we all attain, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ,
to whom be the glory and the power unto the ages of ages. Amen.
