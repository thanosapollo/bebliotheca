* Passion of Saint Artemius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

A MEMORANDUM, THAT IS, AN ACCOUNT OF THE HOLY AND GLORIOUS GREAT-MARTYR AND
WONDERWORKER ARTEMIUS, COMPILED FROM THE ECCLESIASTICAL HISTORY OF
PHILOSTORGIUS AND OF CERTAIN OTHERS, BY JOHN THE MONK.
=96.1252= αʹ. Being about to narrate the exploits of the great and glorious
martyr Artemius, and the contest, and the nobility that was his from above and
from ancestors, O sacred assembly and God-gathered company, I invoke that
martyr himself, and the grace of the Spirit that overshadows him, to become a
fellow-worker of the discourse for me and a helper; and I put you yourselves
also in entreaty, needing your prayers, so that this undertaking may become
for me a fair path and without stumbling, and may be directed toward that
narration of his martyrdom and confession at which I have set out. And let no
one blame me as I attempt these things, looking to the first and ancient
memorandum of this wonderful and celebrated man; for the one who composed that
wrote as =96.1253= the time then required, and as he had power, while the
affairs of that time were held in much tumult and disturbance. Nor indeed was
he himself one of those who were considered and who had been eager about
letters, but a simple and unadorned man, caring only for the truth, and aiming
at speaking in whatever way; and according to the proverb, touching the sacred
education of letters with the tip of a finger.
βʹ. Just as I myself also do not have sufficient and adequate power for the
narration of the discourse, even if the longing for the martyr draws down and
tyrannizes my thought and forces me toward speaking, yet he is praiseworthy
for the eagerness and for the faith concerning the martyr, because he dared,
however it might be, to take hold of the narration concerning his martyrdom,
especially when the apostate and impious Julian had commanded those seized for
the martyrdom of Christ to make neither a memorandum nor any other record
whatsoever, as the earlier emperors had legislated, but that the greater
number of them should perish without a defense; when this command had gone
down everywhere, those who confessed Christ were punished; but according to
the prevailing custom, none of the commentarienses from the people, or of the
so-called tachygraphers, took thought of this command. For the lawless man was
eager to make even the glory of the martyrs itself vanish. So only with
difficulty did certain men, hiding in some gloomy and unlighted places, dare
to take hold of such memoranda, fearing the savagery of the one in power. Many
myriads of the Christians throughout the whole inhabited world were being
destroyed, not even being thought worthy of a single interrogation according
to the prevailing custom; but this in no way harmed the athletes of Christ,
that they did not obtain a memorandum; for of those whose names God was
writing down in the heavens, there was no need of the memoranda among men. But
of these things enough.
γʹ. But I, O sacred assembly, and people of Christ gathered by God, a holy
nation and a royal priesthood, having met with many writings, and especially
of those who composed the histories and deeds of the emperors, and then also
of those who were eager about the ecclesiastical history, and having found the
name of the martyr here and there


being noised about and =96.1256= carried around, and all confessing him a man
conspicuous and celebrated, I did not think it right to bury these things in
the depths of forgetfulness, but to bring them into the open, and to set them
before your hearings, since you are both lovers of Christ and lovers of the
martyrs; so that such great advantages of the divine martyr may not be
overshadowed in a few syllables; and at the same time, being eager also to
gladden your hearings, both from the narrations of the history and from the
exploits of the martyr that have lately appeared.
δʹ. Many then of those who wrote the histories have remembered this celebrated
man, both Eusebius who is named of Pamphilus, and Socrates of the heresy of
Novatus, and Philostorgius who himself also belongs to the heresy of Eunomius,
and Theodoret and many others. Of whom Eusebius, being and being known in the
times of Constantine the Great, and being the most learned of the bishops
then, introduces the martyr as being one of the senate, and known in the
affairs of the emperor, and a most exact devotee of the companionship, or
rather friendship, concerning Constantius his son. For the blessed man appears
never at all to have been torn away from the friendship toward Constantius,
showing forth the glorious and great-working quality of his nature; but
Philostorgius, even if he is a burning lover of the heresy of Eunomius, yet
more than all deifies the martyr, having made a great insistence and exactness
of his deeds, having indicated the nobility belonging to the martyr from the
times above, even before he took hold of the contests of martyrdom. I too then
will begin the history concerning him, as the tablets of the ancients declare.
εʹ. When idolatry had just been brought to a stop, and the deceit of the
demons had been quenched, from the philanthropy that came about from the great
God and our Saviour Jesus Christ concerning the blessed and celebrated
Constantine, the most splendid and pious emperor, the son of Constantius and
of the blessed Helen, how he called him back from the vain error of the idols,
and from the showing of the life-giving cross in heaven, and how he prevailed
over the hostile and lawless emperors through the working and power of the
precious cross, and how the God-beloved horn of the Christians was raised,
being supported and increased by his eagerness and faith, so that the
preaching of Christ filled all the inhabited world, and all the altars of the
idols and the images and all the temples, wherever on earth they happened to
be, were taken down, and the churches of God were rebuilt, which the
Christ-hating and impious emperors had consumed.
ϛʹ· These things then coming about, the devil who begrudges good things did
not bear so great a change, but he raised a storm and a disturbance through
his own shield-bearers. For Arius, named from the madness, being a presbyter
of the Church in Alexandria, terribly disturbed it, having set in motion a
lawless dogma full of every blasphemy. For he said that the only-begotten Son
of God, the pre-eternal, was a creature, and alien from the substance of God
and the Father. For these reasons then the synod of the holy three hundred and
eighteen Fathers is gathered in Nicaea, and having deposed Arius, it proclaims
the Son of God and our Lord Jesus Christ consubstantial with the Father. But
these things both the histories of those outside declare, and not a few of our
own have examined them exactly and made them clear; but for me to spend time
on these things the present season will not be fitting, another more exact
leisure and consideration being needed concerning them.


But as to the present I will narrate the end of his life that came upon him.
ζʹ. The Christ-loving emperor Constantine then, having advanced into the first
and thirtieth year of his reign, and having entered upon the second, when he
learned that the Persians were preparing for war against him, having set out
from his own city, was seen as far as Nicomedia of the Bithynians; where he
also ends his life from a plot of his own brothers, who poured a destructive
drug upon him, a star, as they say, a comet, having foretold his death. And
Constantine had these brothers on the father's side, Dalmatius, Anaballianus,
and Constantius; for he himself alone was from Helen to his father Constantius
while still a private man; but from Theodora, the daughter of Maximian
surnamed Herculius, other children were born to him: both the aforesaid
Dalmatius, and Anaballianus, and Constantius; whom Constantine also honored as
Caesars and nobilissimi. Of these Constantius from the wife joined to him
begets both Gallus and Julian who was surnamed the transgressor, because he
denied Christ and inclined toward the Hellenic religion; who also, when he was
emperor, punished the great martyr of Christ and much-contending Artemius,
because of the faith toward Christ and the godly zeal. But these things came
about later and after a time.
ηʹ. And I, having narrated the things done in the times above, will turn the
discourse again upon the things that happened to the martyr; both how the
lawless and transgressor Julian and in what manner came to the dignity of the
empire; and how Artemius the martyr of Christ ran into the all-holy stadium of
the confession of Christ. When the great Constantine had just died, the rule
of the Romans was divided into three rules, his sons, Constantine, =96.1260=
and Constantius and Constans, having partitioned these. And to the first
Constantine the upper Gauls and the parts beyond the Alps, and the British
islands, and as far as the western ocean, were given as a lot; but to
Constans, as the last, the lower Gauls, that is Italy and Rome itself. And
Constantius the second of the sons of Constantine, who was then upon the
affairs of the East contending against the Persians, takes to himself the
Eastern part; and Byzantium, then renamed Constantinople, and New Rome, he
makes an imperial city, and from Illyricum as far as the Propontis as many
things as were subject to the Romans, and Syria and Palestine and Mesopotamia
and Egypt, and all the islands, he sets as tributary to his empire and polity.
θʹ. And the great Artemius was with Constantius at every time and affair, as
being a most excellent friend, and of those made splendid in virtue and
education, and a burning lover of the faith of the Christians. No one has
handed down to us a written memory of his fatherland and race, except that the
thrice-blessed man was of the well-born and the great. From which these things
also have been written concerning him, that the all-holy relics of the
apostles of Christ, Andrew and Luke and Timothy, it is he who was commanded by
Constantius to make their translation, as the discourse going forward will
teach; and I will narrate all these things in order, passing over to the times
and setting them out according to the due exactness of the affairs. As then it
has been said, the emperors being three, and each of them ruling his own
portion, the first of them, Constantine, having departed from his own share
and having gone against the inheritance of the last brother, when that one had
gone away to Rome, was attempting to accomplish something unjust against the
brother; and he was slandering him not being present, as


the affairs had not been well distributed, and that he had appropriated the
greatest part of the rule belonging to him. But the generals and guards of the
country, whom Constans had appointed, said they were not able without that
one's mind and counsel to move anything small or great; for it was unholy; but
he strips for war, and moves arms against the one who had done no injustice.
Constantine then falls in the war fighting, and desiring the share of others,
and he lost in addition even what he seemed to hold securely.
ιʹ. His people then incline toward Constans, and the whole rule of the West
comes under that one who had been eager about none of this; God having judged
these things, Do not move the boundaries of your fathers, nor lay hold of the
furrow of the neighbor; for the one who works evil against the neighbor
gathers destruction for himself, drawing the judgment of God upon himself.
Constans then reigns over the whole Western rule, having joined the two
inheritances into one, and having set both parts as one rule. There was not
much time in the middle, and Constans, having inclined into revels and
drunkennesses, and strange courses of loves, was playing the whole rule
carelessly as with a pebble, going outside the greatness of the empire; he too
then is plotted against by one of the generals, Magnentius, and together with
the empire he loses also his life. When this man had fallen, Magnentius holds
the rule, with whom Nepotianus and Vetranio also took part in the tyranny.
ιαʹ. Constantius, having learned these things from the letters of his sister,
having set out from the East, and having come toward the West, joins war
against both, and conquers by force, Vetranio having inclined toward him; when
also the sign of the cross, very great and appearing terribly entire, so as to
outshine by the striking quality of the radiance the light of day, was seen
over Jerusalem about the third hour especially of the day, the feast called
Pentecost being at hand, extending from the place called the Skull even as far
as the Mount of Olives, from where the Saviour had made the ascension.
Constantius then holds the whole empire, being left alone of the sons of
Constantine the Great.
ιβʹ. Having then gazed toward the greatness of the rule, and grown dizzy, as
being a human being, and not having the one from the race shielding with him;
for neither had a child been born to him, nor had any of the brothers been
left; and fearing lest some tyrant should again rise up against him, rising
against his empire, he considers taking one of the kinsmen as a co-heir and a
shield-bearer of the empire. Which indeed he also did, setting Gallus the
brother of Julian forward as Caesar; and Gallus was a cousin to him on the
father's side; for Constantine, the father of Gallus and Julian, was a brother
of Constantine the Great. Having then appointed this man in Sirmium,
Constantius also yokes to him as wife his own sister, for the sake of faith
and firmness; and he gives him rulers, having appointed them himself; for it
was not permitted to that one, being Caesar; sending Thalassius as prefect of
the praetorium; and Montius, over the imperial =96.1264= affairs, whom it is
their custom to name quaestors, having also made him a patrician. And Gallus,
as then sent by Constantius upon the East, held to the affairs; whom the
Persians at once, having learned, feared, having heard that he was young and
hot-working toward deeds, and they no longer made the expedition against the
Romans. And he was in Antioch of Syria; but Constantius was settling the
affairs in the West; and then especially the rule of the Romans was purely at
rest, by both


being guarded. And these things had this manner.
ιγʹ. But Gallus, having put on the purple of Caesar, and already beginning to
step upon the first steps of the empire, did not remain upon the same mind and
faith which he had made toward Constantius, but he was heavy and unrestrained
and implacable in anger; for having laid hold of untimely pride and uneven
counsel, he overstepped the bounds, and made light of the covenants which he
had made toward Constantius, laying hold of the affairs more imperially, and
disposing them with much boldness and arrogance. For the rulers whom
Constantius had sent with him, being arbitrators of the imperial and civic
affairs, both the prefect of the praetorium Domitianus (for Thalassius had
died), and Montius who was over the quaestorship, because they did not obey
and serve his unreasonable and unrestrained impulses, having commanded the
soldiers to fasten ropes to their feet, he ordered them to be dragged in the
marketplace, and he killed both, men distinguished in dignities, and found
higher than every gain and taking; whom the bishop of the city, having
wrapped, buried, having reverenced the unsurpassable quality of their virtue.
ιδʹ. But Constantius, as soon as he learned what had happened, made Gallus
summoned to himself. And he, knowing on the one hand that he was being called
not for good, considering again on the other that, if he should not be willing
to obey, it was necessary to make war, having taken up arms against
Constantius straightway, chooses rather the things of peace, and having sent
the wife ahead to Constantius to mollify him, he himself also went off a
volunteer upon the danger. Constantina then set out first to meet the brother
beforehand, and being eager to make him respectful on behalf of the husband,
that he should not plan anything incurable against him; but using much
eagerness about the journey, she fell into sickness while traveling in
between, and having set foot in Bithynia, she died at a certain station of it
called Gallicanum. And Gallus, having made this unexpected thing that happened
to him also a great misfortune, yet not standing aside from the things
decided, still going forward. And when he arrived at the Norici, a city of
theirs called Poetovio, here then from Milan there is sent down the general
Barbatio, Constantius happening at that time =96.1265= to be there; who takes
the purple from Gallus, and having changed him into a private man, he set him
as an exile to a certain island of Dalmatia.
ιεʹ. And when Gallus had been led away to the island, those who had arranged
the whole against him, and Eusebius especially was the eunuch who had the
honor of the praepositus, and those with him, persuade Constantius to put
Gallus out of the way as quickly as possible. And he, having been persuaded,
sends those who would kill him; and when these were already arriving,
Constantius again was turned toward pity, and he sends through swift other
letters recalling Gallus from the suffering. But Eusebius and those with him
persuade the magistrianus who had been sent not to present himself showing the
letter before he should learn that Gallus had been destroyed. These things
happened, and Gallus had died. But Constantius, having feared about the
affairs, lest he should not be able alone to be master of the whole rule, and
otherwise also because the Gauls are raised most sharply, and whenever they
are eager, into tyrannies, through strength of body and lightness of thoughts,
then regretted having taken Gallus out of the way; and having reckoned that
the kindred is far more secure than the foreign and alien-born toward a
sharing of the empire, having sent for Julian the brother of Gallus from Ionia
in Milan, he showed him as Caesar, and


having given his own sister Helen to him in marriage, and having made the
pledges toward him, he sent this man out into the Gauls to be a guard of the
empire there; and he himself, having arrived among the Illyrians, was spending
time in Sirmium. ιϛʹ. And having heard these things, that the barbarians
beyond the Ister are about to campaign against the rule of the Romans, having
set out from Sirmium he crossed toward the Ister, and having spent not a
little time at the bank itself, when the companies of the barbarians were at
rest, he was driving again upon Thrace; and when he happened to have come
among the Odrysians, where the emperor Hadrian, having founded a city, left
his own name to the place, he learned from one of the bishops that the bodies
of the apostles of Christ, Andrew and Luke, happen to be buried in Achaia,
Andrew in Patras, and Luke in Thebes of Boeotia. When then the emperor
Constantius heard these things, he both was pleased at the word and shouted
greatly, and he says to those present: Call Artemius for me. And when he had
come with speed, I rejoice with you, he said, most God-beloved of all men. And
he to him: And may you be one in whom I rejoice, O emperor, through all, and
may none of the troublesome things ever overtake you. And the emperor: And do
you seek something more gracious, O best of friends, than the finding of the
bodies of the apostles of Christ? And the great Artemius: Who and from where,
O master, is the one who has made this treasure appear to us today? And
Constantius: The bishop of Achaia who now oversees in Patras; but go, best of
men, and make with speed their coming up in Constantinople.
ιζʹ. Having heard these things from the emperor, the great Artemius was going
the road upon the apostles, to bring up their all-holy relics upon
Constantinople; and the one writing the history says such things concerning
Constantius and the martyr. It is said then concerning Constantius that he was
not only eager and lovely toward God, even if he inclined toward the Arian
heresy, having been driven together by the impious and most godless Eusebius
the bishop of Nicomedia, since in the other things at least he was moderate,
and taking thought especially of seemliness, and having laid hold of extreme
temperance about both the diet and the other manner; and he made very much
eagerness toward the churches, being ambitious to surpass his own father by
far in the eagernesses concerning these things. And he built the greatest
church in the city of the father, near the senate, having begun the work from
below and from the foundations; and honoring the tomb of the father, he built
a greatest temple there as a place of worship; and having transferred Andrew
the apostle from Achaia, as I said before, he set him there; and indeed also
Luke the evangelist he transferred from the same Achaia, and Timothy from
Ephesus of Ionia.
ιηʹ. And the one who was commanded to make the conveyance of these was of the
best of men. And as a prize of the liturgy the emperor, the bishops having
asked, granted him the rule of Egypt. And these things the one gathering the
history says concerning the martyr, bearing witness with him, that even before
the contests of the testimony he was revered by all because of the flashing
virtue of his life. And concerning Luke Anatolius the eunuch of those from the
imperial bedchamber also narrated such a thing, himself having received the
trial of the holiness upon himself. This Anatolius then said that he was in a
poor state, and the suffering already greater than according to the art of the
physicians; when then the coffin in which Luke was lying, when those bringing
it had sailed up, was just being conveyed from the sea upon the temple, he
himself from eagerness went under and helped to bear it with those carrying it
as far as the power had for him, and then


immediately he was released from the disease, and was drawn along into the
time of his life afterward upon a number of not a few years. Constantius then
built the temple of the Apostles earlier, even if later Justinian constructed
it toward the more magnificent, and with better materials made it bright
toward the more seemly. And the temple is now where the bodies of the apostles
lie, being called by the common name of the Apostles.
ιθʹ. The great Artemius then was going upon Egypt, having put on the dignity
of the dux; and Constantius, having set out from Constantinople, was making
the road upon Syria, and having reached the great city Antioch, he encamps
there, preparing the war toward the Persians. And when he had spent time in
the city and was equipping the army, letters arrived to him making clear the
uprising of Julian. For Julian, as has =96.1269= been made clear to me also
before, when I was making the discourse concerning these things, having been
shown as Caesar by Constantius upon a guard of the Western parts into the
Gauls, himself not bearing to be still longer in the form of Caesar, both puts
on the diadem, and lays hold of the greater empire. And when he had taken hold
of the affairs, he no longer thought anything small, nor did he judge that he
ought to delay; but wishing for the present to make all Europe, as much as
obeys the Romans, under himself, having arranged himself with the army he was
going through the Germans upon the Ister, and having taken hold of the far
bank, he was driving through the regions of that; having escaped the notice of
both prefects, both the one of the Italies, Taurus so called, and Florentius
of the Illyrians. And when he came about the Paeonians, having crossed the
river to the other side, at once he had both all the Illyrian land under
himself, and the Italies, and all the nations as far as the western ocean, as
many as were of the Roman dominion.
κʹ. And Constantius, having learned these things through the letters, was
disturbed as was likely, and having feared especially about Constantinople
lest, what that one also was intending, he should anticipate, having made it
under himself, he was hastening then as far as possible to occupy it
beforehand. And while the army was being gathered for him, scattered among the
cities of the East, and was about to be equipped as toward so great a road, he
signifies to the bishops that they should arrive at Nicaea before him as
quickly as possible; for he was planning to hold a second synod in it, being
urged by the impious Arians against the consubstantial. And when, having gone
through Cilicia, he arrived at the springs called those of Mopsus, a certain
weakness fell upon him suddenly, and he was no longer able to go forward. And
when he perceived that he was already in a poor state and would not live, he
sends for Euzoius the bishop of Antioch with all speed, and he entrusts him to
baptize him; and having been baptized and having lived a little longer, he
leaves life there, having reigned the whole forty years, the half with the
father, and the remaining alone. And the army, having lamented him, and having
accomplished the customary things upon him, placed him in a coffin, having
prepared the corpse with the things usual for lasting, and having placed him
in a wagon, they were conveying him upon Constantinople, each following him
with their own arms, and according to the same order in which also when he was
living they happened to have been arranged under the leaders. καʹ. These then
were holding through Constantinople bringing the corpse, and Julian
accompanied, having arrived from the Illyrians, and already holding the whole
empire securely, no one having dared to oppose him after the death of
Constantius. And when the corpse was being conveyed upon the temple of the
Apostles, where they were about to set him down near the father, he himself
was leading the bier, having taken the diadem from the head. And when they had
buried him, already


departing upon the palaces, he both set the diadem =96.1272= on again, and he
was master of the affairs, having already girt himself alone with the whole
empire of the Romans. When then Constantius was out of the way, toward those
left, and especially those who had furnished the cause of the destruction of
Gallus by envy, he cooled the boiling of the anger; and at once he takes the
head from Eusebius the praepositus, because he appeared from the beginning,
from his own slanders, to have mixed the whole murder for Gallus. And Paul the
Spaniard, who belonged among the notaries of the emperor, he gives over to
fire, as having embittered Gallus very much. Both of these he sent across to
Chalcedon, and there he brings each under his own justice; and he also
destroyed Gaudentius, general of Africa, and certain others, as many as had
committed some outrage against him.
κβʹ. But these he punished through letters; yet the martyr of Christ and noble
athlete Artemius he himself through himself, being present in Antioch and he
being present, punished inhumanly because of the confession toward Christ, and
stripped him of the present authority, not bearing his frankness and
insistence. For Julian, as has been made clear, having girt himself with the
empire of the Romans, was eager especially to restore Hellenism. Sending
letters then everywhere, he commanded to raise their precincts and the altars
with much eagerness and zeal; and as many revenues as Constantine the Great
had assigned to the churches, and his son Constantius, having taken these
away, he dedicated to the temples of the demons; having set up instead of
bishops and presbyters and deacons temple-servants, and sacristans, and
sprinklers, and sacrificers, and basket-bearers, and as many names as the
Hellenic nonsense applies; these things then and others he was accomplishing
in Constantinople.
κγʹ. And after these things, having a brother of his mother, Julian by name,
who had denied the religion of Christianity for that one's favor, and was
showing much eagerness on behalf of Hellenism, he sent him out as ruler of the
East, whom they call comes, having commanded him to harm and destroy the
affairs of the churches, and everywhere and through every form to increase and
raise Hellenism. And he, having arrived in Antioch, was trying to appear
greater than the things commanded in the deeds, and indeed he takes away from
all the churches all the treasures, as many as continued in silver and gold
and silk weavings, and he also shuts the churches, that no one should go into
them for the sake of prayer, having put locks and bars upon the gateways. And
these things the ruler of the East was working in the city of Antiochus.
=96.1273= And the emperor Julian still in Constantinople was drawing out a
certain time, having strengthened the things in it toward what was thought
especially to benefit the empire, and considering and working how Hellenism
might be raised for him toward the more magnificent; having then set out from
Constantinople with the whole army he was making the road upon Syria. Having
gone through all Phrygia then, and having arrived at its last city, Iconium so
called, he turned aside leaving Isauria, and having gone up over the so-called
Taurus, he came upon the cities of Cilicia, and having drawn near the station
in Issus, he encamps there, having imitated Alexander from Macedonia; for
there that one also in Issus held the war toward Darius the king of the
Persians, and having conquered him, he made the place notable. From there
having crossed the Issic gulf, he came in the city Tarsus; and from there into
Antioch, fighting in spirit against the Christians and threatening to wipe out
their name entirely.
κεʹ. When then the tyrant had come in the city Antioch and


had encamped at the palace houses, and had not been at rest even toward one
day, there were brought to him, as if having been slandered from some command,
Eugenius and Macarius, being presbyters of the Church in Antioch; whom the
accursed man, having sat forward upon the tribunal, commanded to stand by, and
he says to them: What men are you, and of what life and fortune happening to
be, have you been brought upon our tribunal? Eugenius said: We are Christians,
and we happen to be leaders of the flock of Christ; this is life for us and
fortune and pursuit. And the emperor: Where then is the flock of Christ of
which you happen to be leaders? And Eugenius: All the inhabited world as much
as the sun looks upon, and as many human beings as are upon it. And Julian:
And of whom do we rule and of whom are we emperors today, O wretched and most
unfortunate little man, if as much land as the sun looks upon Christ has
acquired as a flock? And the martyr: Of the same flock and herd, O emperor,
you also preside, of which we also happen to be leaders; for through him
emperors reign, and tyrants hold the earth, and he himself has given you the
empire today; and if you yourself have appeared ungrateful about the

room;

μαʹ. And that the empire is more fitting of our race; for my father

denied Christianity and inclined toward the Hellenic life; knowing well that

For who is ambiguous in mind, seeing the sun riding through in heaven and the
moon bull-driving upon a gold-reined chariot? for the one makes the day

fashioned certain myths toward pleasure; yet they do not honor their images as
gods, away with that. For this is understood by the simpler and rustic race of
human beings; since those who embrace philosophy, and examine the things of
the gods exactly, know to whom they assign the honor, and toward whom the
worship of the divine statues passes. =96.1292= μγʹ. I exhort then your
