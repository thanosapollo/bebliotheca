* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Rivers flowing uphill: this is the proverb. A Cynic man says Diogenes is
vainglorious, and he does not wish to bathe in cold water, though he is very
strong in body and bursting with vigor and in the prime of his age, lest he
should take some harm, and this when the god is already approaching the summer
tropic. But he also mocks the eating of the octopus and says Diogenes has paid
sufficient penalties for his folly and vainglory, destroyed by the food as if
by hemlock. So far has he advanced in wisdom that he knows clearly that death
is an evil. This the wise Socrates supposed he did not know, and after him
Diogenes also. At any rate they say that when Antisthenes was sick with a long
and hard-to-recover illness, Diogenes handed him a small sword, saying: if you
need a friend's service. So little did that man think death terrible or
painful. But we who have received the scepter from there, by a greater wisdom,
know that death is hard, and we say that being sick is more terrible than
death itself, and shivering with cold harder than being sick. For the sick man
is sometimes treated softly, so that the illness becomes luxury itself,
especially if he is rich. I myself, by Zeus, have seen some living more
luxuriously in their diseases than those same men when healthy; and yet even
then they lived in splendid luxury. Whence it occurred to me to say to some of
my companions that it would have been better for these men to become servants
rather than masters, and to be poor, more naked than the lily, than to be rich
as they now are. For then they would have ceased being sick and living in
luxury at the same time. Some then count it a fine thing to be puffed up in
sickness and to be nursed luxuriously in this way; but a man who endures the
frost and holds out against heat, does he not fare even more wretchedly than
the sick? At any rate he suffers without consolation.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Come then, let us set down in common whatever we have heard from teachers
concerning the Cynics, for those going into this life to consider; if they
should be persuaded by these things, I know well that those now attempting to
play the Cynic will be no worse; but if they disobey, if they should practice
something brilliant and august, outdoing our speech, not in words but in
deeds, our speech will be no hindrance; but if through greediness or softness
or, to say the chief point summing it up in brief, being enslaved to bodily
pleasure they should slight the words and laugh at them besides, as sometimes
the dogs piss against the porches of the schools and the law-courts,
Hippocleides does not care; for we do not mind even the little dogs when they
commit such offenses. Come then, let us go through the argument from the
beginning in headings in order, so that rendering what is fitting concerning
each we may both ourselves accomplish more easily what we intended and make it
easy for you to follow. Therefore since Cynicism happens to be a kind of
philosophy, not at all the meanest nor the most dishonored, but a rival of the
strongest, we must first say a little concerning philosophy itself.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

The gift of the gods to men, sent down together with most brilliant fire
through Prometheus from the sun, with the portion of Hermes, is nothing other
than the distribution of reason and mind. For Prometheus, the providence that
oversees all mortal things, putting a warm breath as an instrument under
nature, gave a share of incorporeal reason to all; and each partook of as much
as it was able: lifeless bodies of habit only, plants already also of life,
animals of soul, and man also of rational soul. Now there are some who think
one nature has come through all these, and some who think they differ also in
kind. But not yet this, or rather let this not even be examined in the present
discourse, except for this reason: that whether one should take philosophy, as
some suppose, to be the art of arts and the science of sciences, or likeness
to god according to what is possible, or, as the Pythian said, Know thyself,
it will make no difference to the argument; for all these appear to stand in a
very close relation to one another.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Let us begin first from Know thyself, since this exhortation is also divine.
Therefore he who knows himself will know concerning soul, and will know also
concerning body. And this alone will not suffice, to learn that man is a soul
using a body, but he will go on to the essence of the soul itself, then he
will track out its powers. And not even this alone will suffice him, but also,
if there is something of the soul in us that is better and more divine, which
indeed we all, being persuaded without teaching, think to be something divine,
and this we all commonly suppose to be seated in heaven. Then going on again
he will examine the principles of the body, whether it is composite or simple;
then advancing by the way, concerning its harmony and affection and power and
simply all that it needs for continuance. After this he will look also to the
principles of certain arts by which the body is helped toward continuance,
such as medicine, farming, and others of this sort. Nor yet will he be
altogether ignorant of any of the useless and superfluous things, since these
too have been invented for the flattery of the passionate part of our soul.
For he will shrink from clinging to these, thinking such a thing shameful,
fleeing what seems laborious in them; but as a whole he will not be ignorant
of what sort they seem and to which parts of the soul they fit. Consider then
whether knowing oneself is not the leader of all science and of all art, and
has also gathered together the universal accounts. For it is fitting to know
the divine things through the divine portion that is in us, and the mortal
things through the mortal-like portion, and besides these, he said, to know
the living being between these, man, mortal in the particular, but immortal in
the whole, and moreover that the one and the particular is composed of a
mortal and an immortal portion.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

That moreover becoming like to god according to one's power is nothing other
than procuring the knowledge of beings that is attainable for men, is clear
from this. For we do not bless the divine for wealth of money nor for any
other of the supposed goods, but as Homer says /the gods know all things,/ and
moreover concerning Zeus /But Zeus was born first and knew more;/ for the gods
differ from us in knowledge. For perhaps among them too the first of noble
things is to know themselves; and by as much as they are better than we in
essence, by so much, knowing themselves, they possess knowledge of better
things. Let no one then divide philosophy for us into many, nor cut it into
many, or rather let him not make many out of one. For as truth is one, so also
philosophy is one; and it is no wonder if we travel toward it by different
roads. Since even if some stranger, or by Zeus some citizen of old, should
wish to return to Athens, he could both sail and walk, and going by land he
could use either the broad highways or the paths and short roads; and it is
possible also to sail along the shores, and indeed, after the manner of the
Pylian old man, cutting the middle of the sea. And let no one bring this
against us, if some of those going by these very roads have wandered and,
having come somewhere else, as if baited by Circe or the Lotus-eaters with
pleasure or glory or something else, have been left behind from going forward
and reaching the end; but let him look at those who held first place in each
of the schools, and he will find all things in agreement.

Therefore the god at Delphi proclaims Know thyself, and Heraclitus /I searched
myself,/ and Pythagoras also and those from him down to Theophrastus say
becoming like to god according to one's power, and Aristotle too. For what we
are at times, this the god is always. It would then be ridiculous that the god
should not know himself; for he will know nothing at all of the other things,
if he should be ignorant of himself; for he is all things, if indeed he has in
himself and with himself the causes of whatever things are, whether immortal
causes of immortals, or of perishable things causes not mortal nor perishable,
but everlasting and remaining always, and which are for these the causes of
everlasting generation. But this argument is greater.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

That truth is one and philosophy one, and that of this all are lovers both of
those I mentioned a little earlier and of those whose name I might justly now
speak, I mean the companions of the man of Citium, who, seeing the cities
running away from the too unmixed and pure freedom of the dog, covered him, I
think, with screens, with household-management and money-making and union with
a wife and the rearing of children, so that, I think, they might set him as a
guardian near the cities; and that they set Know thyself as the chief point of
philosophy, you would be persuaded not only from the writings they composed
concerning this very thing, if you should wish, but much more from the end of
philosophy; for they made the end to live in agreement with nature, which it
is not possible for one ignorant of who and of what sort he is by nature to
attain; for he who does not know who he is will not know, I suppose, what it
is fitting for him to do, just as he who does not know iron will not know
whether it is fitting for him to cut or not, and what iron needs in order to
be able to do its own work; but that philosophy is one and that all, so to
speak, aiming at one thing, came to this by different roads, it is enough to
say this much now.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

But concerning Cynicism we must still inquire.

If then the writings had been made by the men with some seriousness, and not
in play, it would have been necessary, following these, to attempt to examine
each of the things we think concerning the matter against the opponent, and if
they appeared to agree with the ancients, not to prosecute us for false
witness, but if not, then to banish them from hearing, as the Athenians did
the false writings from the Metroon. But since there is nothing of this sort,
as I said; for the much-talked-of tragedies of Diogenes are said to be of a
certain Philiscus of Aegina, and if they should be of Diogenes, it is not at
all strange that the wise man should play, since many of the philosophers also
appear to have done this; Democritus too, they say, used to laugh seeing men
taking things seriously; let us not then look to their play, like those who
least desire to learn anything serious, comparing them to a happy city, full
of many temples and many secret rites, and of countless holy priests remaining
within in holy places; and for this very reason often, I mean for the sake of
keeping all the inner things pure, they have driven out the superfluous and
disgusting and mean things of the city, public baths and brothels and taverns
and simply all such things; then having come as far as this they do not go
inside. For he who has met with such things, then thinking this to be the
city, is wretched if he flees away, and more wretched if he remains below,
when it is possible, going a little further, to see Socrates; for I will use
those words with which Alcibiades praises Socrates. For I say that the Cynic
philosophy is most like those Sileni sitting in the herm-carvers' shops, which
the craftsmen make holding pipes or flutes; who, opened in two, appear within
having images of gods. So that we may not suffer some such thing, thinking
that whatever he played he was in earnest about; for there is something even
in those things not useless, but Cynicism is something else, as I shall try to
show at once; come, let us look in order from the deeds, as the tracking dogs
run after the game.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

It is not easy then to find a leader to whom one must first refer it, even if
some suppose this belongs to Antisthenes and Diogenes. This at any rate
Oenomaus seems not unreasonably to say: Cynicism is neither Antisthenism nor
Diogenism. For the nobler of the dogs say that the great Heracles also, just
as he became the cause of the other goods for us, so also left this life as
the greatest example to men. But I, wishing to speak well of the gods and of
those who have gone to a divine lot, am persuaded that even before this some,
not among Greeks only but also among barbarians, philosophized thus; for this
philosophy seems somehow to be common and most natural and to need no labor of
any sort; but it is enough only to choose the serious things from desire of
virtue and flight from vice, and one need neither unroll countless books; for
much learning, they say, does not teach understanding; nor suffer any other of
such things as those going through the other schools suffer, but it is enough
only to hear these two things that the Pythian advises, Know thyself and
Deface the currency; the founder of the philosophy has therefore appeared to
us, the same who I think became for the Greeks the cause of all noble things,
the common leader and lawgiver and king of Greece, the god at Delphi, whom,
since it was not lawful that anything should escape him, the fitness of
Diogenes also did not escape. And he exhorted him not as the others,
stretching the advice in verses, but teaching by deed what he wishes,
symbolically through two names, saying Deface the currency. For Know thyself
he said and says not to that man only but also to the others, for it is set, I
think, before the precinct. We have found then the founder of the philosophy,
as somewhere also the divine Iamblichus says, and also the chiefs in it,
Antisthenes and Diogenes and Crates, whose aim and end of life was, I think,
to know themselves and to despise empty opinions, and to grasp with the whole
mind, they say, the truth which leads all goods for gods and all for men, for
the sake of which I think Plato and Pythagoras and Socrates and those of the
Peripatos and Zeno endured all toil, wishing both to know themselves and not
to follow empty opinions, but to track out the truth in the things that are.

Come then, since it has appeared that Plato practiced not one thing and
Diogenes another, but one and the same; if at any rate someone should ask the
wise Plato /how much do you think Know thyself is worth?/ I know well that he
would say everything, and he says it in the Alcibiades; come then, tell us
next, O divine Plato and offspring of gods, /In what manner must one be
disposed toward the opinions of the many,/ he will say these things and
besides will expressly command us to read the whole Crito dialogue, in which
Socrates appears advising us to care nothing for such things; he says at any
rate: /But why, O blessed Crito, do we care so much for the opinion of the
many?/ Then are we, overlooking these things, willing simply thus to wall off
and tear apart from one another men whom the love of truth and the contempt of
opinion and the common breath toward the zeal of virtue have brought together?
But if it seemed good to Plato to work these things also through words, and
for Diogenes deeds were enough, is he for this worthy to hear ill from you?
And see whether this very thing is not better by far, since even Plato appears
swearing off the writings. /For there is no writing of Plato,/ he says, /nor
will there be, and those now current are of Socrates, a man fair and young./
Why then do we not examine from the deeds of Diogenes what Cynicism itself is?

Therefore since of a body there are parts, such as eyes, feet, hands, and
other things supervene, hair, nails, dirt, a kind of such residues, without
which a human body cannot be, is he not ridiculous who thought nails or hair
or dirt and the foul of the residues to be parts, and not the most honorable
and serious things, first the sense-organs and of these themselves whichever
are more causes of understanding for us, such as eyes, ears? For these serve
toward prudence, whether it is buried in the soul, so that being more quickly
purified it might be able to use the pure and unmoved power of thinking, or,
as some think, the soul bringing in through such channels. For gathering, they
say, the particular sensations and holding them together by memory it
generates the sciences. But I think, if there were not something of this sort
inspired or complete but hindered by many and various other things, which
makes the perception of the outside things, it would not even be possible for
the perception of sensibles to come about. But this argument does not belong
to the present matters.

Therefore we must bring it back to the parts of the Cynic philosophy.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

These men too appear then to have thought philosophy two-fold, like Aristotle
and Plato, theoretical and practical, clearly understanding and conceiving
this very thing, that man is by nature at home with action and knowledge. And
if they turned aside from the theory of physics, this is nothing to the
argument. Since Socrates also and many others appear to have used much theory,
but this not for the sake of something else, but of action; since they thought
knowing oneself to be this, learning accurately what must be assigned to soul
and what to body; and they reasonably assigned leadership to the soul, and
service to the body. They appear then to have practiced virtue, self-control,
freedom from vanity, freedom, having come outside of all envy, cowardice,
superstition. But we do not think these things concerning them, but we suppose
them to play and to gamble about what is dearest, having so despised the body,
as Socrates said, speaking rightly, that philosophy is a practice of death.
This those men practicing every day do not seem to us more to be emulated, but
some wretched and altogether foolish men; and for what did they endure these
toils? Not, as you yourself said, for the sake of vainglory. For how were they
praised by the others when they took raw bits of flesh? And yet you yourself
are not a praiser. At any rate, imitating the cloak and the hair of such a
man, as the paintings of the men, do you then think that what you yourself do
not even consider worthy of admiration is in good repute with the crowd? And
one or two praised then, but more than ten myriads from the nausea and disgust
had their stomachs turned and became without appetite, until the servants
recovered them with scents and ointments and cakes. Thus the famous hero
struck them with a deed ridiculous to such men, /such as mortals now are,/ but
not ignoble, by the gods, if one should explain it according to the
understanding of Diogenes. For what Socrates says concerning himself, that
thinking he was performing a service to the god in examining in every way the
oracle given concerning him he embraced the elenctic life, this Diogenes also,
I think, being conscious to himself that the philosophy was given by the
Pythian, thought he must test all things by deeds and not be affected toward
the opinions of others, perhaps true, perhaps false. Therefore neither if
Pythagoras said something, nor if some other like Pythagoras, did he seem
trustworthy to Diogenes. For he had made the god, and no man, the founder of
the philosophy. What then, you will say, has this to do with the eating of the
octopus? I will tell you.

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Some suppose eating flesh to be according to nature for men, and others think
it least fitting for a man to do this, and much argument has been spent
concerning this. If then you should not wish to be idle, swarms of books
concerning such a thing will appear. These Diogenes thought he must test. He
thought then thus: if someone eating flesh without fuss, as I think each of
the other beasts to which nature assigned this, should do it harmlessly and
without burden, or rather even with benefit to the body, he supposed eating
flesh to be altogether according to nature; but if some harm should come from
this, he perhaps thought this not the work of man, but that one must abstain
from it with all force. One such argument concerning the matter would perhaps
be rather forced, but another more proper to Cynicism, if I should first go
through still more clearly concerning its end.

For they make impassivity the end; and this is equal to becoming a god.
Sensing then perhaps himself Diogenes as impassive in all other things, but
disturbed only by such food and nauseated and enslaved to an empty opinion
rather than to reason; for they are flesh no less, even if one should boil
them ten thousand times, even if one should sauce them with ten thousand
dressings; and he thought he must take this from himself and make himself
altogether free of the cowardice. For this at any rate, know well, is
cowardice. Since, by the Thesmophoros, if we touch boiled flesh, for what
reason do we not also take it simply, tell us. For you have nothing else to
say than that it has been so customary and we have so been habituated. For it
is not that before being boiled it is by nature disgusting, but having been
boiled it has become purer than itself. What then must he do who was appointed
by the god as by a general to strike out all the currency, and to judge things
by reason and truth? Overlook himself being troubled by this opinion, so as to
think that meat boiled is pure and edible, but not worked by fire somehow
abominable and disgusting? Are you so mindful? Are you so serious? You who so
much reproach the vainglorious, as you would say, Diogenes, but according to
me the most serious servant and minister of the Pythian, for the eating of the
octopus, have eaten down countless salt-fish, /fish and birds and whatever
came to his dear hands,/ being an Egyptian, not of the priests, but of the
omnivorous, for whom it is law to eat all things as the herbs of the field;
you recognize, I think, the words of the Galileans. It almost escaped me to
say that all men living near the sea, and already some even of those far off,
gulp down sea-urchins without even heating them, oysters and simply all such
things; then you suppose those men worthy of emulation, but you think Diogenes
wretched and disgusting, and you do not consider that these are flesh no more
than those; except perhaps these differ from those in that some are soft,
others harder. At any rate the octopus also is bloodless like those, and the
shellfish are living beings just as this one is; at any rate it feels pleasure
and pain, which is most proper to living beings. And let not the Platonic
opinion now trouble us, which supposes even plants to be living. But that the
noble Diogenes did nothing unreasonable nor unlawful nor unaccustomed to you,
unless one should examine such things by the harder and the softer, by
pleasure of the throat and by disgust, is clear, I think, to those able in any
way to follow reason. You do not then loathe eating raw, you who do similar
things, not upon bloodless animals only, but also upon those that have blood.
And in this perhaps you differ from that man, that he thought these things
must be taken simply and according to nature, but you, seasoning with salt and
many other things for the sake of pleasure, that you may force nature. And
this much is enough on this.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

The aim and end of the Cynic philosophy, as indeed of all philosophy, is to be
happy, and to be happy is in living according to nature, and not toward the
opinions of the many. Since it happens also to plants to fare well, and indeed
to all animals, whenever each attains unhindered the end according to nature;
but also among the gods this is the definition of happiness, that they have
themselves as they are by nature and are their own. Therefore also for men it
is not fitting to busy oneself searching for happiness hidden somewhere else;
nor does an eagle nor a plane-tree nor any other of the existing animals or
plants busy itself about golden wings and leaves, nor how it will have silver
shoots or iron, or rather adamantine, spurs and stings, but if those things
with which nature adorned them from the beginning should be added robust and
serving them toward speed or toward strength, they would most think they fare
well and flourish. How then is it not ridiculous, if someone born a man should
busy himself about happiness somewhere outside, thinking wealth and birth and
power of friends and simply all such things worth everything? If then nature
had given us, as to the animals, this only, to have bodies and souls similar
to theirs, so as to busy ourselves no further, it would then suffice, as the
other animals, to be content with bodily advantages, busying ourselves about
being happy there. But since a soul in no way similar to the other animals has
been sown in us, but either differing in essence or indifferent in essence but
better in activity alone, as I think already-pure gold than that mixed with
sand; for this account also is said concerning the soul as true by some; we
then, since we are conscious to ourselves of being more intelligent than the
animals; for according to the myth of Protagoras nature as a mother dealt with
those too ambitiously and generously, but to us in place of all things mind
was given from Zeus; happiness must be set here, in the strongest and most
serious of the things in us.

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

Consider then whether Diogenes was not most of this choice, who freely gave
his body to toils, that he might make it more robust than nature, and thought
he must do only whatever should appear to reason to be done, and the tumults
falling from the body, such as this surrounding thing often forces us to busy
ourselves about for its sake, he did not admit even in part. And by this
training the man had so brave a body as none, I think, of those who contended
for the crowns, and was so disposed in soul as to be happy, so as to be king
no less, if not even more, as the Greeks then used to say of the Great King,
meaning the Persian. Do a man /cityless, homeless, deprived of fatherland,
having neither obol nor drachma nor even a servant,/ appear small to you, and
not even barley-cake, of which Epicurus, having plenty, says he is not
inferior even to the gods in the account of happiness, not competing with the
gods, but living more happily than the one seeming to men most happy, and he
said he lived more happily. But if you disbelieve, having tried that life in
deed and not in word you will perceive.

Come then, first let us test him through arguments.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

Does freedom seem to you to lead all goods for men, these much-talked-of ones?
For how will you not say so? Since money and wealth and birth and strength of
body and beauty and simply all such things without freedom are not goods of
the one seeming to have been fortunate, but of the one who has acquired him?
Whom then do we suppose the slave? Is it perhaps that man whom we would buy
for so many drachmas of silver or two minae or ten staters of gold? You will
say, I suppose, that this man is truly a slave. Is it for this very reason,
that we have paid down the silver for him to the seller? Thus those also of
the captives whom we ransom would be servants. And yet the laws also have
given these their freedom when they have been saved home, and we ransom them
not that they may be slaves, but that they may be free. You see that it is not
enough to pay down silver so as to declare the ransomed man a slave, but that
man is truly a slave of whom another is master to force him to do whatever he
commands, and to punish him when he does not wish, and, as the poet says, /to
bring him near to evil pains/? See then next whether all are not our masters
whom it is necessary for us to serve, so that we may not suffer nor be grieved
being punished by them. Or do you think this only punishment, if someone
stretching out the staff should strike the servant? And yet not even the
harshest of masters do such a thing upon all the servants, but often a word is
enough, and a threat. Never then, O friend, think yourself to be free, so long
as the belly rules you and the things below the belly, and those who are
masters of providing the things toward pleasure and of preventing the same,
and even if you should become stronger than these, so long as you are a slave
to the opinions of the many, you have not yet touched freedom nor tasted the
nectar, /No, by him who gave the tetractys in my breast./ And I do not say
this, that one must be shameless toward all and do what must not be done; but
of the things we abstain from and whatever we do, let us not do and abstain
because they seem to the many somehow serious or mean, but because to reason
and to the god in us, that is to mind, these things are forbidden. Nothing
then prevents the many from following the common opinions; for this is better
than being altogether shameless; for men have by nature a kinship toward
truth; but for a man already living according to mind and able both to find
and to judge the right accounts, it is fitting to follow in no way at all the
things thought by the many to be done well and worse.

Therefore since there is something of our soul more divine, which we call mind
and prudence and the silent speech, whose herald is this speech through the
voice going forth from names and verbs, and something else is yoked to this,
various and of all sorts, something mixed with anger and desire and a
many-headed beast, one must not look fixedly and unswervingly toward the
opinions of the many, before we tame this beast and persuade it to obey the
god with us, or rather the divine. For many emulators of Diogenes, leaving
this aside, became all-grabbers and polluted and not better than even one of
the beasts, and that this is not my argument, I will tell you first a deed of
Diogenes, at which the many will laugh, but to me it seems most august. For
when one of the young men in a crowd, Diogenes also being present, broke wind,
that man struck him with his staff saying: then, O you filthy thing, having
done nothing worthy of daring such things in public, do you begin from there
to despise opinion for us? Thus he thought one must first become stronger than
pleasure and spirit, before coming to the most complete of the
wrestling-bouts, stripping against the opinions of the many which become
causes of countless evils for the many.

Do you not know how they lead the young away from philosophy, babbling one
thing after another of the philosophers? The genuine chorus-members of
Pythagoras and Plato and Aristotle are said to be wizards and sophists and
puffed-up and sorcerers. If anywhere one of the Cynics has become serious, he
seems pitiable; I remember at any rate a nurse once saying to me, when she saw
my companion Iphicles having squalid hair and his chest all torn and a cloak
altogether mean in a terrible winter: what daemon then has turned this man
into this misfortune, by which he himself is pitiable, and more pitiable his
fathers, who reared him with care and educated him as seriously as possible,
and he now goes about thus, having left all, no better than the beggars? Of
that woman then I do not know how I then dissembled; but know well that the
many think these things also concerning the true dogs. And this is not the
terrible thing, but you see that they also persuade men to love wealth and
hate poverty and serve the belly and endure every toil for the sake of the
body and fatten the bond of the soul and set a costly table and never sleep
alone at night, but do all such things hidden in the dark? Is this not worse
than Tartarus? Is it not better to go down under Charybdis and Cocytus and ten
thousand fathoms under the earth, than to fall into such a life enslaved to
the private parts and the belly, and not even to these simply as the beasts,
but to have trouble, so that we may also escape notice working these things
under the dark? And yet how much better to abstain from them altogether? And
if it is not easy, the laws of Diogenes and Crates concerning these things are
not to be dishonored; hunger looses love, and if you cannot use this, a noose.
Do you not know that those men did these things giving to life a road of
frugality? For tyrants, says Diogenes, do not come from the
barley-cake-eaters, but from those who dine expensively. And Crates indeed has
made a hymn to Frugality: /Hail, goddess mistress, delight of wise men,
Frugality, offspring of famous Temperance./

Let the dog then not according to Oenomaus be shameless nor without shame nor
a despiser of all things together, divine and human, but reverent in the
things toward the divine, as Diogenes; at any rate that man was persuaded by
the Pythian, and he did not repent of having been persuaded; but if, because
he did not go to nor serve the temples nor the images nor the altars, someone
thinks it a sign of atheism, he does not think rightly; for he had none of
such things, no frankincense, no libation, no silver with which to buy them.
But if he thought rightly concerning gods, this alone was enough; for he
served them with the soul itself, giving, I think, the most honorable of his
own things, the consecrating of his own soul through his thoughts. And let him
in no way become shameless, but following reason let him first make the
passionate portion of the soul tame to himself, so as to take it out
altogether and not even know that he is master of the pleasures. For it is
better to come to this, to being altogether ignorant even if one suffers such
things; and this comes to us not otherwise than through the exercises. And so
that no one may suppose me to say these things idly, I will write out for you
a few things from the play of Crates: /Glorious children of Memory and
Olympian Zeus, Pierian Muses, hear me as I pray; Give always continually
fodder to my belly, which has always set for me a simple life without slavery.
Make me useful to friends, not sweet. And I do not wish to gather famous
money, seeking the wealth of a dung-beetle and the substance of an ant, But to
have a share of justice and to gather wealth easy to bear, easy to get,
honorable toward virtue. And having obtained these I will propitiate Hermes
and the holy Muses, Not with luxurious expenses, but with holy virtues./ If I
must write to you concerning these things, I have more of the man. And if you
meet with Plutarch of Chaeronea, who wrote the life of Crates, you will need
to learn nothing of the man as a side-work.

But let us return again to that, that he who is beginning to play the Cynic
must first rebuke himself bitterly and test and not flatter, but examine
himself as accurately as possible, whether he delights in the costliness of
foods, whether he needs a soft bed, whether he is weaker than honor or glory,
whether he emulates being looked at and, even if it should be empty, thinks it
honorable nonetheless. And let him not give way to going about with crowds,
and let him not taste luxury even, they say, with the tip of the finger, until
he has altogether trampled it. Then already, if such things fall in his way,
nothing prevents him from touching them. Since I hear also of the weaker bulls
that they withdraw from the herd and feeding by themselves gather their
strength in part and little by little, then thus they come on and challenge
and contend for the herd with those who hold it before, as being more worthy
to stand at the head. Whoever then wishes to play the Cynic, let him not love
only the cloak nor the wallet nor the staff and the hair, that he may walk as
in a village lacking barbers and schools, unshorn and unlettered, but let him
take reason in place of the scepter and the purpose in place of the wallet as
the marks of the Cynic philosophy. And he must use frankness first when he has
shown how much he is worth by nature, as I think Crates and Diogenes, who from
every threat of fortune, whether one must call it play or drunken violence,
were so far from bearing it ill, that Diogenes when captured by the pirates
used to play, and Crates made his property public, then being harmed in body
he used to mock himself for the lameness of his leg and the hump of his
shoulders, and he used to go to the hearths of friends uninvited and invited,
reconciling the closest to one another, if ever he perceived them at variance,
and he used to rebuke not with bitterness but with grace, not that he might
seem to slander those being corrected, but wishing to benefit both those men
themselves and those hearing.

And this was not their leading end; but, as I said, they looked to how they
themselves would be happy, and they cared for the others so much as they
understood, I think, man to be by nature a social and political animal, and
they benefited those living with them in the city not by examples only but
also by words. Whoever then should wish to be a Cynic and a serious man,
having first cared for himself, as Diogenes and Crates let him drive out of
the soul all the passions from every part, and having entrusted his own
affairs to right reason let him be steered by mind. For this, as I think, was
the chief point of the philosophy of Diogenes.

And if the man ever went to a courtesan; and yet this perhaps happened once or
not even once; when for us in the other things he has become serious according
to Diogenes, if it should appear good to him to do even some such thing openly
in the eyes of all, we will not blame nor accuse. First however having shown
us the aptness of Diogenes and the quickness of mind and the freedom in all
other things, self-sufficiency, justice, temperance, reverence, grace,
attention, so as to do nothing at random nor in vain nor without reason; since
these too are proper to the philosophy of Diogenes; let him trample vanity,
let him mock those who hide the necessary works of nature in the dark; I mean
the excretions of the residues; but in the middle of the marketplaces and the
cities practice the most violent things and nothing proper to our nature,
seizures of money, slanders, unjust indictments, prosecutions of other such
rabble-affairs. Since Diogenes also, whether he broke wind or eased himself or
did some other such thing, as they say, in the marketplace, was trampling
their vanity, teaching them that they practice things much meaner and harder
than these. For the former are according to nature for us all, but the latter,
so to speak, for no one, and all are practiced from distortion.

But the present emulators of Diogenes, having chosen the easiest and lightest,
did not see the better; and you, wishing to be more august than those, have
wandered so far from the choice of Diogenes that you thought him pitiable.

But if you disbelieved these things said concerning a man whom all the Greeks
then admired after Socrates and Pythagoras in the time of Plato and Aristotle,
of whom the guide of the most temperate and most intelligent Zeno became a
hearer, all of whom it is not likely were deceived concerning a man so mean as
you mock, O best of men, perhaps you would have considered something more
concerning him and gone further in experience of the man. For whom of the
Greeks did the endurance of Diogenes not strike, being not outside of royal
greatness of soul, and his love of toil? The man used to sleep upon a pallet
in the jar better than the Great King under the gilded roofs on the soft bed,
he used to eat the barley-cake more pleasantly than you now eat the Sicilian
tables, he used to bathe his body in cold water, drying it in the air instead
of the linens with which you wipe yourself, most philosophical man. It well
becomes you to mock that man, because you have overthrown Xerxes, as
Themistocles, or Darius, as Alexander the Macedonian. If you had unrolled the
books a little and studied as we political and busy men, you would have
learned how Alexander is said to have admired the greatness of soul of
Diogenes. But none of these things is serious to you, as it seems to me; how
should it be? Far from it; competing you have admired the life of wretched
women.

If then the speech has done something more, the gain is not mine more than
yours; but if we accomplish nothing at once concerning such things, having
strung them together without taking breath, as the saying is; for it is a
two-day side-work, as the Muses know, or rather you yourself also; let remain
for you whatever you knew before, and we shall not repent of the good speech
toward the man.
