* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Dion, son of Hipparinus, a Syracusan, born of a noble family, involved in
both tyrannies of the Dionysii. For the elder had Aristomache, sister of
Dion, in marriage, from whom he begot two sons, Hipparinus and Nisaeus, and
as many daughters, named Sophrosyne and Arete, of whom he gave the former
in marriage to his son Dionysius, the same to whom he left the kingdom, the
latter, Arete, to Dion. Dion, however, besides a generous kinship and the
noble fame of his ancestors, had many other goods from nature, among them a
teachable, courteous talent, fitted for the best arts, great dignity of
body, which commends not least, and besides great riches left by his
father, which he himself had increased by the gifts of the tyrant. He was
intimate with the elder Dionysius, and not less because of his character
than because of kinship. For although the cruelty of Dionysius displeased
him, yet he was eager that he be safe because of the relationship, and
still more for the sake of his own people. He was present in great affairs,
and the tyrant was much moved by his counsel, except in a matter where his
own greater desire had intervened. All the more illustrious embassies
indeed were administered through Dion: which he, by diligently attending
and faithfully administering, softened the most cruel name of tyrant by his
own humanity. Him, sent by Dionysius, the Carthaginians so regarded that
they never admired anyone speaking the Greek tongue more.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
Nor indeed did these things escape Dionysius: for he felt how great an
ornament he was to himself. Whence it came about that he indulged this one
man most and loved him no otherwise than a son: who indeed, when the report
had been brought into Sicily that Plato had come to Tarentum, could not
refuse the young man that he should send for him, since Dion burned with
desire of hearing him. He therefore granted him this leave and brought him
to Syracuse with great pomp. Whom Dion so admired and loved that he gave
himself wholly to him. Nor indeed was Plato himself less delighted with
Dion. And so, although he had been cruelly outraged by the tyrant, inasmuch
as he had ordered him to be sold, yet he returned to the same place, led by
the prayers of the same Dion. Meanwhile Dionysius fell into a sickness.
When he was more heavily afflicted by it, Dion asked of the physicians how
he was, and at the same time asked of them that, if perhaps he were in
greater danger, they confess it to him: for he wished to speak with him
about dividing the kingdom, because he thought the sons of his sister born
of that man ought to have a part of the kingdom. The physicians did not
keep this silent and reported the talk to the son Dionysius. Moved by this,
lest Dion have power of acting, he compelled the physicians to give a
sleeping draught to the father. The sick man, having taken this, was put to
sleep and died his last day.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
Such was the beginning of the quarrel of Dion and Dionysius, and it was
increased by many things. Yet in the first times a simulated friendship
remained between them for some while. As when Dion did not cease to entreat
Dionysius to send for Plato from Athens and use his counsels, he, who
wished in some matter to imitate his father, complied with him. And at the
same time he brought back Philistus the historian to Syracuse, a man no
more a friend to the tyrant than to tyranny. But about this man more things
have been set forth in that book which was written concerning the Greek
historians. But Plato had so much authority with Dionysius and so prevailed
by eloquence that he persuaded him to make an end of the tyranny and
restore liberty to the Syracusans. Deterred from this will by the counsel
of Philistus, he began to be somewhat more cruel.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
Who indeed, when he saw himself surpassed by Dion in talent, authority,
love of the people, fearing that, if he kept him with himself, he would
give some occasion of his own overthrow, gave him a trireme, by which he
might be carried to Corinth, showing that he did this for the sake of both,
lest, when they feared one another, either should forestall the other. When
many were indignant at this deed and it was of great odium to the tyrant,
Dionysius put all of Dion's goods that could be moved onto ships and sent
them to him. For thus he wished it to be thought, that he had done this not
from hatred of the man, but for the sake of his own safety. But after he
heard that he was gathering a force in the Peloponnese and trying to make
war on him, he gave Arete, Dion's wife, in marriage to another, and ordered
his son to be so brought up that by indulgence he might be steeped in the
most shameful desires. For before the boy was of age, harlots were brought
to him, he was overwhelmed with wine and feasts, and no time was left him
sober. He so could not bear the changed state of life, after his father
returned to the fatherland (for guardians had been set who should lead him
from the former way of living), that he threw himself from the upper part
of the house and so perished. But I return to that point.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
After Dion came to Corinth and Heraclides, who had been prefect of the
horse, expelled by the same Dionysius, fled to the same place, they began
to prepare war by every means. But they did not accomplish much, because a
tyranny of many years was thought of great resources: for which reason few
were brought to a partnership of the danger. But Dion, relying not so much
on his own forces as on hatred of the tyrant, with greatest spirit, having
set out with two merchant ships to attack a rule of fifty years, fortified
with five hundred long ships, ten thousand horse and a hundred thousand
foot, which seemed admirable to all nations, so easily overthrew it that on
the third day after he had touched Sicily he entered Syracuse. From which
it can be understood that no empire is safe unless fortified by goodwill.
At that time Dionysius was absent and in Italy was awaiting the fleet of
his adversaries, thinking that no one would come against him without great
forces. Which thing deceived him. For Dion, by those very men who had been
under the power of his adversary, repressed the royal spirits and obtained
that whole part of Sicily which had been under the power of Dionysius, and
in like manner of the city of Syracuse except the citadel and the island
joined to the town, and he brought the matter to this, that the tyrant
wished to make peace on such terms: that Dion should hold Sicily, Dionysius
Italy, Syracuse Apollocrates, in whom alone he had the greatest trust. Dion

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
These so prosperous and so unexpected affairs were followed by a sudden
change, because fortune by her own mobility began to sink him whom a little
before she had raised. First she exercised her force on the son, of whom I
have mentioned above. For when he had brought back his wife, who had been
given to another, and wished to recall his son to virtue from ruined
luxury, the father received a most heavy wound by the death of his son.
Then a dissension arose between him and Heraclides, who, because he did not
yield him the first place, gathered a faction. Nor was he less strong among
the optimates, by whose consent he was set over the fleet, while Dion held
the army of foot. Dion did not bear this with an even mind, and recited
that verse of Homer from the second rhapsody, in which this is the sense:
that the republic cannot be well managed under the commands of many. Which
saying was followed by great odium: for he seemed to have disclosed that he
wished all things to be in his own power. This he strove not to soften by
compliance, but to crush by harshness, and when Heraclides had then come to
Syracuse, he caused him to be killed.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:
Which deed cast the greatest fear upon all: for no one, after that man had
been killed, thought himself safe. He however, with the adversary removed,
more freely distributed to the soldiers the goods of those whom he knew to
have felt against him. Which having been divided, when the daily expenses
became very great, money quickly began to fail, nor was there anything at
which he might stretch his hand except the possessions of friends. That was
of such a kind that, when he had reconciled the soldiers, he would lose the
optimates. He was distressed by care of these things, and unaccustomed to
being spoken of badly he did not bear with an even mind that he was thought
ill of by those by whose praises a little before he had been lifted to
heaven. But the crowd, with the soldiers' will offended against him, spoke
more freely and kept saying that the tyrant was not to be borne.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:
He, looking at these things, when he did not know how to settle them and
feared where they would issue, a certain Callicrates, an Athenian citizen,
who had come with him from the Peloponnese into Sicily, a man both crafty
and sharp for fraud, without any religion or faith, went to Dion and said:
that he was in great danger because of the offense of the people and the
hatred of the soldiers, which he could in no way avoid unless he gave a
charge to someone of his own, who should pretend to be an enemy to him. If
he found a suitable man, he would easily learn the minds of all and take
off his adversaries, because his enemies would open their feelings to one
at variance with him. This counsel having been approved, Callicrates
himself took these parts and armed himself with the imprudence of Dion. He
seeks companions for killing him, meets his adversaries, confirms them by a
conspiracy. The matter, which was being done with many aware, was brought
out and reported to Aristomache, sister of Dion, and his wife Arete. They,
terrified with fear, go to him whose danger they feared. But he denies that
plots are being made against him by Callicrates, and says that those things
which were being done were done by his own command. The women nonetheless
lead Callicrates into the temple of Proserpina and compel him to swear that
there would be no danger from him to Dion. He by this religious bond was
not only not deterred, but was stirred to hasten, fearing that his plan
would be disclosed before he had accomplished what he had thought.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:
With this mind, on the next feast day, when Dion, having withdrawn from the
assembly, kept himself at home and had lain down in an upper chamber, he
hands over the more fortified places of the town to those aware of the
crime, hedges the house with guards, sets certain men at the doors who
should not leave, equips a trireme with armed men and hands it to
Philostratus, his own brother, and orders him to ply it in the harbor, as
if he wished to exercise the rowers, thinking that if perhaps fortune had
opposed his plans he might have a means by which to flee to safety. From
the number of his own men he chooses certain Zacynthian young men both most
daring and of greatest strength, and gives them the charge that they go to
Dion unarmed, so that they might seem to come for the sake of meeting him.
They, because they were known, were let in. But as they had entered his
threshold, the doors having been bolted, they attack him lying on the bed
and bind him: a noise is made, so that it could be heard outside. Here, as
has often been said before, how hateful singular power is, and how pitiable
the life of those who prefer to be feared rather than loved, was easy for
anyone to understand. For those very guards, if their will had been ready,
could have broken the doors and saved him, so long as those unarmed men,
calling for a weapon from outside, held him alive. When no one came to his
aid, a certain Lyco, a Syracusan, gave a sword through the window, by which
Dion was killed.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:
The slaughter having been finished, when a crowd had entered for the sake
of seeing, some are cut down by the ignorant as if they were the guilty.
For a swift rumor having been spread that violence had been done to Dion,
many had run together to whom such a crime was displeasing. These, led by a
false suspicion, kill the innocent as if they were criminals. When his
death was made public, the will of the crowd was wonderfully changed. For
those who while he lived had called him tyrant, the same proclaimed him the
liberator of the fatherland and the expeller of the tyrant. So suddenly had
pity succeeded hatred that they desired, if they could, to redeem him from
Acheron with their own blood. And so in the most frequented place in the
city, carried out at public cost, he was given a monument of a tomb. He
died about fifty years old, in the fourth year after he had returned from
the Peloponnese into Sicily.
