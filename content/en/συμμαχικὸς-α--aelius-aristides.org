No one would wonder at Philip, men of Thebes, doing his usual things; for he
has mastered far more by deceiving than by arms, which is also what he now
hopes against you as he is present. And the people of the Athenians, keeping
the post customary and ancestral to itself, has sent us here, if you do not
know, to teach you in what situation you are, and if you do know, to offer the
aid and by Zeus to show it together with the words. And it is yours not to
become worse than fortune, nor to help establish as valid whatever the moment
holds against you, and later to seek those things that stand as a
counterweight, once you have let them go. One thing, then, Philip has done
well in admitting: that these many and wonderful things which the ambassadors
just now went through were done for his own sake, not for yours; for otherwise
he would not have stopped here, nor would he be asking for what he now asks.
And if you are going to give equal for equal, we shall not resent it, but let
what seems right to him be just for you as well; but if the favor is to be a
slave, I think I understand neither of the two, neither what such thing he has
done well for you, nor what so great a thing you blame in yourselves, that you
would of your own will give so great a penalty. And consider at once from this
very thing the difference of each embassy. For Philip has sent, claiming that
you should help him, but we have come ourselves, willing to help you, if you
receive us. And that we are doing nothing new nor outside of our customs,
recall one thing known to all. For when you first got free of the
Lacedaemonians after that long friendship and intimacy, which you did not
dissolve, but they did,

having formed the same intention about you as Philip has, then of all the
Greeks no one, I mean neither those who inhabit Europe nor those who inhabit
Asia, stood with you except us here; so far has the present gone beyond that.
For then, when you came to us, we listened; but now we have come of our own
accord, having taken the post of counselors from you, or if you will, having
taken it in advance. For so great does the surplus of fears seem to us for all
Greece that we count it a thing to be content with, if even after extra
pleading someone will be able to persuade those who remain to be willing to be
saved. And we do not recognize these things now for the first time, but even
at the barbarian's expedition of old we roused all whom we were able, and we
were not ashamed to plead for the common advantage.

Toward those, then, who think the same as we do, a short speech is enough, and
perhaps this much: that we have left the cavalry and the hoplites behind. But
toward those who are not yet considering anything of what is to come, but are
still of the deceit and of the hopes, I say this one thing first, that I even
consider the speeches themselves a harm to the affairs, unless someone reduces
them to the least possible; then I shall go through whatever the moment
already allows. If Philip were being judged among you first, it would have
been a piece of good fortune for the other Greeks; but this perhaps held
danger for you, who had not yet seen in advance nor seen from outside his
manner. But as it is, let the others' stumbles become for you a part of good
fortune, and setting aside the speeches from both sides, trust his deeds.
Would that such examples from one another did not exist for the Greeks, but
that all inhabited their cities in quiet with prosperity. But not even to
think it right to have been educated from what we have seen would be a
complete disaster. There was a time when there were Olynthians. How did Philip
treat them? I think more kindly than they even needed. And who could tell it
all? But they held Anthemus of his territory, which they had not even been
able to set foot on before. And so much more

account did he make of friendship toward them than of enmity toward us that he
robbed us of Potidaea and gave it to them, and made the danger his own, but
the prize theirs. What then did these things profit the Olynthians? Or did
they bear some fair fruits? But the man who before gave what no one asked is
the one who later left them nothing, but instead of then giving Potidaea, not
handing them over to others, but destroying them utterly, and instead of
stopping his greed at Olynthus, taking thirty-two cities besides and making a
Scythian desolation for the Greeks in Thrace. And yet, by the gods, how much
do you think the Olynthians would have paid, when they were being destroyed
and suffering those many and terrible things, to have been able to know these
things for themselves earlier and to have deliberated better about their
affairs? I think they would have paid everything. This, then, as it seems, was
not granted to them, but they knew and had perished at the same time. But you,
while you are still masters, use the example and do not wish yourselves to
become for others an example of the like. But whenever Tilphossaeum and the
Phocians' land appear to you some marvelous gain, then set against them your
own city and land and temples and tombs and customs and reputation and all
such things as one might name, and judge, and ask yourselves whether they are
not worth more; of all of which, if you look toward those things, it is laid
down that you be deprived. For it is strange not even to wish to approach
first those who lend, let alone to be grateful, if they do this at a high
rate, and yet not to think that those who grant favors with such an end and
demand so much in return ought to be owed hatred and distrust from all. And
consider, likening your own things to those of the Olynthians, except for
fortune. For do not think that if the names of the places are different, the
facts at least differ. Did he give them gifts as he has you, or did he not?
And many. And did it profit them in the end, or did it not?

Come then, consider this too: how in turn the Olynthians stood toward us in
those times. As Philip would most have prayed, and I will add, as he also
arranged: they distrusted,

they hated, they would not even endure the name. What then? When they were in
danger against their benefactor and an Iliad of evils had surrounded them, who
came to them for safety? No one either before us or after us. But here too the
difference between our nobility and his greed was shown most beautifully. For
we who had been judged by them in the rank of enemies, when they needed true
friends, were present first and alone, while he, giving all less than he
wished, this man here, had seized and held everything: them, the arms, the
city, the land; and through Philip at least they have been made desolate, but
through our virtue those who escaped are saved at Athens. Are we not better as
enemies than Philip as a friend, and ought they not to owe more gratitude to
us for our enmity than to Philip for his benefits? And indeed one cannot even
say this, that you will display some such kindness to Philip, for which even
contrary to the nature that is his he would spare you. For neither will those
outside marvel at your worth, nor would you surpass the Amphipolitans and the
Pydnaeans, of whom some sacrificed to him as to a god, and the others had a
temple of his father to point to. But nevertheless he drove the one out when
he was sated with them, and he slaughtered the others in the temple of his
father, still swearing, and the slaughter overtook the libations. And yet do
not think that he is so harsh and envious toward those outside the Gates and
those dwelling in Thrace, who have always lived considering whose they must
be, but that he makes no account of you who are counted among us Greeks, and
of those whom the former rulers of the Greeks could not endure a single blow,
but would gladly see you faring better than is advantageous to him; you are
not conscious of anything so humble in yourselves that you need either not
fear Philip, or not believe that this is in him toward you, but I think he has
judged you enemies from the same things from which he judged us. For he knows
that we would in no way yield to being in the rank of slaves, and taking you
to be other such enemies

he reasonably considers you. If then he is not even coming against us, but is
lying in this part, believe him in the other, that he will do nothing contrary
to you at least; but if it is not reasonable to distrust what he has admitted,
by the same things he is also convicted concerning what he denies. So that it

not think it right to be slaves, you will make war. And indeed if having been
kindly once prevents the plots, he has no war against us even now, since he
once released captives to us as well and made treaties of friendship; but if
greed is a terrible thing, what need is there to mention the Phocians? For he
did not destroy the Phocians for your advantage, no, but for a starting-point
for his own affairs, nor in order to make you stronger, but in order to make
the Greeks weaker in common, unless he will also say that he has destroyed
thirty-two cities in Thrace for your sake, and that he holds all Thessaly,
occupying it with garrisons and tetrarchies, so that you may keep your
constitution exactly, and that he attacked Megara for your sake, and is
snatching Euboea because of you, and is intriguing in the Peloponnese and
making slaughters, so that you may be in the least danger, and is harassing
Paeonians and Triballians and those beyond the north, and causing trouble for
all mankind, so that the city of the Thebans may flourish and nothing fearful
from abroad may ever come upon it, and now he is coming against the Athenians
for your sake; very much so, as is shown from the rest.

Why then, wishing what, does he not also admit what is against you, but
conceals it? Especially, men of Thebes, Philip has removed the heralds from
wars and uses them only as far as the theater. For he thinks, I suppose, that
it is much nonsense not to announce beforehand in the athletic contests the
holds, nor what one must guard against, but in the preparation of war and the
assembling of affairs to send ahead the preventer, as if fearing for the
enemy. Therefore he neither announced to the Olynthians what he wished, before
he was already laying hold of their very bodies like some disease, and toward
the Phocians

as he came he sent ahead the reputation of coming as a friend to them. If
then, I think, even now he were able to approach you in silence, he would have
valued this most highly, but since it was necessary that he become visible as
he marched through Boeotia, he has yielded in the things of necessity, but in
the rest he uses his nature, and he says he is coming against us, not in order
to declare the war against us, but in order to conceal the one against you,
thinking it makes no small difference to contend against both, or against the
one side. And if this too should be added for him, that those with whom he
ought to have been in danger while they were with us, he should come against
us together with these, it is a marvelous find. For these reasons he has
admitted that, but denies this. And besides he supposed that if he engaged you
openly by the law of war, we would come and prevent it, but if he separated
the cities, he would have you before you raised a hand, and would array
himself against us alone. And he is not doing this now for the first time, but
from all time and through all affairs, so that the cities may never come to be
in the same place; since even when the affairs concerning the Phocians were
then going on into time, knowing in advance that you would use us as allies as
you were accustomed, he knocked both of us out, putting peace in front of us,
and receiving you as friends. Then, then, we were both led on in an
old-fashioned and poor way, but now let us appear not worse than he at
calculating. But nevertheless he demands gratitude from you for his own greed,
himself having destroyed all the gratitude. For a man who, in return for the
little he then gave, has now come to take everything simply from you, what
gratitude has he left himself among you? Just as if he should also try to
demand gratitude from the Phocians for what they have suffered. For you ought
not to think that because he destroyed the Phocians as well, he is
well-disposed toward you for that reason, but that he destroyed the Phocians
as well against you, and now he wishes to use you as he then used them; and
you ought to be on your guard, and not because of those things to concede
these as well, but from these present things to hate those too.

But indeed, as to the charges and the slanders which the ambassadors have used
against us, consider that first they fall upon themselves, if they speak the
truth. For if we have something to accuse of one another, this would be a
difference of ours toward one another, but not of one who has no share in the
complaints. So that it was not fitting that he send someone to you to provoke
you, but that you, if you thought you must remember old wrongs, call both him
and all whom you could against us. But when you yourselves know how to be
moderate concerning yourselves, how will you attend to him when he is stirring
things up? Unless someone should say this, that on behalf of yourselves you
will bear it mildly, but on his behalf you will remember old wrongs, and this
in things in which you, not he, were insulted, if one must say any of these
things. Then consider this too, lest you appear at once both angry and having
taken the penalty from yourselves, and having become so much worse than we
concerning yourselves, that we have come ready to help save the affairs with
you, while you: what would one say that is blasphemous? For if it is because
of all those things you have ever blamed, and because of which they say you
must be indignant, that Philip is coming on, let us grant these men something
to say; but if it is from his not being able to restrain himself, let Python
show, if you have even condemned something so great of yourselves, that in
remembering old wrongs against yourselves you would think you were
deliberating rightly. And apart from these things I think all would agree that
for us, being Greeks and of the same race, to contend with one another over
rule and rank is without envy and among the customary things, but that one
wholly alien ought to be admitted to no anger and no strife, but in the same
way as if two brothers were disputing the first place among themselves, they
would at least have known this one same thing, if some stranger and one
belonging in no way were forcing his way, to shut him out with common doors,
so in Greek affairs none of those from outside should have a hold, our own
secrets among ourselves (for so it is also worth speaking of them); but to
think that the wars of the Greeks against one another and the complaints

resemble factions, and that one must especially, if possible, look to it that

use this if you wish; but if the moment is stronger than such smallness of

nothing else, one would have this common thing to blame. But Philip nowhere
leaves a man who will say what he has suffered at his hands; so badly does he
practice to defend himself about these things.

And indeed if in the very moments of the complaints you were going to be
ungrateful in something toward us, perhaps there would be some pardon; but as
it is, consider what they are teaching you, how they are neither just nor
advantageous to you. For if, when we were in enmity with one another, you
restrained yourselves, but when in deed everything has been dissolved by us
and we are present wishing to campaign together, at that time you will be
angry, look lest you do the opposite of what you wish. For you will not seem
to remember old wrongs against those who do wrong, but to be angry at those
who have chosen to do well. And indeed you would thus treat both contrary to
what is needed, [and for you in both ways contrary to what is needed],
preferring the one who has come against you to those on your behalf, and
treating us in turn more moderately as enemies than as friends. And strangest
of all, that the complaints are common from both sides, but the being angry is
among you alone, and this when the army is not at the girdle nor on the
borders of Attica and Boeotia, but on the borders of Phocis and your land, in
the very forecourts of Thebes, so that I shudder now for the gates. Why then,
when the affair is yours, have we come in alarm? Especially, I think, some
good fortune for both cities is general, and then it was most shameful in deed

Not only, then, do most things exist for us city toward city in relation to

companionship of Heracles and Theseus, or what more in season for the Greeks?
Who wore the same gear and, resolving the same things, tamed the earth. And

they are the presidents of the contest of the war, and we from all have been
chosen as the contestants. Why then do we not imitate the founders? A fair

yourselves of it, saying that the city was not itself, but that those
