#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Lament for Adonis edited by J. M. Edmonds
#+END_QUOTE

I wail for Adonis; /fair Adonis is dead;/
/fair Adonis is dead/ the Loves wail in answer.
Sleep no longer, Cypris, in purple robes;
wake, wretched one, dark-robed, and beat
your breast and say to all /fair Adonis is dead./
I wail for Adonis; the Loves wail in answer.
Fair Adonis lies in the mountains, his thigh by a tusk,
struck, white by a white tusk, and he grieves Cypris
as he faintly breathes out; and the black blood drips
over his snowy flesh, and under the brows the eyes grow numb,
and the rose flees from the lip; and around that
the kiss too dies, which Cypris will never take up.
The kiss even of one not living is pleasing to Cypris,
but Adonis does not know that she kissed him dying.
I wail for Adonis; the Loves wail in answer.
A savage, savage wound Adonis has in the thigh;
but greater is the wound Cytherea bears upon the heart.

Around that boy the dear dogs lamented
and the mountain Nymphs weep; but Aphrodite
having loosed her tresses wanders through the thickets
mourning, unbraided, unsandaled; and the brambles
shear her as she goes and harvest her holy blood;
and shrilly wailing she is borne through the long glens
crying her Assyrian husband and calling the boy.
And about him the black blood hung by the navel,
and the breast was reddened from the thighs, and the breasts
that were snowy before grew purple for Adonis.
/alas for Cytherea/ the Loves wail in answer.
She has lost the fair man, she has lost with him the holy beauty.
Beauty was fair for Cypris while Adonis lived;
but the form died with Adonis. /alas for Cypris/
all the mountains say, and the oaks /alas for Adonis./
And the rivers weep the griefs of Aphrodite,
and the springs in the mountains weep for Adonis,
and the flowers redden from the pain; and Cythera
through all the ridges, through every glen, sings piteously
/alas for Cytherea, fair Adonis is dead./
And Echo cried back /fair Adonis is dead./
Who would not have wept, alas, for Cypris's dread love?
When she saw, when she perceived Adonis's unendurable wound,
when she saw the crimson blood about the wasting thigh,
spreading her arms she wailed: ‘Stay, Adonis,
ill-fated one, stay, Adonis, that I may catch you at the last,
that I may fold you round and mix lips with lips.
Wake a little, Adonis, and kiss me this last time again,
kiss me so much as the kiss may live,

until you breathe out into my mouth and into my liver
your spirit may flow, and I will milk your sweet love-charm,
and drink the love from it, and I will keep this kiss
as Adonis himself, since you flee me, ill-fated one,
you flee far, Adonis, and you go to Acheron
to the grim and savage king, and I, wretched,
live and am a goddess and cannot follow you.
Take my husband, Persephone; for you yourself
are far stronger than I, and every fair thing flows down to you;
and I am all-luckless, and I have unsated grief,
and I weep for Adonis, who died to me, and I fear you.
You die, O thrice-desired, and desire has flown from me like a dream,
and Cytherea is a widow, and the Loves are empty through the house.
And with you the cestus is lost. Why, bold one, did you hunt?
Being so fair, were you mad to wrestle with a beast?’
Thus Cypris lamented; the Loves wail in answer
/alas for Cytherea, fair Adonis is dead./
The Paphian sheds as many tears as Adonis
sheds blood; and all of it becomes flowers upon the earth.
The blood begets the rose, and the tears the anemone.
I wail for Adonis, fair Adonis is dead.
Wail the man no longer in the thickets, Cypris.
A deserted bed of leaves is not a good couch for Adonis;
let Cytherea have your bed, and Adonis even as a corpse.

And even as a corpse he is fair, a fair corpse, as one sleeping.
Lay him in the soft robes in which he used to sleep,
in which with you through the night he labored the holy sleep
on the all-golden couch; it longs even for grim Adonis.
Cast him with garlands and flowers; all with him,
as he has died, the flowers too have all died.
Sprinkle him with Syrian unguents, sprinkle with myrrhs;
let all myrrhs perish; your myrrh, Adonis, is lost.
Delicate Adonis is laid in purple garments;
and around him the Loves weep and groan aloud
having shorn their hair over Adonis; and one the arrow,
and one threw it upon the bow, and one the feather, and one the quiver;
and one loosed Adonis's sandal, and some in a basin
of gold carry water, and one washes the thighs,
and one from behind cools Adonis with his wings.
/alas for Cytherea/ the Loves wail in answer.
Hymenaios quenched every torch at the doorposts,
and he scattered the wedding wreath; and no longer Hymen,
Hymen no longer sings his own song, but he sings in answer
/alas/ and /Adonis/ still more than Hymenaios.
The Graces weep for the son of Cinyras,
/fair Adonis is dead/ saying among one another.
And /alas/ they say sharply far more than Paean.
And the Fates call Adonis /Adonis,/

and they sing him in answer; but he does not hear them;
not that he is unwilling, but the Maiden will not let him go.
Cease from wails today, Cytherea, hold from the breast-beatings;
you must weep again, again in another year you must shed tears.
