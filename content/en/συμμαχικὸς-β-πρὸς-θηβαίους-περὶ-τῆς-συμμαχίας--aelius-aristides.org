We have come not to contend against Philip in speech, but in deed to help you
and the remaining affairs of the Greeks. And still much more clearly now, O

:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

men of Thebes, we know that we were right to be anxious on your behalf. For
that Philip has come to this pitch of daring and cunning, so as first of all
to send ambassadors to you at all, and then ambassadors who would ask for aid,
in order that he may take you without a fight and settle his affairs with us
as he wishes, is, I think, very much worthy both of his custom and of
forethought on your part. The matter perhaps seems new to you, that when the
enemy is advancing, others have come fearful on your behalf, and that you have
not only unasked aid, but even an exhortation added to receive it; but we ask
you to imitate the city of the Athenians this far toward your own affairs, and
not to incur shame before these very ambassadors first. For if we, against
whom they say so many things exist contrary toward you, have come after
cancelling all that, thinking it right to join in saving the city with you,
while you, who have nothing to blame in yourselves, will give up everything
without a struggle, how will you not do the most unreasonable of all things?
And the difference of the present day is not so much as one might say, but has
come to the extreme: to be slaves yourselves to Philip, to help enslave the
other Greeks, to behave rustically toward those who began in kindness, and to
get among all the reputation of the three most shameful things, clumsiness,
greed, and ingratitude; or to inhabit the city in the ancestral form, standing
at the head of the common freedom with us. Which I also wonder at, if you
would justly think it right to hate those who hold that those former things
belong to you more than these, and yet will make more of Philip than of us,
who puts you under the charge of those things; and while you would count those
who slander in speech as enemies, you will set in the rank of ally that man
who in deed confirms the worst things against you. If then even the having the
same interests is a sufficient pledge of kinship, then surely also to think
the same things proper is a firm token of friendship. We, then, what we think
proper for ourselves, these things also for you: to be well spoken of, to take
counsel in advance, to be honored before the other Greeks.

So how could you justly think others better disposed than we? For it is not
that there are other prizes for us if we win, and other things that await you,
but what we would wish to come about for us, these things we share with you.
So that what we ask of the gods for our own city, these things we pray
together for yours, a thing least, I think, possible for Philip, to make the
same prayers for himself and for anyone else. For such a thing is the dynast:
that no one be honored with equal honor; reasonably, or rather necessarily.
For at the same time another will share in equal things, and he himself has
lost what he seeks. For one, I think, must rule, and the others be ruled; and
these two do not mean the same. And these things we need not prove by words,
but Philip confirms them in deed. For who is an ally of this man who does not
fare worse than his enemies? The wars in turn, then, he mostly makes in
secret, as now he has come against you hiding the truth as much as he can; but
the opinion he holds once and for all, he confesses to all and discloses by a
single phrase, if one watches rightly. For when in his letters and his
treaties first of all things there is, King of the Macedonians, how does he
not then proclaim to all the secrets of his mind, and that no one who desires
freedom should have dealings with this man nor trust him? For no one is so
without feeling, nor so far from sense, as to think that he himself is proud
of ruling one nation, and that one full of evils, of which there have already
been countless kings, but of Greece, which alone of things under the sun has
never yet had a master of herself, of this he neither desires to be called
king nor has what he does any reference to anything else of all things, or to
put together these words in sequence, Philip, King of the Greeks. Not so, Zeus
Averter of Evil and you gods. For the other things are fairer and more just:
that we be masters of his. Then they say that the cattle of Attica will come
into Boeotia, as if interpreting some oracles. But Philip at least knows

not only how to give the cattle from Attica, but even those from Macedonia,
for what is advantageous to himself. And know it by looking at Anthemus, which
he gave up to the Olynthians at the start. Then, I think, he later demands
great wages in return; for not only cattle, but even children and women and
walls and cities and everything he has taken together, as Homer said happens
to those who have sworn falsely, that they pay the penalties later with many
and great things. And it differs so much, that these things do not now happen
to those who swear falsely, but this man, swearing falsely, works them. And
besides the cattle he knows, giving even gold from Macedonia, to buy the
affairs in this way, as he bought the cavalry of the Olynthians with these
things; and even of well-grown timber, as they say, from Macedonia, which he
gave up to their magistrates. But nevertheless nothing more came of it for the
Olynthians, when the timber from Macedonia had come to them, but instead of
those things with which one of them roofed his own house, in return for these
the whole city was being razed to the ground. And indeed he who had given them
Potidaea too had in a way given the cattle from Attica, for he had given our
things; then, I think, he used them as cattle, this whatever he wished.

Are these things, then, gifts and humanity and profits of those who received
them, and however one might speak of them fairly under a milder name, or bait
and a snare and a hunt of affairs and a price of the dishonored and
destruction of those led on and grounds of disasters and devices against the
Greeks and all such things, better to call them? As they say the wooden horse
was given as a gift to the Trojans from the Achaeans. These, then, are the
gifts of the accursed man and those which one would least willingly receive
for a city, and these the donations, since even of the affairs toward the
Phocians (and let none of you be struck having heard it suddenly, but follow
the argument as putting down counters, always fitting the second to the
former) even of these he will appear, if you examine rightly, owing to pay a
penalty, not being just to receive thanks. For if, having helped you pull them
down, after

this he kept quiet and meddled in nothing further, perhaps someone would have
said he had done things advantageous to you; but if, desiring rule of the
Greeks, and in order that he might come against you yourselves more without
fear and more easily whenever he wished, why do you not from that day set him
down as an enemy the same, from which he first destroyed the Phocians? For he
was not more punishing the Phocians on your behalf than he now comes against
you on behalf of the Phocians. And indeed one cannot even say that the
Phocians furnished him fear and he thought it advantageous to destroy them,
inasmuch as they had earlier thought about the rule of the Greeks and had
gotten a wonderful reputation for their deeds, but that the city of the
Thebans, where he once was a hostage, he thought a slight thing to make his
own and hold. For there are three cities toward which it is necessary for him
to strain his calculations; of which that of the Lacedaemonians is farthest
and already almost convicted by you, and for both these reasons of less
account to him; and our city even he himself does not attempt to say he does
not treat as hostile; but yours by its position is first and for this reason
necessary to take in advance, and for fortune's sake this is not without fear,
nor less than ours suspect, which looking the other way he seeks to take and
to collect the customary wages of his benefactions. For these reasons the
Phocians and Tilphossaeum are hymned. For with the same calculations with
which he was granting favors, with these he reminds you of the service, buying
it, as if even hunters should say to the birds that they had spent no small
toil because of them, and at the same time showing the bait should demand the
favor in return. Whom it was necessary, if these things had most of all
happened to have been done on your behalf, that he himself, and not you,
should remind, but now, having passed over all these things, he advances with
arms, displaying his own wickedness and bringing out the ends, as of a drama,
contrary to hopes.

The ambassadors seem to me to say marvelous things and altogether contrary to
themselves, who say that you must be grateful to Philip, if he ever did you
any good, but command you to give up all the affairs

that are in your power, and to become so much worse than Philip concerning
yourselves that he, even if he did not wish this, nevertheless did some things
for you in season, while you, of things than which you could have nothing
greater, will of your own will go wrong against yourselves, and you will be so
far from imitating that man, that he handed over your enemies to you, but you,
if you do what these men persuade, will hand yourselves over to the enemies.
For what remaining reason, tell me, will you be grateful to Philip, if you
care nothing about being saved? For I think from both sides the present is
contrary to that former partnership. For if truly being well-disposed to you
he was eager in those things, it is shameful to praise that man for his
eagerness, and to neglect yourselves; but if it was a trial against you and a
preparation of the time to come, he owes you a penalty of two things, not of
one, both of cheating in those former things and of now coming on with arms.
So that it is not because of those things that these must be obeyed, but for
both one must use the punishment when he has been caught. And it would be
well, having used Philip's wickedness, to guard against what of penalty is
attached to it. No one denies that the Phocians have justly perished, but not
for this is Philip justly to be trusted. But if one must say something even
about the Phocians, in the part that concerns themselves they have justly
suffered these things, but as much as looks to Philip, not rightly. For when
he appears to have joined in these things not for the god's sake nor for
yours, but for his own greed, which he shows in all things, those men perhaps
have justly perished, but this man has not justly destroyed them. Do not then,
because the Phocians have reasonably perished, give up your own things at
random, nor join in condemning yourselves to suffer what the Phocians
suffered. For of those men we could say that they suffered what was fitting,
having offended concerning the god, but you, in return for what you will now
undergo this penalty, we at least are not conscious of. And this too seems
terrible to me, if the Phocians owed a penalty, because they seized the
sanctuary at Delphi,

but he who has not only plundered the sanctuary at Delphi, but instead of
plundering the sanctuary holds the Pythian games and leads the Greeks in
things that in no way belong to him, and instead of plundering the one at
Delphi alone has of thirty-two cities together in Thrace not plundered but
destroyed the sanctuaries, and besides these is plundering all Greece and
making nothing exempt, not temples, not dedications, not walls, not horses,
not arms, not money, not bodies, not harbors, not constitution, but moving all
things as by thunder, and some things making vanish utterly, and using others
however he wishes, this man will not seem to owe a penalty for these things,
but as a benefactor will be crowned, and that by you, whose rank he has
ruined. But if not, when were you faring better, and that you may not be at
all angry, was it when Epaminondas and Gorgidas and Pelopidas were leading you
and you knew Philip as a hostage, or when this man had come into the rule and
when this man was leading you, granting so many things, through which he was
going to use you weaker thereafter? And I think if at the very start Philip
had asked you this wage for the aid, to bow down and hand over the city to
him, no one would have valued the rivalry toward the Phocians at so much.

Do not then give now, in return for those things, what you would not have
endured from the start, but consider that first nothing less of what you wish
has been done for you, so that you no longer have any great need of this man;
then even if there were a choice of two things, it was better to make war on
the Phocians than to let this man in. For of those you would have prevailed
both by yourselves and with us, as formerly of the Lacedaemonians, and perhaps
in some other way; but if once this man will be master of you, I do not know
what greater thing you will later find from yourselves. Then that would have
been not to take a penalty from the Phocians, but this, not to have wished to
save yourselves. And it is surely better to spare others for one's own sake
than to think it right to perish oneself because of others. Nor, then, do the
slanders these men have spoken about us have any sense. For what could be more
unreasonable than

I would gladly ask those who are ambassadors on the contrary side, whether
they think these things should be judged from the time to come, or from the
past. For if from the time to come, the things from us are freedom, safety,
being ranked first among the Greeks, all the fairest things; but the things
from that man, what would one say, except as much as to turn it upon his own
head, the gods? But if at least they must be judged from the past time, let
him show all that Philip has ever done toward you worth two aids of ours, both
the former one to Haliartus and the later one against the garrison; of which
the one drove the Lacedaemonians from Boeotia, the other from the Cadmea. Are
these like helping against the Phocians? Do you not know that against those
you were fighting from a surplus, but in the necessities in which you were,
then you needed no others except us, and compare also who the Phocians are
beside the Lacedaemonians and how each of them stood. Were not the one rulers
of the Greeks, and the others faring no better than anyone? And I pass over
saying that the Lacedaemonians invaded Boeotia as it were through Attica,
passing by our city, and that when it was unwalled at the start; but for
Philip there was no trouble from the Phocians, but he had the Phocians in the
midst of his own allies. But indeed the future is more intimate to all than
the past, for the one has been alienated and one would fare neither worse nor
better by it, but the future is coming on; and we from both of the seasons so
far prevail, that in that former one we have become much better concerning you
than this man, and from this present one a single vote is laid up for you to
come to be with us. Thus winning in both we more greatly excel by the more
sovereign. But there is nevertheless one good thing of his embassy. For it
shows how he has feared the cities and that if we come together he has not any
hope at all about the affairs. It is ours, then, seeing his fear and where he
is unsound, to let go no strength, but to press on and take away

his holds and not to prefer what is advantageous to him. For it is not if
something difficult exists for us toward one another that this leads him now,
being indignant on your behalf, but in order that he may use both of us up,
for this he searches these things out. So that the more things he is well
supplied with, the more he must be distrusted. So then not even that man
trusts you, so that he has not sent asking for the aid simply, as one who is
confident in what exists, but he says you should choose whichever you wish,
either to invade Attica together, or to let him through, as one who would
justly obtain neither. For consider, if, men of Thebes, you should invade
together, and should choose to gratify him in this way, you become worse by
more than he has offered. For when he makes it equal, whether you wish to keep
quiet or to campaign together, and you choose the greater, what you add, I
think, this becomes an excess of baseness. And yet how is it tolerable, or how
having a moderate reputation, when one ought not even to listen at all to the
dynasts, and that against the Greeks and those of the same race, you gratify
with the greater of the things they put in your power, and betrayal goes
beyond the need? But if at least you will let him through, it will come in
time not to a favor for you, but to a complaint. For now, I think, serving the
moment he promises he will be content, but if he should be rid of the enmity
toward us, he will make the not campaigning together a pretext. And this one
can learn from the very things already happened. For against you, men of
Thebes, the Lacedaemonians, having once accused this, the not invading the
Piraeus together, came to war. And that the Lacedaemonians are children
compared to Philip in account of cunning and of doing harm, we all surely
know. And indeed we know this too about the Lacedaemonians, since I have
mentioned these things, that when they were fighting the war against us, they
said they were fighting for the freedom of the Greeks, and by this they were
drawing the many to them; but when they had done what they wished, they turned
first against the allies themselves and transferred the war onto them.

The Chians at least, who had been most eager of the islanders about them and
had almost begun for all the Greeks the revolt from us, they took away the
constitution and the walls, and filled them with so many exiles and
slaughters, that all the hardships of war seemed to them to be peace; and they
were besieging the Great King by land and sea, and they were showing a
garrison against you, and a little later they were present at Corinth.

If then, men of Thebes, now too Philip should see you letting go what is in
your hands and counseling about what is later, and having settled the war
against us according to his mind should imitate the Lacedaemonians and, having
found some pretext, or even having let all the pretexts go, should simply in
this way attempt you, will you say that you have served him these things
against us Athenians, or against yourselves? And which way, by Zeus, do you
think he would come against you better and more eagerly, whether while we are
flourishing and holding the post which we now hold, or when you alone have
been left and there is no fear from us for one attempting some innovation? And
indeed this much at least you know, that if then among the Greeks what was
said by some had prevailed, that it was necessary to enslave our city and to
give up the land to sheep, you would not, when the Lacedaemonians were making
the expedition against you, have had anyone to go to. But as it is, the being
saved and surviving of our city came to need not only for you, but also, as it
seems, for all the Greeks. It is quite clear then that not even if we should
now suffer something, would your affairs be without danger, nor even if you
are not yet the first to be in danger, but one day will set both those things
there, for it is fair to speak so, and the things here into the need. And

that we no longer had a charge, and we voted to help. Is it then, men of

That then you will also counsel worse about yourselves than about us if you

when it is unclear whether we shall prevail, is it unclear whether we shall be
slaves? And indeed that at least, if you attach yourselves to Philip, you have
both done and will do what is commanded, is known to everyone. For one must, I
think, follow with the others and do whatever seems good to that man. How then

in which we contend about these things, then the not seeming to have of our

And yet the contrary also no less reasonably urges you. For if even now,

speeches, let us leave the Thessalians; for the penalty they give to Philip is

these things we would wish also for you, and I think even much more greatly,
by how much more honorable from the start the ground of the Thebans is to us
and more in season for the Greeks toward the present affairs. For us, then,
both in word and in deed some things have been contended, and others, as it
seems, will be contended; but it is time already for us too to have
deliberated and, before seeing Philip, to take our stand with the arms at what
you will do, so that he may know that the hands are being saved for the
Greeks, as long as Athens and Thebes fight shield to shield.
