* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Lysias son of Cephalus was of Syracusan parents, but he was born at Athens
while his father was living as a metic, and he was educated together with the
most distinguished of the Athenians. Being fifteen years old he went sailing
to Thurii with two brothers, to take part in the colony which the Athenians
and the rest of Greece were sending in the twelfth year before the
Peloponnesian War, and he continued there living as a citizen in much
prosperity and being educated under Tisias and Nicias until the disaster that
overtook the Athenians in Sicily. After that calamity, the people having
fallen into faction, he is expelled together with three hundred others,
accused of Atticism. And having come again to Athens in the archonship of
Callias, being in his forty-seventh year, as one might conjecture, from that
time he continued making his sojourns at Athens. And having written very many
speeches fitted to courts and councils and toward assemblies, and besides
these panegyric, erotic, epistolary ones, of the orators who had been before
or who flourished at the same time he eclipsed the reputations, and of those
who came after he left few any room to surpass him, nor in all the forms of
speeches, and by Zeus not at least in the meanest. What character of speeches
he has used and what virtues he has introduced and in what he is better than
those who flourished after him and in what inferior and what one ought to take
from him, I shall now already try to say.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

He is very pure in expression and the best canon of the Attic language, not of
the ancient, which Plato and Thucydides have used, but of that current in that
time, as it is possible to judge from the speeches of Andocides and those of
Critias and many others. In this part then, which is first and most sovereign
in speeches, I mean the purifying of the dialect, none of those later
surpassed him, nor even did many have power to imitate him except Isocrates
alone; for this man seems to me at least to have become the purest of the
others after Lysias in the words. This one virtue then worthy of zeal and
imitation I find in the orator, and I would exhort those who wish to write or
speak purely to make that man a model of this virtue.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And another no less than this, which many of those who flourished at the same
time emulated, but none displayed more securely: and what is this? The
expression that brings out the thoughts through proper and common and everyday
words. For one would least find Lysias using figurative phrase. And it is
worth praising him not only for this, but because he makes the matters appear
solemn and extraordinary and great while using the most common words and not
touching poetic construction. But the earlier ones did not have this
reputation, but wishing some adornment to attend the speeches they departed
from the ordinary man and took refuge in poetic phrase, using many metaphors
and hyperboles and the other figurative forms, and by the use of glossematic
and foreign words and by the exchange of unaccustomed figures and by the rest
of novel speech striking the ordinary man. Gorgias. of Leontini shows this, in
many places making the construction quite vulgar and overblown and uttering
some things /not far from certain dithyrambs/, and of his associates those
about Licymnius and Polus. And the poetic and figurative phrase touched also
the orators at Athens, as Timaeus says, Gorgias having begun when as
ambassador to Athens he struck the hearers with his public speech, but as the
truth has it, it had always been somewhat admired even earlier. Thucydides at
any rate, the most inspired of the historians, using poetic construction both
in the funeral speech and in the public speeches, in many places altered the
expression into bulk together with an adornment of words more unusual. But
Lysias practiced nothing such at least in the forensic and deliberative
speeches written in earnest to do, except some little in the panegyrics; for
about his epistolary and companionable ones and the others which he wrote with
play I have no need to speak. And seeming to converse like ordinary men he
differs as much as possible from an ordinary man and is the strongest poet of
speeches, having found for diction released from meter a certain private
harmony of speeches, by which he both adorns and sweetens the words, they
having nothing bulky or vulgar. This second virtue I bid one take from this
orator, if any think it worthy to converse in the same manner as he. Many
writers and orators then became zealots of this choice, and nearest to it
after Lysias of the older men Isocrates, flourishing as a young man, laid
hold, and one would not be able to name, looking further than these, other
orators who have displayed such strength and power in proper and common words.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

A third virtue I declare about the man, clarity, not only that in the words
but also that in the matters; for there is also a practical clarity not known
to many. And I judge this, that of the diction of Thucydides and Demosthenes,
who became most powerful at speaking out matters, many things are hard for us
to guess and unclear and needing interpreters. But the diction of Lysias is
all evident and clear even to one who seems to stand very far from political
speeches. And if the clear came about through weakness of power, it would not
be worth loving it, but as it is the wealth of proper words from much
abundance displays this virtue for him. So that his clarity too is worth
emulating. And indeed the bringing out of the thoughts briefly together with
clearly, it being a thing hard by nature to bring both these together and mix
them in measure, in which especially Lysias is displayed no less than any of
the others, who at least would seem to those who have the man in hand to take
nothing either of untimely speech or of unclarity. And the cause of this is
that the matters are not enslaved to the words with him, but the words follow
the matters, and he takes the adornment not in departing from the ordinary man
but in imitating him.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

And he is not such in expression but in the matters some untimely and long
man; he is compacted if anyone is and densified with thoughts, and he is so
far from saying anything of the unnecessary that he would even seem to omit
many even of the useful things, not by Zeus doing this by weakness of
invention, but by measuring of the time toward which the speeches had to come
about. Brief then is this man, sufficient for an ordinary man wishing to make
the matters clear, but not enough for an orator seeking to display abundance
of power. One must imitate then also the brevity of Lysias; for a more
measured one would not be found in another orator.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

After these I find in Lysias a virtue altogether admirable, of which
Theophrastus says Thrasymachus began, but I think Lysias; for in times this
man seems to me at least to precede that one (and I speak as of both having
come to a common peak of life), and if this were not granted, at least by
having been more worn about the true contests than that man. I do not however
assert which of the two began this virtue, for the present, but that Lysias
excelled more in it, this I would declare with confidence. And what is the
virtue I mean? The diction that compactly gathers the thoughts and brings them
out roundly, very proper and necessary to forensic speeches and to every true
contest. Few have imitated this, but Demosthenes even surpassed it, except he
did not use it so whitely nor simply as Lysias, but elaborately and bitterly;
for let it be said, as it appears to me. About which I shall speak at the
proper time.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

And the diction of Lysias has also much vividness. And this is a certain power
bringing the things said under the senses, and it comes from the taking of the
attendant circumstances. He then who applies his mind to the speeches of
Lysias will not be so clumsy or hard to please or slow in mind as not to
suppose he sees the things shown happening, and to converse as with persons
present whom the orator introduces. And he will seek nothing, such as it is
likely some would have done, others suffered, others thought, others said. For
he became strongest of all orators at spying out the nature of men and
assigning to each the fitting passions and characters and deeds.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

I assign to him then also the most becoming virtue, called by many
characterization. For simply I can find in this orator no person either
uncharacterized or lifeless. And there being three things in which and about
which this virtue happens to be, thought and diction and third composition, in
all these I declare him to succeed. For he not only hypothesizes the speakers
thinking things good and reasonable and moderate, so that the speeches seem to
be images of the characters, but he also assigns diction proper to the
characters, by which they are naturally shown best of themselves, the clear
and proper and common and most customary to all men; for bulk and the foreign
and everything from artifice is uncharacterized. And he composes it very
simply and plainly, seeing that character comes not in the period and the
rhythms but in the dissolved diction. And in general, that I may speak also
about this virtue, I do not know if any other of the orators at least of those
who have used a like construction of speech composed either more pleasantly or
more persuasively. For the character of his harmony seems to be some unmade
and unartificial thing, and I would not wonder if it furnished some such
opinion to all ordinary men, and not a few even of the scholars, as many as
have not great practice about speeches, that it is put together without design
and not according to art, but somehow automatically and as it chanced. But it
is constructed more than any technical work. For this unmade thing has been
made by him and the dissolved has been bound and in the very not seeming to
have been constructed powerfully it has the power. One then practicing the
truth and wishing to become an imitator of nature would not err using the
composition of Lysias; for he would not find another truer than this.

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

And I think the diction of Lysias has also the fitting no less than any of the
ancient orators, the strongest of all virtues and most complete, seeing it
sufficiently fitted both to the speaker and to the hearers and to the matter
(for in these and toward these is the fitting). For both by age and race and
education and pursuit and life and the other things in which persons differ
from persons he assigns the proper voices, and he measures the things said
fittingly toward the hearer, not conversing in the same manner with a juror
and an assemblyman and a panegyric crowd. And his diction also takes
differences according to the forms of the matters; for to one beginning it is
settled and ethical, to one narrating persuasive and unelaborate, to one
proving round and dense, to one amplifying and being impassioned solemn and
true, to one recapitulating dissolved and short. One must take then also the
fitting of diction from Lysias.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

That it is persuasive and convincing and showing much of the natural and all
that belongs to such a form, perhaps one need say nothing to those who know;
for this is already a nuisance and there is no one who having learned both by
trial and by hearing does not agree that he is the most persuasive of all
orators. So that this virtue too must be taken from the orator.

Having many fine things to say about the diction of Lysias, which taking and
imitating one would become better in expression, I shall let the rest go
aiming at the time, but I shall still display one virtue of the orator, having
judged it both finest and most sovereign and itself alone most able of the
others to confirm the character of Lysias, which none of those later
surpassed, but many imitated and by this very thing seemed better than others
though differing in the rest of power not at all; about whom, if it be
allowed, I shall speak in the proper place. And what is this virtue? The grace
that blooms upon all the words and equally, a thing stronger than all speech
and more wonderful. For it is easiest to be seen and equally evident to every
ordinary man and craftsman, but hardest to be shown in speech and not even to
those. most able to speak well easy.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

So that if someone should think it worthy to be taught this power by speech,
whatever it is, he would not be too soon in demanding an account also of many
other fine things hard to utter; I mean, on beauty of bodies, what in the
world this is which we call prime, on movement of limbs and weaving of sounds,
what the well-fitted is called, on symmetry of times, what the order is and
what the well-rhythmed, and on every work and matter taken together, what the
so-called timely is and where the measured. For each of these is grasped by
sense and not by speech. So that what the musicians bid those wishing to hear
harmony accurately do, so as not to miss even the smallest diesis in the
intervals, to train the hearing and seek no criterion more accurate than this,
this I too would suggest to those reading Lysias and wishing to learn what the
grace in him is to practice, by much time and long practice and irrational
affection to train together the irrational sense. This however I set as both
strongest virtue and most characteristic of the diction of Lysias, whether one
must call it a good fortune of nature or a work of labor and art or a mixed
habit or power from both, by which he surpasses all the remaining orators. And
whenever I am in doubt about some of the speeches attributed to him and it is
not easy for me to find the truth through the other signs, I take refuge in
this virtue as in a last vote. Then if the graces of the diction seem to me to
adorn the writing, I set it of the soul of Lysias and I think it worthy to
look no further than this. But if the character of the diction has no pleasure
nor charm, I am abashed and suspect that perhaps the speech is not of Lysias
and I no longer force the irrational sense, not even if the speech seems to me
to be very powerful at least in the rest and extraordinarily worked out,
thinking that writing well belongs to many according to certain other private
characters of diction as well (for this is many-formed), but writing
pleasantly and graciously and charmingly to Lysias.

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Using then no other better proof than that the things said by this man are
expressed according to pleasure, by which already many of the speeches
attributed to him and believed by the crowd to be among the very genuine of
Lysias, and having the rest not unreasonably, because they do not strike the
Lysianic grace nor have the fluency of that diction, having suspected and
tested I found not being of Lysias. Of which is also the one about the statue
of Iphicrates, which I know that many would even think a character and canon
of that man's power. This speech however, which seems both to have been
expressed strongly in the words and to have been invented extraordinarily in
the enthymemes and having many other virtues, is graceless and far from
showing the Lysianic mouth. And it became most clear to me that it was not
written by that orator when I calculated the times. For if one will set Lysias
as having died at eighty years in the archonship of Nicon or of Nausinicus,
the death of the orator would precede the writing of the decree by seven whole
years. For after the archon Alcistheles, in whose time the Athenians and
Lacedaemonians and the King swore the peace, Iphicrates having given back the
armies becomes a private man, and the affair about the statue was then, Lysias
having died seven years before the writing, before composing this contest for
Iphicrates. Likewise also the defense of the man, itself too being referred
carried to Lysias, having neither the matters unreasonably nor the words
weakly, I took under suspicion, the Lysianic grace not blooming on the
diction; and setting the times beside I found it later than the death of the
orator by not a few years but even twenty whole ones. For in the Social War
Iphicrates contested the impeachment and underwent the audits of the
generalship, as becomes clear from the speech itself; and this war falls under
the archons Agathocles and Elpines. Whose speeches then of the orator those
about the statue and about the treason are, I cannot say securely. That both
are of one man, I would be able to say by many proofs; for the same choice and
power is in both, about which it is not the time in the present to examine.
And I guess they are of Iphicrates; for the man is both terrible in war and
not contemptible in speeches, and the diction in both has much of the vulgar
and soldierly and does not so show rhetorical sharpness as soldierly self-will
and boastfulness. But about these things it will be shown elsewhere at more
length.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

One must go back, from where we digressed into these things, that the
strongest of the works of Lysias and most characteristic of his power is the
grace that adorns and flowers his diction, which none of those who came after
either surpassed or imitated to the peak. And the good things of the orator
about expression are these; for I shall sum up the things said: the purity of
the words, the exactness of the dialect, the bringing out of the thoughts
through proper and not figurative constructions, the clarity, the brevity, the
compacting and rounding of the thoughts, the bringing the things shown under
the senses, the hypothesizing no person lifeless or uncharacterized, the
pleasure of the composition of the words imitating the ordinary man, the
fitting of proper speeches to the underlying persons and matters, the
persuasiveness and the convincing and the grace and the timing that measures
all. These taking from Lysias one would be benefited. But the diction of
Lysias is not high and magnificent nor striking by Zeus and wonderful nor
showing the bitter or the powerful or the fearful, nor has it grips and strong
tensions, nor is it full of spirit and breath, nor, as it is persuasive in
characters, so strong in passions, nor as it can sweeten and persuade and be
graceful, so force and compel. And it is more safe than ventured, and not so
able to show strength of art as to liken truth of nature.

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

And it is worth wondering what in the world Theophrastus suffered that he
thinks him to have become a zealot of vulgar and elaborate speeches and to
pursue the poetic rather than the true. At any rate in the things written On
Diction he both blames the others who have been busy about antitheses and
parisoses and paromoioses and the figures like these and indeed has also
numbered Lysias among these, setting beside the speech for Nicias the general
of the Athenians, which he spoke among the Syracusans being a prisoner, as
written by this orator. And perhaps nothing will prevent setting down the
diction itself of Theophrastus as well. And it is this: /And antithesis is in
three ways, whenever the opposites are additionally predicated of the same, or
the same of the opposite, or opposites of opposites. For in so many ways it is
possible to be yoked. And of these the equal and the like is childish and as
it were a poem; wherefore it also fits less with seriousness. For it appears
unfitting for one being serious about the matters to play with the words and
to strip the passion by the diction; for it relaxes the hearer. As for example
Lysias in the defense of Nicias wishing to make pity: I bewail of Greeks an
unfought and unseafought destruction, ourselves sitting as suppliants of the
gods, and showing you traitors of the oaths, and calling on kinship,
goodwill./ For these things if Lysias really wrote, he would justly be thought
worthy of censure for being graceful at an ungraceful time. But if the speech
is of some other, as indeed it is, the one accusing the man of things that did
not belong is more blameworthy. That Lysias did not write the speech for
Nicias nor is the writing either of that soul or of that diction, being able
to prove by very many proofs I have not time in the present speech. But
composing a private treatise about the orator, in which both the other things
will be shown by me and which are his genuine speeches, I shall try in those
to render the exactness also about this speech.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

But now I shall speak about the next things, what the practical character of
Lysias is, since I have rendered the account about diction; for this part
still remains. For the man is inventive of the arguments inherent in the
matters, not only of those which we all would find, but also of those which no
one would. For Lysias simply omits none of the elements from which speeches
come, not the persons, not the matters, not the actions themselves, not
manners and causes of them, not occasions, not times, not places, not the
differences of each of these as far as the cutting to the least, but from
every theory and every division he selects the proper starting-points. And
those of the speeches without witnesses and those composed about paradoxical
hypotheses show most the power of his invention, in which he speaks very many
and finest enthymemes and makes the things that seem to the others quite
impassable and impossible appear passable and possible, being a critic of what
one must say and, when it was not possible to use all the things found,
selective of the strongest and most sovereign, if not even most of the other
orators, at least no less than any. And he has used a certain simple
arrangement of the matters and for the most part of one form, and about the
working-out of the arguments he is some simple and unelaborate man; for
neither with pre-preparations nor with approaches nor with divisions nor with
varieties of figures nor with the other such tricks is he found. using,. but
he is some un-overdone and free and un-wicked man at managing the things
found. From these then I exhort those reading him to emulate the invention of
the enthymemes and the judgment, but the arrangement and the working of them,
being more deficient than what is fitting, not to accept from the man, but
from others, who became better at managing the things found, about whom I
shall speak later, to take this element.

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Having rendered the account about the virtues and the elements, I shall now
speak also about the kind of the disputes in which theorems the political art
is. And the rhetorical speech having been divided in three and having taken
three kinds different in their ends, the forensic and the deliberative and the
so-called epideictic or panegyric, in all these the man is worthy of account,
but most in the forensic contests. And even in these themselves he is better
at speaking the small and paradoxical and impassable finely than the solemn
and great and passable powerfully. Let him who wishes to learn the power of
Lysias accurately look at it rather from the forensic speeches than from the
panegyric and deliberative. And that it may be possible for me to say the
fitting things also about the forms, I shall let these things go and I shall
speak about prefaces and narrations and the other parts of the speech and I
shall show of what sort the man is in each of the forms. And I shall divide
them as it pleased Isocrates and those adorned after that man, beginning from
the prefaces.

* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

I say then that the orator is the cleverest of all at the entries of speeches
and the most graceful, considering that to begin well is not easy, if indeed
one should wish to use the fitting beginning and not to say the chance speech
(for not the first thing said, but that which would help the proposed speech
nowhere more than at first, this is both beginning and preface), and seeing
the orator using all things which both arts bid and the matters wish. For at
one time speaking from his own praise he himself begins, at another from the
slander of the adversary, and if he himself happens to have been previously
slandered, he first acquits the charges against himself; at another praising
and cultivating the jurors he makes them intimate to himself and to the
matter, at another he indicates his own weakness and the advantage of the
adversary and that the contest is not about equal things for both; at another
he says that the matters are common and necessary to all and not worthy to be
neglected by the hearers, at another he constructs something else of the
things able to benefit him and diminish the adversary. And having taken these
up briefly and simply with good thoughts and timely maxims and moderate
enthymemes he hastens to the proposition, through which having said beforehand
the things about to be said in the proofs and having prepared the hearer ready
to learn toward the coming speech he sets himself to the narration; and the
proposition is for him as a border of each of the forms for the most part, and
already sometimes he even began from this alone. And sometimes without preface
he entered taking the narration as beginning. And he is not lifeless nor
unmoved about this form; and one would especially wonder at his power in the
prefaces, considering that having written not fewer than two hundred forensic
speeches he has appeared in none either prefacing unpersuasively or using a
beginning detached from the matters, nor even has he applied the same
enthymemes nor been brought down upon the same thoughts. And yet this even
those who have written few speeches are found to have suffered, I mean
applying to the same topics; for I pass over that taking also the things said
among others almost all do not set the deed in shame. But this orator is new
in each of the speeches at least in the entries and the prefaces and able to
accomplish what he should wish; for wishing neither to move goodwill nor
attention nor readiness to learn would he ever miss the mark. In this form
then I declare him either first or second to none.

* Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

And in narrating the matters, which part I think needs most care and watch, I
uncontroversially think him strongest of all orators, and I declare him limit
and canon of this form. And I think also the arts of speeches, in which
something noteworthy has been said about narration, have taken the precepts
and the starting-points not from any others more than from those written by
Lysias. For these narrations have especially the brief and the clear, and they
are pleasant as no others and persuasive and they bring the proof along with
them unnoticed, so that it is not easy for either any whole narration or a
part of it to be found false or unpersuasive; so much persuasion and charm the
things said have and so they escape the hearers whether they are true or
fabricated. So that what Homer said praising Odysseus as persuasive to speak
and to fabricate the things not happened, this I think one might say also of
Lysias: /he likened many lies speaking to things like the true./ And to all
and most of all I would have exhorted to practice this part making the
exercises in the examples of Lysias. For he would display this form strongest
who most imitated this man.

And in confirming the matters such a man is the man.

* Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

And I shall begin from the so-called artistic proofs and I shall speak
separately about each part. These having been divided in three into the matter
and the passion and the character, those from the matter Lysias is able to
find and speak out no worse than anyone. For the man is both best conjecturer
of the probable and of the example, most accurate judge of where it is
naturally like and where different, and most able to divide the signs that
attend the matters and bring them to the reputation of proofs. And the proofs
from the characters too he seems to me at least to construct quite worthily.
For often from the life and the nature, often from the former actions and
choices he constructs the characters as trustworthy. And whenever he takes no
such starting-point from the matters, he himself characterizes and constructs
the persons by the speech as trustworthy and good, hypothesizing for them
urbane choices and attaching moderate passions and assigning reasonable
speeches and introducing them thinking things following their fortunes and
making them grieved at the unjust both in words and in deeds, and choosing the
just, and constructing all the things like these from which a reasonable and
moderate character would appear. But about the passions he is softer and able
to construct neither amplifications nor indignations nor pities nor all that
is like these very youthfully and vigorously. One must not then seek these
from Lysias. And in the epilogues too he enumerates the recapitulative part of
the things said moderately and gracefully, but that pathetic one, in which
there is exhortation and pity and entreaty and the things brother to these, he
renders more deficiently than what is fitting.

* Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

Such then is the character of Lysias, as I have opinion about him. And if
anyone has judged other things besides these, let him speak; and if they are
more persuasive, I shall know him much thanks. And that it may become better
for him who wishes to learn whether we have been persuaded of these things
rightly and fittingly or have also missed the judgment, I shall make the
examination upon the things written by that man, and having taken in hand one
speech (for it is not allowed to use many examples) from that I shall display
both the choice and the power of the man, thinking it enough for well-educated
and moderate souls that small things of great and few of many become samples.
And the speech is from the guardianship ones, entitled Against Diogeiton, and
having a hypothesis of this sort:

* Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

Diodotus, one of those enrolled with Thrasyllus in the Peloponnesian War,
being about to sail out to Asia in the archonship of Glaucippus, having infant
children, made a will leaving them as guardian his own brother Diogeiton, and
of the children both uncle and grandfather from the mother. He himself then

This preface has all the virtues which a preface ought to have. And the canons
of the arts set beside it will show it. For all those who have composed the
arts, I suppose, bid, whenever the contest is toward one's own, to watch that
the accusers will not appear knaves nor busybodies. And they bid first to turn
the cause both of the charge and of the contest upon the adversaries and to
say that the wrongs are great and it was not possible to bear them moderately
and that the contest is for more necessary persons and deserted ones and less
worthy to be overlooked, whom not helping they would have appeared worse; and
that challenging the adversaries to reconciliations and leaving the affairs to
friends and enduring to be diminished in the possible things they were able to
obtain none of the moderate things. These things then the writers of arts bid
to do, in order that the character of the speaker may seem more reasonable.
And this can make goodwill for them and is the strongest part of the
construction. I see all these things to have come about through this preface.
