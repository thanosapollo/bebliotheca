EUSEBIUS PAMPHILI AGAINST THE ACCOUNT WRITTEN BY PHILOSTRATUS ON APOLLONIUS OF
TYANA, ON ACCOUNT OF THE COMPARISON OF HIM AND OF CHRIST TAKEN UP BY
HIEROCLES. So then, my friend, are those things too of the writer worthy for
you to admire, which he told as marvels when he compared the man of Tyana with
our Saviour and teacher? For against the rest of what is in the Lover of
Truth, for so he thought it well to entitle his discourse against us, it would
be no serious matter to take our stand at present, since they are not his own,
but have been pilfered very shamelessly, I may almost say not in thoughts only
but even in words and syllables, from others. These too would meet with the
fitting refutation in due season, but they have already in effect been
overturned and refuted in advance, even before a writing of our own against
them, in the eight books in all that Origen wrote against the True Word of
Celsus, titled more boastfully than the Lover of Truth. Bringing that work to
account without omission, in as many as we have said, the author named, taking
up in a lump whatever on the same 370 subject has been said or will be said by
anyone, forestalled and dissolved it. Sending those who wish to know our
affairs accurately and with a love of truth to those books, come, for the
present let us examine only the comparison concerning our Lord Jesus Christ in
this Lover of Truth here, thinking it no serious matter to fight against the
rest of what he has pilfered from elsewhere. Only the things about Apollonius
we will reasonably look at now, since to this man alone, among all who have
ever written against us, the setting of this man beside our Saviour, and the
comparison, has now been granted as a special thing. He admires at any rate
and accepts him, saying that he worked wonders by a certain divine and
unspeakable wisdom, and not by the tricks of sorcery, and believing and
asserting without proof that these things truly are so as they chanced. Hear
then what he says in the very syllables: "Up and down they go prating,
magnifying Jesus as having granted the blind to see again and as having done
certain other such wonders." Then after saying something in between he adds:
"Let us consider, however, how much better and more intelligent is the way we
take such things, and the opinion we have about virtuous men." And after
these, passing by Aristeas of Proconnesus and Pythagoras as being more
ancient, he goes on saying: "But in the time of our ancestors, in the reign of
Nero, Apollonius of Tyana flourished, who from a quite young boy, and from the
time he served as priest at Aegae in Cilicia to Asclepius the lover of
mankind, accomplished many and wonderful things, of which, omitting the
greater part, I will make mention of a few." Then he lists the marvels
beginning from the first, after which he also adds these things 371 word for
word: "Why then did I mention these? So that it may be possible to compare our
accurate and firm judgment on each point and the lightness of the Christians,
if indeed we consider the one who has done such things not a god but a man
pleasing to the gods, while they, because of a few prodigies, proclaim Jesus a
god." To these he adds after other things, saying: "And this too is worth
reckoning, that the things of Jesus Peter and Paul and some like them have
vaunted, men who are liars and uneducated and sorcerers, while the things of
Apollonius Maximus of Aegae and Damis the philosopher who spent time with him
and Philostratus the Athenian, men who have come to the height of education,
honouring the truth through love of mankind, not

wishing the deeds of a noble man and friend of the gods to escape notice."
These things have been said in the very words by Hierocles, who titled his
discourse against us the Lover of Truth. Damis then, who spent much time with
Apollonius, coming from the land of the Assyrians and first mixing with him
there in his own country, related the companionship that came to him with the
man from that time; but Maximus recorded quite brief things of what was done
by him in detail; Philostratus the Athenian, however, saying that he himself
gathered all that is current together from the writings of Maximus and of
Damis himself and of others, has made the most complete history of all of the
man's life from birth even to the end. If then it is possible to set side by
side the readiness and lightness charged against us and the accurate and firm
judgment of the Lover of Truth on each point, come, let us inquire, not who
has become more divine. 372 nor which of them accomplished more wonderful and
more numerous marvels, nor that our Saviour and Lord Jesus Christ alone among
the Hebrew wise men from of old, who lived before countless years, was
prophesied by divine inspiration as about to come among men, nor that he
turned more to the word of his divine teaching, nor that he gained genuine and
truly true disciples prepared I may almost say even to die readily for his
words, nor that he alone established a school of a sober life even for the
time afterward, nor that by his own godhead and virtue he saved the whole
inhabited world and still even now draws countless multitudes from every side
to his own divine teaching, nor that he alone of all who have ever been,
warred against for very many years already by nearly all men, rulers and
ruled, has been shown stronger and far more powerful than the unbelievers who
drive him out bitterly, by a divine and unspeakable power easily going after
those who at times rise against his divine teaching, and strengthening the
divine word fixed by him and handed down unto infinite age throughout the
whole inhabited world, nor that still even now he displays the virtue of his
inspired power, driving out certain wretched and base demons that lie in wait
for souls and bodies of men by the mere unspeakable name of his, as we have
grasped by the experience itself. For to seek these things concerning
Apollonius would be foolish even to ask. Let us examine only the writing of
Philostratus, through which we will bring him to account, as not worthy to
rank 373 him among philosophers, nor even among decent and moderate men, much
less to set Apollonius beside our Saviour Christ, so far as concerns the
writing of the author who in the Lover of Truth has come to the height of
education but does not honour the truth. For such, among the others, was also
Philostratus the Athenian for him, from whom we may easily learn also of the
rest of those of his sort who have come to the height of education but have
not been accurately exact in the examined history concerning Apollonius. For
when these things have been distinguished for us, at once the firm and, as it
seems to himself, accurate judgment on each point against Christians of the
Lover of Truth who has taken the highest and general courts will be made
clear, and again of us, who are thought by them fools and beguiled, the
readiness and lightness charged against us by them. Another then, going at him
at once, would have slandered him from the spot, blaspheming as an enemy and
foe the one against whom he made the discourse; but I, my companion, used to
think the man of Tyana had become someone wise in human things, and I would
still wish of my own will to hold to the same mind, and I would set out to
you, if you asked, my own opinion about him: if anyone should wish to count
the man among philosophers, troubling himself no further with the mythologies
about him, I would not grudge it; but if he should leap over the bounds and
think things above philosophy, whether some Damis the Assyrian, or
Philostratus,

or some historian or speech-writer should grow bold, turning aside sorcery in
word, but in deeds more than in words loading the man, putting on like a mask
the Pythagorean way of life, the philosopher will be gone from us, and an ass
hiding in a 374 lion's skin, some sophist truly gathering crowds through the
cities, will be caught as this very thing, a sorcerer outright instead of a
philosopher. And how do I say this, and from where do I start, you ask? Learn.
Bounds of nature, encompassing beginnings and middles and ends of the being of
the whole, measures and ordinances for all, through which this entire
contrivance and architecture of the whole cosmos is brought to completion,
have been arranged, keeping the all-wise will of the providence that manages
all things by unlooseable laws and unbreakable bonds. No one then could move
or shift anything of the order of the things once arranged. And so everyone in
whom there is a bolder desire to step upon the things beyond is ruled by a law
of nature as overstepping the divine law. Neither then will the fish that
lives in water, going over to the dry land, be able to live contrary to
nature, nor will what was reared on dry land sink down into the waters,
embracing a continual stay there, nor will anyone of those on earth, leaping
great, be lifted aloft into air, desiring to go about with eagles; and yet
those at least could come upon the earth, weighting their power toward the
below and slackening the wing and letting go their nature, since this too has
been defined by divine laws, that what is aloft can come down from the height,
but not conversely that the low and earthy be raised to the height. In this
way also the mortal race of humans, sharing in soul and body, has been marked
off by divine bounds. Neither then would anyone of those on earth ever go
through air with the body, despising the sojourns on earth, without at once
paying the penalty of the folly, nor, lifting the soul in conceit, will he sit
among the unattainable, or he would fall into a disease of melancholy. 375 He
would be sober if he is borne on the earth with sound feet for the body, but
props the soul with education and philosophy, and he would pray also that some
helper come from somewhere above from the sojourns in heaven and appear to him
as a teacher of the salvation there. For by a strong example it is lawful for
the physician to visit one who is sick, and for the teacher to join one being
introduced to the lessons, and for the one somewhere above and in height to
come down with the low, but not also conversely. Whence then no argument would
bar a divine nature, being a benefactress and saviour and provident of the
things that come upon humans, from coming at some time into converse, this too
having been granted by a bound of divine providence, for he was good according
to Plato, and in a good man envy arises about nothing; therefore the one who
steers this whole, being good, will not care for bodies only, but far more for
souls, to whom the prize of being immortal and self-ruled has been granted. To
these then, as being lord of the whole economy and of the graces with which he
benefits them by giving, since they are of a nature able to receive, he will
grant ungrudgingly, as it were, rays of the light from himself of those about
him, sending out at times those most close at hand for the salvation and help
of those on this side, of whom if it should fall to anyone to be fortunate,
this one, having had the mind purified and having scattered the mist of
mortality, will truly be written down as divine, bearing a great god as a
statue in the soul. Such a one then would move the race of all humans by a
mind, and more than the sun would light the inhabited world, leaving the work
of the everlasting godhead to be seen also into the coming time, having
furnished the pattern of the inspired nature no less than the 376 products
from lifeless matter. And in this way human nature might share in what is
above human, but otherwise it is not lawful to overstep the bounds, nor,
having a wingless body, to practise the things of birds, nor, being a human,
the things of demons

to busy oneself with. Whom then after these do you bring in to us as
Apollonius, O writer? If indeed a divine one and better than a philosopher
and, to speak simply, a nature above human, keep the thesis for me through the
whole history and still even now display the work of the godhead. For is it
not most absurd that the work of carpenters and builders, even after the death
of the craftsmen, should last for a very long time, almost immortal, posting
up the memory of those who established it, but a divine nature that has shone
upon humans should accomplish something dark and short-lived, and not display
the virtue unto the age, not begging of humans toward some one Damis or even a
few others, but making its coming for the benefit of countless people, not
only of those in the time when it was known, but also of those who will be
born afterward? In this way also the wise men of old made zealots and
successors of the virtue, having furnished to humans a truly immortal
achievement. But if you sketch the man's nature as mortal, see that, granting
more to it than is fitting, you will pay the penalty of absurdity. Yet he has
been brought in by him as a divine man, taking on from birth the form and mask
of a sea demon. "For as she was with child," as he says, "a phantom of a sea
demon came to his mother, Proteus who in Homer changes. And she, fearing
nothing, 377 asked him what she would bring forth, and he said 'me'; and when
she said 'who are you?' he said 'Proteus the Egyptian.'" Then he writes that a
certain meadow, and swans, midwife the woman, not saying from where this very
thing was taken; for he will not set down Damis the Assyrian as a guide of
this too. But not long into the same history he brings in Apollonius to this
same Damis as being of a divine nature, saying these very things: "I, my
companion, understand all languages, having learned none"; and "do not wonder,
for I know also what humans are silent about." And again he sketches him as
honoured in the temple of Asclepius by the demon and as having from a child a
certain natural and untaught foreknowledge and as born outright better than a
human from the very birth and through the whole history for us. When at any
rate he once loosed himself from bonds he adds, saying: "Then first Damis says
he accurately understood the nature of Apollonius, that it was divine and
better than human. For without sacrificing anything, for how in a prison? and
without praying, and without saying anything, he laughed at the bond." And at
the end he says that a tomb of him is found nowhere on earth at all, but that
he went into heaven with his body itself with hymns and a dance. Reasonably
then, as being so great, he says "the man came to philosophy more divine than
Pythagoras and Empedocles and Plato." Therefore through these let the human be
written down among gods for us, and let envy be away from the self-grown and
self-taught understanding of all languages. Why then does he lead him to a
teacher and slander the one who has learned no language as having become Attic
in tongue from practice and study, but not from nature? 378 For he says, you
know, that as he went on toward age he showed letters and strength of memory
and power of study, and his tongue was Attic. And "when he was fourteen years
the father leads him to Tarsus to Euthydemus from Phoenicia, who was a good
rhetor and educated this one, and he clung to the teacher." Then "Platonists
and Chrysippeans and those from the Peripatos philosophized with him. He also
heard the words of Epicurus, for he did not neglect these either, but he took
in the Pythagoreans by a certain unspeakable wisdom." So much was the one who
learned no language, and who by divine power had grasped in advance "what
humans are silent about," being educated. And the writer, pausing, again
admires him, to what a point of understanding of the language of animals he
came, and he adds saying "and he came also to understanding of the language of
animals, and he learned this as he travelled through the country of the
Arabians, who know and practise it best. For it is

possible already among the Arabians to hear swans and birds prophesying, as
much as the oracles. And they come to an understanding of the speechless, some
say, by eating the heart of serpents, others the liver." Here surely it was
likely that the Pythagorean, who abstains from food of living things and does
not even dare to sacrifice to demons, should taste of the heart of serpents,
no doubt, and of liver, so that he might share also in the wisdom among these.
For when one is taught by such, how else would the practice succeed, if he did
not emulate the like things to the teachers? We have then, besides those
listed, also the wise men of the Arabians as teachers of the man's science in
bird-augury, starting from which reasonably he later at some time, by telling
in advance that the sparrow also 379 wished, calling the others together to
food, seemed to furnish a very great wonder to those present. In this way
also, seeing the lioness just then slaughtered together with the eight cubs on
the road to the Assyrians, at once matching the sight he prophesied the time
of the stay that would be his among the Persians. And the same writer relates
that he attempted things following upon the schooling among the Arabians also
among the Persians; for forbidding Damis, who was his only pupil and
companion, to go to the magi, he himself alone, so that he might not learn the
things of magic with one without sense, of course, made his sessions with them
at midday and around midnight. And further, when he came to speech with
Vardanes the Babylonian king, he sets him down as speaking somehow thus: "My
wisdom is of Pythagoras of Samos, a man who taught me to serve gods in this
way and to understand them, both those seen and those not seen, and to go into
discourses of gods." Who would grant him these things, when no such writing
has been left by Pythagoras, nor any secret books, so that one might even
suspect him to start from those, and his teacher of the philosophy according
to Pythagoras has been witnessed by Philostratus himself to differ in nothing
from the Epicureans, speaking somehow thus: "His teacher of the words of
Pythagoras was not very serious, nor using philosophy in action, but a slave
of belly and of love-matters and fashioned after Epicurus. And this was
Euxenus from Heraclea of Pontus. He knew the maxims of Pythagoras as the birds
learn them from humans." Alas for the absurdity, if someone should say he had
taken from 380 this man the understanding of discourse toward the gods. But
let it even be granted that he heard other guides, although the writer has in
no way marked this; which then of these did he himself, as having learned from
Pythagoras, profess to know understanding and going into discourses of gods
seen and not seen, and to teach others? And indeed not even the famous Plato,
who of all shared most in the philosophy of Pythagoras, nor Archytas, nor that
very one who handed down the conversations of Pythagoras in writing,
Philolaus, nor if any others became acquaintances of the man, who have handed
down his opinions and maxims in writing to those after him, prided themselves
on any such wisdom. Therefore having learned such things from elsewhere, but
not from Pythagoras, he ascribes to himself with a pretence, talking solemnly,
the address of philosopher; and even if someone should most unreasonably grant
that the false has become as true, I do not know how he says he learned the
things about these from that very Samian who passed from humans countless
years before. Therefore besides the Arabians this one too must be registered
as his teacher of the supposed secret science about gods. If then he was of a
divine nature, the account has lied about his teachers; but if this was true,
the myth was false and the writing about his having become divine was not
true. It does not yet occur to me to busy myself with the phantom of Proteus
and to ask for the faith about this, nor of the

swans' proofs, whom he relates, ridiculously, midwifed his mother as she bore
him, nor do I think it right to furnish the witness of the mythology about the
thunderbolt, 381 for of these things too, as I said, Damis will not come to us
as a joint witness, having joined the man long afterward at Nineveh in
Assyria. I then, being very readily persuaded by things likely and holding to
truth, even if some greater things should be said by way of excess in praise
of a good man, think them credible and acceptable to me, provided they are not
only the monstrous and full of nonsense. I would not then grudge it, if the
writer says that an ancient race, attached to the founders, belonged to the
man, and that he was rich, if it so chanced, beyond all the natives, and that
when young he met not only the teachers shown, but, if it seems good, that he
himself became teacher and guide of these too. Let him besides these be clever
also in common things, as that he released from the illness, through a most
excellent opinion, the one who had come to the temple of Asclepius for the
sake of healing, for to one sick with a disease of dropsy he suggested a diet
of self-control suited to the sufferer and by this set him healthy, and in
this at least the youth is acceptable for good sense, and as he barred the one
prepared to sacrifice expensively, as was likely, being notorious for vice,
for he posits him as richest and most conspicuous of all the natives. And
there is no envy against enrolling him among the sober, both because he railed
at the lover who tried his youth, and because he kept himself pure of
intercourse with women to the end, as the account says. Let the things of his
five-year silence according to Pythagoras also be credible, and the things,
how he accomplished this, worthy of praise. These then and as many such as are
human and truly not far from philosophy and truth I would accept, honouring
greatly the 382 fair-minded and truth-loving; but to posit the nature as
better than human and at the feet to contradict and to be unmindful of the
thesis given, I think will attach blame and slander both to the writer himself
and much sooner to the one written about. And these things from the first
book. Let us go on also to the things from the second. The account, taking
him, leads the journey from Persia to the Indians. Then, having suffered
something tasteless, as if some marvel, he says that seeing a certain demon on
the road, which he also names an empusa, he drove it off with railings
together with those about him, and that when animals were brought to them for
food he said to Damis that he would grant to him and to the companions to eat
of the meats, for the abstaining from them looked to no advance for them, but
for himself to what had been agreed toward philosophy from a child. And yet
who would not be at a loss, that he did not even bar the one whom he seemed to
have as the only zealot of the life and whom he urged toward philosophy, as a
dearest friend, from the food of living things, as being unholy according to
Pythagoras, but he, I do not know by what account, while saying it would not
go with himself, confessed that for them he saw nothing advancing from the
abstinence? Upon these Philostratus, who has been witnessed by the Lover of
Truth to honour the true, see what sort of proofs of the truth he presents: he
says that Apollonius, when he was among the Indians, provided himself an
interpreter and through him conversed with Phraotes, and that this is the name
of the king of the Indians, and the one who a little before, according to him,
understood all languages now again according to the same needs an interpreter.
And again the one who knew the things according to the mind 383 of humans and
all but, after the god among them, understood the deaf and heard the one not
speaking, asks through an interpreter what sort of diet the king has, and
thinks it right that someone become a guide for him of the journey to the
Brahmans. And in between the king of the Indians, and this though barbarian in
nature, putting the interpreter out of the way, uses Greek converse toward
him, displaying education and much learning, but he not even so, that he
himself too was not unlearned of the

language among them, as he ought, was ambitious, but even when the Indian
spoke in the Greek tongue he is struck with amazement, as Philostratus says,
writing things consistent, as it seems, with himself. For how would he have
been amazed if he had not thought him a barbarian? And having admired such a
one he would never have expected him to speak Greek; then, as if struck by
some marvel handed over and still not knowing the cause of this, "Tell me,"
said Apollonius, "O king, from where do you have the Greek language so? And
the philosophy about you, from where is it here? For you do not, I think,
refer it to teachers, since it is not likely that there are teachers of this
for Indians." These are the marvellous utterances of the one who has taken all
things by foreknowledge, which the king answering tells that he had teachers
and who these were and each of the things that had happened about him from his
father. Then also when the Indian is judging for some about a treasure found
in a field, whether one ought to assign it to the buyer or to the one who sold
the place, the all-philosopher and man pleasing to the gods, asked, decides
for the buyer, adding a reasoning in these very words, "that the gods would
not have taken away even the land from the one, if he were not 384 base, and
again would not have given even the things under the earth to the other, if he
were not better than the seller." So that according to him it is gathered that
the well-off and those differing in wealth, even if they should be most
shameful and utterly ruined, are to be thought thrice-blessed and dear to the
gods, and only the poorest are ill-fated and wretched, even if it should be
Socrates, even if Diogenes, even if that very Pythagoras, even if another, the
most sober and most decent of all men. For someone following the account would
say that according to him the gods would not have taken from the poor, these
very ones who differed in philosophy, even the things toward necessary food,
unless they were altogether base in character, and would not have furnished to
those more unrestrained in habit abundant things, even the unnecessary, unless
they happened to be better than the former, from which the absurdity of the
conclusion is clear to everyone. Having set these also from the second, let us
go to the third, to look at the things concerning the much-talked-of Brahmans;
where the things beyond Thule that are unbelievable, and if anything else
monstrous has ever been fabricated by certain myth-tellers, will appear very
credible and most true, as in comparison with these, to which it is worth
setting the mind because of the arrogance of the Lover of Truth, who attaches
to us readiness and lightness of manner, but to himself and those like him the
accurate and firm judgment with understanding. See at any rate on what marvels
he prides himself, preferring Philostratus to our divine evangelists, as not
only having come to the height of education but also having taken care for
truth. First then on the journey of Apollonius toward the Brahmans a woman 385
is brought in to him, in colour from the head to the loins most white, but the
other part of the body black, and as they go forward on the road to the
Brahmans mountains planted with pepper and apes farmers of this, and certain
serpents paradoxical in size, from whose head torches of fire are thrown, whom
if anyone should kill, he would find, he says, marvellous stones in the heads,
like that of Gyges in Plato. And these are the things before the hill of the
Brahmans. And when they came also to it, a well of sandarach there, of
paradoxical water to see, and a mixing-bowl of fire nearby, whose flame is
sent up leaden, and two jars of black stone, one of rains, the other of winds,
from which the Brahmans supply those to whom they would be gracious in the
country. But also statues among them of Athena Polias and Apollo Pythius and
Dionysus of the Marshes, and of certain other Greek gods, and Iarchas is
called the teacher over all, whom he also saw sitting in a shape far from
philosophical, but rather satrap-like, upon a most high chair, and this was of
black bronze and

was wrought with golden statues, as it is likely that the philosophers, in the
manner of vulgar craftsmen, labour with fire and iron to craft them, or after
the manner of wonder-workers accomplish the workmanship of itself, but the
chairs of the others, of the teachers after him, were of bronze, he says, but
unmarked and less high. For it was necessary, I suppose, that the teacher of
the divine philosophy be thought worthy, in the shape of a tyrant, of
precedence in statues and gold. And first he says that Iarchas, seeing
Apollonius, both addressed him by name in the Greek language and asked for the
386 letter which he was bringing to him from Phraotes, having already grasped
this by foreknowledge, and displayed the inspired character of the
foreknowledge by saying beforehand that the letter lacked one letter, the
delta, and began tastelessly at once from the first converse, after the manner
of one inexperienced in wealth, to show off the advantage of the
foreknowledge, listing father and mother of Apollonius and race and rearing
and education and the journeys abroad according to times and the journey to
himself and the things done and spoken by him on it. Then this same wonderful
writer says that the Brahmans together with Apollonius, having anointed
themselves with an amber drug, bathed and, standing around as in a chorus,
struck the earth with their rods, and the earth, arched, sent them up two
cubits into the air, and they stood aloft in the air itself for some interval
of time, and they also draw fire from the sun, when they wish, of itself. To
these the wonder-teller adds a marvel, that four Pythian tripods were wheeled
out of themselves, going about, and he likens them then to the Homeric ones,
and upon these he sets four cupbearers of bronze as servants in material, and
he adds that the earth of itself strewed grass under them. And of the tripods
two, he says, flowed with wine, and of the other two one furnished a mix of
hot water, the other again of cold. And the bronze cupbearers drew in good
measure of the wine and of the water, and drove the cups around in a circle,
as in drinking-bouts. These things to Hierocles, who has been entrusted with
the highest and general courts, after much examination seem to be true and
credible, and of us readiness and lightness 387 in very great measure have
been condemned by him, but he himself, trusting such things to Philostratus,
prides himself in these very words, saying "Let us consider, however, how much
better and more intelligent is the way we take such things, and the opinion we
have about virtuous men." Upon such a symposium then, according to the same
Philostratus, a king sojourning among the Indians is brought in drinking with
the philosophers, and he relates that this one insults and in his cups
outrages philosophy and gets drunk among them and matches himself against
Helios and boasts, and again that Apollonius learns the things from this one
through an interpreter and again converses toward him with Iarchas
interpreting underneath; and how is it not worth wondering how it was likely

the same teachers as he, as you say, both shone in the times when they were
known and have left to those afterward the virtue of their philosophy sung,
unless perhaps, attempting things beyond what is fitting, he stood clear to
those who think well, at once there are of those now who say they have grasped
meddlesome devices laid up in the name of the man. But it is not dear to me at
least to set the mind on these. But also about the end of the man, composing
things following the former, he showed forth that he knew nothing true, for
some 408 relate that he ended in Ephesus, others in Lindos having gone into
the temple of Athena, others in Crete, and having poured out so great an
ignorance of the end about him he wishes him to have gone into heaven with his
body itself. For he says that when he had run into a temple the gates were
shut and a certain unexpected song of maidens fell out, and the song was: "Go,
go into heaven, go." And he says that he has not yet met with a tomb or a
cenotaph of the man, although he said he had gone over the earth, as much as
it is (most of it), and his mind wishes that the man not touch death at all,
for first doubting about the manner in which he ended he says "if indeed he
ended," and later he says outright also that he went into heaven. Whence as
being such a one both according to the preface of the whole writing and
according to the whole writing he says he came to philosophy more divine than
Pythagoras and Empedocles. But as the account is marked off in these, come let
us take in a few things about Fates and destiny, distinguishing what the
account also wishes through the whole thesis for him, taking away what is in
our power, but bringing in necessity and destiny and Fates, for in this way
the false opinion of the man in doctrines will also be brought to account for
us completely. If then according to the account of the true philosophy every
soul is immortal, for the ever-moving is immortal, and that which moves
another and is moved by another, having a pause of motion, has a pause of
life, and the cause is of the one who chooses, God is without cause, what
account takes it that the ever-moving nature, unwilling, and not according to
choice, like a lifeless body moved from somewhere outside and as if pulled by
strings this way 409 and that, should be led, in no way at all acting from its
own impulse and motion, nor bringing back upon itself the cause of the things
done, and in this way neither being praiseworthy when philosophizing nor again
blameable when full of vice and wickedness? Why then, railing at Euphrates, do
you blame him, my good man, if not from himself, but from destiny giving
himself over to gain, as you yourself think it right, he made little of
philosophy? And why do you insult sorcerers, calling them false-wise, as being
dragged by Fates, as you think, upon the ill-fated life? And why do you name
vice simply, and some wicked one of humans is not judged in justice by you,
fulfilling the bound fated from necessity? And conversely by what account,
talking solemnly of Pythagoras, do you register him as a wonderful teacher,
and a plaything of Fates, but not

being a lover of philosophy do you not leave off praising him, and Phraotes
and Iarchas the philosophers of the Indians, why rather have they carried off
a reputation of gods with you, having carried off nothing of their own of
education, nor a fame of virtue? And of Nero likewise and of Domitian why do
you not attach to Fates and necessity the unrestrained insolence, freeing the
men from every cause and every charge? But also if it has been fated for
someone, as you say, to be of racing and of archery and of carpentry, so too
if being a sorcerer in manner he is to appear a magus and a murderer and
wicked and unrestrained, altogether I suppose from necessity such a one will
turn out. Why then going about do you proclaim virtue to those not able to
obtain correction? Or why do you blame those most absurd in the fate, but not
in the choice? And why also, if it was fated for you yourself, being divine in
nature, to rise above a reputation of kings, did you go to tea 410 chers and
philosophers and busy yourself with Arabians and magi of Babylonians and wise
men of Indians? For altogether I suppose even without the sharing of these the
things from the Fates were accomplished for you. And why also to those you
think gods do you throw the honey-cakes and the frankincense in vain, and
putting on piety do you urge the companions to turn to prayers? And yourself
praying, what do you ask from gods, when you confess that destiny rules even
these? And indeed you ought, having passed by the other gods, to sacrifice
only to Necessity and Fates and to honour destiny more than Zeus himself. And
so for you there would no longer be gods, and reasonably, inasmuch as they are
not even able to benefit humans. But also if it was fated that the Ephesian
citizens be taken by a plague, why legislating the opposites do you cheat
destiny? Or rather how did you rise above Fate, having taken up as it were a
trophy against her? And if the thread of Clotho had taken an end upon the
girl, from where from the beginning after death, having bound the spindle to
the thread, have you appeared as a life-maker for her? But perhaps Fates led
you yourself too upon these. You will not yet say according to worth, far from
it, you who say that before the access into this body you had been of those
who spend time in sea and waves, but from necessity, as this too is likely.
Not then wonderful either for the first birth and rearing, or for the
encyclical education, or for the sober leading in the prime, or for the
practice in philosophy, there was then some necessity of Fates driving even to
Babylonians, and being pushed as it were you also conversed with the wise of
the Indians, and upon the Naked ones of the Egyptians not the choice, nor the
desire of philosophy, 411 but Fate led, choking, and to Gadeira and the
pillars of Heracles and to wander the eastern and western Ocean and forced the
very spindles to be turned around in vain. And if then someone should say that
he had taken a share of some wisdom from these, Fate is cause of these too and
the man would no longer be listed among the lovers of learning, nor would he
reasonably be admired for the philosophy furnished to him not according to
judgment but according to necessity. And in equal he would be compared
according to him, Pythagoras himself and some monstrous and cast-off slave,
Socrates himself dying for philosophy and those who wrote him as worthy of
death, Diogenes and the youths of the Athenians, and to speak simply the most
wise would not differ from the most senseless and the most unjust from the
most just and the most unrestrained from the most sober and the most cowardly
from the most manly, all these having been shown playthings of destiny and
Fates. But toward these the herald of the truth will cry out saying: O humans,
mortal and perishable race, where then are you borne, having drunk the unmixed
of ignorance? Cease at last and sober from the drunkenness and with right eyes
of mind behold as in a mirror the solemn face of the truth. It is not lawful
that truth war with itself and fight, nor that of two most opposite things one
and the same cause stand. Providence of God ruling all things, the whole has
been arranged by divine laws, and a bound of the soul of humans

sets himself as self-ruled and judge and leader and lord of himself, teaching
by natural laws and by doctrines of philosophers that of the things that are
some are in our power, others not in our power, and in our power are as many
as would come about 412 according to choice and action, which also by nature
happen to be free, unhindered, unimpeded, but the things not in our power
weak, slavish, hinderable, alien, which also about the body and the things
outside, being both lifeless and speechless, have the subsistence altogether
alien from the own nature of the rational animal. And of the things in our
power each has acquired in the choice itself the impulse upon either side of
virtue and vice, and that which masters and leads the whole at once
accomplishes, going about according to nature, and justice always follows it,
avenger of those who fall away from the divine law, and of the impulses upon
the actions neither Fates nor destiny nor necessity is cause; the cause is of
the one who chooses, God is without cause. If then someone should grow bold,
warring against what is in our power, let this one not veil himself; let him
declare atheism openly, confessing no providence, no god, nor anything else
except Fates and necessity, and let him list besides with uncovered head the
things following these, let him call no one wise, no one senseless, no one
just, no one unjust, no one virtuous, no one base, no one a sorcerer, no one
divine in the nature of humans, let him say there is no philosophy, no
education, nor any art at all, nor science, nor any other good or wicked in
nature, but that all things in a lump are whirled around by necessity and by
spindles of Fates. Let this one then be written down as atheist and impious in
a court of the pious and of philosophers, but if, veiling himself, he should
attempt to opine other things, providence and gods, and someone upon these
should proclaim Fate and destiny, standing by fighting and opposite doctrines,
let him be written down as having paid the penalty of folly among the
senseless. These things then thus. But if upon these in schools of
philosophers 413 some should still think it right to list the man, it will be
said that if they should cleanse him of the outside filth, and also of the
gear wheeled in upon him from this writing, all envy would be out of their
way, but if going forward beyond bounds of truth someone should try to deify
him beyond philosophers, he would escape his own notice rubbing onto him a
slander of a sorcerer outright, since these writings, fabricated
sophistically, seem to me at least to contain nothing except refutation and a
terrible slander of the man among those who have sense.
