* On Leo the Armenian
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Concerning the emperor Nikephoros and how he left his limbs in Bulgaria. In
the ninth year of the reign of the emperor Nikephoros, this same emperor
Nikephoros entered Bulgaria, wishing, as he supposed, to destroy it. He took
with him his son Staurakios and his son-in-law Michael, who was also surnamed
Rangabe, and all the patricians and commanders and officials, and all the
tagmata, and the sons of the commanders who were fifteen years of age and
above, whom he also made a company for his son, naming them Hikanatoi.

As he was entering the kleisourai, the Bulgars, hearing the multitude of the
host that they were bringing, as if they could not resist, left all that they
had and fled to the mountains. And he himself, going in, encamped in the court
of the ruler of Bulgaria, named Krum, and finding a certain army of chosen
Bulgars under arms, who had remained for the guard of the place, as many as
twelve thousand, he joined battle with them and killed them all. Likewise
again another fifty thousand who met him, engaging with them, he destroyed
them all.

Then the wretched man, lifted up in mind and heart, as if he had already done
this through his own righteous deeds, which indeed he also said to those who
were with him: "See," he said, "what righteousness accomplishes." Entering
then into the court of Krum, searching his treasuries and finding very great
spoils, he began to distribute to his host by a register bronze and garments
and certain other various goods, and opening the storehouses of his wines he
distributed them to all his own, so that they might drink their fill. And
going up into the quarters of the court, moving about in the sun-rooms of the
dwellings, he exulted and said: "See, God has delivered all these things to
me, and I wish to found a city here in my name, that I may become famous unto
all the generations to come."

And after spending some days he went out from the court of the godless Krum,
and as he went out he burned all the dwellings together with the enclosure,
which was made of wood. And thereafter, no longer taking thought to go out in
haste, he passed through the midst of Bulgaria, wishing to go as far as
Sardica, thinking that he had wiped out all Bulgaria. And after spending
fifteen days he wholly neglected the affairs against him, and his wits being
turned and becoming like one beside himself, he was no longer in his right
mind, but was altogether confused, held by the stupor of arrogance, and not
coming out from his tent, nor giving to anyone a word or a command. And when
some cried out against him and sent his son to speak to him about going out
from there, he paid no heed at all, but rather even dishonored his son,
wishing even to strike him. The host then, finding opportunity, plundered
without sparing, burning the lands that were unharvested, and hamstringing the
oxen and tearing straps from their loins, the beasts crying with a great voice
and struggling, and slaughtering the sheep and the swine, and doing things
that are not lawful.

Then some, seeing the disorder and the unsteadiness of Nikephoros, and that no
one dared to speak to him, began little by little to run away and to go out by
some device. And the Bulgars had made a fearful barrier, hard to pass through,
of great timbers after the manner of a wall.

Then the Bulgars, taking opportunity and seeing from the mountains that they
were being carried about wandering, hiring Avars and the neighboring
Sklaviniai, and arming the women in manly fashion, on the fifteenth day of
their entry, at the dawning of Saturday, the twenty-third of the month of
July, fell upon them while they were still almost asleep. These, rising and
arming themselves in haste, began the battle. And since the themes were
encamped far from one another, they did not at once know what had happened;
for they fell only upon the imperial fossaton, and then they began to be cut
down. And when they had resisted a little and were able to do nothing, but
were being slaughtered exceedingly, the rest, seeing this, gave themselves to
flight. And in that same place there lay a river very marshy and hard to pass.
And not finding at once a ford by which to cross, being pursued by the enemy
they fell into the river. And entering with their horses and not being able to
get out they sank into the marsh, and being trampled by those coming behind,
and falling one upon another, the river was so filled with men and horses that
the enemy passed over them unharmed and pursued the rest who, as was likely,
thought that they had been saved. There then all the patricians and the rest
of the commanders fell.

And as many as seemed to have escaped from the harm of the river came as far
as the barrier which the Bulgars had constructed, being strong and very hard
to pass through; and not being able with their horses to cut through it,
leaving their horses, crawling up with their own hands and feet, they hurled
themselves down to the other side.

And since there was outside a digging of a deep ditch, being hurled from the
height, their limbs were torn apart; and some died at once, and others going a
little forward and not being able to walk fell to the ground, and so being
punished they died in hunger and thirst. And in other places some set fire to
the palisade; and when the bindings had been burned, and the palisade had
fallen together over the digging, then the fugitives, unexpectedly sinking,
fell into the ditch of the fiery digging, both they and their horses. And this
was a worse disaster than the danger of the river. Who will not weep hearing
these things? Who will not lament?

And the children of the commanders, both of old and of young, being in
multitude, bearing the very flower of their age, having bodies very fair,
shining with whiteness and with the blondness of hair and of beards, and with
a countenance of well-set beauty, of whom some had been newly yoked to wives
shining in nobility and beauty, all died there: some consumed by the sword,
others drowned in the river, others hurled down from the barrier, others
burned up by the fire of the digging, and a few being saved, and even these,
after coming home, almost all died. On that same day also the emperor
Nikephoros was slain in the midst of the first battle, no one being able to
report the manner of his death. And his son Staurakios also was struck,
receiving a mortal wound in the vertebrae of the spine, from which he also
died, having reigned over the Romans only two months. And many of the Romans
who were taken alive, after the battle was ended, were compelled by the
godless Bulgars, who had not yet then been baptized, to deny Christ and to
partake of the pagan and Scythian error; who, being strengthened by the power
of Christ, enduring every torment, bound on the martyr's crown by various
punishments.

Thus the emperor Nikephoros, from ill counsel and arrogance, destroyed both
himself and all the strength of the Romans, having reigned eight years and
seven months. And this man was of two cubits, broad, pot-bellied,
thick-haired, with projecting lips, having a large face and a great beard that
had gone quite grey, and thick in body, and very shrewd and cunning and sharp
to understand, especially in the affairs of the public treasury, and both
parsimonious and a lover of money to excess. Wherefore he also inherited
eternal destruction.

But we, brethren, let us remember our brothers and fathers who have gone
before, and let us entreat our good and just God to deliver us from such a
condemnation, and through care of the divine commandments of Christ to attain
the goods promised to the righteous; for he is blessed and glorified unto the
ages of ages. Amen.
