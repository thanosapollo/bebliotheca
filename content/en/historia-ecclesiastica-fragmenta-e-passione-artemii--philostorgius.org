2.4β Artemii Passio 43 Julian's speech:

For Constantine, as you yourself also know, being found easy to deceive among
men, both ignorant and foolish, made innovation in religion, and having set
aside the laws of the Romans, turned aside to Christianity, terrified by his
unholy deeds, and because the gods drove him off as accursed and unworthy of
their religion, being filled with kindred blood. For he killed his brothers
who had done nothing improper, and his wife Fausta, and his own son Priscus,
who was a good and excellent man. The gods therefore, abhorring these unholy
works, drove him off and led him far away and far astray from their sacred and
most holy religion, and they made his accursed and utterly polluted seed and
his whole race vanish from among men.

Ibid. 45 Artemius' reply: But because you mocked the blessed Constantine, who
was above all emperors, and his race, calling him an enemy to your gods and
mad and full of murder, and filled with kindred blood, these things I will
plead for him in his defense: that rather your father Constantius and his
brothers began the wrong, mixing a deadly poison for him and procuring a
destructive death, having suffered nothing unjust from him. But he killed his
wife Fausta quite justly, as one who imitated the Phaedra of old and slandered
his son Priscus as being disposed erotically toward her and attempting
something by force, just as that woman did Theseus' Hippolytus; and indeed
according to the laws of nature as a father he punished the son. Later
however, having learned the truth, he killed her as well, having judged upon
her a judgment most just of all.

2.16α on l. 1 / p. 27 Artemii Passio 7: The Christ-loving emperor Constantine
then, having come to the thirty-first year of his reign and entering upon the
second, when he learned that the Persians were preparing for war against him,
setting out from his own city reached as far as Nicomedia of the Bithynians;
and there he ended his life by a plot of his own brothers, who poured deadly
poison into him, a comet-star, as they say, having foretold his death.
Constantine had these brothers on his father's side: Dalmatius, Anaballianus,
and Constantius; for he alone was born to his father Constantius from Helena
while he was still a private citizen, but from Theodora, the daughter of
Maximian surnamed Herculius, other sons were born to him, both the
aforementioned Dalmatius and Anaballianus and Constantius; whom Constantine
also honored as Caesars and nobilissimi. Of these Constantius, from the wife
joined to him, begets both Gallus and Julian who was surnamed the Apostate
because he denied Christ and turned aside to the Hellenic religion.

Ibid. 41 Julian's speech You know ... that the empire is more fitting for our
family; for my father Constantius was born from Theodora, daughter of
Maximian, to my grandfather Constantius; but Constantine was born to him from
Helena, a certain base woman differing in nothing from the streetwalkers, and
this when he had not yet become Caesar but was still in the station of a
private citizen. Constantine therefore by boldness of mind seized the empire
and unjustly killed my father and both his brothers.

3.1α Artemii Passio 8: When the great Constantine had just died, the rule of
the Romans was divided into three rules, his sons Constantine, Constantius,
and Constans sharing them. And to the first, Constantine, the upper Gauls and
the lands beyond the Alps and the British islands and as far as the western
Ocean were given as his lot; but to Constans as the youngest the lower Gauls,
that is the Italies and Rome itself. But Constantius, the second of
Constantine's sons, who was then engaged in the affairs of the East, fighting
against the Persians, takes as his portion the East; and Byzantium, renamed
Constantinople and made New Rome, he makes his imperial seat, and as many as
were subject to the Romans from Illyricum as far as the Propontis, and Syria
and Palestine and Mesopotamia and Egypt and all the islands, he makes
tributary to his empire and government.

Ibid. 9: When therefore, as has been said, there were three emperors and each
of them was reigning over his own portion, the first of them, Constantine,
departing from his own share and coming upon the inheritance of the last
brother, that one having gone away to Rome, attempted to accomplish something
unjust against his brother. And he slandered him in his absence, saying that
the affairs had not been well distributed, and that he had appropriated the
greater part of the rule belonging to himself. But the generals and guards of
the country whom Constans had appointed said they could not change anything
small or great without that man's judgment and counsel; for it was unholy. But
he strips for war and moves arms against one who had done no wrong.
Constantine therefore falls fighting in the war, and desiring the share of
others, he lost in addition even what he seemed to hold securely.

§ 10 His people then turn to Constans, and the whole rule of the West comes
under that man who had made no effort concerning it, God having judged these
things, who said: "Do not move the landmarks of your fathers, nor lay hold of
your neighbor's furrow." For he who works evil against his neighbor brings
destruction upon himself, drawing God's judgment upon himself. Constans
therefore reigns over the whole western empire, joining the two inheritances
into one and establishing both parts as one rule.

3.2α Artemii Passio 17: But he who writes the history says such things
concerning Constantius and the martyr (namely Artemius): for it is said
concerning Constantius that he was not only earnest and beloved in the things
toward God, even if he inclined toward the Arian heresy, driven by the impious
and most godless Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia; since in other respects he was
moderate and taking thought especially for seemliness and holding fast to
temperance to the highest degree, both in diet and in the rest of his manner,
and he showed very great zeal toward the churches, ambitious to surpass his
own father by far in eagerness concerning these things. And he built the
greatest church in his father's city near the senate, beginning the work from
below and from the foundations. And honoring his father's tomb, he built there
a greatest temple as a place of worship; and bringing the apostle Andrew from
Achaia, as I said before, he transferred him there; and indeed he also
transferred Luke the evangelist from the same Achaia and Timothy from Ephesus
in Ionia.

3.22α-26a Artemii Passio 10 immediately after στησάμενος above, p. 30, 28: Not
much time in between and Constans, having turned aside into revels and
drunkenness and strange courses of loves, was playing at dice with the whole
empire carelessly, dancing away the greatness of the imperial office; he is
therefore plotted against also himself by a certain one of the generals,
Magnentius, and together with the empire he loses his life as well. When this
man had fallen, Magnentius holds the rule, with whom Nepotianus and Vetranio
joined in the tyranny.

§ 11 Constantius, having learned these things from his sister's letters,
setting out from the East and coming to the West, joins battle with both and
conquers by force, Vetranio having turned to him; when also the sign of the
cross, appearing very great and terribly, so as to outshine the light of day
by the astonishing brilliance, was seen over Jerusalem about the third hour of
the day especially, the feast called Pentecost being at hand, stretching from
the place called the Skull as far as the Mount of Olives, whence the Savior
made his ascension. Constantius therefore holds the whole empire, being left
alone of the sons of the great Constantine.

§ 12 Looking then at the greatness of the rule and growing dizzy, as being a
man and not having one of his family to shield with him (for no child had been
born to him, nor had any of his brothers been left), and fearing lest some
tyrant again rise up against him, rising against his empire, he considers
taking one of his kinsmen as co-heir and defender of the empire. Which indeed
he also did, setting Gallus, the brother of Julian, forward as Caesar. Gallus
was his cousin on the father's side; for Constantius, the father of Gallus and
Julian, was brother of Constantine the Great. Appointing this man then at
Sirmium, he also yokes to him as wife his own sister Constantia, for the sake
of faith and security, and he gives him officials whom he himself appointed
(for it was not permitted to that man, being Caesar), sending Thalassius as
praetorian prefect, and Montius over the imperial affairs, whom they are
pleased to name quaestors, having also made him patrician.

3.28α Artemii Passio 12 immediately after αὐτὸν ποιησάμενος above, p. 52, 33:
But Gallus, being then sent by Constantius, held the affairs in the East; whom
the Persians immediately, having learned of it, feared, having heard that he
was young and hot for deeds, and they no longer made the expedition against
the Romans. And he was in Antioch of Syria, but Constantius was settling
affairs in the West. And then especially the Roman empire was purely at peace,
being guarded by both. And these things happened in this manner.

§ 13 But Gallus, having put on the purple of Caesar and already beginning to
mount the first steps of empire, did not remain in the same mind and faith
which he had made toward Constantius, but he was heavy and ungovernable and
inexorable in anger; for having seized upon untimely pride and uneven counsel
he overstepped the bounds and despised the covenants which he had made with
Constantius, laying hold of affairs in a more imperial manner and issuing
commands with much boldness and arrogance. For the officials whom Constantius
had sent with him, being umpires of imperial and civil affairs, both the
praetorian prefect Domitianus (for Thalassius had died) and Montius who was
over the quaestorship, because they would not obey and serve his unreasonable
and ungovernable impulses, having commanded the soldiers to bind ropes to
their feet, he ordered them to be dragged through the marketplace and killed
both, men who had distinguished themselves in offices and were found above all
gain and taking; whom the bishop of the city, wrapping them, buried, having
respected the unsurpassable quality of their virtue.

4.1α Artemii Passio 14 immediately after ἀνυπέρβλητον above, p. 55, 16: But
Constantius, as soon as he learned what had happened, made Gallus summoned to
himself. But he, knowing on the one hand that he was not being called for
good, but considering again that if he should not wish to obey, it was
necessary to make war, taking up arms against Constantius immediately, chooses
rather the things of peace. And having sent his wife ahead to Constantius to
mollify him, he himself also went as a volunteer toward the danger. Constantia
therefore set out first, eager to meet her brother beforehand and to win his
respect on behalf of her husband, that he not plan anything irreparable
against him. But using much eagerness about the journey, she fell into illness
while traveling in between and, having entered Bithynia, died at a certain
station of it called Gallicanus. But Gallus, making this too, which happened
contrary to his expectation, a great misfortune, nevertheless went forward,
not departing from what had been decided. But when he arrived among the
Noricans, at a city of theirs called Poetovio, there a general Barbatio is
sent down from Milan, Constantius being there at that time; who strips Gallus
of the purple and, having changed him into a private citizen, set him as an
exile on a certain island of Dalmatia.

§ 15 When Gallus had been taken away to the island, those who had arranged the
whole against him, and it was especially Eusebius the eunuch who held the
honor of praepositus and those with him, persuade Constantius to put Gallus
out of the way as quickly as possible. And he, being persuaded, sends those
who would kill him. And already as these were arriving, Constantius again was
turned to pity, and he sends quickly another letter recalling Gallus from the
suffering. But Eusebius and those with him persuade the magistrianus who had
been sent not to present himself showing the letter before he should learn
that Gallus had been killed. These things happened, and Gallus had died.

4.2α Artemii Passio 15 immediately after ἐτεθνήκει above, p. 58, 26: But
Constantius, having feared concerning affairs lest he not be able alone to be
master of the whole empire, and besides the Gauls being raised most sharply,
whenever they were eager, toward tyrannies, both through strength of body and
lightness of minds, already regretted having removed Gallus, and having
reasoned that the kindred is far safer than the foreign and alien for
partnership in the empire, having sent for Julian the brother of Gallus from
Ionia, he proclaimed him Caesar at Milan; and having given him his own sister
Helen in marriage and having made pledges toward him, he sent this man out to
the Gauls to be guardian of the empire there.

4.3α ibid. continuation He himself, having arrived among the Illyrians, was
spending time at Sirmium.

§ 16 And having heard that the barbarians beyond the Ister were about to
campaign against the Roman empire, setting out from Sirmium, he crossed to the
Ister. And having spent no little time at the very bank, when the barbarian
companies were quiet, he marched again toward Thrace.

lays hold of the greater empire. And when he had taken hold of affairs, he no
longer thought anything small nor judged that he ought to delay; but wishing
for the time being to make all Europe subject to himself, as much as obeys the
Romans,

§ 20 But Constantius, having learned these things through the letters, was
disturbed, as was indeed likely, and having feared especially concerning
Constantinople lest, which that man also intended, he should anticipate making
it subject to himself, he therefore hastened as far as possible to occupy it
first. And while the army was being collected for him, having been scattered
among the cities of the East, and was about to be equipped as for so great a
journey, he signals to the bishops to arrive at Nicaea before him as quickly
as possible; for he was planning to convene a second synod in it, being urged
on by the impious Arians against the homoousion. But when, having passed
through Cilicia, he arrived at the so-called springs of Mopsus, a certain
illness suddenly fell upon him and he was no longer able to go forward. And
when he perceived that he was already in a bad way and would not live, he
sends for Euzoius the bishop of Antioch with all speed and entrusts him to
baptize him. And having been baptized and having lived a little longer, he
leaves life there, having reigned in all forty years, the half with his
father, and the remaining alone.

6.6a-7a ibid. continuation The army, having lamented him and having performed
the customary rites over him, placed him in a coffin, having prepared the
corpse with the usual things for preservation. And having placed him in a
carriage, they were conveying him to Constantinople, each following him with
their own arms and arranged in the same order in which they happened to be
posted under their commanders even while he was alive.

§ 21 These then were holding Constantinople, bringing the corpse; and Julian
accompanied them, having arrived from the Illyrians and already holding the
whole empire securely, no one having dared to oppose him after Constantius'
death. And as the corpse was being conveyed to the church of the apostles,
where they were going to lay him near his father, he himself led the bier,
having taken the diadem from his head. And when they had buried him, already
departing to the palace, he put the diadem on again and was master of affairs,
having already girt himself with the whole empire of the Romans alone. When
therefore Constantius was out of the way, he vented the boiling of his anger
upon those who remained and especially those who had furnished by envy the
cause of Gallus' destruction. And immediately he takes off Eusebius the
praepositus from his head, because he appeared from the beginning, from his
own slanders, to have mixed the whole murder for Gallus; and Paul the
Spaniard, who belonged among the emperor's notaries, he delivers to fire, as
having embittered Gallus very greatly. These two then, having sent them across
to Chalcedon, he subjects each there to his own justice; and he also destroyed
Gaudentius, general of Africa, and certain others, as many as had committed
some outrage against him.

§ 22 But these he punished through letters.

these were gone from among men, and he himself was already master of affairs,
then indeed, having stripped himself in the open, he burst all at once with
all eagerness into Hellenism.

Ibid. 22: For Julian, as has been shown, having girt himself with the empire

everywhere therefore he commanded that their precincts and altars be set up
with much zeal and eagerness.

7.8a Artemii Passio 49 end He himself (namely Julian) set out for Daphne, the
most beautiful dwelling-place of Antioch.

Ibid. 51: But Julian set out for Daphne the suburb, as we said before,
preparing sacrifices for Apollo and expecting to receive oracles from him. And
Daphne is a suburb of Antioch, lying upon its higher districts, shaded with
groves of every kind; for the place is most abundant in trees and most
abundant in fruit, where of all other trees of every kind and indeed of
cypresses an extraordinary quantity has been planted, incomparable for beauty
and height and size, and streams of drinkable waters run everywhere, very
great springs welling up there, from which the city too among few of cities
seems to be most well-watered. And indeed with splendid buildings of lodgings
and baths and the other constructions both for use and for adornment the place
has been equipped very expensively. Here there were temples and statues of
other demons as well, but especially that of Apollo was there from ancient
times being worshipped. For there also the Hellenic myth fashioned the passion
that happened concerning the virgin Daphne, whose name indeed the place still
especially seems to bear.

§ 52 And the statue of Apollo was of such construction: the body had been put
together for him from vine-wood by a most wondrous art, fitted toward the
appearance of a single natural growth, and the robe lying about him, being
clothed entirely in gold, together with the parts of the body that were left
bare and ungolden, joined in some unspeakable beauty; and standing, he had the
lyre in his hands, imitating some leader of the Muses, and the hair and the
crown of laurel bloomed mixed with the gold, so that much grace was about to
flash forth for those who would behold; and two great hyacinth stones filled
out for him the form of the eyes, in memory of the Amyclaean boy Hyacinthus.
And always the beauty and size of the stones added the greatest adornment to
the statue, the craftsmen having stretched them excessively toward its
dignity, in order that it might happen that as many as possible be deceived
concerning it, being baited by the exceeding beauty of the appearing form into
worshipping it. Which indeed Julian himself also had suffered; at any rate he
brought more worship to it than to all the other statues together, sacrificing
whole hecatombs to it from each kind.

§ 53 But when, though he was doing and working everything so that the statue
might give oracles, there was nothing more, but a deep silence held both this
and all the rest of the statues there, then, thinking that what was lacking
was from the sorcery which the Hellenes call sacred rite, having sent for a
certain Eusebius who had acquired the greatest fame among the Hellenes for
being able to do these things, he commanded this man to show the statue as
inspired as possible and active, sparing nothing of what he might think it
still needed for this. But when he, having set in motion all his devices and
with nothing left that he might still devise, had fallen silent in the same
natural manner, speaking no more than before, then already being asked by
Julian also why it was especially silent, and this when all the things
customary among them had been completed upon it, he said that Babylas was most
responsible for the silence of this and the rest of the statues, lying in
Daphne, as the gods abhorred his corpse and for this reason not enduring to
visit their seated images; for he did not wish to speak the truest cause, not
having been altogether unaware of it, that it was clearly a stronger power
that had bound the activities of the demons, and besides the demon himself who
was playing the part of Apollo having said clearly and expressly, as it is
told, that he could not answer because of Babylas.

§ 56 But Julian was preparing a multitude of victims and offerings, as on the
next day he would go up with them to Daphne, now at least hoping altogether,
if not of the others, at least of Apollo to obtain him answering; for upon
this was all the hope of his eagerness and the strain, as such a thing
mattered more to him than to another, both according to the mantic art and
because the place Daphne had been dedicated to him, thinking that he, in his
own place at least, would reasonably have more power than any of the other
demons. But Eusebius and the so-called priests and the multitude of the
sacristans, awaiting the emperor, were in great agony, and they were keeping
vigil about the statue, working everything so that when he arrived he might
obtain it speaking, as no other pretext for delay was still left to it. But
when it was far in the night, fire having been brought down suddenly from
heaven struck the temple, and at once having seized it from every side it
burns it with the statue itself and the offerings themselves. And as all were
being burned together and the fire was being raised to a great height, there
was immediately a great cry about the temple and an uproar like none; and
indeed though many were eager to help, there was no one who would be able to
withstand the fire; but some were running to the city to report to Julian the
governor of the East, and the rest of the crowd stood under astonishment,
becoming spectators of the unexpected quality of the disaster that had
overtaken them. But the fire was touching none of the other things, though so
great and so abundant a wood had grown there, except that, having fallen most
alone, it burned the temple with what was in it, so that the statue and all
that was in offerings was utterly made to vanish, and small foundations of the
buildings were left as memorials of the passion, which even now are still
shown more clearly as the proof of the God-sent fire.

7.15a Artemii Passio 69 immediately after πόλεσιν above, p. 96, 34: But
Julian, setting out from Antioch with the whole army, was journeying toward
the Persian land; and having reached the city Ctesiphon he seemed, having
accomplished some great work, to pass over to other better things. But the
utterly polluted one escaped his own notice being deceived; for having
acquired a diabolic love of idol-madness and having hoped through his godless
gods both to have the empire long-lasting and to become a new Alexander, and
also to prevail over the Persians and to wipe out entirely the race and name
of the Christians, he fell from his proud mind. For having met an old Persian
and having been deceived by him, so as to take over the palaces of the
Persians and all their wealth without labor, he cast him upon the Carmanian
desert into pathless places and pits and into desert and waterless places with
the whole army; and having pressed them with thirst and hunger and having
killed all the cavalry, the Persian confessed that he had willingly led them
astray, in order that they might be destroyed by him and he might not behold
his own fatherland being plundered by the most hateful. This man therefore,
having immediately cut him up limb from limb, they sent to death; and
immediately also after such great misery they fall unwillingly upon the army
of the Persians and, an engagement having occurred, Julian himself, running
this way and that and issuing commands, falls upon a spear, as some say, of a
soldier; but as others, of a Saracen of those with the Persians; but as the
true and our account of the Christians, of the Master Christ having arrayed
himself against him. For a bow having been suddenly stretched from the air and
sending a missile upon him as upon a mark and having rushed through the flanks
clean through wounded him in the hypochondria. And having groaned a heavy and
ill-sounding groan he seemed to see our Lord Jesus Christ standing before him
and laughing at him. And he, filled with darkness and madness, having received
his own blood in his hand and having sprinkled it into the air, coming to his
last breath, cried out saying: "You have conquered, Christ; be filled,
Galilean." And thus, having met the terrible and most hateful death, he ended
his life, having greatly reviled his own gods.

8.8a ibid. continuation And when he had reached the Galatian province, there
at Dadastana, in a certain place so called, he suddenly ends his life. And the
people remain without an emperor forty days, until having come to Nicaea they
proclaimed Valentinian. And Valentinian proclaims his own brother emperor on
the fifth day before the kalends of March, after thirty-two days of his own
reign. The bishops of the pure and right faith therefore, having met
Valentinian, asked that a synod take place. And he answered them that "to me
God has given to rule the affairs of the world, but to you those of the
churches. I therefore have nothing in this matter; where therefore it seems
good to you, having come together, make the synod." These things then he said
while still having the right mind and not yet corrupted. And the bishops,
having gathered at Lampsacus (and it is a city of the Hellespont),
recapitulate the right doctrines of the faith; and having set beside them the
faith of Lucian the martyr, they anathematized the unlike; and having
subscribed the faith previously set forth by the holy fathers at Nicaea, they
sent it around to all the churches. And not long after, the emperor Valens
having been brought under the heresy of the unlike, the bishops began again to
be driven and exiled, Eudoxius with Aetius and Eunomius and the rest of the
heretics, who hold the unlike, taking the lead.
