* The Assault on Constantinople
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Narrative concerning the war that took place in Constantinople in the year
6930, when Amurat Peis fell upon it with a heavy force and all but took it,
had not the all-pure Mother of the Lord guarded it, written by kyr John
Kananos.

1. "It is good to hide the mystery of a king, but to proclaim the works of God
is glorious"; for the angel said this to Tobit. This now both strongly drives
me and emboldens me and makes me a daring herald, that I may narrate the
all-wondrous miracle of my Panagia. Inexperience of speech cuts me off, but
again the peculiar thing of desire, the human passion, pushes me back and
draws me. And I am defeated, and I confess the defeat, and from these things I
have begun the history. And I beg those who read this and have experience of
letters, neither to be wearied by the satiety of the discourse nor to condemn
the solecistic-barbarous phrasing, since I too confess the weakness of my
inexperience in letters. But I have not written these things for the wise or
the learned, but for the unlearned only, as I too am unlearned, so that the
unlearned as unlearned may read this without curiosity and without blame. For
all these reasons then I became a daring writer and herald, and I have related
the much-famed miracle of the Panagia and the inroad of the Hagarenes against
us and the siege of the city according to the power of my speech, and from
these things I have begun.

2. In the thirty-second year of the rule of the emperors Manuel Palaiologos
and his son John, in the six thousand nine hundred thirtieth year, of the
fifteenth indiction, on the tenth of the month of June, on the third day, at
the second hour of the day, there suddenly ran down against us again a host of
Muslims, twice five thousand in number, having also as commander a mad and
bloodthirsty man, and his name was called Michalpais, and every city and land
under the imperial authority they made their own in one moment of time, and
they stripped us of all that was near this city, and they destroyed its
villages, plundered, took captive, and some of the men they slaughtered, and
others they conveyed far off to Iconium and Aratzapes Kyphas, and the women
they wantonly outraged, and the infants they offered to Muhammad for
circumcision, and every beast under yoke and of those without yoke they
destroyed and made vanish. And the damage of the crops and the ruin of the
vineyards, what tongue then and of what kind will be able to trace it out? For
at the time of the closing they ran down unexpectedly and shut us in, and all
that was outside came under their lordship. And how and from where and for
what cause the disaster and destruction and these most terrible hardships came
upon the unfortunate and wretched Romans, it seemed better to us to keep
silent. But the end of that most bitter fight and most dreadful war must be
narrated.

3. For the first army of those Muslims ran down on the tenth of June and shut
us in, as we said before, but on the twentieth of this another army of Muslims
appeared like a cloud full of hail and a deadly shrieking, and they darkened
all and every kind of land under the Romans, and like a flame of burning
lightning they burned and set fire to everything, and every fruit-bearing
timber and fruitful tree and the stocks of the vineyards they cut off from the
root, and they wrought every other terrible and deadly thing against us. And
the great commander and emir and lord of all those arrived. He came mad and
savage, boastful


haughty, overweening and exulting and lifting his brow to heaven, and he
thought himself high above all, and supposed that all things were administered
by him, and that the whole world is led under his authority. But also bringing
very many most mechanical and most warlike works, expecting through them to
throw down a part of the wall of the city and from this to take it and to
capture the Romans and to quench the name of Christ. But also armies of armies
and races of races and multitudes of multitudes, of foot and of horse, he had
so many that that whole world was covered by them.

4. Then at the command of this Murat Peis, and at the call of the lord of the
Turks, they make one palisade. But this one surpassed many most great ones;
for from the one end of the city, of Chrysia, as far as the other end of
Xyloporta it held. And the palisade was near the fortress as much as a
bowshot, and no more. And it was all solid and most strong from most great
timbers and thick planks. And they set wattles of withes in front of the
palisade, that they might receive the arrows of the bows and of the crossbows
of the Romans, and the stones of the bombards. And behind this he ordained
that soldiers from all the races of this army, daring and brave, should stand,
in part, as is the custom, for the watching of the palisade, in part that they
might be guarded through it, and others that they might fight from behind it
with crossbows and bows and the engines great and small, the countless ones
which they brought for this.

5. And those greatest engines, in which they had the hopes that through them
they would throw down the walls of the city and from this take the city
captive, they set with care in a place in which it had no moat like the other
moats, but it was broken down, and of old full of earth, and opposite this a
tower was found by chance much decayed and split from above to below. And the
Turks expecting that the stones of the greatest bombards would throw down that
decayed tower, and since the place has no moat to hinder the Turks, they would
reach the outer fortress unhindered, and from the breach they would drive out
the Romans, and they would enslave the city. Yet the expectation of the
impious came at last to nothing, since seventy shots of that greatest engine
struck that decayed tower, and this caused no harm to the Romans, nor yet
benefit to the Turks. For the place and moat and tower were near Saint
Kyriake, between Saint Romanos and the gate of Charsis, and nearer than these
to the river named Lykos. And these things concerning the ineffectiveness of
the bombards.

6. And concerning the other warlike constructions, those of every kind wrought
against the city for the siege of it, and the destruction of the Romans, who
then will narrate them? Yet of the many I will now narrate a few. They then
made most great wooden towers with many iron-bound wheels, as high as the
towers of the city; rather they even overtopped those. And they had ready
yokes of countless oxen and buffaloes, that they might haul the towers with
ropes and bring them near the moat and from them fight the outer fortress and
destroy it. But they also made falconets and tortoises and bear-wagons and
many other wooden and most mechanical works, and they constructed helepoles
and mechanical arms with small wheels, and they made covered ways large and
middling beyond number, and opposite the gates of the city they wrought great
monsters, they made wooden fortresses over-huge with iron-bound wheels, so
that those looking at them and not knowing of fortress


wars, and the uselessness of them, were greatly astonished and much alarmed.

7. But others also were digging the earth from behind the palisade, and they
went down to a great depth, so that from below from the lower face of the moat
they might pass unseen by night while Romans were standing above and fighting,
and reach the walls of the main and greatest fortress of the city, and cut
this secretly of all, and shore it with props according to the custom of those
who wage siege, then kindle fire in the woods of the props, that they might
burn up the wooden construction holding the fortress, and suddenly the walls
of the city would fall, and at once immediately the armies of the Turks would
rush in boldly against the Romans and from the breach drive them out and take
the city. Others were searching out the conduits which of old brought the
water into the cisterns of the city, that they might hit upon some of them,
and secretly from the Romans through the conduit they would come within the
city by night, and unexpectedly they would take it and capture it.

8. Yet the lord of the Turks also sent heralds from every side, and they
proclaimed thus, and said on oath that the wealth of the city and all the
people the emir gives over as plunder and booty to the Muslims, and come to
the gain. These things he said, with such a device that all the armies of the
Muslims might be gathered, which also then came to pass. For when the rumor
was poured out among the nations of the Muslims how the city had been given
over for plunder and captivity, from almost every land and race of Muslims
they arrived for the gain, not only those skilled in raids and wars, but also
the unskilled and sarlides, that is merchants, money-changers, perfume-sellers
and cobblers, but also many monks of the Turks. For such a cause all were
gathered, the soldiers for the booty, and the sarlides that they might buy the
booty, that is the captives, and some the women, others the men, others the
infants, and others the beasts, and others the goods, and the Turkish monks
that they might have our nuns as gain and booty, a benefaction from the lord
of the Turks. For this expectation then innumerable multitudes were gathered
from all the land of the Muslims, so that all who beheld marveled at the
fullness of the host.

9. Then also they gathered arrows, so innumerable a multitude that at first we
disbelieved the rumor of the word until the hour of the war. And the gathering
of these came about thus. Every house of every man found in the lordship of
the Turks of all the east and west gave of these, each from ten to twenty
arrows of the bows. But they also shot more than these at the hour of the war,
such as the armory of the Turk had and the quivers of the host. He also had
with him then iron-bound the ambassadors of the emperor, whom he himself had
rather asked to be sent concerning peace and friendship. But he as a raw and
inhuman barbarian condemned them to irons and prisons, and threatened the
guiltless as guilty with death, putting forward as if a cause, but a cold one;
for he said: "Because the Romans spoke with me shamelessly, for this I have
condemned them to prison."

10. But then also one of the Muslims, high and great and fearful among them,
inasmuch as he was descended from the line and race of Muhammad. This man they
also honored as a patriarch, and they worshipped him as a prophet, and they
revered him as Muhammad himself. So great and such a most great man then was
that one that the daughter of the emir and lord of the Muslims, being a virgin


he seized against that one's will and corrupted and kept, and he had her in
his chamber and his bed, and no one dared to mock him or say anything to that
one. But rather the emir and brother of the girl said: "The intercourse of the
patriarch, the kinsman of Muhammad, has sanctified her, and let what is born
of her be holy as of the blood of Muhammad according to the law and
sanctification of Rasul Mahumet." And this most great and much honored among
them, the noble patriarch, whom they held as a seer and prophet, named
Mersaites in the Persian dialect, sent ambassadors from Prousa where he dwelt
to the lord of the Turks, and said: "See that you do not join war with the
Romans and lose the army and the races of the Muslims, until I arrive and
declare the hour of the joining of the war, as the great Rasul the prophet
teaches us. And I know this as a seer and prophet." And the lord of the Turks,
hearing these things, did as he was commanded, and awaited the false prophet.
And after a few days he arrived.

11. Mersaites came, the patriarch of the Turks, with five hundred Turkish
monks, sitting upon a mule, most solemn in appearance and most terrible in
majesty. And the multitudes of the Muslims ran out to his meeting, and as an
angel come down from heaven the marvel-foolish races of the Turks received
him, and not only they kissed his feet and his hands, but also the bridle of
the mule and the feet of that on which he rode. Likewise also the lord of the
Turks received him in a slavish manner. And he was seen by all as solemn and
over-magnificent. Yet being puffed up and exulting as high and great and as a
descendant of Muhammad he received them barely, and uttered these things:
"Know, Muslims, and you yourself the lord of all these. I have been sent by
the great prophet Rasul Mahumet, that I may tell you the hour of the war, as a
seer and prophet, that we may enslave the Romans and take captive also the
city. And prepare yourselves for this until the hour arrives, which I know."
And having said these things he got down from the mule on which he rode, and
entered into the tent standing of felt, and began to read the books of
Muhammad and to practice the Ramblia. And he did these things with hypocrisy,
that the Turks might be deceived and worship and glorify him as a prophet,
just as also the lord of the Turks and all the Muslims hold as true and sure
all things spoken from this man, and all awaited his command, that he might
order the hour of the war and they might all run and take the city and take
all the Romans captive.

12. This empty and vain hope then filled the Turks then with great daring and
boldness and gladness, and from this they rejoiced and lived riotously. But
also day and night they clapped their palms, and awaited the voice of
Mersaites as of a most true angel, that they might take the Romans captive.
But also day and night and hour they sharpened their tongue against us as a
two-edged sword and spoke wild and untamed voices shamelessly and insultingly.
Not only did they send up most shameful voices against patriarchs and
emperors, but also against our faith, daringly reproaching and shamelessly
blaspheming, they said: "Where is your God, O dark Romans? Where is your
Christ? Where are your saints to help you? Tomorrow we take the city, and we
have you as captives and slaves, and we will dishonor your women and your
children before you, and we will marry your nuns to our Turkish monks. By our
faith, truly our prophet says so." These things


the impious had as sure and confessed, and for this they reproached our God
impiously with these things.

13. When then the complete day of the war of the Turks arrived, which among
them the great Mersaites their prophet had fore-ordained, he said to the
commander and lord of the Muslims: "Delay no longer, but quickly order the
ranks to be armed, and having every most mechanical and most warlike work
ready, and with these let it come as near the city as a bowshot to the
fortress. And a signal," he said, "I command such as this. When you see me
sitting on a high horse and behold a bared sword in my right hand and hear my
voice three times, with shout and cry and rush and clappings and instruments
rush in against the Romans. And of themselves the walls of the city will fall,
and unhindered become within and take it captive." And these idle tales
Mersaites, confessing as sure, speaking to the Muslims said, as having the
prophecies from Muhammad and the books of Rasul Mahumet. And he said these
things; and the commander and lord of the Turks, fulfilling what was commanded
and ordering all the ranks to be armed, and bringing all the preparation of
the war in their hands, immediately the multitudes of the Muslims and all the
armies fulfill what was ordered, and with weapons of every kind and the
greatest preparations and the most mechanical works they were drawn up in
rank. And some brought ladders, and ladders of every kind, small and great,
others clubs, and others boring-tools, and others fire with fire-pots, others
handguns, others iron warlike reaping weapons with long spears named falces,
others solid and great shields, and all with irons, and others wattles, and
others pavises, others iron grapnels, and every other warlike work they
brought in their hands. And all were fully armored with strong and iron
panoply and most great helmets. And so great was the multitude of that army
that from the one end of the city, of Chrysia, as far as the other end of
Xyloporta it held.

15. And the generals of the host set the arrays of the Turks in order near the
fortress, as the false prophet ordained. And those countless archers of the
Turks they ordered all together to shoot upon the fortress and the
battlements, that they might wound and kill some of the Romans, and the others
be afraid and flee from the wall, and from this they might find leave and the
Muslims might go up unhindered, and take the city. And the people of the
Romans, seeing the warlike and fighting works of the Turks, and the fullness
of the host of countless races, and the rush of the Tartars and the daring of
the Muslims, and the things done a little before, that within in the moats the
Turks had killed Romans and others in front at the gates, they feared greatly,
and almost the greater part looked toward flight. O that most bitter hour, O
the unbearable grief, O greatest despair.

16. Who did not shudder that day? Who did not tremble at this hour seeing the
Romans in so great cowardice and the Muslims in so great daring? And who of
the unshaken then was not struck and of the brave was not afraid, not of
death, not of death I say, for it is natural, but of the taking of this great
city and the captivity of the race, the dishonors of the women, the outrages
of the chaste, the circumcision of the infants, the destruction of the
temples, the mockeries of the holy icons, the Wisdom of the greatest God being
made a hymn-place of Muhammad and a dwelling of demons and a mosque of Rasul
and Muhammad. And the worse and more harmful and


myriadfold of the worst death, most evil, in the changing and destruction of
the pious faith of the Christians toward the worship of the Muslims and the
circumcision of Muhammad. O that hour worthy of greatest lament, O uncheckable
storm. Who of us did not join in mourning the wailings then? What hearing
bears the hearing, what sight the spectacle? For in so many and such and so
great necessities we had no one helping or to stir up or to urge on, since the
one emperor was worn down from illness and old age, and could not arm himself
or mount a horse, but was found within the palace and did what was according
to his power. And the other mounted a horse armed as was due, and went out the
gate of Saint Romanos, and stood there near the gate.

17. And when the hour arrived which the godless Mersaites awaited, that is the
first hour after midday, he mounted a high and great horse, and the five
hundred men about him whom he brought, Turkish monks, that they might have as
gain and booty the nuns of the city, shouted a hymn in the Persian dialect to
Muhammad and praises to Mersaites. And they did this three times, until he
came to the fight. Yet they also carried before him a shield greatest beyond
nature, that from this the marvel-foolish nations of the Muslims might marvel
at that Mersaites. And when he came near to the arrays of the Muslims, he
shouted greatly, "Rasul, Rasul Mahumet." And having bared the sword and urged
the horse, and cried to the arrays "Allah Tangry, Rasul Mahumet," the
multitudes of the Muslims shouted together at once, and with rush and cry and
clappings and instruments and myriads of other alalai and trumpets they
reached the walls of the fortress from the Xyloporta as far as Chrysia, I mean
the same and one hour of that day. And they brought every warlike instrument
in their hands, and they leaned against the walls, they set ladders, they went
up onto the fortress, they bored the towers. And no one was found who hindered
them from the greatest fear and cowardice which the Romans had taken.

18. For who did not tremble at that hour? Who did not shudder at the sight of
it? What hearing bears the hearing, what sight the spectacle? For in one
moment of time they shot myriads of missiles, that is arrows, upon the Romans,
and they fell on the walls of the city and within, so that they covered even
the air and hid the very light of the sun, and fear held us all and cowardice
struck us, and we hid ourselves a little. But not far, but we stood behind the
battlements. And when the Turks, seeing the fortress bare of men, supposed it
to be unguarded, and with greatest daring they all rush in against us. And
some went up with ladders through which onto the fortress, others also with
the grapnels and those falces. Others bored the towers with boring-tools,
others threw down the fortress with clubs, others burned the gates of the
outer fortress, and the impious did every daring and brave thing toward our
dismay. Seeing these things the unfortunate and despairing Romans came back to
themselves, and having considered how from where, having from elsewhere no
hand of help nor anyone to stir up and urge the people toward the fight of the
Turks in that most dreadful hour. And bringing themselves forth they are
stirred toward bravery and daring. For those formerly cowardly and fugitives
and worn down were suddenly found daring and noble warriors and brave and
despisers of wounds and terrible hurts and deaths of every kind.

19. And strengthened in daring by the help of the Panagia they rush sword in
hand against the impious and with stones, and as a swarm moved by smoke one
upon another is


stirred, and every race and every age rises, and each with such weapons as he
could, others even without weapons, others with swords and spears. And others
did not even have abundance of these, but they bound with ropes the boards
where they ate and the lids of the barrels, and they carried them instead of
shields. And some did not even come to the war with these, but with stones
only they fought daringly and bravely, as if fully armored with weapons of
every kind. And one another, and another another, and toward one another they
contended bravely and boldly, and they urged one another toward that most
vehement fight,
saying "Yes, O friends, O
brothers,
O
fellow-countrymen,
let us contend
today,
let us be eager,
let us put ourselves in danger for ourselves and women and children and for
the freedom of the race and of our fatherland and of this great city, and what
is greater, of the true faith of the Christians. What then, if we do not die
now? Shall we not die any more? Let us make the necessity a generosity, let us
run toward the danger of the war as the martyrs toward the stadium of the
tyrants." As they spoke these things and urged one another they all hastened,
and they contended as if drunken and baptized from wine, and as from a signal
at once from the one end of the city as far as the other in one voice and rush
and cry they rush in against the enemies, and a most vehement war having
broken out and a most stubborn fight for a sufficient hour many from both
sides were wounded and died.

20. And the unshakenness of the most manly and most noble Romans showed the
Muslims cowardly and turnable. And those going up with the falx upon the outer
fortress they cut off their heads and brought them to the emperor, and the
other Muslims who were boring the fortress and the towers, they killed within
in the holes. And the other multitudes of the Turks, the exulting and puffed
up, they wounded, killed, drove, and they took their warlike and most fighting
works. And not only the soldiers and those skilled in war wrought these
things, but also the magistrates of the polity and the skilled of the land and
the whole commons and the companies of the priests and of the monks and the
better of the hierarchs and the most holy of the holy spiritual ones; and the
men of the outer lands appeared daring and noble and despisers of wounds and
hurts and deaths.

21. But also many women, changing into a man's daring at that most dreadful
hour of the war, did not hide, nor did they fear as women, but rather at the
hour of the war they reached the outer fortress, and some brought up stones
onto the wall toward the warriors of the Romans, and they made them brave, and
pushed them toward the fight and the war; others held eggs and tow, and healed
the wounded; others gave them waters and wines to drink, burning with thirst
from the war; others hindered their own brothers and children and spouses from
coming down from the wall of the fortress and leaving the war, but told them
to fight the Turks fearlessly and unshakenly and boldly. But they also walked
there with attention and in a company. And they also arrayed themselves toward
one another, one admonished the other, and said: "See that you do not approach
the great fortress, for the arrows of the Turks strike there and in that part,
but walk near in the part of the outer fortress, that you may be covered by
it." Yet some also were wounded with arrows. And not even this alarmed the
others, that they should enter within the fortress of the city, but they too
were eager, as they could, in the fight until they should see the end of the
war.


22. And when the light of the sun declined toward the west, and the Romans
stood unshaken toward the most dreadful fight of the Turks, and contended also
bravely, the impious from these, found ineffective and ashamed and defeated,
turning shamelessly fled. And it is clear that of their warlike and fighting
weapons some they left unwillingly near the fortress to the Romans, and the
farther ones they willingly gave to the fire. And this seemed a wonder and an
extraordinary work, how those formerly puffed up and exulting were suddenly
found downcast and useless, and how those who formerly desired the war of the
Romans, unexpectedly thereafter fled from them. For truly this work was sent
invisibly from a divine power. And the Romans seeing this were astonished at
the miracle, but rejoiced at the flight of those enemies, and rejoicing they
said: "O sweetness of this day and hour, O wondrous change and unhoped-for
exchange, O unexpected freedom and gladness, O unspeakable cheer and
inexpressible pleasure."

23. For as the much-tried Romans said these things they rejoiced at once and
leaping they were cheerful, they clapped their palms, and clearly they gave
thanks to God, and they sent up hymns to the Panagia, and glorifying from the
soul they said: "This much-hymned and much-famed and extraordinary and
excellent and wondrous and wonder-worthy miracle of the Panagia was truly and
without doubt, as we also have the testimonies from witnesses not to be struck
out according to the piety-loving laws. For they say that the testimonies from
the enemies are more trustworthy. And since our enemies and foes have given
such a testimony, the miracle of the Panagia is altogether most true. For
Mersaites, patriarch of the Turks, said that the city was destined by us to be
taken captive, as the wise of Persia in the time of Muhammad voted concerning
this, and they found that in the year and the month and the day of this hour
the city is destined to be taken by us. And this was about to come to be
according to the power of the stars and the art of the astronomers."

24. But that woman wearing purple and walking the fortress and upon the
battlements fearlessly at the hour of the war, she opposed the powers of the
stars and the art of the astrologers. Yet she had the power not from earth nor
from men, but from heaven and an invisible power. Likewise also all the armies
of the Turks bore witness on oath to that Mersaites, and they narrated to all
that at the hour of the war, when with daring and uncheckable rush they
reached the walls of the fortress, that they might go up and drive out the
Romans and take the city captive, then they saw a woman wearing a purple robe
and walking upon the battlements of the outer fortress. And seeing her,
darkness and dizziness and trembling and fear suddenly entered the souls of
all, and they looked toward flight, and not at all toward war, and from the
power and art of that woman they took the cowardice, and the city was freed.

25. And these things came to be at the time of the six thousand nine hundred
thirtieth year, on the 24th of the month of August, on the second day, at the
seventh hour of the day. And from the Turks more than one thousand were
wounded and died, and from our own, with truth, fewer than a hundred were
wounded, and not more than thirty died. And rejoice, O friends, rejoice, the
one at the freedom of the city, the other at the nobility and unshakenness of
the Romans, the other at the all-wondrous miracle of the Panagia, and hymn
her, glorify her, and send up hymns of thanksgiving for the greatest work of
the freedom, of which


we were benefited by the glorious and ever-virgin Mother of our Lord and God
and Savior Jesus Christ. To him be the glory unto the ages. Amen.
