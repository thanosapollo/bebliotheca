is indicated through these things, and that through all bodily death is shown,
but through many the death that comes by chance. But none of these things does
the sequence of the argument now show; for there is no distinction in it, but
he goes through it as concerning one universal matter. He is eager, however,
to show that even if the wicked one prepared Adam's trespass, procuring for
himself the beginning of his kingdom against men, still the Lord, having
sojourned, bestowed a greater gift. Rom 5:16–17 I.e. the gift is toward the
one sin of Adam; for it was great and abundant and surpassing the sins of all
by loving-kindness, which, teaching, he adds: for the judgment was from one
unto condemnation, but the gift is from many trespasses unto justification.
For he wishes to say that Adam's sin was one, yet though it was one it
condemned the many from one sin because they imitated Adam, but the gift of
the Lord was not measured toward the one sin, but toward the many sins of all.
So that, having released from many trespasses, it led unto justification; for
in this is the abundance of grace, that it not only released from the
trespasses, but also led by the hand into righteousness. Rom 5:18–19 Having
examined and thoroughly exercised both the loss inflicted on men through Adam
and the benefaction granted by the Lord, he then adds more declaratively,
teaching that Adam's trespass condemned and put men to death,

but the Lord's justification made them alive. And what is Adam's trespass?
Disobedience. And what is the Lord's justification? The obedience with which
he obeyed the Father according to becoming man and suffering for men, as the
apostle says in other places: being found in fashion as a man he humbled
himself, becoming obedient unto death, even death of a cross. So that
obedience overthrew disobedience and by the best overcame the worst. 85 Rom
5:–21 Having said that it might abound, he showed that even before the law
there were trespasses and that men knew they were at fault. Not indeed all
that they failed in did they recognize as faults, but the law, when given,
stripped them bare and showed them to be more than they supposed. And he said
came in besides accurately; for between Adam's disobedience and the Savior's
sojourn the law received a passage into life. But where sin abounded, grace
superabounded. Where then did sin abound? Where the law was. And if the law
was among the Jews, there grace also superabounded; for from there came
evangelists and apostles and the head of salvation, Christ. Rom 6:1–4 What
then shall we say? he says, shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?
May it not be! He added May it not be! securely; for not those who sin with
evil craft are worthy of forgiveness, but those who through slackness and
inattention receive the devices of the devil, these are justly to be pitied by
God. We who died to sin, how shall we still live in it? Since when we are
baptized we confess that we have died to the world and that we renounce sin
and the devil. Rom 6:5 He has set grown together accurately; for of stocks
planted together the nature is often united into the same through the
grafting. So then also those genuinely baptized into the death of Christ are
united to him through faith. Rom 6:9–11 If it were possible, he says, for
Christ to die both twice and thrice for those who sin, perhaps it would be
without danger to run again upon the old custom of sins, which always has a
like hope of loving-kindness as well. But if, having died once, he dies no
more, so also we, having been buried with him and raised with him, no longer
die to sins; for there is no second baptism, nor a second death of Christ. For
which reason one must live more securely. For that which he died, he died to
sin once for all, that is 86 for the sins of human beings; but that which he
lives, he lives to God, that is he lives forever, inasmuch as he is God. So
then you also, be dead to sins, but live to God, through whom you live. Rom
6:12–14 Do not present, he says, your members as weapons of unrighteousness to
sin, i.e. do not you yourselves give weapons against yourselves to the wicked
one and strength by sinning. For sin shall not lord it over you; for you are
not under law but under grace. What sin shall not lord it? The one before
baptism, which the grace of Christ remits. And this the law was not able to
do. You are not under a law that cannot rescue, but rather punishes. Rom 6:19
I was able, he says, to say something more perfect and greater and worthy of a
surpassing benefaction, but I say to you so much as you can hold, being weak
in the flesh. Rom 6:23 Those who sin are paid wages by the devil in this way:
selling temperance and buying license, selling kindness and taking cruelty in
return. Such people then, having become as it were a proper possession of sin,
subject themselves to death; for the wages of sin is death. Or perhaps he says
this, that the things wage-paid by the enemy are productive of death. Rom
7:1–3 From this he establishes that while the law was in force before Christ
it was not permitted to be joined to another, that is to grace, but when the
law had ceased and in a manner died it is permitted thereafter for the church
to be joined to the

heavenly bridegroom. For he sets both the law and Christ in the place of a
husband: the latter of the church, since as the husband is the beginning of
the wife, so Christ of the church, and the law once of the synagogue. The law,
he says, lords it over the man for as long a time as he lives. Who lives? The
law. And he discourses about it as about a husband and one who dwells with
him. Rom 7:5 For when, he says, we were in the flesh, i.e. in the law; for
those in the law were gratifying the flesh. And he said accurately the things
through the 87 law, not the things from the law; for the law did not furnish
sinning, since it commanded not to sin. He said then through the law, since by
allowing luxury it gave the passions a pretext, so that the law was not
bringing about what it wished, but what it did not wish. Rom 7:7 But sin, he
says, I did not know except through law. He did not say "but I did not do sin
except through law," but I did not know, so that the law is cause not of doing
but of discerning sin. The law then is educative, not allowing us through
ignorance to fall out into the nature of the irrational. That, however, must
be inquired, how having said here that he did not know sin except through the
law, in other places he shows that we have in ourselves the power of
discernment, in which he says: for when nations that have not law do by nature
the things of the law, these, not having law, are a law to themselves. Through
these he presents that even without law it was possible to see what is due.
But one must reckon that even if it was possible, it was not of all things. So
even now still some things among other nations are practiced as good and
lawful, but among others are cast out as lawless and badly held. For which
reason indeed the giving of the law was necessary, defining for us both what
must be done and what must not, and articulating for us and illuminating the
way of life of the just. Rom 7:8 He seems to call the devil sin. Just as
Scripture calls the Savior life and righteousness because he is cause of life
and righteousness, so also it calls the opposing power from its own working
now sin, now falsehood, now death, and so also in what follows, having said
sin lived again, he names the devil sin. But sin, taking occasion through the
commandment. How 88 then did the devil take occasion through the commandment?
Because, having made the precepts of the law as it were a certain matter of
his own craft, he turned the human being around to the opposite of the
commandments. And it is clear that through himself the apostle indicates the
common human being. Rom 7:9–11 But when the commandment came, he says, sin
lived again, and I died. If it lived again, it is clear that it once lived
and, having died, lived again. When then did it live? When, Adam having
received a commandment and knowing the transgression to be destructive, the
devil deceived him and overcame him. Likewise also when Cain, for he too
sinned in knowledge, having been commanded not to murder his brother, through
Have you sinned? Be still. When therefore there was in between neither
commandment nor law, sin was deadened by the ignorance of those who sinned;
for they were ignorant of most things when they failed, so that they were not
even condemned by conscience. What then from these things? Was it profitable
not to give the law? But those who were going to do right would have been
damaged. And it is absurd, because of the slack and pleasure-loving, to take
away from the earnest and virtuous the ground of salvation. Rom 7:13 The law,
he says, is a refutation of the devil's wickedness; and he called the law
good. The wicked one then appears as being terribly wicked, when through the
good that was given he procured death for those who received the good law.
Sinful, he says, is sin. Most manifestly now he has shown that he was calling
the devil sin, and now he named him sinful; for sinful is the one productive
of sins. Rom 7:14 Reasonably the law is called spiritual; for the precepts in
it, when kept, made the human being spiritual, or also because, he says, it
has been legislated according to the working of the Holy Spirit, so that those
who obey it can be receptive of the Holy Spirit, having been cleansed by the
pedagogy of the law. Sold under sin: not sold by some other, but

himself by himself, making light of the things commanded. 89 Rom 7:15 Not that
he does not know that sin is sin, he says, but i.e. how I do it and how I am
stolen away I do not perceive, as when often, having judged to master anger, I
was defeated by myself. One must know, however, that he is not accusing
himself, but he sketches in himself the common lot of human beings, declining
to set the burdensome thing upon another; for he shows that we desire virtue,
but are defeated by pleasure, as many of us as are under law, not however as
many as are under grace, who have also come to be outside sin through grace.
Rom 7:21 To I find the law there is lacking hard to accomplish; for he says
that from the things even when I wish to do something good I cannot, but I am
turned aside toward the worse. I find the law hard for me to accomplish,
wanting to do the good; for the more ready things, the evil ones, lie beside
me toward doing them. And through lies beside he shows that it is not
laborious to go after wickedness. Rom 7:22–23 In these things too he describes
the common human being; for it belongs to the common human being to see with
the mind what is due, but not altogether to accomplish it, but to the one who
has believed in Christ to accomplish it with the mind through the help of the
Spirit; for which reason such a one is also called spiritual. And he shows
that the devil as it were legislates against the legislation of God; for he
persuades human beings to do the opposite of what God commanded. And very
guardedly he says that the law of sin is in the members, showing that the soul
is not pleased with the passions of the body, even if it is often dragged down
together with it. 90 Rom 7:24 It must be noted that it is not simply from this
body, but from the death of the body; for he does not pray to be delivered
from the body, but from the death that holds the body through sin. Rom
7:25–8:1 He continues, refuting the old man's warring against himself, the
body not being at peace with the soul. And he refutes these things, in order
that he may display the achievements of the Savior's sojourn and the union and
peace of soul and body that has come to be through the regeneration of the
bath. He has therefore gathered what he wished, and has come upon what he was
establishing, saying: there is therefore now no condemnation to those in
Christ Jesus. For having shown that those under law, inasmuch as they live
according to flesh, are under sin and condemnation, he added that those under
Christ are not under condemnation, since they do not walk according to flesh.
Rom 8:2 He said law of the Spirit, distinguishing it against the law of the
letter, i.e. the life-giving grace from Christ. Rom 8:3–4 For the
impossibility of the law, in which it was weak through the flesh. It was not
the law, he says, that commanded impossible things, but the flesh was not 91
able to fulfill the way of life of the law because of the wicked one who
impedes, whom Christ condemned through his own flesh. God, he says, sending
his own Son in the likeness of flesh of sin. When the apostle looks toward the
Lord's flesh, being truly flesh, from the things from which he was also born
and reared and conducted his life, he openly names it flesh, as he also adds
here: he condemned sin in the flesh, and writing to Timothy: God was
manifested in flesh. But when he looks toward the flesh's being without sin
and pure and holy, he names it a likeness of flesh, not because it differed
according to nature, but because it was like fleshly sin. God then, he says,
sent his Son not in the likeness of an angel or of some such power, but in the
likeness of flesh, in order that, flesh having been defeated in the case of
Adam, flesh might overcome the wicked one in the case of the Lord. And the
Lord, he says, was sent because of the sin that had prevailed over human
beings; for this is concerning sin, i.e. because of sin, and the and is
superfluous. In order that the justification of the law might be fulfilled, he
says, in us, as if the law wished to fulfill

justification, but was weak because it allowed them luxury. Why then did it
allow? Because it would not have been received, if it had not come down
together with their weakness. What then did it benefit by coming down, if it
did not justify? That it recalled the mind that had fallen out into the
insensibility of ignorance. To those who do not walk according to flesh, he
says, i.e. according to the law; for the law had fleshly observances, and it
was also fleshly to allow luxury. Rom 8:9 But you, he says, are not in flesh
but in spirit, i.e. you are not in fleshly desires, but you live in spirit. If
indeed the Spirit of God dwells in you. Stirring them to keep the mind awake
and not to lie back as if they had already succeeded, he brought out the word
with a hesitation, saying: if indeed the Spirit of God dwells in you; for to
make them brow-lifted and puffed up as already having the Spirit of God was
harmful and enough to cut off zeal. 92 Rom 8:11 Having said before Spirit of
Christ, now again he says the Spirit of him who raised Christ dwells in you.
Having brought the Spirit back from Christ to the Father, he teaches nothing
else but clearly that from the Son the Spirit also is not alien to the
paternal godhead, and that those who have one working have also altogether the
substance coeternal with the Father. Accordingly, however, when the Holy
Spirit has dwelt and sin has been put to death, he says that the prizes follow
those who contend, of which the first and greatest was the resurrection from
the dead. Rom 8:15 He called the law a spirit of slavery and those under the
law slaves; for to be commanded by the law and, having neglected the command,
to fear the threatened punishment is a sign of slavery, but to be permitted to
call God Father through purity of life announces hopes of adoption. And
whether a spirit of slavery was given or of adoption, it is the same Spirit
upon both, having worked according to what is profitable and fitting for each
people according to the worth of both. Rom 8:16 He showed through this that he
also calls the soul spirit, when it is spiritual, and the gift of the Spirit
spirit. This then is what he says elsewhere: for what we should pray we do not
know, the Spirit itself intercedes for us with unspeakable groanings. And this
too needs interpretation; and it is such a gift of prayer as was upon the
apostles. Since we often ask even the things that are not profitable, for this
reason someone would stand up among the apostles, the one who had the gift of
prayer, and would ask God for the things profitable for the people, using
unspeakable groanings, in order that he might draw all toward reverence and
fear. And this is what he says elsewhere: each of you has a psalm, has a
prayer. Therefore there was also a gift of psalming and a gift of praying. And
it bears witness with our spirit, since the one who had the gift was praying
in spirit for the whole people, and praying he said Our Father who art in the
heavens. For this reason, he says, the 93 spirit of prayer bears witness with
our spirit which we have through baptism, that we are children of God; for
this spirit, that is the gift of prayer, cries Abba, Father. Rom 8:17–18 If
indeed we suffer with him, he says, that we may also be glorified with him. To
suffer with does not indicate sharing pain and being grieved with the one who
has suffered, according to the sense of custom; for Christ was not suffering
anything against his will, nor was he weak if he wished to ward off those who
had grieved him. What then is it to suffer with Christ? To choose to endure
the same sufferings which Christ, advocating the truth, bore to suffer from
Jews. If in the same manner, being persecuted, we endure, being dishonored we
do not take it hard, if we are ready toward death for piety as having suffered
with him, we shall also be glorified with him in becoming children of God and
joint-heirs of Christ. And since he said that suffering with Christ becomes a
cause for us of being glorified with him, and such a thing seemed to be a wage
of labors and not a gift according to grace, even if the gift of the sins that
have gone before

grants amnesty. Correcting this he added that the fruit of our way of life is
not measured to us by God according to proportion of the labors, but is many
times more and far surpasses the worth of the things labored; for God, being
both good and just, keeps the just toward those who sin, rendering to the one
who has sinned so much as the ground of the sins requires, but displays the
good toward those who do right, not only giving them the things according to
desert, but also surpassing with gifts the proportion of what they have
practiced. Rom 8:19 He says we shall be in so great a gift of the good things
of God, that the hope and expectation of the creation awaits the revelation of
the sons of God, as it were their hidden blessedness that is shortly afterward
to be revealed. But the things written give most people a suspicion that the
visible creation is ensouled and that the world has a certain rational
perception. For if it groans together and travails together and awaits the
revelation, it is clear, he says, that it also perceives and is moved by
reasoning. But it is not so, but he now calls creation not the soulless
materials but the powers set over them, which have been appointed by God to
manage the creation according to the 94 will of the Creator, according to what
is written: when the Most High divided nations, when he scattered the sons of
Adam, he set the bounds of nations according to the number of the angels of
God. And so also upon bless frost and heat and again hail and snow and the
like one must understand it as said to the powers set over these; for he does
not command unfeeling materials to praise God. For just as let all the earth
worship you and psalm to you and just as the Lord has become king, let the
earth rejoice he means the human beings upon the earth, and wine shall mourn
and the vine shall mourn he means the farmers set over them, so also now he
calls creation the powers set over the soulless materials, themselves also
being created, just as a man's clothing and a laugh of teeth and a step of a
foot will announce the things concerning him. Rom 8:–21 The material nature
having been enslaved to the wicked one and often plotted against by him by
God's permission, so that famines and plagues and diseases and wars and other
destructions are prepared by him; for it is likely, when those who dwell live
impiously, that the wicked one has room, as to trample upon his own, and upon
their harming to harm the elements around them. When these things have
happened, the powers appointed over the good order of creation groan and
travail and await the freedom of the human being, with whom the creation also,
having been freed together, will be released from the authority of the devil.
What then befalls the rational powers he has attributed to the soulless
material, as we also now say: the house is in uproar, the city is indignant,
the country is angry, often signifying from the places administered and gone
about those who administer and go about. And according to another account the
creation awaits being freed from the slavery of corruption, since by the
devil's contrivance it is unwillingly worshipped by its fellow-slaves; for
they worshipped and served the creation rather than the one who created. So
that, unwillingly enjoying the master's honor, it bears heavily what is
happening and travails and is distressed at the vain worship of human beings
and desires release from these things. And he says that it was not from the
beginning willingly, nor having deserted to the devil, that it was subjected
to him, but it was subjected when God permitted, and it was subjected 95 upon
a hope that was to come. What hope? This, that the creation itself also shall
be freed. And when shall it be freed? At the time of the freedom of the just;
for which reason reasonably the eager expectation of the creation awaits the
revelation of the children of God. Rom 8:23 Not only, he says, does the
creation groan and travail, awaiting the freedom through the Spirit, but we
also, although already having received the firstfruits of the Spirit and
having tasted his benefaction, even if

we have not yet received the perfection of the promise, which is the adoption
through the redemption of the body. Rom 8:29–30 Not even in these things does
the argument abolish free will; for he first said that God foreknows, then
that he predestines. And foreknowledge is clearly not compulsive of the
impulse and purpose of those who live. And what has been said would be clearer
if, beginning from the last of the things said here, we should go up to the
first. Whom then did God glorify here? Those whom he justified. And whom did
he predestine? Those whom he foreknew, those who are called according to
purpose, that is those who through their own purpose are worthy to be called
and to be conformed to Christ. 96 Rom 8:31–32 Having shown through the deeds
that all things work together for us unto good through God's love concerning
us, those whom he found called according to purpose, he adds the great proof
of the love of God, which he displayed in not sparing the Only-begotten; for
what of it, even if death did not lord it over the Savior, since it was also
in the Lord himself to lay down his soul and to take it again? But
nevertheless one must observe that Christ subjected himself to human
sufferings, fulfilling both his own will and the Father's. God then managing
all things for us, who will impede? Who is so strong as to hinder the gifts of
God toward us? For reasonably he who did not spare the Son for us will also
manage all the other things for us and will add the grace of the good things.
Rom 9:1–5 Necessarily he confirms the word with the accompanying oath; for
having said before that there is nothing that will be able to separate him
from the love of Christ, he adds that he is overcome only by his kindred. He
says then that for the Jews he would gladly have given over both body and
soul, in order that they might call back again the same things from which they
have fallen, having believed in Christ. For what of the good things was not
the Jews', in which the nations now pride themselves? The adoption, the glory,
the covenants, the promises, the worship, the fathers beloved of God, and the
greatest: from whom is Christ according to the flesh. From them, he says, is
Christ; but God is not of them only, but is God in common over all. From all
these things then the Jews have fallen, but in order that they might recover
these, he says, I would gladly have become anathema from Christ for them. And
anathema indicates the one who is alien, since what has been dedicated is
alien to the one who dedicates. And necessarily he shows how great a
disposition he has concerning them, in order that no one may suppose that the
refutations against them are spoken from hatred. Rom 9:6–9 Since he indicated
that the things promised to the Israelites have been transferred unto the
nations, which was an accusation of God as falsifying the promises,
establishing the unlying of God he shows that Scripture was not indicating the
97 Israelites according to flesh, but those who through piety show that they
are worthy to be called Israelites and children of Abraham; for in Isaac, he
says, shall a seed be called to you, and what follows. In Isaac, i.e. through
Isaac, which interpreting he says: that is, not the children of the flesh,
these are children of God, but the children of the promise are reckoned unto a
seed. And he wishes to say that not those from the flesh of Abraham, these are
children of God, but children are those from promise, as it were the pious and
just, whom according to foreknowledge God promised as sons to Abraham, since
Isaac, having come to be from promise, was shown just; for having foreseen him
just he also promised; for God would not have undertaken to grant an impious
son. So then he also promised that Abraham would be father of nations, having
foreseen the piety of the nations that would be. For which reason he said: in
Isaac shall a seed be called to you, that is through promise according to the
type of Isaac, who was just and a type of the just. And the whole construction
of the argument is that the promise of God is according to foreknowledge, and
the promise according to just judgment, and as upon Isaac, so also upon the
nations. Rom 9:11 98 It is a scriptural idiom from the Hebrew

things hyperbatically, and some also last-first and to set them conversely.
And the use of these is great not only upon the old but also upon the new
covenant; for the apostle, unto proof of what lay before him, having recalled
both Esau and Jacob, says thus: for the children not yet having been born nor
having done anything good or bad, that the purpose of God according to
election might remain, i.e. the election according to their purpose; for it
was not according to his own purpose that God elected the one and thrust the
other away, for as far as it depended on him he wished both to be good, but
according to the purpose of each of these he made the election, having loved
the one because of the virtue of his purpose, and having hated the other
because of the wickedness of his judgment, from which it is clear that the

purpose according to election. Rom 9:14–21 What then shall we say? Is there
unrighteousness with God? May it not be! For he says to Moses: I will have
mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have
compassion. So then it is not of him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God
who has mercy. Since before the birth of Esau and Jacob God appears accepting
the one and having hated the other, and this seemed to be unrighteousness with
those who do not reckon the just judgment of God according to foreknowledge,
he teaches not to follow the example bare, since many other such things have
been said about God in the Scriptures that have a deeper sense. For behold God
says to Moses: I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have
compassion on whom I have compassion. So then according to you it is not of
him who wills nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy. And the one who
is zealous is superfluous. But it is not so; for God is not unjust so as not
to give each what is according to desert. He says then, he says, not all but
the one who is worthy. And again the Scripture says to Pharaoh that for this
very thing I raised you up, that I might show in you my power, and that my
name might be declared in all the earth. Therefore it is not Pharaoh who is
cause, but the one who raised him toward a display of his own power. But this
too is not so; for he would not have raised him up, even though through him he
was chastening the others, if he had not foreseen him sick beyond healing. And
if nothing is in our power as you think, he says, O 99 man, but all things are
in God, both Pharaoh's being hardened and another being shown mercy, you would
consequently say: why does he still find fault? For who has resisted his will?
For if he himself works all things through us, we are in the power of none of
the things we do. Yes rather, O man, who are you who answer back to God? Shall
the thing formed say to him who formed it: why did you make me thus? and what
follows. Correcting all the things that went before he both rebukes them as
rashly busying themselves with the will of God, and he also shows the
cheapness of human nature, taking a fitting image, the potter's clay. For why
do you busy yourself, he says, with the Creator? The potter knows how he will
use the clay and what vessels unto dishonor and what unto honor he must make
for himself. And one who is clay, being clay, must not judge the Creator's
craft; for even if you seem to have rational perception, O man, know that you
fall as far short of the economy and craft of God as unfeeling clay of the
potter's will. Do not then dare to accuse God, nor think that he has mercy on
one and hardens another as it happens, but that according to the power of
