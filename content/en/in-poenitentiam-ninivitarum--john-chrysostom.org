HOMILY II. Of our father among the saints John, Archbishop of Constantinople,
Chrysostom, on the repentance of the Ninevites.
=64.424=
Bless, Father. Seeing both the preceding days demanding teaching, and myself
compelled by weakness to be silent, I was distressed, and I was impatient,
like some eager athlete forbidden, at the time of his contests, from entering
the stadium. I saw you longing for the rational food, and myself unable to
furnish it. I saw the season of trade, and myself unable to make any contract
with you. I beheld the hunt abundant, and the hunter without strength for the
catch. But even so, though I was in great pain at this, I thought it better
for myself to measure out my labors, so as both to be able to visit the church
each day, and to share with you in the rest of the ministry, rather than,
having toiled only two or three days more, to be nailed to the bed again, and
again to be left behind from you. For this reason, omitting the daily
discourses, I have come today through the Master's grace to pay the debt to
you. Come then, let us draw the ship into the sea of the Ninevites'
repentance. For different words are useful to different people, but the
medicines of repentance I think beneficial to all; since no one is free of
sin, it is quite clear that no one is without need of repentance. And for
those who need this, the example of those saved through repentance is
necessary, introducing them also by the same manner toward health.
Let us see, then, those who had lived in wickedness becoming renowned for
virtue. Let us see those who resembled beasts taking on the rank of angels.
Let us see those who by their works were overturning the city raising the city
up again by a way of philosophy. Let us see the enemies of God becoming
friends of God, and those who of old had the Master as accuser later acquiring
him as advocate. Let us see the condemned, after the sentence, contending
against the judge, and making the sentence void. Let us see God, through love
of mankind, speaking falsely. We heard the other day how the prophet was
proclaiming, and the city was in uproar, and that just as a harsh wind
troubles the sea, so the voice of Jonah, falling upon the people of the
Ninevites, filled everything with squall and storm. We heard how the city,
considering the proclamation trustworthy, did not fall into =64.425= despair,
but ran to repentance; and that, though it did not have confidence concerning
salvation, it attempted to heal and to reconcile God. And what were the things
done for the reconciliations? It has been said in part; but I must render you
the whole account: for they proclaimed a fast, and they put on sackcloth from
the least of them even to the greatest of them. O strange wonder, fearful to
men, but longed for by angels! Consider for me those many myriads of men
together with women and children wearing sackcloth, and gathered into the same
place; all occupations and all human actions idle, and no work being
accomplished by anyone anywhere, but a wailing of all together and an uproar,
and a cry being sent up to heaven.
Consider the inequality of masters and slaves thrown out, of rulers and ruled,
the king present with the crowds in the same appearance, and arranging the
service of God as if some civic battle-line. For the word, it says, came near
to the king, and he rose from his throne, and he stripped off his robe, and
put on sackcloth, and sat upon ashes. O wise king. He himself leads the
repentance, so as to make the whole city more earnest. For who was still going
to grow soft, seeing the king himself contending for salvation? He heals the
wounds of the purple by the sackcloth; he washes out the sins of the throne by
sitting on ashes; he cures the disease of pride by the humility of the
appearance; by fasting he medicates the sores of luxury; and having shown
these things by deeds, he rouses all through a proclamation to equal zeal. For
it was proclaimed, it says, by the king: Let the men, and the cattle, and the
oxen, and the sheep taste nothing, let them graze nothing, and let them not
drink water. A novel legislation of a king: as a physician he orders fasting
to the sick; or rather the luxurious lawgiver imitates the apostolic teachings
of pious fasting, and he commands the irrational creatures to share in it with
the humans, drawing God to greater pity through the sinless nature. And the
men and the cattle, it says, put on sackcloth. O heavenly battle-line! O
phalanx terrible to the devil! The devil stood lamenting, seeing his army gone
over in a body to God and warring against the demons; then this battle both
children and women, infants together with men, were fighting; and already the
nature of the cattle was taken on as an ally into the battle-line. And see a
novel spectacle for the devil: beasts contributing righteousness along with
men, and fasting for the salvation of their owners: Let the men, and the oxen,
and the cattle taste nothing, nor graze.
The king, having thrown away the symbols of rule, took on the rank of the
priests. He stood haranguing, and teaching the Ninevites =64.428= to
philosophize. Terrible, he says, O friends, are the evils that surround us.
Having subdued the whole inhabited world, we as wicked are being destroyed by
a divine vote, and though we reign over all men, we as more worthless than all
have been condemned to a strange manner of death, and having been counted
blessed above all others, we shall seem more wretched than all, and we shall
be a myth and a tale for the whole of life, since our former splendor does not
even allow our evils to escape notice. Having conquered many wars, O
Ninevites, let us now contend more nobly for our own salvation; for of old we
did everything so that we might become masters of others; but now the prize
for us is not to perish utterly with wives and children. Against the uprising
of a strange war let us contend in a strange way: let us shoot the heaven with
hymns; let us hurl psalmody there instead of spears; let us sling God with
prayers; with unceasing tears let us soothe his wrath; let us break the
phalanx of wicked deeds; let us cast down the fortresses of vice; let us fight
with the weapons of virtue; a dart does not wound a breastplate of
righteousness; a thunderbolt cannot kindle a shield of faith; God does not
endure to crush a helmet of hope; the wrath from above does not split a tunic
of temperance; a threat of overthrow has not the strength to pull down a wall
of confession; a sentence of destruction, seeing earnest repentance, is
dissolved at once like smoke; if we fight thus, O friends, and with such
weapons, we shall master heaven as we have mastered the earth. Take courage,
Ninevites, take courage: a king who loves mankind wages war against us; having
only thrown his enemies down as suppliants, and having well set his wrath, he
becomes a benefactor of his foes.
With such hortatory words the king was stirring the people toward repentance.
And the Ninevites eagerly carried out what was commanded: For the men and the
cattle, it says, put on sackcloth, and they cried out earnestly to God. It was
not that the word was ready while the reasoning was aloft; it was not that the
tongue uttered while the mind wandered; it was not that the voice was brought
forth while the purpose was carried aside; but both, intently, were entreating
God. Night coming on, and increasing the fear, was driving sleep away, and
sending up the hymn more earnestly. Day again, showing the heaven gloomy, and
being struck with unceasing lightnings and thunderings, was moving all into
extraordinary trembling and a very great cry. The earth being shaken was
working a great agitation in their souls. All the infants, running in fear to
the bosoms of their mothers, and wailing in their arms, were making an
unspeakable lament and mourning for those who saw them. Herds of oxen and
sheep, and all the flocks, barred from pasture and waters, and giving forth
various voices, were moving even adamantine souls to weeping. And though the
threat and the fear were troubling them from every side, they did not endure
to throw away the good hope. For who knows, it says, if God will repent,
=64.429= and will turn from the wrath of his anger, and we shall not perish?
From the deepest heart, then, they were crying out to God: King, Lord of life
and of death, we indeed are worthy of the sentence you threatened, and we owe
each penalty, having not known you the true God, and having lived a corrupted
life. But you, making use of the sea of your compassions, stop the punishment
against us; conquer the intervening justice by goodness, and our ingratitude
by love of mankind; if you had brought the blow suddenly, the punishment would
have had a reason, and you would have been killing those who were aliens and
worthless; but now, having given a term through kindness, if you slay those
who have been changed, you are destroying not aliens, but your own. Did you
keep the impious, and now do you destroy those who are becoming faithful? Did
you save them when they were wicked, and now do you kill those who are doing
good? Did you increase the city devoted to idols, and make it a queen, and do
you overthrow it once it has become yours? Not knowing you, we mastered the
inhabited world through you, and now, worshipping you, are we being cast out
even of life itself? Spare your own name, O King; do not, by exacting
penalties from the Ninevites, make piety something to be fled as unprofitable;
do not, by justly punishing us, shut the door of repentance to men.
For if, having obtained a lawgiver and teachers, we had gone forward into much
vice, destroy us inexorably, do not grant pardon to those who entreat, bring
on a penalty harsher than the one you threatened; but if, neither having
learned nor having been instructed, we continued in worthlessness and impiety,
do not, O King, vote so great a destruction of our ignorance. You destroyed
men of old by a flood justly; for they did not attend to Noah when he was
teaching; you foretold the destruction, and they did not flee the sin. You
burned the Sodomites reasonably, for they were not willing to receive the
counsels of Lot. The Egyptians were swallowed up fittingly with Pharaoh, for
they did not obey Moses when he was teaching, though they had been struck many
times, and had taken experience of your right hand. Did we dare to contradict
anyone? You have now sent only one to us, and not even this one as a teacher,
but as a reporter of evils, and having believed we honor your worship in a
body. You have given us a small term, and in it we have shown a very great
change. When destruction was proclaimed to us, we did not despair of your love
of mankind. When the prophet announced the overthrow, we hoped in your mercy.
Do not falsify our hopes, O good one! Do not stop tongues that wish to hymn
your kindness! Do not kill men who are eager to proclaim your goodness to all!
Do not prevent us from becoming teachers of piety to all who are ours! Do not
remain decreeing the same against those who have no longer remained in the
same things! We have been changed; be reconciled yourself. Stay the wrath, as
we have stayed the error.
Stop the punishment yourself, as we have stopped the vice. You have trained us
sufficiently by fear; give us time henceforth to show the affection toward
you. =64.432= Spare the flocks lowing through famine; spare the sinless
children being choked by weeping. Spare untimely infants weakened by fasting.
Do not destroy the guiltless together with the guilty. Pity mothers who
through your wrath are not furnishing milk to their dearest; for parents have
denied those mercies, that they might draw your mercy. All men endure the same
wickednesses as we; if you do not receive us who are repenting, you have cut
off the hopes of the whole world. With such words, as is likely, the Ninevites
were speaking at the beginning; and the works also were a match for the
words: fasting, sackcloth, earnest prayer, tears, flight from wickedness. For
they cried out, it says, to God earnestly, and each turned from his own wicked
way, and from the injustice that was in their hands. They take hold of
philosophy from its very extremities, and they make the crown of virtue the
foundation of virtue; for whatever work was most beautiful, and most dear to
God, this each was practicing. They saw a heavenly war being raised against
them, and they fortified themselves with good works, as a wall [with a
wall].
They put on a certain marvelous panoply, the sackcloth. They arrayed prayers
from below, and supplications, against the thunderbolts being borne from
above. To the crash of the thunders they opposed the sound of hymnody.
Fasting became for them a manifest tower. With streams of tears they quenched
the phalanx of sins. They made friends of their enemies, that they might
reconcile God to themselves. They themselves let go wrath toward those who had
grieved them, that they might stop the Master's wrath against them. They tore
the bond of debts below, that they might obtain forgiveness from above for
those who had stumbled. They gave freedom to slaves, that they themselves
might receive freedom from punishment. They added their possessions to the
needy, that they might securely acquire their own substance. Having thrown
luxury away, they were pursuing self-control. Having fled incontinence, they
were embracing temperance. Having hated the bulk of dignities, they all
changed into a humble and mourning appearance. Having ceased from wares, and
contracts, and all works, they were serving God. The criminals, with no one
compelling them, were confessing the robberies. The judges were not exacting
penalties from those who confessed. All, as if dead to life, were confessing.
Wealth was thrown unguarded, and the thief appeared from nowhere. Gold was
furnished to the needy, and each fled possession as a loss. One thing only was
being pursued by all, salvation. One course, both for rich and for poor, the
labor concerning life was set before them. And everything that did not bear
toward this was thrown out as unprofitable and worthless. Salvation was
unclear, and it was being pursued among them as if it were confessed. They did
not have confidence in the end of the repentance, and as if they would live in
any case they were taking care of every good deed. O philosophical barbarians!
O wise uneducated men! They knew the things that were needed without being
taught, they were reshaped without a lawgiver, they were brought to temperance
without teachers, from the last vice they ran up to the heavenly =64.433=
citizenship. This is the change of the right hand of the Most High. One rain
of threat, and the Ninevites sprouted a crop of countless goods; they were
recast as some old statues.
They were remade out of the repentance; from sinners they have become
righteous, beloved to angels, longed for by God. They held the city that was
being shaken, they set upright what was falling, they made firm what was being
tossed. They were turned, and they were not overturned; they were changed, and
they were not destroyed. For God, it says, saw their works, that they turned
from their wicked ways, and he repented of the evil which he had spoken to do
to them. O paradoxical things! The judge retried the case, he dissolved the
murderous vote, and he voted the one that saves. The Ninevites repented, and
God repented. The repentance split the command of destruction. They tore the
wickedness, God tore the sentence. O repentance that can do all things!
It is accomplished on earth, and it overturns the things in heaven. So let us
also, O friends, repent; so let us fast; let us yoke good deeds to the
fasting; let us abstain from wicked deeds; let us fear the threat of Gehenna;
through repentance we shall acquire the salvation from God. To him be the
glory, and the power, unto the ages of ages. Amen.
