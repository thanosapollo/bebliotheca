[lost]  I did not, then, come upon forensic speeches of his, but a few
deliberative ones and also some handbooks, and the greater number epideictic.
And the character of the form of his speeches is of this kind, and he praises
those of the Athenians who distinguished themselves in wars: /For what was
absent from these men, of the things that ought to belong to men? And what was
present, of the things that ought to be absent? May I be able to say what I
wish, and may I wish what I ought, escaping the divine vengeance, and fleeing
the human envy. For these men possessed a virtue that was god-inspired, and a
mortality that was human, preferring in many things the present equity to
stubborn justice, and in many things the rightness of words to the exactness
of law, thinking this the most divine and most common of laws, to say and to
be silent and to do what is needed at the needed time, and having practiced
especially the two things that are needed, judgment and strength, counseling
the one and accomplishing the other, servants of those who suffer unjustly,
punishers of those who prosper unjustly, stubborn toward advantage,
even-tempered toward the fitting, stopping the mindless by the prudence of
judgment, insolent toward the insolent, orderly toward the orderly, fearless
toward the fearless, formidable in formidable things. And as witnesses of
these things they set up trophies of the enemy, statues of Zeus, and these as
dedications, not inexperienced either of inborn Ares or of lawful loves or of
armed strife or of peace that loves the beautiful, solemn toward the gods by
justice, pious toward their parents by service, just toward the citizens by
equality, faithful toward friends by trust. Therefore when they died the
longing did not die with them, but lives immortal in bodies that are not
immortal, of those who are not living./  [lost]  / So then the affairs of the
cities were in faction, and those that lagged somewhere, by report of what had
happened before, brought the excess far on toward innovating the minds, both
by the artful contrivance of enterprises and by the strangeness of the
punishments. And they exchanged the accustomed valuation of names toward the
deeds by their justification. For reckless daring was counted as loyal
courage, and provident delay as specious cowardice, and the temperate as a
pretext of the unmanly. And intelligence toward everything was counted
altogether idle, and the impulsively sharp was assigned to a man's portion,
and to plot was safety, a reasonable pretext of prevention. And the one who
was harsh was always trusted, and the one who contradicted him was suspected.
And someone who plotted and hit the mark was intelligent, and one who
suspected still more formidable. And one who planned ahead so that he would
need nothing was both a dissolver of the companionship and panic-struck at the
opponents. And simply, the one who anticipated the one about to do some harm
was praised, and the one who urged on the one not intending it. And moreover
kindred became more alien than the companionable, because it was more ready to
dare without pretext. For such gatherings were not for help with the
established laws, but against the established ones for advantage. And the
trusts toward themselves they strengthened not so much by the divine and
lawful as by jointly breaking some law; and what was well said from the
opponents they received with a guard of deeds, if they should have the
advantage, and not with nobility. And to take revenge on someone in return was
of more account than not to suffer first oneself. And oaths, if anywhere they
happened of reconciliation, being given in the immediate toward the difficulty
for each, had force when they had no power from elsewhere./  This, then, was
the kind of diction that is altered and extraordinary and constructed and
filled up with all the added ornaments, of which Thucydides is the definition
and canon, whom none of those who came after either surpassed or imitated to
the peak.

And the other diction, the plain and simple one that seems to have both
construction and force of a kind that belongs to a private man's speech, and a
likeness to it, had many good men as champions, historians and philosophers
and orators. For both those who published genealogies and those who treated
local histories and those who philosophized on nature and the makers of
ethical dialogues, of whom the whole Socratic school was, except Plato, and
those who composed deliberative or forensic speeches, nearly all belonged to
this choice. And Lysias son of Cephalus perfected it and brought it to the
peak of its own virtue, having been born in the same times as Gorgias and
Thucydides. And what his choice was and what his power was has been shown in
the writing before this one, and there is no need now to speak again about the
same things. It will suffice to say only this much, that these men tuned
toward one another the harmony through all the notes, having chosen and
perfected with a daemonic zeal both extremes of diction, which stand farthest
from one another. And the relation that the nete has toward the hypate in
music, that the diction of Lysias has in political speech toward that of
Thucydides. For the one can strike the mind with amazement, and the other can
please it, and the one can gather and tighten the thought, and the other can
loosen and soften it, and that one can lead on into passion, and this one can
settle into character. Except that to force and to compel something is also
peculiar to the diction of Thucydides, and to deceive and to steal the facts
is peculiar to that of Lysias. And innovation and boldness belong to the form
of the historian, and safety and the absence of risk to that of the orator.
[lost]  that it does not appear in artifice  [lost]  it wishes to be
unartificial. Each, then, has been constructed and has come to the peak of its
own construction; but the one leans toward seeming more than it is by nature,
and the other toward less. And I think there is no need of examples of this
diction either, in the present. These, then, are two characters of diction, so
different from one another in their courses, and the men who held first place
in them, whom I have gone through, both formidable in their own works, and
incomplete in that in which they were equal to one another.

A third  [lost]  of diction was the mixed and composite one from these two,
which the first who fitted it together and established it into the order that
now exists was either Thrasymachus of Chalcedon, as Theophrastus thinks, or
someone else, I cannot say. And those who received it and reared it up and did
not fall far short of perfecting it were, among orators, Isocrates the
Athenian, and among philosophers, Plato the Socratic; for it is impossible to
find any others of these men besides Demosthenes who either practiced the
necessary and useful things better, or displayed fine speech and the added
constructions more finely. The diction of Thrasymachus, then, if it really was
some source of the mean, seems to have the choice itself worthy of zeal; for
it is blended in a good way and has taken the useful itself of each. But in
power it has not used a power equal to the will, and an example from one of
the deliberative speeches is this: /I would have wished, men of Athens, to
have a share in that old time and those affairs, when it was enough for the
younger men to be silent, both because the affairs did not compel speaking and
because the elders were managing the city rightly. But since the daimon has
set us into such a time that  of the city to hear, and the disasters
themselves and the greatest of these are not works of the gods nor of fortune
but of those who took charge, there is a necessity to speak. For he is either
without feeling or most enduring, whoever will still offer himself to go wrong
at the hands of those who wish it, and will himself undergo the charges of
others' plotting and wickedness. For the time that has gone by is enough for
us, both to have come into war and danger instead of peace, up to this time
loving the day that has passed and fearing the one that is coming, and instead
of concord to have arrived at enmity and disturbances toward one another. And
the multitude of goods makes the others both insolent and factious, but we
were temperate with the goods, and in the evils we went mad, the things that
are accustomed to make the others temperate. Why then would someone delay to
cry out and speak, for whom at least  to be pained at the present things and
to think he has something of such a kind, that nothing of such a kind will
still be? First, then, I will show that those who differ toward one another,
both of the orators and of the others, have suffered toward one another
contrary to reason, which those who contend without judgment must suffer; for
thinking they are saying opposite things to one another they do not perceive
that they are doing the same things, nor that the speech of the others is
inside their own speech. For consider from the beginning what each of the two
sides is seeking. First the ancestral constitution provides them a
disturbance, being easiest to know and most common to all the citizens. As
many things, then, as are beyond our judgment, † it is necessary to hear to
speak  of the older men, and as many as the elders themselves saw, these one
must inquire from those who know./ Such, then, is the Thrasymachean
interpretation, a mean of the two and well-blended and a timely starting-point
into both characters.

And the diction of Isocrates, who acquired the greatest name among the Greeks
for wisdom, the man having never contended in any contest, private or public,
but having composed many fine writings into every form of speeches, what
character it seemed to me to have I showed before at greater length. And
nothing will prevent me now also from saying the most necessary points in
summary: that it has the purity of the Lysianic diction and the exactness; for
it has used neither archaic nor coined nor glossematic words, but the most
common and most customary. And it is ethical and persuasive and pleasant, and
it has fled the tropical, as that one has, the simple phrasing, and it has
taken from Thucydides and Gorgias the magnificence and solemnity and fine
speech. And toward teaching the hearer most clearly what it would wish, it
practices the simple and unadorned interpretation of Lysias, and toward
striking amazement by the beauty of the words and putting solemnity and grand
speech around the facts it has copied the added and constructed phrasing of
those around Gorgias. And it fails in the places where it sometimes makes
itself pretty, emulating the youthful figures of Gorgias (for the antitheses
and parisoses and the things like these, neither being moderate nor coming at
the right time, shame its magnificence), and still more in the places where,
pursuing elegance and good rhythm, it takes through caution the clashing of
the vowel letters, and through caution it makes the using of any of the things
that roughen. And it pursues from every way the period, and not even this a
round and compact one, but some leading-on and broad one that takes many bends
into bays, as rivers that do not flow in a straight line do. These things,
however, in many places make it both longer and less true, passionless and
soulless and more panegyric than agonistic. And I will use the examples a
little later, when the occasion demands.

And the Platonic dialect wishes itself also to be a mixture of both
characters, the lofty and the thin, as I have said before, but by nature it is
not equally fortunate toward both characters. Whenever, then, it practices the
thin and simple and unmade phrasing, it is extraordinarily pleasant and
humane. For it becomes sufficiently pure and translucent, like the most
transparent of streams, and exact and subtle beside any other of those worked
into the same dialect. And it pursues the commonness of the words and
practices clarity, having looked down on every added construction. And the
patina and the bloom of antiquity run over it gently and without being
noticed, and it sends up some green and flourishing flower full of season. And
as from the most fragrant meadows some pleasant breeze is carried from it. And
neither does the clear-toned seem to display chatter, nor the elegant the
theatrical. But whenever it takes an immoderate impulse into wordiness and
fine speaking, which it is often accustomed to do, it becomes much worse than
itself; for it appears both more unpleasant than the other and worse at
Hellenizing and thicker, and it darkens the clear and makes it like gloom, and
it draws the thought out long, when it ought to gather it in few words. And it
pours out into tasteless periphrases, displaying an empty wealth of words, and
looking down on the proper ones that lie in common use it seeks the coined and
the strange and the archaic-seeming. And it is storm-tossed most of all about
tropical phrasing, abundant in epithets, untimely in metonymies, and hard and
not preserving analogy in metaphors. And it wraps itself in many long
allegories, having neither measure nor occasion. And it luxuriates untimely
and boyishly in poetic figures that strike the last unpleasantness, and most
of all in the Gorgianic ones. And there is much of the initiate-master in such
things with him, as Demetrius of Phalerum has said somewhere, and many others
before. For the tale is not mine.

And let no one suppose that I say such things as condemning the whole of the
altered and constructed diction that Plato has used; for may I not become so
clumsy or so without feeling as to take this opinion about a man of such size,
since I know many great and wonderful things about many subjects brought out
by him from the peak of power; but wishing to show this, that he is accustomed
to fail in such failures in the constructions, and that he becomes worse than
himself whenever he pursues the great and extraordinary in phrasing, and
better by some long way whenever he brings in the thin and exact dialect that
seems to be unmade but has been constructed with a blameless and simple
construction. For either he fails in nothing at all, or some very small thing
and not worthy of accusation. And I thought a man of such size ought to have
been guarded from every censure. The same things, however, those who were born
in his own time also censure in the man as failing, whose names I have no need
to say, and he himself to himself; for this indeed is the most brilliant. For
he perceived, as it seems, his own tastelessness and set a name on it, the
dithyramb, which I would now have been ashamed to say, though it is true. And
he seems to have suffered this, as I at least think, having been reared in the
Socratic dialogues, which are thinnest and most exact, but not having remained
in them, but having fallen in love with the construction of Gorgias and
Thucydides. So that he was likely to suffer nothing outside the probable,
having drawn some of the failures as well, together with the goods that the
characters of those men have.

And I make an example of the lofty diction at least from one book of the very
celebrated, in which Socrates sets out the erotic speeches toward one of his
acquaintances, Phaedrus, from whom the book has taken its title. For in this
writing these first things have much bloom and are full of graces:  /My dear
Phaedrus, where then and from where? From Lysias, Socrates, the son of
Cephalus. I am going then toward a walk outside the wall. For I spent much
time there sitting from dawn/ until the reading of the Lysianic speech and
after the reading up to a certain point. Then, as a great wind breaking down
out of a clear and still air, it disturbs the purity of the phrasing, carrying
it out into poetic tastelessness, beginning from here: /Come then, Muses,
whether through a form of song you are clear-voiced, or through the musical
race of the Ligyans you have this name, take hold of the tale with me./ And
that these are noises and dithyrambs, having much boast of words and little
thought, he himself will say. For going through from what cause the name eros
was set on the passion, and having used this: /For desire that has mastered a
judgment without reason that is rushing toward the good, leading toward a
pleasure of beauty and of the desires akin to itself, having been strongly
strengthened toward beauty of bodies, having conquered, a leading that took a
name from the strength itself, was called eros/ and having lengthened out so
great a periphrasis of a thing that could be taken in few words, he lays hold
of his own untimeliness himself and says: /In silence, then, hear me. For in
truth the place seems to be divine. So that if I often become nymph-seized as
the speech goes forward, do not wonder. For now I am no longer speaking far
from certain dithyrambs./ These things, not by others, but we are caught by
our own words according to the tragedy, most daemonic Plato, having loved
noises and nonsense of dithyrambs. And what Socrates has said again in the
palinode, making atonement for eros, beginning from here: /The great leader in
heaven, Zeus, driving a winged chariot, goes first, ordering all things and
taking care. And there follows him an army of gods and daimons ordered
according to eleven parts. For Hestia remains in the house of the gods alone.
And of the others, as many as among the number of the twelve gods have been
stationed as ruling gods, they lead according to the order in which each was
stationed, and there are many blessed sights and goings-out within heaven,
which the race of happy gods turns about, each doing through himself their own
things. And there follows always the one who both wishes and is able; for envy
stands outside the divine chorus./ These things and the things like them,
which are many, if they took tunes and rhythms as the dithyrambs and the
hyporchemes do, would seem to resemble the poems of Pindar spoken toward the
sun, as at least it appears to me: /Ray of the sun, what have you contrived,
much-seeing mother of swift eyes? Highest star, stolen in the day you have set
a helpless winged strength for men and a road of wisdom, rushing a shadowed
path. Are you driving something newer than before? But I beseech you, toward
Zeus, horse-driving, swift, lady, that you turn some unharming road for
Thebes, a wonder common to all. And if you bring a sign of some war or a
wasting of fruit or an exceeding strength of snowstorm or a destructive
faction or an emptying of the sea over the plain or a frost of the earth or a
southern summer wet with raging water, or having flooded the earth you will
set a new race of men from the beginning, I lament nothing, because I will
suffer with all./  And here the allegory is not untimely, as it is in Plato.

But, in order that the speech may not go forward for me beyond what is needed,
I will let Plato go, and I will proceed to Demosthenes, for whose sake I
enumerated both the characters of diction that I thought were strongest, and
those who held power in them, not all of them; for Antiphon and Theodorus and
Polycrates and Isaeus and Zoilos and Anaximenes and those born in the same
times as these practiced nothing either new or extraordinary, but constructed
their own dictions from these characters and beside these canons. Demosthenes,
then, having found the political diction in such a state, so variously moved,
and having come in after men of such size, did not think it right to become an
emulator of any one character or of any one man, thinking them all some
half-works and incomplete, but selecting from all of them as many things as
were strongest and most useful he wove them together and completed one dialect
out of many, magnificent plain, extraordinary unextraordinary, altered
customary, panegyric true, austere cheerful, tense relaxed, pleasant bitter,
ethical passionate, differing in nothing from the Proteus mythologized among
the ancient poets, who took on every form of shape without toil, whether that
one was a god or some daimon deceiving human sights, or some varied thing of
dialect in a wise man, deceptive of every hearing, which one would rather
guess, since it is not holy to attach humble and unshapely sights either to
gods or to daimons. I, then, have some such opinion about the diction of
Demosthenes, and I assign him this character, the mixed one from every form.

And if I have judged the fitting things, it is open to whoever wishes to
examine, making the test on the examples themselves. The things, then, that
have been constructed by the orator beside the character of Thucydides are
some such as these: /Many speeches, men of Athens, being made almost in every
assembly about the things in which Philip, from the time he made the peace,
wrongs not only you but also the other Greeks, and all, I know well, would
have said, even if they do not do this, that one must both speak and act so
that that man will cease from the insolence and will pay justice, I see all
the affairs led down into this and given up, so that I fear it may be
blasphemous to say, but true: even if all who come forward wished to speak,
and you to vote, from which the affairs were going to be in the worst state, I
do not think they could be disposed worse than now./ In respect of what, then,
do I think this diction resembles that of Thucydides? In respect of that in
which I am persuaded that that one too most differs from the others. And this
is that the thoughts have not been brought out according to a straight
interpretation, nor, as it is customary for the others to speak, simply and
plainly, but the dialect has been altered and turned away from the things in
custom and according to nature into the things not customary for the many, nor
as nature demands. And what I say is of this kind. The speech would have been
simple and brought out according to a straight interpretation, if someone had
constructed it thus: /Many speeches, men of Athens, being made in almost every
assembly, about the things in which Philip wrongs both you and the other
Greeks, from the time he made the peace./ But as it is, both the /almost/
taken on instead of /nearly/ and the /Philip wrongs/ divided and recovering
its sequence through a long stretch, and the /not only you but also the other
Greeks/, when it was possible even without a negation to show the fact through
the conjunction alone, have made the diction altered from the customary and
overworked. And likewise also what is said after this, if it needed to be
spoken simply and without overwork, would have been reported in this way, I
suppose: / and all saying, even if some do not do this, that one must both
speak and act these things, from which that man will cease from the insolence
and will pay justice./ But brought out thus: /and all, I know well, would have
said/ it does not preserve the straight road of the diction. For the /I know
well that/ did not have a necessary place, and the /would have said/ taken
instead of /asserting/ displays not the simple dialect but the altered and
overworked. And like these are those too: /Then do you think that those whom
he would not have been able to do any harm, and they themselves would perhaps
have guarded against not suffering, he chooses rather to deceive these than,
speaking in advance, to force them?/ For here the diction would have had
nothing overworked or crooked, if he had brought it out in this way: /Then do
you think that he, those whom he saw able to dispose him no worse, and who
would perhaps have guarded against not suffering, chooses rather to deceive
these than, speaking in advance, to force them?/ But with the case exchanged
and many connectives gathered into a short space, I think, the dialect has
become overworked and uncustomary and altered. Still those too are of the same
form: /But now he did not do this, in which he would have honored the people,
nor did he play the youth in any such thing. But me, who, whether someone, men
of Athens, wishes to think it madness (for perhaps it is madness to do
something beyond power), or even ambition, I undertook to be chorus-leader, so
openly and foully insulting he followed along, that at last he did not keep
his hands off even my sacred garments nor the chorus nor my body./ What, then,
again in these is the thing that disturbs the report according to nature?
First, before finishing the leading thing, whether one must call it a thought
or a colon, inserting another, and when the second does not even have an end,
yoking on the third, then putting the sequence of the second thought at the
third that has taken an end, and then after all, what was a part of the first,
giving it back through a long stretch and when the thought no longer admits
it. But me who (this does not yet have an end) whether someone, men of
Athens, wishes to think it madness (this another, separated from the former,
incomplete itself too) for perhaps it is madness to do something beyond power
(this again is not a part of either of the things said before but itself by
itself; for it is some summary assertion) or ambition (and this was a part
of the second, of whether someone wishes to think it madness. And what is said
after all these, the /I undertook to be chorus-leader/, was a part of the
first, of /But me, who/. There are countless such things in Demosthenes, and
most of all in the speeches against Philip, or rather  [lost]  not being thus
except one speech, the one about Halonnesus, and many also in the forensic
contests, at least the public ones. And almost in these and in the
deliberative speeches, by the sign I said, you would discern most readily the
character of Demosthenes. And by the man's having used them less or more,
looking toward the natures of the subjects and the dignities of the persons,
someone will be led astray; which perhaps is not unreasonable.

Come then, let us also say in what the diction of Demosthenes that has been
constructed beside the same character differs from that of Thucydides; for the
argument demands it. Not in the quality, by Zeus; for this both practice
similarly, I mean the altering from the customary and pursuing not the common
but the extraordinary; but in the quantity, and still more in the occasions.
For the one has used the construction without measure, and is led by it more
than he himself leads, and he does not even know how to take its occasion
skillfully, but he often fails even against this. In respect of which the
measurelessness of the alteration makes his diction unclear, and the not
mastering the occasions makes it unpleasant. But the orator aims at the
sufficient and measures the occasions, constructing the diction not only
toward a dedication and a possession as the historian does, but also toward
use. So that he has neither stepped out of the clear, of which agonistic
speeches first have need, and he has taken on in addition the seeming to be
formidable, about which he appears to be most zealous. Such, then, are some of
the things that Demosthenes has constructed, imitating the lofty and
constructed character altered from the customary, whose whole strength was in
force, and Thucydides who held first place in it.

And the things beside the thin and exact and pure and emulable  [lost]  which
from the one who shone in it would reasonably be called Lysianic, are such.
And nothing will prevent, and perhaps it will also make the theory more
graceful, if first the diction of Lysias is set down, which I am persuaded the
diction of Demosthenes resembles, containing some insolent narrative: /For
this Archippus, men of Athens, stripped for the same wrestling-school where
also Tisis, the one fleeing the suit, did. And anger having come about they
came into jeers toward themselves and contradiction and enmity and abuse.
There is, then, Pytheas, a lover of the youth (for all the truths will be said
toward you), and a guardian left by the father. This man, when Tisis related
to him the abuse in the wrestling-school, wishing to gratify and to seem
formidable and plotting, ordered him, as we have perceived both from the
things done and have learned from those who know well, for the present to be
reconciled, but to watch how he would take him somewhere alone. And having
been persuaded of these things and having been reconciled and using him and
pretending to be a friend, to this point of madness, being of such an age, he
stood off,  and it happened there was a horse-race of the Anakeia  and him
with me passing by the door; for they happen to be neighbors of one another;
first he ordered him to dine together, and when he did not wish  upon  a
revel, saying that with himself also the  so having dined already as it was
growing dark we came and knocked the door. And they ordered us to go in. And
when we had come inside, they throw me out of the house, and having seized
this man they bound him to the pillar, and Tisis, taking a whip, having
stretched many blows, shut him into a room. And it was not enough for him to
fail in these things only, but having emulated the most wicked of the young
men in the city, and having lately taken over the paternal things and
pretending to be young and rich, again he ordered the household slaves, day
having already come, having bound him to the pillar to whip him. And with the
body already so badly disposed, having sent for Antimachus he said nothing of
the things that had happened. And he said that he himself happened to be
dining, and this man came drunk, and having knocked out the door and gone in
he spoke ill of him and Antimachus and their women. And Antimachus was angry
at them as having failed greatly, but nevertheless having called witnesses he
asked him how he had gone in. And he said at the order of Tisis and of the
household. And those who had come in advising to loose him as quickly as
possible and thinking the things that had happened to be terrible, they gave
him back to the brothers. And not being able to walk, they carried him into
the Deigma on a couch and showed him to many of the Athenians and to many also
of the other foreigners so disposed, so that those who saw were angry not only
at those who had done it but also accused the city, that it does not punish
publicly and at once those who fail in such things./

The diction of Isocrates, then, that seems to be finest of the others is of

other in the words and is exact in the dialect, and it is plain and common and

And to assert that he has used vulgar and unpleasant words, from where it came
upon him to say it, I at least have wondered above all. For I find none of
these lying in Demosthenes, of the things Aeschines says he has said, such as
that /one must not tear off the alliance from the friendship/ and that /some
vine-work the city/ and /the sinews of the people have been cut under/ and /we
are being basket-stitched/ and /some thread through the narrows like needles,/
upon which he himself puts, mocking through: /And these, you fox, what are
they? Verbs or wonders?/ Nor have I been able to find some other vulgar and
unpleasant words in any of the speeches of Demosthenes, and this though that
man has left behind five or six myriads of lines. If however in the falsely
titled speeches there are some unpleasant and vulgar and rustic constructions,
as in those against Aristogeiton  2 and in the defense of the gifts and in the
one about not giving up Harpalus and in the one against Neaera and in the one
about the treaties toward Alexander and in many others, which Demosthenes did
not write, in another treatise the things about Demosthenes are shown by me.

Aeschines has censured him, these are enough. And already somewhere those too
some have noted, some as characteristic and some as a failure of the orator, I
mean the making the same fact clear by many words sometimes, of which kind are
these: /And it will be possible for Philip both to act and to do whatever he
wishes/ and /this Meidias, not knowing who he ever is, nor recognizing/ and
/in the presence of the sister, still a girl and a child/ and all such things.
As many, then, as show it a peculiarity of the character of Demosthenes, speak
rightly; for the man has used it usefully, as also the cutting and the brevity
more than all at least and more timely. And as many as bring it in the share
of a failure, not having examined the causes through which he was accustomed
sometimes to prevail in the words, accuse him not as they ought, but those who
sycophant this seem to demand the brevity from every way, which, as I said, he
furnishes more than all and more timely, and of the other virtues none, no
longer seeing together that the orator must also aim at clarity and at
vividness and at increase and at the well-rhythmedness about the composition
of the words, and above all these at making the diction passionate and ethical
and agonistic, in which is the greatest share of the persuasive. And each of
these virtues brevity is not able to make most strongly, but also the
prevailing of some words, which Demosthenes too has used. And I would have
brought you examples of the things said, if I were not going to appear
wearisome, speaking toward you indeed. These things, strongest Ammaeus, we had
to write to you about the diction of Demosthenes. And if the daimonion saves
us, also about his pragmatic force, a theorem still greater than this and more
wonderful, in the things that will be written next we will give back the
argument to you.
