animal is an animate sensitive substance. See: substance is genus, and the

matter and form, as: a statue is something made of bronze, signifying the form
of a man; the bronze, then, is matter, and the form of the man is form. Matter
is analogous to genus, and form to difference. It is also taken from subject
and end, as: medicine is an art occupied about human bodies, productive of
health. See: the subjects of medicine are human bodies, and its end is health.
Description is composed of the non-essential, that is properties and
accidents, as: man is capable of laughter, walking upright, broad-nailed; for
all these are non-essential. Therefore it is also called description, as
shading, as it were, and not making clear the essential existence of the
underlying thing but the consequents. The descriptive definition is mixed from
essential and non-essential, as: man is a rational animal, walking upright,
broad-nailed. Definition is so called by metaphor from the boundary-marks of
the land;


for just as the boundary-mark separates what is proper to each from what is
alien, so also the definition separates the nature of each from the nature of
the other. Health of a definition is neither to fall short nor to exceed in
the words, and vice both to fall short and to exceed in the words. A complete
definition is one that converts with the defined, and incomplete one that does
not convert. Neither, then, the one that falls short in the words converts,
nor the one that exceeds. For when it exceeds in the words, it falls short in
the things; and when it falls short in the words, it exceeds in the things. So
that one may say: nature has invented a wondrous device, want that is rich and
wealth that pretends to want. For example the complete definition of man,
"rational mortal animal." See, it converts; for every rational mortal animal
is a man, and every man is a rational mortal animal. If then it falls short by
one word, it exceeds in the things, as "rational animal." See, it fell short
in the word, for I did not say "mortal," and it exceeded in the things, for
not only man is a rational animal but also angel, and it does not convert. But
if I say "rational animal, mortal, grammatical," again it does not convert;
for it exceeded in the word by saying "grammatical," and it fell short in the
things. For it did not define every man, but only the grammatical. For every
rational, mortal, grammatical animal is a man, but not every man is a
rational, mortal, grammatical animal; for not every man is grammatical.
Complete definitions, then, are those that convert with the defined. But since
the property also converts with that of which it is a property (for if
anything is a man, this is capable of laughter, and if anything is capable of
laughter, this is a man), we must, after further determining, say that
complete definitions are those taken from genus and constitutive differences
and neither falling short in the word nor exceeding and converting with the
defined. Likewise also those taken from the combination of both, both the
subject and the end, are complete, and those from the combination of both,
both matter and form, and sometimes also those from the subject alone, when
that subject is not subject to another art, as glass to glass-working, and
from the end alone, when that end cannot be the end of another art, as in
shipbuilding. So that from all these one must know that completeness of a term
is converting. Definition differs from term by the more universal and the more
particular; for term is more universal than definition. For term signifies the
boundary-mark, and it also signifies the decree, as we say "the king
determined," and it also signifies that into which the proposition is
analyzed, as we shall learn in what follows with God, and it also signifies
the definition. But definition signifies only the concise account that makes
clear the nature of the underlying thing. One must know that definition is
taken of substance alone and of its species. We can make a definition neither
of an individual nor of accidents, but a description, because the definition
is composed of genus and constitutive differences, and the description of
non-essentials. 9 Concerning genus. One must know that in the case of homonyms
three things must be sought. Whether it is of the homonyms, and how many
things it signifies, and about which of them the account is. One must first
say, then, what a homonym is. Homonyms, then, are when two or more have one
name, but each of them signifies something else and is of another substance,
that is admits another definition, as also now in the case of genus; for genus
is of the homonyms. For genus is said first of that from the fatherland and
from the begetter, and each of them in two ways: either immediately or
remotely. From the immediate fatherland, as someone is called a Jerusalemite,
and from the remote a Palestinian. Likewise from the immediate begetter, as
when Achilles, being son of Peleus, is called Peleides, and from the remote,
as when the same Achilles from his grandfather Aeacus is called Aeacides; for
this man was father of Peleus. Genus is said again of the relation of one
toward the many from him, as when all who have their descent from Israel are
called Israelites. Concerning the genera already spoken, then,


there is no account among the philosophers. Genus is said again of that under
which the species is ranked, as under animal are man, horse, and other
species; animal, then, is a genus. Concerning this genus the account among the
philosophers is. Genus, then, is what is predicated of more and of things
differing in species in the what it is, just as animal, being a genus, is
predicated of man and horse and ox and of several others in the what it is,
which differ from one another in species; for the species of man is other, and
that of horse other, and that of ox other. And it is predicated in the what it
is. For being asked "what is man," we say "animal." Likewise also horse: being
asked "what it is," we say "animal." So that genus is that to which the
species is subject. For the genus is divided into species and is more
universal than the species and contains the species and is above them. For one
must know that the more universal is said to be above, and the more particular
below and subject toward predication. For there is a subject toward existence,
as substance, for it is subject toward existence to the accident; for in it
the accident subsists. And there is a subject toward predication, the
particular; for the genus is predicated of the species and the species of the
individuals. And it is clear that the genus is more universal than the species
and the species than the individuals, about which we shall learn more exactly
in what follows with God. Now, having spoken concerning genus, let us speak
also concerning species. 10 Concerning species. And species too is of the
homonyms, said in two ways; for the shape of each is also called species,
according to which it has been said: "First a form worthy of tyranny." Again
there is species, the essential, that is ranked under the genus. And again
species is that of which the genus is predicated in the what it is. And again
species is what is predicated of more and of things differing in number in the
what it is. But the first two descriptions differ only according to the
relation, as ascent and descent, and they fit every species. The third and
last description is only of the most specific species that is immediately
above the individuals, that which contains the hypostases, as we say the
species of man. We said, then, that genus is said in three ways: from the
begetter and from the fatherland and in each of the two ways, and according to
a third mode, that under which the species is ranked. And species too is said
in two ways: as the shape of each according to one mode, according to a second
mode that of which the genus is predicated, that which is also ranked under
the genus as divided from it. Concerning this genus and this species the
account among the philosophers is. And since also when discoursing concerning
genus we mentioned species, saying "genus is what is divided into species,"
and again when discoursing concerning species we mentioned genus, saying
"species is what is divided from the genus," one must know that just as when
speaking of a father we of necessity mention also a son (for this man is a
father who has a son) and when discoursing of a son we of necessity mention
also a father (for this man is a son who has a father), so also here it is
impossible to treat of the genus apart from the species or of the species
apart from the genus; for the genus is in every case divided into species, and
what does not have species divided from it is not a genus. And the species are
divided from the genus, and those that do not have a genus are not species.
And just as the first man, that is Adam, not having had a father is not called
a son, but is called a father, for he had sons; and Seth is called both son of
the one who begot him, for he had Adam as father, and father of the one born
from him, for he begat Enos; and Abel is called a son, for he had Adam as
father, but is not called a father, for he did not have a son: so also in the
case of genus and species. The first genus, which is not divided from another
genus and does not have a genus before it, is genus only and is not a species
and is called most generic genus, which also they define saying: Most generic
genus is that which, being a genus, is not a species, because it does not have
a genus above it. And those divided from it, if they have beneath them other
species divided from them, are both species of those before


them, that is those above, from which they are cut, and genera of those cut
from them, that is those below, and they are called both genera and subaltern
species. And the last and lower species, those that do not have other species
beneath them, that is that do not contain species but individuals, that is
hypostases, are not called genera but species only; for it is impossible for
what does not contain species to be called a genus. The species, then, that
does not contain species but hypostases is called most specific species,
because, being a species, it is not also a genus, just as the genus that is
not also a species is called most generic genus. One must know that of
necessity the species admit both the name and the definition of the genus and
of the genus of their genus as far as the most generic. But the species cannot
admit one another's definition. (ῃ. ι. 138 ς) (ῃ. ι. 125–127) In order, then,
that what is said may be clearer, let us speak thus: Being is a
self-subsistent thing and not needing another for constitution, or what cannot
be by itself but has its existence in another. And substance is a
self-subsistent thing and not needing another for constitution only. See:
substance did not admit the whole definition of being. So that being is not a
genus of substance nor is substance a species of being; for the species admits
the definition of its genus whole without omission. But neither is accident a
species of being. For it does not admit the whole of its definition but the
half. For accident is a thing that cannot be by itself but has its existence
in another only. See: neither substance admits the whole definition of being
nor accident, but substance the half and accident the other half. So that even
if being is cut into substance and accident, still it is not a genus of them.
Substance is cut into body and incorporeal. See: body and the incorporeal are
species of substance; for each of them admits both the name and the complete
term of substance. So that substance is not a species, not having a genus
above it, but it itself is first and most generic genus. (ῃ. ς. 128–131) Again
body is cut into animate and inanimate; see, again body, being a species of
substance, is a genus of the animate and of the inanimate. The animate again
is divided into sensitive and insensitive. Sensitive, then, is the animal that
has life and sense, and insensitive the plant; for it does not have sense. And
the plant is called animate because it has nutritive and augmentative and
generative power. Animal again is cut into rational and irrational, the
rational into mortal and immortal, the mortal into man, horse, ox, and such
things, which are no longer cut into other species but into individuals, that
is hypostases; for man is divided into Peter, Paul, John, and the remaining
particular men, who are not species but hypostases. For the species, as we
said, do not admit one another's definition, as body does not admit the
definition of the incorporeal, man does not admit the definition of horse; but
Peter and Paul and John admit one term, that of man, and likewise the
remaining particular men, so that they are not species of man but individuals,
that is hypostases. Again the species when cut imparts to those below it both
the name and the term. But Peter when cut into soul and body imparts neither
to the soul nor to the body the name and the term; for neither the soul alone
is Peter nor the body alone but the combination of both. Further, every
division from genus into species reaches as far as two or three, rarely four;
for it is impossible for a genus to be divided into five species and above.
But man is divided into all the particular men, who are infinite in number.
Therefore some do not even say that the division from species into individuals
is called a division but an enumeration. Whence it is clear that Peter and
Paul and John are not species but individuals, that is hypostases. Nor is man
a genus of Peter and Paul and John and the remaining hypostases but a species.
Therefore man is a most specific species; for it is a species of that above as
contained by it and a species of those


below as containing them. For both what is contained by a genus is a species,
and what contains the individuals, that is hypostases, is a species. This,
then, is the most specific species, that which is immediately above the
individuals; which also they define saying: Species is what is predicated of
more and of things differing in number in the what it is. Likewise also horse
and dog and such things are most specific species. And those between the most
generic genus and the most specific species are subaltern genera and species,
species of those above and genera of those below. And these are the essential
and natural differences and qualities, which are called divisive and
constitutive, divisive of those above and constitutive of those below; for
body and the incorporeal divide substance, likewise the animate and the
inanimate divide body, likewise the sensitive and the insensitive divide the
animate. These, then, constitute the animal. For I take an animate sensitive
substance and I make the animal; for animal is an animate sensitive substance.
I take again an inanimate insensitive substance and I make the stone. Again I
take an animate insensitive substance and I make a plant. Again the rational
and the irrational divide the animal, the mortal and immortal divide the
rational, the terrestrial and the aquatic divide the irrational. I take
animal, being a genus of these, and the rational and the mortal, and I
constitute man; for man is a rational mortal animal. And I take animal and the
irrational and the mortal and the terrestrial and I constitute horse, dog, and
such things. And I take animal and the irrational and the mortal and the
aquatic and I constitute a fish. And they are called essential and natural
differences, because these make a species differ from another species and a
nature and substance from another substance and nature. 11 Concerning the
individual. The individual is said in four ways. For an individual is what is
not cut nor partitioned, as the point and the now and the monad, which are
also called without quantity. Individual is said also of what is hard to cut,
that is what is cut with difficulty, as the adamant stone and such things.
Individual is said also of the species that is no longer cut into other
species, that is the most specific species, as man, horse, and such things.
And individual is said properly of what is cut, but after the cutting does not
preserve the first species, as Peter is cut into soul and body, but neither
the soul by itself is a complete man or a complete Peter nor the body.
Concerning this individual the account among the philosophers is, which in the
case of substance signifies the hypostasis. 12 Concerning difference.
Difference and quality and property according to the subject are one, but
according to the activity other and other; for rationality is called both a
quality of man and a property and a difference, but according to another and
another mode. For as making and as it were shaping the substance it is called
quality, then as having become proper to this substance it is called property.
And in the comparison toward the irrational, an ox perhaps or a mule or a dog,
it is called difference; for in it man differs from the irrational. And this
is said in three ways, commonly and properly and most properly. For it is
impossible that two things should not differ from each other in something. In
some things, then, species differs from species, and in others a hypostasis
from the hypostasis of the same species and of the same substance, and in
others a hypostasis from itself. For the species of man differs from the
species of horse according to the rational and irrational. And the rational
and irrational is called an essential difference. Likewise also all things by
which species differs from species are called a natural and essential and
constitutive difference and quality and a natural property, which exists in
the whole species without variation, which is called among the philosophers
difference most properly as more intimate and indicative of the nature. Again
man differs from man or horse from horse or dog from dog, that is an
individual from an individual of the same species, in that the one is tall and
the other short, the one old and the other young, the one snub-nosed and the
other hook-nosed, the one prudent and the other foolish. All these are called
non-essential differences and qualities, which is the accident, concerning
which we shall speak in order. 13 Concerning accident. Accident is what comes
to be and


departs without the destruction of the subject. And again, what admits in the
same thing both to be and not to be; for it admits that a man both be white
and not be white, likewise also tall and prudent and snub-nosed. This neither
when present preserves the species, for it is not taken into the definition of
the species, nor when absent destroys it; for even if the Ethiopian is not
white, he will in no way be harmed as to being a man. It comes to be, then,
and departs and does not harm the underlying substance. For we said that
substance is a subject and a kind of matter for the accident. This accident is
divided into two, into the difference said commonly and into the difference
properly. Commonly, then, a difference is the separable accident, as one sits
and another stands; it admits, when the one sitting stands up and the one
standing sits down, that their difference be separated and that they take
another instead of another. And someone is said to differ from himself
according to the separable accident; for he differs from himself in sitting
and standing up, in being young and growing old, in being sick and being
healthy, and such things. And properly a difference is the inseparable
accident, as someone is snub-nosed; it is impossible for snubness to be
separated from him, and likewise grey-eyedness and such things. According to
these inseparable accidents, then, an individual differs from an individual,
that is a hypostasis from a hypostasis, but he himself never from himself. And
these do not contribute to the definition of the nature; for it admits that a
man be snub-nosed and not be. And when grey-eyedness departs, the man remains
a man no less. 14 Concerning property. Property is said in four ways. First,
what belongs to the species alone, but not to all, as doing geometry to man;
for man alone is a geometer, but not every man is a geometer. Second, what
belongs to all of the species, but not to it alone, as being two-footed; for
every man is two-footed, but not only man is two-footed but also a dove and
such things. Third, what belongs to all and to it alone, but not always, as
going grey to man; for this fits every man and man alone, but not always, but
in old age. Fourth, what comes about when the first three come together, that
is to all and to it alone and always, which also converts, as being capable of
laughter to man and being capable of neighing to the horse and such things;
for man alone has the capacity for laughter, and every man, and always, even
if he does not always use it. For if anything is a man, it is in every case
capable of laughter, and if anything is capable of laughter, it is in every
case a man. This is converting. Concerning this signification the account
among the philosophers is. Which also describing we say: Property is what
belongs to all of the species and to it alone and always. And they divide this
in three ways: from the organization, that is formation, as being broad-nailed
and walking upright to man; from the activity, as to fire being carried
uppermost; from the power, as we say that there belongs to fire the heating
power surpassing the heats of the other bodies. Property is said to be
non-essential. 15 Concerning the things predicated. Every predication is
either of more or equally, but never of less. And of more, when the more
universal are predicated of the more particular; more universal, then, are
those above, and more particular those below. Most universal of all, then, is
being, therefore it is predicated of all; for both substance is called a being
and accident is called a being. But we cannot say that being is substance; for
being is not only substance but also accident. Likewise also the genera are
predicated of the species as more universal, but the species are not
predicated of their genera; for the species are more particular than the
genera. Substance, then, is predicated of animal and animal of man; for animal
is a substance and man is an animal. But it does not convert; for every man is
an animal, but not every animal is a man. For horse too and dog are animals,
and likewise every animal is a substance, but not every substance is an
animal; for stone too and wood are substance, which are not animals. Likewise
also the species is predicated of the individuals contained


by it, that is hypostases, as more universal. But the individual, that is the
hypostasis, is not predicated of the species; for the hypostasis is more
particular than the species. Peter, then, is a man and Paul is a man, but not
every man is Peter or Paul; for there are also other hypostases under the
species of man. And the differences too are predicated of the species in which
they exist, and of their individuals. For the differences are more universal
than the species; for the rational is more universal than the species of man.
For every man is rational, but not every rational is a man; for the angel too,
being rational, is not a man. And this is predication of more; and predication
equally, when it converts. For the properties are predicated of the species of
which they are properties, but the species too are predicated of their
properties; for every man is capable of laughter, and every thing capable of
laughter is a man. For even if an ape is said to laugh, it does not laugh with
the heart but with the figures; for it is an imitative animal. So that the
predication of the genera of the species and that of the differences of the
species and that of the species of the individuals is said to be of more, and
that of the properties equally. And these that convert equally are also called
counter-predicated. 16 Concerning synonymous and homonymous predication.
Synonymous predication is when the subject admits both the name and the term
of that very name, as animal is predicated of man. And man admits both the
name and the term of animal; for animal is an animate sensitive substance. And
man too admits this term; for man too is a substance and animate and
sensitive. And homonymous predication is when it admits the name, but the term
not at all, as the image of a man admits the name of man, but does not admit
the term of man; for the term of man is a rational animal, mortal, capable of
intellect and knowledge. But the image is not even an animal; for it is not
animate nor rational nor capable of intellect and knowledge. One must know
that whatever is predicated of something of a subject, that is synonymously,
will also be predicated of what is subject to it, as animal is predicated of
man as of a subject, that is synonymously, and man of Peter; for Peter is
subject to man. Animal, then, is predicated also of Peter; for Peter too is an
animal. 17 Concerning the subject. The subject is said in two ways, one toward
existence, and one toward predication. And toward existence, as substance is
subject to the accidents; for in it they have their being and apart from it
they do not subsist. And the subject toward predication is the particular; for
the particular is subject to the more universal toward predication, since the
more universal is predicated of the more particular, as animal is predicated
of man. And the universal is said of a subject, and the more particular a
subject toward predication. And accident is said in a subject, the substance,
and substance a subject toward existence. 18 Concerning predication in the
what it is and predication in the of what sort it is. Predication in the what
it is is the predication of the genera of their species, that is that which
signifies what the subject is, and also that of the species of their
individuals; for being asked "what is man" we say "animal" and being asked
"what is Peter" we say "man." And predication in the of what sort it is is
that of the differences of the species in which they exist, and of the
accidents of the individuals in which they exist, that is that which signifies
of what sort the subject is; for being asked "what sort of animal is man" we
say "rational mortal." And the rational and the mortal are essential
differences. Again being asked "what sort of man is Peter" we say "tall
perhaps or short or snub-nosed or hook-nosed." And the on account of what it
is signifies the end, that is the cause for which the thing sought came to be;
for being asked "on account of what a bed" we say "on account of the rest of
men." 19


Communities and differences of the 5 voices. One must know that these five
voices share with one another in that all are predicated of more. And they
differ from one another, in that genus is predicated of more and of things
differing in species in the what it is, and species is predicated of more and
of things differing in number in the what it is. And difference and accident
are predicated of more and of things differing in species in the of what sort
it is, and property is predicated of more and of things differing in number,
that is of one species and of the individuals under it, in the of what sort it
is. And difference differs from accident, in that difference is essential,
that is a part of the substance of the subject, and accident not as a part of
the substance but non-essential. 20 Community and difference of genus and
difference. Common to genus and difference is that both are containing of the
species and that they are predicated synonymously of the species and of the
individuals. One must know that whatever is predicated of something as of a
subject, that is synonymously, will also be predicated synonymously of what is
subject to it, but whatever homonymously, not at all. Proper to genus toward
difference: that genus is of more than the differences under it and than the
other three voices; that genus contains the differences in potency; that genus
is by nature prior to the differences; that genus is predicated in the what it
is, and difference in the of what sort it is; that the proximate genus is one,
and the differences several; that genus is analogous to matter, and difference
to shape. 21 Community and difference of genus and species. Common to genus
and species: being predicated of more in the what it is; being by nature prior
to those under them; both being some whole. Proper to genus and species: that
genus is more universal than species; that species exceeds genus by the
differences; that genus is predicated synonymously of species, and species
does not convert; that neither is genus most specific nor species most
generic, nor will the most generic be a species nor the most specific be a
genus. 22 Community and difference of genus and property. Common to genus and
property: that both follow the species, that is are predicated; that they are
predicated equally of those of which both are predicated; that they are
predicated synonymously. Difference of genus and property: that genus is by
nature prior to property; that genus is predicated of more species, and
property of one; that property converts with the species, and genus no longer;
that property belongs to the species alone, and genus not to it alone. 23
Community and difference of genus and accident. Common to genus and accident
is being predicated of more. Proper to genus and accident: that genus exists
prior to the species in which the accidents exist, and the accidents later
than the species; that of genus the participation is equal, and of accidents
no longer; that accident belongs first to the individuals and then to the
species, and genus the reverse; that genera are predicated in the what it is,
and accidents in the of what sort it is or how it is disposed. 24 Community
and difference of difference and species. Common to difference and species:
that they are participated equally; that they are always present in those by
which they are participated. Proper to difference and species: that difference
is predicated in the of what sort it is, and species in the what it is; that
difference contains more species and the individuals of these, and species
only the individuals under it; that difference is by nature prior to the
species; that difference is compounded with difference, but species with
species not at all. 25 Community and difference of difference and property.
Common to difference and property: being predicated equally of all that
participate; being always present to the whole species. Proper to difference
and property: that difference contains many species, and property one only;
that difference does not convert with the species, and property converts. 26
Community and difference of difference and accident. Common to difference and
accident, that both are predicated of more in the of what sort it is, and that
difference and the inseparable accident are always present in those of which
they are predicated.


Proper to difference and accident: that differences contain and are not
contained, and accidents are contained. For both contain the species as being
predicated of more species, but difference is not contained, because the same
species does not admit the contrary differences, and accident is contained,
because the same species and the same individual admit many accidents, often
even the contraries; that difference does not admit the more and the less, but
accidents do; that contrary differences are unmixed, but contrary accidents
are mixed. 27 Community and difference of species and property. Common to
species and property: that they are counter-predicated of one another, that is
they convert; that they are participated equally; and to be participated
equally is that the one does not impart itself more or less than the other to
the participating individuals. Difference of species and property: that
