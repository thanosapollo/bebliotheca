* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

We have come to the Emperor Claudius, who must be set down in writing with
care, out of regard for Constantius Caesar. I could not refuse the task,
because I had already written of others, mere makeshift emperors and petty
kings, in that book I published on the Thirty Tyrants, which even now still
holds the line of Cleopatra and Victoria. Things have gone so far that the
comparison with Gallienus has made even the lives of women worth writing. It
would have been wrong to pass over in silence an emperor who left so great a
posterity of his house, who finished the Gothic war by his own valour, who as
victor put an end to the public disasters, who drove Gallienus, that
monstrous emperor, from the helm of the state, even if he was not the author
of the plan, yet was himself about to rule for the good of the human race, and
who, had he lingered longer in this commonwealth, would have given us back the
Scipios and the Camilli and all those men of old by his own strength, his own
counsel, and his own foresight.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

His time in power was short, I cannot deny it; but it would have been short
even if a man of that kind had been able to rule as long as human life allows.
What in him was not wonderful? What not outstanding? What not to be set above
the oldest triumphs? In him the valour of Trajan, the piety of Antoninus, the
moderation of Augustus, and the good qualities of great emperors were so
present that he did not take his pattern from others, but, even if those men
had never lived, he would have left a pattern for the rest.

The most learned of the astrologers judge that a hundred and twenty years are
given a man to live, and they boast that no one has been granted more, adding
even this, that Moses alone, the familiar of God, as the books of the Jews
say, lived a hundred and twenty-five years. When he complained that he was
dying young, they report that an uncertain deity answered him that no one
would live longer. And so, even if Claudius had lived a hundred and
twenty-five years, his life, astonishing and wonderful, teaches that not even
a natural death was to be looked for, as Tullius says of Scipio.

What greatness did that man lack at home or abroad? He loved his parents; what
wonder? He loved his brothers too; that can already be worth a marvel. He
loved his kin; a thing to be compared with a marvel in our times. He envied no
one, he hunted down the wicked, he condemned thieving judges openly and in
public; to fools he was almost carelessly indulgent. He gave the best laws. He
was such in the state that the highest emperors chose his line for empire,
and a better senate desired it.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Someone may think I speak to win the favour of Constantius Caesar, but your
own conscience and my life are witnesses that I have never thought, said, or
done anything for favour. I speak of the Emperor Claudius, whose life,
honesty, and all he did in the state gave him such fame among later men that
the senate and people of Rome marked him after death with new honours. For him
a golden shield, or, as the grammarians say, a golden clipeum, was set in the
Roman Senate-house by the judgement of the whole senate, and even now his face
is seen on it, with the breastplate in relief. For him, as for no one before,
the Roman people at its own cost set a golden statue of ten feet on the
Capitol before the Temple of Jupiter Best and Greatest. For him, by the
judgement of the whole world, a palm-wreathed column was set on the Rostra,
with a statue fixed on it of fifteen hundred pounds of silver. He, as if
mindful of things to come, carried on the Flavian house, which had also been
that of Vespasian and Titus, though I will not say of Domitian. He finished
the Gothic war in a short time. The senate, then, is a flatterer, the Roman
people a flatterer, the foreign nations flatterers, the provinces flatterers,
if indeed every order, every age, every city honoured a good emperor with
statues, standards, crowns, shrines, arches, altars, and temples.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

It matters both to those who imitate good emperors and to the whole human
world to know what decrees of the senate were passed about that man, so that
all may learn the judgement of the public mind. For when it was announced, on
the ninth day before the Kalends of April, the very Day of Blood in the
sanctuary of the Mother, that Claudius had been made emperor, and the senate
could not be assembled because of the rites being celebrated, they put on
their togas and went to the Temple of Apollo, and when the letter of the
Emperor Claudius had been read these things were said of Claudius:

/Augustus Claudius, may the gods keep you,/

said sixty times.

/Claudius Augustus, we have always wished for you as emperor, or such as you
are,/

said forty times.

/Claudius Augustus, the republic was asking for you,/

said forty times.

/Claudius Augustus, you are brother, you are father, you are friend, you are a
good senator, you are truly emperor,/

said eighty times.

/Claudius Augustus, deliver us from Aureolus,/

said five times.

/Claudius Augustus, deliver us from the Palmyrenes,/

said five times.

/Claudius Augustus, free us from Zenobia and from Vitruvia,/

said seven times.

/Claudius Augustus, Tetricus has done nothing,/

said seven times.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

As soon as he was made emperor he fought a battle and drove Aureolus from the
helm of the state, a man who had been the heavier burden on the republic
because he pleased Gallienus so much, and by edicts sent to the people, and by
speeches given also to the senate, he judged him a tyrant. To this is added
that when Aureolus begged and asked for a treaty, the emperor, grave and
stern, would not hear him, and turned him away with this answer:

/Those things should have been asked of Gallienus; a man who agreed with his
ways could also be afraid./

In the end, by the judgement of his own soldiers at Milan, Aureolus had an end
worthy of his life and habits. And yet some historians have tried to praise
even this man, and ridiculously too. For Gallus Antipater, a handmaid of
honours and a disgrace to histories, began his account of Aureolus:

/We have come to an emperor of his own name./

A great virtue, no doubt, to take a name from gold. But I know that this name
has often been given among gladiators to good fighters. Your own programme of
shows had this name lately in the list of the games.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

But let us return to Claudius. For, as we said above, those Goths who had
escaped at the time when Marcianus hunted them down, and whom Claudius had not
allowed to be let go, lest that should happen which did happen, stirred all
the nations of their people to plunder the Romans. In the end various peoples
of the Scythians, the Peucini, Greuthungi, Ostrogoths, Tervingi, Visigoths,
Gepids, and even Celts and Heruli, broke into Roman ground from greed for
plunder and laid waste a great part of it there, while Claudius was busy with
other things and while he was fitting himself, as an emperor should, for that
war which he finished, so that the Roman fates seem to have been slowed by a
good emperor's occupation. But I believe it was so that the glory of Claudius
might grow and his victory become more glorious through the whole world. There
were then three hundred and twenty thousand armed men of the nations. Let the
man who accuses us of flattery now say that Claudius is less to be loved.
Three hundred and twenty thousand armed men. What Xerxes, pray, had this? What
fable invented that number? What poet composed it? There were three hundred
and twenty thousand armed men. Add slaves, add households, add the
wagon-train, and rivers drunk dry and forests consumed, and at last the earth
itself labouring under so much barbarian swelling as it took in.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

There is still extant a letter of his own, sent to the senate to be read to
the people, in which he reports the number of the barbarians, and it is of
this kind:

/Claudius the emperor to the senate and people of Rome./

(This, they say, he dictated himself; I do not look for the words of the
master of the records.)

/Conscript Fathers, hear with wonder what is true. Three hundred and twenty
thousand barbarians have come armed onto Roman ground. If I conquer these,
repay my services in turn; if I do not conquer, know that I mean to fight
after Gallienus. The whole republic is worn out. We fight after Valerian,
after Ingenuus, after Regalianus, after Lollianus, after Postumus, after
Celsus, after a thousand others who, in contempt of a bad emperor, deserted
the republic. There are no shields left now, no long swords, no javelins.
Tetricus holds the Gauls and the Spains, the strength of the republic, and, it
shames me to say it, Zenobia holds all the archers. Whatever we do is great
enough./

These, then, Claudius overcame by that native valour of his, these he crushed
in a short time, and of these he allowed scarcely any to return to their
native ground. I ask, what price is a shield in the Senate-house for so great
a victory? What price one golden statue? Ennius says of Scipio:

/How great a statue will the Roman people make, how great a column, to speak
your deeds?/

We can say that Flavius Claudius, the one emperor on earth, is helped not by
columns, not by statues, but by the strength of his fame.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

They had besides two thousand ships, twice, that is, the number with which all
Greece together and all Thessaly once tried to storm the cities of Asia. But
that is the fiction of a poet's style; this is what true history contains. Are
we writers, then, flatterers of Claudius, who destroyed, crushed, and wore
down two thousand barbarian ships and three hundred and twenty thousand armed
men, who now had so great a wagon-train burned as this host of armed men could
fit out and prepare for themselves, and now assigned it with all the
households to Roman slavery? As is shown by a letter of the same man, which
he wrote to Junius Brocchus, who was guarding Illyricum:

/Claudius to Brocchus. We have destroyed three hundred and twenty thousand
Goths, we have sunk two thousand ships. The rivers are covered with shields,
all the shores are spread with long swords and little lances. The fields lie
hidden, covered with bones, no road is clear, a huge wagon-train is abandoned.
We have taken so many women that a victorious/

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

/soldier can take two and three women apiece. And would that the republic
had not suffered Gallienus! Would that it had not endured six hundred tyrants!
If the soldiers whom various battles took away had been saved, if the legions
whom Gallienus, a wretched victor, killed had been saved, how much would have
been added to the republic! As it is, our care is gathering the limbs of the
public wreck for the safety of the Roman state./

There was fighting among the Moesians, and many battles at Marcianopolis. Many
perished by shipwreck, many kings were taken, noble women of various nations
were taken, the Roman provinces were filled with barbarian slaves and Scythian
tillers, and a Goth was made a settler of the barbarian frontier. Nor was
there any region that did not have a Gothic slave in a kind of triumphal
bondage. What barbarian oxen did our ancestors see? What sheep? What Celtic
mares, which fame has made celebrated? All this belongs to the glory of
Claudius. Claudius gave the republic both security and an excess of wealth.
There was fighting besides at Byzantium, the surviving Byzantines themselves
fighting bravely. There was fighting at Thessalonica, which the barbarians
had besieged while Claudius was away. There was fighting in various regions,
and everywhere under Claudian auspices the Goths were beaten, so that already
then Claudius seemed to be making the republic safe for Constantius Caesar,
his future grandson.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

And this comes aptly to mind, that the lot which is said to have been given
Claudius at Comagena must be set down, so that all may understand that the
house of Claudius was appointed by heaven for the happiness of the republic.
For when, after he was made emperor, he asked how long he would rule, a lot of
this kind came forth:

/You who now steer your country's shores and rule the world, umpire of the
gods, you will surpass the old with your young; for your descendants will
reign and will make kings of their descendants./

Likewise, when he asked about himself in the Apennines, he received an answer
of this kind:

/Until the third summer has seen him ruling in Latium./

Likewise, when he asked about his posterity:

/For these I set no bounds of empire and no times./

Likewise, when he asked about his brother Quintillus, whom he wished to have
as partner in the empire, the answer was:

/The fates will only show this man to the earth./

I have set these down so that it may be clear to all that Constantius, a man
of a divine house, a most holy Caesar, both belongs himself to the family of
the Augusti and will give many Augusti from himself, while Diocletian and
Maximian Augusti and his brother Galerius are unharmed.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

But while these things were being done by the deified Claudius, the Palmyrenes
under the leaders Saba and Timagenes took up war against the Egyptians and
were beaten by them through Egyptian stubbornness and an unwearied stretch of
fighting. The Egyptian general Probatus, however, was killed by a trap of
Timagenes. But all the Egyptians gave themselves to the Roman emperor,
swearing allegiance in the name of the absent Claudius.

In the consulship of Antiochianus and Orfitus the divine favour helped the
Claudian auspices. For when the crowd of barbarian nations that had survived
had gathered in Haemimontus, it suffered there so from hunger and plague that
Claudius already disdained even to conquer. In the end the harshest war was
finished, and the threats against the Roman name were driven off.

Good faith forces the truth to be told, and at the same time, so that those
who wish us to be thought flatterers may know that we do not keep silent what
history demands be said: at the time when full victory was won, many of
Claudius's soldiers, lifted up by success, which /wearies even the minds of
the wise/, turned to plunder so far that they did not think they could be put
to flight by a very few, while, busy in mind and body, they gave themselves to
carrying off the spoil. In the end, in the very victory, nearly two thousand
soldiers were killed by a few barbarians, and those who had fled. But when
Claudius learned this, he gathered the army, seized all who had shown a
rebellious spirit, and even sent them in chains to Rome to be assigned to the
public games. So what either fortune or the soldier had done was cancelled by
the virtue of a good emperor. Not only victory over the enemy, but punishment
too, was taken in hand. In that war, as long as it was waged, the valour of
the Dalmatian horse stood out greatly, because Claudius seemed to claim his
origin from that province, though others said he was a Dardanian and drew his
blood from Ilus, king of the Trojans, and from Dardanus himself.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

In those times there were also Scythians in Crete, and they tried to lay waste
Cyprus, but everywhere they were overcome, the army likewise suffering from
disease.

When the Gothic war was finished a most severe disease grew strong, at the
time when Claudius also, struck by the disease, left mortal things and sought
the heaven that was native to his virtues. When he had gone to the gods and
the stars, Quintillus his brother, a holy man and, if I may say it truly, a
brother of his brother, took up the empire offered him by the judgement of
all, not as an inheritance but by the merit of his virtues, a man who would
have been made emperor even if he had not been the brother of the Emperor
Claudius. Under him the barbarians who had survived tried to lay waste
Anchialus, and even to take Nicopolis, but they were crushed by the valour of
the provincials. Quintillus, however, because of the shortness of the time,
could do nothing worthy of empire. For on the seventeenth day, because he had
shown himself grave and stern toward the soldiers and promised a true emperor,
he was killed in the same manner as Galba, as Pertinax. And Dexippus, indeed,
does not say that Quintillus was killed, but only that he died, and yet he
does not add by disease, so that he seems to feel a doubt.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

Since we have told the affairs of war, at least a little must be said of the
stock and family of Claudius, lest we seem to have passed over what ought to
be known. Claudius, Quintillus, and Crispus were brothers. The daughter of
Crispus was Claudia; from her and Eutropius, a most noble man of the Dardanian
nation, Constantius Caesar was born. There were also sisters, of whom one,
named Constantina, married to a tribune of the Assyrians, died in her first
years. Of the grandparents we know little; for most have handed down varying
accounts.

Claudius himself was marked by gravity of character, marked by a singular life
and a unique chastity, sparing of wine, ready at table, tall in stature, with
burning eyes, a broad and full face, and fingers so strong that he often
knocked the teeth from horses and mules with a blow of his fist. He had done
this also as a young man in the army, when at the Martial games on the Campus
he was showing a wrestling-bout among the strongest men. For, angry at a man
who had twisted not his belt but his genitals, he knocked out all the teeth
with one blow of his fist. The deed earned pardon as a vindication of modesty,
since Decius the emperor, in whose presence it had been done, then publicly
praised both the valour and the sense of shame of Claudius, and, after
presenting him with armlets and torques, ordered him to leave the soldiers'
gathering, lest he do something harsher than a wrestling-bout requires.

Claudius himself had no children, Quintillus left two, Crispus, as we have
said, a daughter.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

Now let us come to the judgements of the emperors, which were issued about him
by various men, and to such a degree that it was clear Claudius would one day
be emperor.

A letter of Valerian to Zosimio, procurator of Syria:

/We have given Claudius, a man of the Illyrian nation, as tribune to the Fifth
Martian Legion, most valiant and most loyal, a man to be preferred to the most
loyal and bravest of the men of old. You will give him as salary from our
private treasury each year three thousand modii of wheat, six thousand of
barley, two thousand pounds of bacon, three thousand five hundred sextarii of
old wine, one hundred fifty sextarii of good oil, six hundred sextarii of
second oil, twenty modii of salt, one hundred fifty pounds of wax, as much
hay, chaff, vinegar, greens, and herbs as is enough, thirty tens of
tent-skins, six mules a year, three horses a year, ten camels a year, nine
she-mules a year, fifty pounds of worked silver a year, one hundred fifty
Philips of our likeness a year, and as New Year gifts forty-seven, and one
hundred sixty thirds; likewise in a drinking-bowl, cup, and kettle eleven
pounds; two military red tunics a year, two military cloaks a year, two
silver-gilt brooches, one gold brooch with a Cyprian pin, one silver-gilt
belt, one two-gem ring of an ounce, one bracelet of seven ounces, one torque
of a pound, one gilded helmet, two gold-inlaid shields, one cuirass which he
is to return, two Herculean lances, two throwing-spears, two sickles, four
hay-sickles, one cook whom he is to return, one muleteer whom he is to return,
two handsome women from the captives, one half-silk white tunic with Girbitan
purple, one under-tunic with Moorish purple, one secretary whom he is to
return, one table-setter whom he is to return, two pairs of Cyprian
couch-covers, two plain undershirts, two men's bands, one toga which he is to
return, one broad stripe which he is to return, two huntsmen to attend him,
one carpenter, one steward of the headquarters, one water-carrier, one
fisherman, one pastry-cook; a thousand pounds of wood daily if there is
plenty, if not, as much as there is and where it is; four scoops of kindling
daily; one bath-attendant and wood for the baths, if not, let him bathe in
public. The rest, which because of their smallness cannot be written, you will
furnish according to moderation, but so that nothing is turned into money, and
if anything is lacking anywhere it shall not be supplied nor demanded in coin.
All this I have assigned specifically not as to a tribune but as to a general,
because he is such a man that even more should be assigned to him./

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

Likewise from another letter of the same man, among the rest, to Ablavius
Murena, prefect of the guard:

/Stop complaining that Claudius is still a tribune and does not receive an
army as a general, about which you kept saying the senate and people
complain. He has been made general, and general of all Illyricum. He has in
his power the Thracian, Moesian, Dalmatian, Pannonian, and Dacian armies. Let
that excellent man, even in our judgement, hope for the consulship, and if it
suits his mind, whenever he wishes, let him receive the prefecture of the
guard. You should know that we have decreed him as much salary as the
prefecture of Egypt has, as much clothing as we assigned to the African
proconsulate, as much silver as the curator of the Illyrian mines receives, as
many attendants as we ourselves decree for ourselves in each city, so that all
may understand what our opinion of such a man is./

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Likewise a letter of Decius about the same Claudius:

/Decius to Messalla, governor of Achaia, greeting./

Among the rest:

/We command our tribune Claudius, an excellent young man, a most valiant
soldier, a most steadfast citizen, necessary to the camps, the senate, and the
republic, to go to Thermopylae, giving him also the charge of the
Peloponnesians, knowing that no one will better tend all we lay on him. You
will give him from the Dardanian region two hundred soldiers, from the
cataphracts one hundred, from the cavalry sixty, from the Cretan archers
sixty, from the recruits a thousand well armed. For new armies are well
entrusted to him; nor is anyone found more loyal, braver, or more grave than
he./

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

Likewise a letter of Gallienus, when it was reported through the grain-agents
that Claudius was angry because he lived too softly:

/Nothing has hit me harder than that you intimated in your note that Claudius,
our kinsman and friend, is gravely angry after many falsehoods have been
whispered into him. I ask you therefore, my Venustus, if you keep faith with
me, to have him soothed by Gratus and Herennianus, without the Daciscian
soldiers knowing it, who are already raging, lest the matter break out badly.
I myself have sent him gifts, and you will see that he takes them gladly. Care
must also be taken that he does not understand that I know this, and judge
that I am angry with him, and in necessity take a last desperate plan. I have
sent him two gemmed bowls of three pounds, two gold gemmed cups of three
pounds, a silver ivy-clustered dish of twenty pounds, a silver vine-leaf
platter of thirty pounds, a silver ivy plate of twenty-three pounds, a silver
fishing-dish of twenty pounds, two silver pitchers set in gold of six pounds,
and in smaller vessels twenty-five pounds of silver, ten Egyptian cups of
various work, two cloaks bordered with true purple, sixteen various garments,
one half-silk white, one three-ounce embroidered tunic, three pairs of our
Parthian boots, ten Dalmatian inner tunics, one Dardanian wrapping-cloak,
one Illyrian mantle, one hooded cloak, two shaggy hoods, four Sarabdenan
napkins, one hundred fifty Valerian gold pieces, three hundred Saloninian
thirds./

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

He had also the senate's judgements, before he came to the empire, of great
weight. For when it was reported that he had fought bravely with Marcianus
against the nations in Illyricum, the senate shouted:

/Claudius, bravest general, hail! To your virtues, to your loyalty! Let us all
vote Claudius a statue. We all desire Claudius as consul. He who loves the
republic acts so. He who loves the emperors acts so. The soldiers of old acted
so. Happy are you, Claudius, in the judgement of the emperors. Happy are you
in your virtues. Consul you, prefect you! Live, Valerius, and be loved by the
emperor!/

It would be long to write out as much as that man deserved. One thing,
however, I must not pass over, that the senate and people loved him so, both
before the empire and in it and after it, that it is well established that
neither Trajan nor the Antonines nor any other emperor was so loved.
