I crossed the Nahe, running in a mist,
and marveled at Vincum, where new walls
are joined upon the old. There Gaul once matched
the Latin field of Cannae, and poor bands
lie unwept upon the acres.

From there I took a lonely road through woods,
and saw no print of human tillage. I passed
dry Dumnissus, thirsty land on every side,
and Tabernae, watered by a ceaseless spring,
and fields lately measured for Sarmatian settlers.
At last I caught the first Belgic shores:
Noviomagus, the famed camp of Constantine the god.

Here the air over the fields is cleaner, and Phoebus,
now cloudless, with a calm light unlocks
the purple heaven. No longer, where the boughs
are knitted in a mutual bond, do we hunt
a shut-out sky through green gloom. The free breath
of a clear day does not grudge those who look
a liquid radiance and the ruddy air.

All that I saw, by a winning look, drove me
toward the face and culture of my shining home,
Bordeaux: villa roofs raised on hanging banks,
hills green with Bacchus, and the pleasant waters
of the Moselle gliding below with a quiet rumor.

Hail, river praised by fields and praised by farmers,
to whom the Belgae owe walls thought worthy of empire.
You plant your ridges with the fragrant vine,
greenest of rivers, and you plant your grassy banks.
You bear ships as a sea. You fall, as a river,
toward your downward waters. You copy a lake
in glassy depth. You can match a brook
in hurrying course, and outdo a cold spring
in the clearness of your drink. You alone hold
all that a spring, a brook, a river, a lake,
and a sea that ebbs with a two-way flow possess.

You glide in quiet water. No wind so much
as murmurs, and no hidden rock contends with you.
No shoal forces your rapid currents to hurry.
No mid-stream island cuts you, lest a split
and shut-out river lose the honor of its name.

A double path is yours. When you run with the current,
swift oars may strike the stirred shallows; and when
the tow-rope never slackens along the banks,
the sailors stretch the mast-ropes on their necks.
How often you yourself marvel at your own back-flow,
and think your lawful currents go almost more slowly.
No mud-born sedge fringes your bank, nor do you
soak your shores with filthy slime. Dry footsteps
go on into the first waters.

Go now, and inlay smooth floors with Phrygian marble,
stretching a marble field through paneled halls.
I, despising what wealth and riches have given,
will marvel at the work of nature, not the grim
want of spendthrifts, glad in their own losses.
Here firm sands strew the wet shores,
and the foot's print does not keep the shape it made.

Look through the glassy deep over that smooth back:
the river keeps no secret. As fostering air
lies open to a clear gaze, and quiet winds
do not bar the eyes through empty space, so we,
the sight lasting through the inmost parts, see
what is sunk far down. The sanctuary of the hidden
deep lies open. The shallows go gently, and the
lapse of water shows shapes scattered in blue light:
how the furrowed sand is crisped by a light course,
how the grasses tremble, bent on the green bottom.
Herbs stirred even under their native springs
suffer the quivering waters. A pebble gleams and
hides, and gravel sets off the green moss.

So the whole shore lies open to the Caledonian Britons,
when the tide bares green seaweeds and red corals
and the white pearl-buds of shells, delights of men,
and under the rich waves necklaces that imitate
our ornaments counterfeit our dress.
Not otherwise, under the glad shallows of the quiet Moselle,
herb of another color uncovers mixed pebbles.

Yet fishes, a slippery swarm, playing between,
tire intent eyes by darting astray.
I may not publish so many kinds, the sidelong
swims, the columns coming up against the stream,
the names and all the young of that numerous stock.
He will not have it so, who drew the second lot
and keeps the trident of the sea. You, Naiad
who dwell on the river shores, tell me the choruses
of the scale-bearing flock, and under the liquid bed
set out the troops that float in the blue stream.

The scaly chub gleams among the weedy sands,
his too-soft flesh packed full of bones. He will
not last six hours upon the board.
The trout wears purple stars along his back.
The roach will wound you with no spiny point.
The grayling, light, flees from the eye in one
swift glide.

And you, barbel, harassed through the jaws
of the winding Saar, where twice three mouths roar
among rocky piles: once you have come down
into a river of greater fame, you swim more free
and loose. You are better in a worse age.
Of all that breathe, old age has not gone
unpraised in you.

Nor would I pass you, salmon, ruddy
in purple flesh. The wandering lash of your broad tail
is brought from mid-eddy to the topmost waves,
when a hidden pulse is betrayed on the quiet surface.
You, scaled with an armored breast, slippery at the brow,
will make a dish for an uncertain dinner,
and bear unspoiled the seasons of long delay,
marked by the spots upon your head. Your prodigal
belly nods, and your paunch flows with a fattened abdomen.

And you, burbot, taken through Illyricum
and through the pools of the two-named Danube
by the foam-signs of the swimmers, were carried
into our water, lest the glad streams of the Moselle
be cheated of so famous a nursling.
What color has nature painted you! Dark points
mark the back above, where a yellow iris rings it.
A blue stain overruns the slippery back.
You fatten stuffed to the middle of the body;
from there the dry skin is rough even to the last
of the tail.

Nor will I be silent, perch, delight of tables,
to be ranked among river fish with those of the sea,
alone ready to contend with the purple mullets.
The taste is not dull. In the solid body the parts
do not join in slices, but are parted by bones.
Here too is the one laughed at by a Latin first name,
dweller of pools, a force most hostile to complaining frogs,
the pike. He besets dark hollows of sedge and mud.
Chosen for no uses of tables, he boils
in smoking cookshops with a rank smell.

Who does not know the green tench, comfort of
the crowd, and the bleaks, prey for boys' hooks,
and the shads that hiss on the hearths, the common people's meat?
And you, between twin kinds, neither and both,
not yet salmon, no longer trout, ambiguous
sea-trout, cut off in an age midway between them?
You too are to be named among the river cohorts,
gudgeon, no larger than two palms without the thumb,
very fat, smooth, more packed in an egg-bearing belly,
and imitating, gudgeon, the barbel's combed beard.

Now you shall be celebrated, sea-flock, great sheat-fish.
I think you a river dolphin, your back as if
smeared with Attic oil. So you glide great through the
straits and scarcely unfold the train of your long body,
caught either in short shallows or in the river's sedge.
But when you ply tranquil courses in the stream,
the green banks, the blue crowd of swimmers,
the liquid waters marvel at you. The tide is spread
in the channel and the farthest waves run forward at the edge.
Such was once a whale in the Atlantic deep,
when by wind or its own motion it is driven to the
shores of earth: the shut-out sea pours over, great waters
rise, and neighboring mountains fear they are shrinking.
Yet here, here the gentle whale of our Moselle
is far from destruction, an honor added to the great river.

Enough of liquid paths, enough of counting
the slippery columns of fish and all their troops.
Let the vines lead on another show, and let
the gifts of Bacchus draw the wandering eye,
where a high peak over a long steep tract,
and rock, and the sunny slope of the ridge,
and every bend and bay, rise dressed in vines,
a theater made by nature.
So the kind vintage clothes the ridge of Gaurus,
and Rhodope and Pangaea shine with their own wine-god.
So Ismarian hill is green above the Thracian sea.
So my own vineyards paint the yellow Garonne.
From the crest down to the last of the slope
the river's margin is planted with green Lyaeus.
A folk glad of the work, and farmers in haste,
press now on the highest peak, now on the sloping back,
and contend with foolish shouts. Then the traveler
treading the banks below, and the sailor gliding by,
sing insults at the late tillers. Rock and trembling
wood and the hollow river shout back at them.

Nor does the scene delight men alone.
Here I could believe rustic Satyrs and Naiads
with sea-green gaze meet at the farthest banks,
when a glad wantonness drives the goat-footed Pans
to leap the shallows and frighten the timid sisters
under the stream, striking the wave with an untaught lash.
Often too, having stolen grapes from mid-hill,
river Panope flees among her Oread friends
from the wanton Fauns, those country powers.
They say, when the fiery sun has stood in mid-heaven,
that Satyrs and the glassy sisters come to one water
and keep shared dances, when a hotter heat
has given hours secret from the gathering of men.
Then the Nymphs leap and play through their own straits
and duck the Satyrs in the shallows, and those unused
to swimming slip out through the midst of their hands,
while they grasp at false slippery limbs, and the waves
favor them in place of a body.
But it is not for me to speak of these things
as seen by anyone or known by sight. Let the secret
be covered, and let the reverence entrusted to it
lie hidden in its own streams.

That sight is public joy, when the blue river
answers the dark hill, and the waters seem to leaf,
and the stream to be planted with vine-shoots.
What color is that in the shallows, when Hesperus
has driven on the late shadows and poured the green
mountain over the Moselle! The whole ridges swim
with curling motion. The absent vine-shoot trembles,
and the vintage swells in the glassy waves.
The deceived sailor counts green vines, floating
in a bark canoe through the middle, where the hill's
image mingles with the river and the stream joins
the borders of the shadows.

These shows too keep such sweet processions,
when oar-footed skiffs contend with the mid-stream,
take various turns, and along the green edges
graze the young shoots on the close-cut meadows.
See the eager masters exult on poop and prow,
and the beardless band wandering over the river's back.
While he watches the day go by, he sets serious
business after play. A new grace shuts out old cares.
Such games Liber looks down on in the Cumaean sea,
when he walks the planted ridges of sulphurous Gaurus
and the vineyards of steaming Vesuvius,
when Venus, glad in Augustus's Actian triumphs,
bade the wanton Loves play at fierce battle,
such as Nile's fleets and Latin triremes
waged under Apollo's citadels of Leucas,
or as the Euboean boats on sounding Avernus
recall the Mylaean dangers of Pompey's war:
harmless collisions of rafts and joking fights
of a sea-battle, such as the blue sea renews
under a green image while Sicilian Pelorus watches.
Not otherwise do the river's youth and the painted beaks
of the skiffs add a sight for the wanton young men.
When the sun has poured them with Hyperion's heat,
it gives back sailor-shapes under the glassy flood
and brings back the bent shadows of inverted bodies.
As they ply agile motions right and left
and shift the weight from oar to exchanged oar,
the wave reports other sailors, wet likenesses.
The sailor youth itself rejoices in its own mimicry,
marveling that the false figures return in the river.

So, when she is about to show her arranged hair
(when first the nurse has brought to her dear charge
a mirror's far-shining honor), the happy girl
enjoys an unknown play and thinks she sees
a sister's form. She gives kisses to the shining
metal that will not be returned, or tries the fixed pins,
or at the brow's edge tries with her fingers
to catch and stretch the waved hair.
Such is the sailor youth at the mockeries of shadows,
enjoying figures that mix the true and the false.

Now, where the bank supplies easy access,
a plundering crowd searches the whole deep
for fishes poorly defended in the inner river.
Here one, drawing wet lines far in mid-stream,
sweeps the deceived swarms with knotted nets.
Here, where the river glides in a quiet column,
another leads floating nets with cork markers.
That one, leaning toward the waves thrown down from the rocks,
bends the curved tips of a pliant rod
and casts hooks dressed with deadly baits.
When the wandering crowd of swimmers, ignorant of the
trick, has seized them in their jaws, and too late,
through the inmost open throat, has felt the wounds
of hidden steel. While they struggle, a sign comes,
and the nodding reed agrees with the curled tremor
of the quivering line. At once the boy, with a whistling
stroke, snatches the shaken prey sideways. The air
takes the blow, as when a whip is cracked through the void:
the air snaps, and the wind hisses in the stirred air.
The wet spoil leaps upon the dry rocks
and fears the deadly shafts of the light-bearing day.
Each that kept its vigor under its own stream
spends life sluggish in our air, gasping.
Now a slow slap is shaken from a failing body.
Now the torpid tail suffers its last tremors.
The jaws do not close, but through the gaping mouth
the gill, breathing out, gives back death-dealing gusts
of drawn air. So, when breath works the smith's fires,
a woolen valve, playing at the beechwood chambers,
takes and holds the winds by an alternating hole.
I have seen some, trembling at the edge of death,
gather their spirits, then, driven aloft, hurl
headlong their stooping bodies into the stream below,
winning again the waters they had despaired of.
The boy, not counting the loss, unadvised, attacks
them from on high, and tries to catch them with a clumsy swim.
So Glaucus of Anthedon through the Boeotian straits,
after he had known by taste the deadly herbs of Circe
and taken plants plucked from dying fish,
entered the Carpathian sea as a new inhabitant.
He, mighty with hook and net, searcher of hidden
Nereus, used to sweep watery Tethys,
and floated a robber among his captive bands.

Such things the villas hanging from the rocks,
their roofs looming, look down on through a long
stretch over the blue. The river wandering in the middle
with winding bends parts them, and palaces adorn
either bank. Who now would marvel at the Sestian sea,
the water of Nephele's Helle, the straits of the
Abydene youth? Who at the sea paved from the
Chalcedonian shore, work of a great king, where a
channel in mid-waves forbids the lands of Europe
and Asia to run together? Here is no grim rage of strait,
no savage battles of raging northwest winds. Here
you may join a commerce of speech and weave talk
with answering stroke. The pleasant shores mix voices
that carry greeting, voices and almost hands. Echo,
running with the waves, reports from both sides
the words that sound.

Who can unfold the countless styles and habits
and set out the builders' forms through each estate?
The winged man of Gortyn would not scorn this work,
founder of the Euboean temple, whom his father's griefs
drove off when he tried to fashion the Icarian fall in gold;
not Cecropian Philo, not he who, praised by the enemy,
drew out the famous contests of the Syracusan war.
Perhaps here the hebdomad celebrated in Marcus's
tenth volume had the illustrious labors of men and works.
Here the arts of famous Menecrates flourished,
and the hand seen at Ephesus, or Ictinus on Minerva's
citadel, whose owl, smeared with magic dye,
draws every kind of bird and kills them by looking.
Perhaps the founder of the Ptolemaic hall was here,
Dinochares, for whom the pyramid itself rises in a cone
on square peaks and consumes its own shadows,
who once, ordered for a pact of incestuous love,
hung Arsinoe in the air of the Pharian temple.
For a magnetic force breathes from the vault of the roof
and draws the girl, blown upon, by her iron-clad hair.
These men, then, or men like them, it is fair to believe
set the scenes of houses in the land of the Belgae,
and built high villas as ornaments of the river.

This one stands lofty by nature on a pile of rock.
This is founded on a projecting bank.
This withdraws and claims the captured river in a bay.
That one, holding the hill that looms most over the river,
takes easy views through tilled land and through rough,
and the rich look-out enjoys its own lands.
Even one founded with a low foot in watery meadows
makes up the natural goods of a high mountain
and, threatening with a lofty roof, bursts into the aether,
showing a high tower, like the Memphite Pharos.
This one has as its own to catch enclosed fish
in a fenced pool among the sunny fallows of the rocks.
This one, leaning on the highest ridges, watches the
rivers gliding below with a gaze already growing dark.
Why mention halls set on greening meadows,
and roofs shining above on countless columns?
Why those that smoke, built on the river's foundation,
the baths, when Mulciber, drawn from his burning covert,
rolls panted flames through the hollow coverings,
gathering enclosed vapor with breathing heat?
I have seen men weary from much sweat of the bath
scorn the pools and the cold of fish-ponds,
to enjoy living waters, then, refreshed by the river,
beat the cold stream with a splashing swim.

But if a guest came here from the Cumaean shores,
he would believe that Euboean Baiae had given these
places a slight copy. So great a culture and a sheen
allures, and the delight begets no luxury.
But what end, after all, in telling your blue waters
and recalling the Moselle, worthy to be named
with the sea, because countless rivers run into you
widely through diverse mouths? Though they could keep
their courses apart, they hasten to consume their name in you.
For Sura, helped by the course of Promea and Nemesa,
hastens, not unworthy, to go under your waves,
Sura that has obliged you with intercepted streams,
more noble mixed under your name than if
she strove for a mouth to father Ocean that would go unknown.
Swift Celbis, and Erubris bright with marble, hasten
to lick you as soon as they can with servant waters.
Celbis is famed for noble fish. That other, twisting
millstones for grain with headlong rotation and drawing
shrieking saws over smooth marble, hears perpetual
uproar from either bank.
I pass thin Lesura and slender Drahonus,
nor do I treat the springs of Salmona as scorned.
The ship-bearing Saar has long been calling me
with its wave-sounding bulk, with its whole garment,
having prolonged its long stream so that it may roll
a weary mouth under the walls of Augusta.

Nor less than this, lucky Alisontia, gliding quiet
through rich soils, grazes fruit-bearing banks.
A thousand others, as each one's own rush urges more,
desire to be yours. So great is the ambition, or the
character, in the hastening waves. But if, divine Moselle,
Smyrna had given you its poet, or famous Mantua,
Simois, remembered on the Trojan shores, would yield,
nor would the Tiber dare to prefer its own honors.
Grant pardon, grant it, powerful Rome. Let envy, I pray,
be driven off and depart, and Nemesis unknown to the Latin tongue.

This better glory fell to you, Tiber, that you
guard the seat of empire and the household gods of Rome.
Hail, great parent of crops and of men, Moselle.
Famous nobles, youth trained in war,
an eloquence that rivals the Latin tongue adorn you.
Even nature has granted your nurslings character
and a glad talent with a serene brow.
Nor does Rome alone display the ancient Catos,
nor does only one Aristides, watcher of the just and equal,
prevail and make old Athens illustrious.

But why, ranging too far with loosened reins,
overcome by love of you, do I wear out your praises?
Put away, Muse, the lyre; strike the last strings of the song.
There will be a time when the honor of the subject will
commend me, soothing cares in the studies of quiet
leisure and cherishing old age in the sun, when I shall
sing, man by man, the deeds of the Belgae
and the famous glories of ancestral character.
The Pierians will spin me soft songs with a fine thread
and will run through webs fitted with a thin weft.
Purple too will be given to our spindles.
Who then will go unnamed by me? I will remember
the quiet farmers and those skilled in laws and powerful
in speech, a high defense for the accused, whom the
senate-house saw as the highest nobles of the burghers
and as their own senate, whom the famous eloquence of
the purple-bordered school brought to the praises of
old Quintilian, and who ruled their cities and made
illustrious a tribunal clean of blood and innocent axes,
or held the Italian peoples and the north-born Britons
with the second title of prefectures,
and who ruled Rome, the world's head, the people and the
fathers, all but under the first name, although
he was equal to the first. Let Fortune at last hasten
to undo her error and, filling up the tasted rewards,
now restore the peak of true honor
to be claimed again by noble grandsons. But for now let
the work begun be woven out, and with the praise of men
deferred let us tell, through the glad stretch of greening
fields, the fortunate river, and consecrate it into the Rhine's waves.

Now, Rhine, spread your blue bays and your mantle
green as glass, and measure out room for a new stream,
to be piled with your brother's waters, and not prizes
in the waves alone, but that, coming to the walls of
the imperial city, it has watched the joined triumphs
of son and father, the enemy driven out beyond the Neckar
and Lupodunum and the source of the Danube, unknown
to Latin annals.
This laurel of a war almost finished has just come.
From here it will bring other laurels and still others.
Go on, you two joined, and drive the purple sea with
a twin course, and do not fear to seem lesser, most
beautiful Rhine. A guest has no envy. You will win
a lasting name. Adopt your brother, safe in your fame.
Rich in waters, rich in Nymphs, a giver to both,
the channel will stretch a parting with twin banks
and will open common ways through diverse mouths.
Strength will be added, which Francia and the Chamavi
and the Germans may fear. Then you will be held a true frontier.
A twin name will be added to you from so great a river,
and though you flow from one source, you will be called two-horned.

These things I, Ausonius, drawing my race from Viviscan
origin, known in the hospitality of the Belgae not through
new treaties, a Latin name, in fatherland and home
among the farthest of the Gauls and the high Pyrenees,
where glad Aquitaine tempers freeborn character,
bold, I sing with slender trust. It is lawful for me
to have touched the sacred river with a thin libation of the Muse.
I do not aim at praise. I ask pardon. You have many,
kindly river, who are used to troubling the sacred waters
of the Aonians and to drinking all Aganippe.
But I, as far as the vein of my liquor has given itself,
when to Bordeaux, my homeland and the nest of old age,
Augustus, the father and the sons, my greatest care,
send me, decorated with Ausonian fasces and curule honor,
after the duties of finished teaching,
I will pursue more widely the praises of the northern river.
I will add the cities under which you glide in a silent
channel, and walls looking out at you with ancient ramparts.
I will add things founded as safeguards in doubtful times,
but now not camps but granaries for the Belgae at peace.
I will add the happy settlers on either bank
and you, among the labors of men and oxen,
grazing the banks and cutting the rich tilled land.
The Loire will not set itself before you, nor the steep Aisne,
nor the Marne, set as a border between Gauls and Belgae,
nor the Charente itself, ebbing with the Santonian tide.
You will yield, Dordogne, river rolled from a cold mountain,
and Gaul will set the gold-bearing Tames behind,
and the Tarbellian Adour, rushing madly through rocks
that roll far into the purple sea, will yet go only
after it has adored the divinity of its mistress Moselle.

Horned Moselle, celebrated through foreign shores,
and not only in the places where from your highest spring
you put forth the gilded honor of a bull's brow,
or where you draw quiet courses through winding fields,
or where you loose your mouths under German harbors:
if any honor will wish to breathe upon a slender muse,
if anyone will deign to lose leisure on these arts,
you will go into the mouths of men and be cherished
by glad song. Springs and living lakes will know you,
blue rivers, and the ancient groves, the glory of the villages.
The Drôme, the Durance unsure with scattered banks,
and the Alpine rivers will honor you, and the Rhone
that goes through a double city and gives the Right Bank
its name. I will commend you to blue pools and to great
sounding rivers, to the sea-like Garonne.

** Symmachus to Ausonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h2
:END:

You ask longer letters from me. That is a sign of true love toward
us, but I, who know how poor my talent is, prefer to keep to
Laconian brevity rather than publish the thinness of my poor
speech in page upon page. Nor is it a wonder if the vein of our
eloquence has grown thin, which you have not for a long time
helped by the reading of any poem of yours or of prose volumes.
How, then, do you demand a large loan of my speech, you who have
credited no literary interest? Your Moselle flies through the
hands and bosoms of many, consecrated by you in divine verses,
but it only glides past our lips. Why, I ask, did you want me
left out of that little book? Either I seemed to you too
unmusical to judge, or else spiteful, not knowing how to praise.
So you have much diminished either my talent or my character.
And yet against your ban I barely reached the secrets of that
work. I would like to be silent about what I feel. I would like
to be avenged by a just silence concerning you. But admiration
of the writings breaks the sense of the injury.

I knew that river, when I long ago followed the standards of the
eternal princes, equal to many, unequal to the greatest. You have
unexpectedly made it greater than the Egyptian Nile by the dignity
of famous verses, colder than the Scythian Tanais, and more famous
than this Tiber of our people. I would not believe you at all
telling great things of the Moselle's rise and course, if I did
not know that you do not lie even in a poem. Where did you find
those schools of river fish, as various in names as in colors, as
different in size as in taste, which you have dyed with the
pigments of that poem beyond the gifts of nature? And yet, often
spending time at your tables, when I marveled at most other things
then in value that were set out for eating, I never caught this
kind of fish. When were these fish born to you in a book, that
were not on the dishes? Do you think I am joking and playing at
trifles? So may God make me worthy of approval, as I join this
poem of yours to the books of Maro.
But now I will cease, forgetful of my pain, to cling to your
praises, lest you draw this too to your glory, that we admire you
when offended. You may scatter your volumes and always leave me
out. We will still enjoy your work, but by the kindness of
others. Farewell.
