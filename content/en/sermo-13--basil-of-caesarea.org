Of the same, an Ascetical Discourse.
Man was made according to the image and likeness of God; but sin marred the
beauty of the image, dragging the soul down into passionate desires. And God,
who made man, is the true life. Whoever has lost the likeness to God has lost
kinship with life; and for one who stands outside God, it is impossible to
come into the blessed life. Let us therefore return to the grace we had from
the beginning, from which through sin we were =31.872= alienated. And let us
again adorn ourselves after the image of God, being made like the Creator
through dispassion. For he who has imitated in himself, as far as is possible,
the impassibility of the divine nature has recovered upon his own soul the
image of God. And he who has been made like God in the manner just spoken has
also, without fail, acquired the likeness of the divine life, remaining
continually in everlasting blessedness. If then through dispassion we take up
again the image of God, and likeness to God grants us the continuity of life,
let us set everything else aside and make this our zeal: that our soul may
never be overpowered by any passion, but that our mind may remain unswerving
and unconquerable in the assaults of temptations, so that we may become
partakers of the divine blessedness.
Virginity is a fellow-worker of such zeal, for those who pursue this gift
according to reason. For the gift of virginity is not accomplished merely by
abstaining from childbearing; the whole life and living and character ought to
be virgin, displaying through every practice of the unmarried the
incorruption. For it is possible also to fornicate in word, and to commit
adultery with the eye, and to be defiled through hearing, and to receive
pollution in the heart, and by excess in food and drink to fall outside the
bounds of temperance. For he who in all these things keeps himself by
self-control under the rule of virginity truly shows in himself the grace of
virginity, perfect and completed through all things.
Therefore if we desire that the character of our soul be adorned according to
the likeness of God through dispassion, so that through this the eternity of
life may also be added to us, let us attend to ourselves, lest by doing
something unworthy of the promise we come under the judgment of Ananias. For
Ananias might at the outset have left the possession unpromised to God; but
since, looking toward human glory, he consecrated the possession to God
through the promise, that he might be admired by men for the munificence, yet
kept back part of the price, he stirred up against himself such indignation of
the Lord, of which Peter was the minister, that he did not even find a space
for repentance. Therefore before the promise of the honorable life it is
permitted to whoever wishes, according to what is conceded and lawful, to
enter upon a course of life and to give himself to the yoke of marriage. But
once already bound by his own confession, it is fitting that he keep himself
for God, as one of the sacred offerings, so that he may not be liable to the
judgment of sacrilege,
having again defiled with the service of the common life =31.873= the body
consecrated to God through the promise.
And I say this not looking only toward the one form of the passion, as some
suppose, who preserve the achievement of virginity in the guarding of the body
alone, but that he who means to keep himself for God must not, in any manner
of passionate disposition, be defiled by any of the worldly passions. Anger
and envy, and remembrance of wrongs, falsehood and pride, elation and untimely
speech, idleness of prayers, and desire for things that are not, neglect of
commandments, and adornment of garments, beautifying of faces, and meetings
and conversations that come about outside of what is fitting and necessary:
all these things deserve such watchfulness from the one who has consecrated
himself to God through virginity, that there is almost equal danger in being
carried along with any one of these and in falling into the forbidden sin. For
all things that come about through passion somehow damage the purity of the
soul, and hinder the divine life. Toward these things therefore the one who
has renounced ordinary life must look, so that in no way he defile himself,
the vessel of God, by passionate use. And this above all must be reckoned,
that, having crossed the measures of human nature, the one who has chosen the
life of the angels has transferred himself toward the bodiless way of life.
For this is proper to the angelic nature, to have been released from the
marital yoke, and not to be lifted toward any other beauty, but to gaze
continually upon the divine face. He therefore who has transferred himself
toward the angelic rank, if he should be defiled with human passions, is like
a leopard's skin, whose hair is neither wholly white nor entirely black, but
spotted by the mixing of the differently colored, and is numbered neither
among the black nor among the white. Let these things then be a more general
charge for those who have chosen the self-controlled and chaste life. And
since it is necessary also to distinguish the particulars with accuracy, it
would be necessary to leave a brief reminder concerning these things also.
Those who have been separated from the common life, and who train themselves
toward the more divine life, let them not train by themselves, nor alone. For
it is fitting that such a life be with witnesses, so as to stand outside
wicked suspicion. And just as the spiritual law wishes those who eat the
mystical passover not to be fewer than ten, so here the company of ten who
train together in the spiritual way of life must rather exceed than fall
short.
Let one leader preside over the seemliness of the life, preferred from the
rest =31.876= in testing of life and character and of every orderly manner of
living, age in years also being taken into account for the preference. For the
more venerable is somehow more reverend in the nature of men. And let this one
have so much authority, the brotherhood of its own will obeying in ready
obedience and humility, that it not be permitted to any of those in the
company to oppose his will in the things in which he guides what contributes
to seemliness and exactness of life. But as the Apostle says, not to resist
the authorities ordered by God (for those who resist are judged by the
ordinance of God); so also here it is fitting that the rest of the brotherhood
be persuaded that such authority has been given to the one who superintends
not of itself, but according to the divine will, so that the progress
according to God may become unhindered, the one proposing everything
profitable to the soul and useful, and the others receiving the good counsels
in ready obedience.
Since therefore it is fitting that the company be in every manner obedient and
subject to the one who presides, before all things it is necessary to choose
such a leader of this way of life, that his life become a pattern of every
good to those who look toward him; and, as the Apostle says, sober, temperate,
orderly, apt to teach. Thus it seems to me one must also examine this one's
life, not only if he should have old age in years (for it is possible even in
gray hair and wrinkle to be youthful according to character); but first of
all, if the character and the manner have been made gray through orderliness,
so that everything said and done by him becomes law and rule for the company.
And a provision of food is fitting for those who pass their time in such a
life, which the Apostle proposes, that working with their hands they may eat
their own bread in seemliness. And it is fitting that the work be arranged
under some elder, attested for honor of life, who will assign the works of
their hands toward the needs that are sought, so that both the commandment
that orders to provide the food in sweat and toil may be accomplished, and
seemliness may remain irreproachable and blameless, in that no necessity comes
upon them through making the need of life a public matter. And let this be the
finest bound and rule of self-control, neither to look toward luxury, nor
toward ill-treatment of the flesh, but to flee excess in either, so that it
may neither, becoming fleshy, be troubled, nor, having become diseased, be
unable to work the commandments.
For equal harm comes to the soul from both, both the flesh being unsubmissive,
leaping off through good condition into unseemly impulses, and when it is
paralyzed and loosened and unmoved, held by pains. =31.877= For the soul does
not have leisure, in such a state of the body, to look freely toward the
things above; but by every necessity it is occupied and inclined toward the
sensation of the pain, being brought down together with the ill-treatment of
the body. Let the use then be commensurate with the need, and let wine be
neither abominable, if it should be taken for healing, nor sought after
without necessity; and let all the other things likewise serve the needs, not
the desires, of those who train. Let the whole life be a time of prayer; but
especially since it is necessary to rest with certain intervals the intensity
of the psalmody and of the kneeling, one must follow the hours appointed by
the saints for prayer. The great David then says that At midnight I rose to
confess to you upon the judgments of your righteousness; whom Paul and Silas
also are found following, at midnight in the prison praising God. Then the
same prophet says: Evening and morning and noon. But also the presence of the
Holy Spirit comes at the third hour, as we have learned in the Acts, when, the
Pharisees mocking the disciples in the varied working of the tongues, Peter
says that those speaking these things were not drunk; for it is the third
hour.
And the ninth is a memorial of the Master's passion, which came about for our
life. But since David says that Seven times in the day I praised you upon the
judgments of your righteousness, and the times of prayer that have been
mentioned do not fill the seven prayer-times, the noonday prayer must be
divided; and one before the offering of the food, and the other after the
offering; so that
praising God seven times in the day according to every period of the day may
also come about for us in type. Let the entrances of the houses of the
ascetics be shut to women; and let not all men enter, but as many as may be
entrusted with the entrance by the leader. For often the indiscriminate
admission of the one entering plants a train of untimely words and
unprofitable narratives in the hearts, and from the vain words it casts them
into vain and unprofitable thoughts. And for this let there be a common law,
that only the leader be asked and answer concerning the things for which it is
fitting that some word come about by necessity; and that the rest not answer
those who chatter in vain conversations, lest they be dragged down together
into the train of idle words. And let there be one common storehouse for all,
and let nothing private be named for each; not a garment, not a sandal, not
any other of the things toward the necessary need of the body. But let the use
be in the authority of the leader, that the suitable thing from the common
things =31.880= serve each, according to the guidance of the one who leads.
And in this common dwelling the ordinance of love does not allow partial
friendships and companionships. For it is entirely necessary that the
attachments that come about in part harm the common concord in many things.
But now it is fitting that all look toward one another with equal measure of
disposition, and that one measure of love prevail over the whole body. And if
anyone be found having a more loving relation toward a monk brother, or a
kinsman, or toward some other on any pretext whatever, let him be chastened as
wronging the common. For excess of affection toward the one person is a great
accusation of deficiency toward the others.
And let the penalties of the one condemned for some fault be according to the
measure of the sin: being barred from standing with the others in psalmody,
not being received into communion of prayer, and being excluded from the
sharing of the food; in which also the one appointed to superintend the common
good order will define the penalty for the one who sins according to the
quantity of the fall. And let the service of the common company be done in
rotation, two in succession in one week accomplishing all the care that need
requires, so that both the wage of humility may be common, and it may not be
permitted to anyone to have more than the company even in the good, and rest
may belong equally to all. For toiling in part, and resting in part, makes the
toil unfelt for those who labor. The one who presides over the order has
authority both to assign according to judgment those for necessary goings-out,
and to command those for whom it is advantageous to keep the house and remain
within. For often in young bodies, even if the hardship from self-control is
sought as much as possible, the good complexion from the age somehow blooms
upon them, and becomes an occasion of passion for those who meet them. If
therefore anyone should be youthful according to the bloom of the body, let
him make such seemliness unseen by hiding himself, until what appears comes
into the fitting condition. Let there be in them no sign of anger, or
remembrance of wrongs, or envy, or contention, either form, or movement; not a
word, not a fixed look, or disposition of the face, or anything else that is
naturally able to stir the one living with them toward anger. And if anyone
should be carried along with any of these, to have suffered first some of the
things that grieve is not sufficient as a defense of the fault in which one is
held. For the evil dared in any time whatever is equally evil. And let every
oath =31.881= be banished from the roll of those who train.
And let the nodding of the head, and the consent through the voice, be judged
in place of an oath, both for the one speaking and for the one hearing. And if
anyone should disbelieve the bare affirmation, he furnishes an accusation of
his own conscience as not having made his speech truthful, and for this the
one who sits as leader will rank him among those who offend, and he will be
chastened with a healing penalty. When the day has passed, and every work,
both bodily and spiritual, has come to an end, before sleep it is fitting
that the conscience of each be examined by his own heart. And if anything has
come about contrary to what is due, either a thought of the things forbidden,
or a word outside of what is fitting, or slackness concerning prayer, or
acedia of the psalmody, or
desire of the common life, let the fault not be hidden, but let him confess it
to the community, so that through common prayer the passion of the one carried
along with such an evil may be healed.
