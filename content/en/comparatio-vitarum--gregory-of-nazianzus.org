A COMPARISON OF LIVES
{[Lives.]} Would you judge for us, stranger? {[Judge.]} And what is the
case? {[L.]} Lives are at war. {[J.]} And which lives, and
what is the dispute? {[L.]} The worldly life and the life of the Spirit, which
of the two is better and livable for the wise. {[J.]} A hard case, yet it must
be heard. {[World.]} Born of the world, I know it and I love these things.
This is piety: to honor the ancestral law. {[Spirit.]} Born of God, I know God
and I worship God. This is piety: to know the perfect worship. If what is
worse is most honored by you, how is what is better not more justly mine?
{[W.]} Flesh is my mother, and I was mixed with dust. I long for the fullness
of the flesh that has been torn. {[S.]} God is my father, and I have been
yoked to God. I long to imitate the form from which I slipped away. {[W.]}
Tell me, how would the race of mortals have stood, if the pairing of flesh had
not come together, ruled by both divine law and nature? {[S.]} That was the
law; but now I must be loosed, hastening toward another life better than the
present, free of bonds and of corruption. {[W.]} How then did the virgin life
come as a newcomer? {[S.]} It was shadowy even of old; but it shines now,
since a virgin appeared as mother of God. For the old law yielded to the new.
The letter has gone, and the power is of the Spirit. {[W.]} Would anyone be
unyoked apart from married life, or anyone just? {[S.]} How very clever you
are, arguing that flesh should come together for the sake of goods. Time has
reared the good or the bad; but the sower poured out the insolence of the
flesh, nothing else. And to think that passion labors along with the will of
God is a joke. {[W.]} What then have you given to life? {[S.]} And what were
you asking for? Branches? So they may wither, or a whirlwind take them? {[W.]}
You give my root; then keep the branches. {[S.]} It is enough to have been
born. Let someone else labor for corruption: feeding, teaching, then suddenly
mourning the one who is no more, looking at lifeless beauty painted on the
walls, a son who does not move. {[W.]} I am someone of noble birth. {[S.]}
From what stock? And who is the fashioner? Do you not know the clay, as one
man? There is one nobility: imitation of God. You hold tombs and new decrees;
from there you were enrolled as noble, you were not reared so. {[W.]} For me,
being rich casts enemies down, melts the wicked with envy, makes friends,
gives thrones, and lets one puff oneself up in the city. {[S.]} For me,
poverty grants not even to have enemies, and pity is safer than envy. Thrones
are dissoluble, and friends last no more than the occasion. If they remain,
then, it is better to be defeated by God than to have the first of all things
seen, [or to be above all things seen;] and may the intelligible city have me
as its brightness. {[W.]} And what security of life has the poor man: walls,
gates, engines, swordsmen? {[S.]} Toiling, so that the body may not be stolen
as well? He possesses only this and a small rag. The robber is of others; let
tyranny perish, the thief of those who have. One wealth is mine: God, whom no
one will ever snatch from the one who possesses him. As for the rest of those
who have, as they please, let all hands snatch my substance; nothing is safer
than a poor man. The rich man sacrifices to his net, greets his own right hand
as a friend, and does not hymn God the giver of good things, until what he
owns falls into a stranger's hands, one he does not wish: what am I saying?
perhaps an enemy, and from there another. The rollings of wheels. But for me,
if I die, all of life comes together; nothing will be left to envy and to
turns. {[W.]} How great a thing, not to look to neighbors' hands, but others
to ours, perhaps more pious.
{[S.]} How great a thing, to look to God's hands alone, who both gives [and]
asks in a fatherly way, and to do nothing shameful for the sake of receiving,
imitating the blood-lapping leech: holding some things, fixing the mind on
others, looking at still others with unlovely eyes, and shaping yet others in
the hopes of dreams, always poorer by what is

desired. {[W.]} And how will one bear the onset of a hard season, carrying
no support against the hardship? {[S.]} You asked me how one will escape a
well-aimed shaft? A hair is not easily split, not even from close at hand.
Be narrow for me, and you will escape the rush of evils. The reed bears the
winds, but not the oaks. For the one yields, and weight overthrows the
others. {[W.]} I am allowed to live in luxury. {[S.]} But my luxury is not
to live in luxury, swelling with satiety and in the bowels, and sick with a
rich disease, stinking with the pleasant mire beyond the throat, narrowing
the mind with the thickness of the slime. {[W.]} The cakes are mine.
{[S.]} Bread is my savory, and the cake from salt is all sweet to me; with
these I spit on the saltiness of those who live in luxury. {[W.]} For me, the
scent of ointments, the delight of songs. Clappings of hands and feet,
well-rhythmed bendings, the concord of instruments sounding together.
{[S.]} You praise these things, and these are those by which wealth is bad
for me as a teacher of evils. Better for us than instruments is psalmody,
tuning the soul to the things conceived.
And Christ emptied for us is more fragrant than every ointment, that he may
dissolve the stench with which the deadness of sin filled me. And I clap my
hands if my murderer falls, introducing some evil word or deed. And the dance
of the feet is a god-inspired turning.
{[W.]} Perhaps you would say this: that poverty suffices even against
diseases, bringing more remedies of bodies. {[S.]} I would not say this. For
the claim is not true. What is true, this too will be said: the poor man is
much stronger than those who have. And God, balancing the gift, gave strength
to the poor, and medicines to the well-off. The poor man toils, works the
matter off with sweat, hungers, shivers, is burned with the midday sun,
travels, is loaded, is rained on, groans. He received the food of the season,
simple and uncompounded; he knows nothing of the tricks which cooks and
servants of luxury furnish from the recesses of earth and sea, setting out a
womanish table for men. Wind, catarrhs, spleens, dislocations, cries,
draughts, palenesses, faintings of the well-off: these are the offspring of
satieties. They neither have pleasure in what they have, and they seek things
by which they will leave bulk, often envying weaker people who are in good
health: deserted of all things, unhappy fortunate ones. Gold, stones,
embroideries, dyes of clothing, and painter's purple of contrasting color of
walls, ceilings, and mosaic worn by the feet; silver, some locked hidden in
the folds of earth, from which it came, worthy of tombs, some on display and
flashing upon the drinks; horses, carpets, chariots, charioteers, dogs,
hunters, trackers of beasts; and these the delights of a bad mistress, the
belly, and the grace of the all-receiving passage, the throat. Spreaders of
couches, doorkeepers, summoners, chamberlains, flower-bearers, sprinklers,
wipers of circles, tasters, shade-bearers; overseers of nods, bathers, mixers
with raisings of fingers, women for haircutting; delights of the eyes: all
things of a rich man's tomb, of painted clay. The needy man stands; and if he
even falls, worn by disease at last with difficulty or after a long time, he
will give no trouble to his own friends; he will die like a lion in roarings,
a corpse far more blooming than the very rich. What remains? Insolence, anger,
frenzy, boldness, drunkenness, unbridled laughter, shameful words, contempt of
God, of blood, of friends: these belong not more to the poor than to the rich.
For wealth brings insolence, and insolence destruction. But he is pressed for
the most part, and is not lifted up, especially if the fear of God should run
together with him, a strong tutor of all good things. Look at each against
each briefly, remaining a just judge of both lives, unless this too seems
insolence for you to bear: as it were, to stand near the poor, or to breathe
the same air, or to be called by one name, and to seem to share a roof and a
voyage. You envy the excellent, and I pity the wicked; for nothing is more
wretched than the wicked, even if they run well toward an insecure end. You
spit on the poor as of another god. I know one fashioning, all coming to one
judgment. Character is another, a second fashioning. You are lifted up with
the prosperous; I am chastened by fear. You are bent by the toilsome; I am
lightened by hopes. For nothing remains, but flows in long time. Night stopped
day, and day night; grief stopped joy

and disaster set the pleasant things still. Therefore one must not attend to
them as if they were firm. For you nothing is terrible, not even among the
extreme evils; for luxury somehow stands against thinking. But to me even the
small things appear as mourning; for sin is alienation from God. And how could
I bear being deprived of God? For you sleep is good, and the ease of turning
from side to side, and sweet dreams, impressions of the day's things. You
drink, dice, take, play, laugh. For me the greater part of life is sleepless;
for the labors sting me even when I sleep, and if I snatch a little sleep, I
weep. The images of a wild night frighten me too: judgment, an unbending
judge, a trembling stand, a spring bubbling from here of unquenchable fire, a
worm from there eating forever; in the middle, conscience, an unwritten
accuser. God is God to you if he brings the right-hand things; to me he is to
be revered even if he brings the opposite. For my toiling is a medicine of
salvation. As much as I am pressed, so near I come to God; for my suffering
binds me tight to God. For even the pursuit of an army brings people into the
walls. Children, a wife, and friends are comforters for you, things which even
the greatest misfortune removes. For me, God is support and consolation when I
hunger and shiver and am in straits. Insult, strike, call me low-born, a
beggar, trample, force: you will insult for a little time. I bear all things
to God, toward whom I also look. I send my thought into the later life; there
I am settled; I know nothing of the things below. Thus I am easily loosed from
what grieves. What of the rest? Today, for you not to receive the vote is
unbearable, as it seems to me. For what always rules does not bear being
ruled. For me it is equal to receive and not to receive. God is enough for me,
even if another carries all things; this is the greatest glory toward victory
for me. {[W.]} What then if some of the highest fall? {[S.]} What then if some
of the second also fall? For it is possible for both to succeed and to fall.
Equal against equal, but I set those who fall in the first life as much worse:
so opposed am I to the wicked. For of those whose rule, when they prevail, is
more honorable, the fall, when they have fallen, is more wretched. And I ask
this: not to reckon it to a way of life if someone is most wicked, for he is
hostile to the way of life; but to look at it in itself and distinguish the
whole, goods and evils alike set opposite, and not to let the lesser weigh
down the scale, for that is not just, but let the greater prevail. Thus you
would know how great the interval between the two lives. Do not match the best
of the donkeys against a bad horse for me, nor the best man of the worldly
life against the worst of mine. For they would appear better, I will not deny.
But if you set highest against highest and bad against bad as opposites, you
would learn how much I prevail. Look at this too: throughout all time, what
has failed among you and what prevails among us stand equal. And they say one
swallow does not bring the pleasant spring, nor a hair old age. What do you
say? Do you give the vote? Or shall I let you be? {[J.]} I give it. How should
I not? I shall think it right to prefer the lower to your life when I, gone
mad, equate some mortal with God. And if I shall not go mad, I shall not be
persuaded of this either. But go: it would be better if you both kept
peacefully toward one another and toward the great God, you assigning the
first things to the first life, and you taking the second as a brother. If
indeed one does not have the first, what prevents having the second? This too
is no dishonor. Thus life would be safe for us.
