p.4 From the East this man too revolts against him and, having gained the
upper hand, lays hold of the empire, giving a wicked return to the one who had
entrusted him, as has been said, with the generalship of the Anatolics:
certain counterfeit men, who had altogether failed to know the nature of the
good and had not even learned to keep a right faith toward those to whom they
owed it, coming together in one mind and breathing as one, while the whole
people so unreasonably followed their will. For among men wickedness is always
accustomed to have the advantage rather than the good; and the multitude
otherwise loves to rejoice in such changes. Thus then Botaneiates is raised to
the height of empire, a man intelligent at first and most able with the hand,
but otherwise worn down by old age and time, having long since undone the
ambitious spirit that belonged to his skill, and having been deprived of
almost all his own energy, and not sufficient for the height of empire. p.5
When this man then had laid hold of the scepters of empire and had been
preferred before the one naturally entitled to it (this was the brother of
Doukas Michael, Constantius the porphyrogenitus), the Comnenus Alexius already
mentioned, seeing the absurdity of the affair, that neither had the rightful
heir of the empire been justified in it, nor had any memory been made of the
right of empire of his uncle who had reigned before, Isaac Comnenus, to whom
the wills of all had run together and whom all of their own accord had raised
to the axles of empire and had chosen to be ruled by, so that someone of those
drawing the line of the family from him might more justly be counted for this,
was distressed, was impatient, was jealous seeing justice set aside, and could
no longer bear what had fallen out. For those who at that time had obtained
the power beside the throne, establishing their own will, counted the ruin of
the Roman empire as nothing, and chose only what served their own interest;
but he, stung in the heart and showing the nobility of his soul, could not
endure to keep quiet at things so done and to see the one entitled to the
empire subjected to another who was not entitled. How then Comnenus approached
the brother of Doukas and was eager to establish him on the throne of empire
under his own power and help, setting even the purple-dyed sandals at his feet
and leading him to the palace, and how, as they were going through the
highways, the people, having become one mouth, cried out plainly that they did
not wish to be ruled by him, and how the said child, dizzy at the noise and
terrified in soul by the people's words, held back from such a purpose, and
also entreated Comnenus to stand off from it and not to force him further:
these things lie in the mouths of all and are confessed among those of sound
mind and as many as, not having drunk the draught of Lethe, are mindful of
what happened. p.6 And Comnenus again chose to run a second road, and taking
the child he comes bringing him to Botaneiates, and he brings this man to
remembrance of the natural right which has been allotted to this child, and he
proposes that he use him more economically in the present and embrace the
brother of Doukas more kindly as the brother of his own lord and emperor, and
put on him the name of empire, while Botaneiates himself should steer the
scepters of empire so long as he should be numbered among the living, and then
set in it the heir of the empire, already fit for its government. But having
fallen from this attempt also, he got this from such frankness: to be counted
suspect by Botaneiates and those about him, and by others, but most of all by
those two slaves of his, this Borillus and Germanos, men bought with silver
and of low birth and


altogether without understanding of the good, who, setting as their aim among
themselves to destroy Comnenus and so to destroy together with him the
porphyrogenitus, the brother of Doukas, stripped of the one guarding him and
become easy to those plotting against him, there being no one to take his
part, send down a command bidding the exile of Comnenus. But when such a
command had come into the hands of Comnenus, he himself in person reported the
matters concerning it to the emperor, and this man, at once respecting the
excess of the man's nobility and prudence, and remembering many and noble
deeds of his, and being put to shame toward these, and blaming those who had
agreed to the command, brought the affair to nothing, and the matter of the
exile was then unwritt. p.7 But Botaneiates, being led about again by the
slaves already shown, often driven aside in every way by their whetting and
thrusting, intended to dig out the eyes of Comnenus; yet he did not judge it
right to bring this so shamelessly to completion, standing in awe of justice
itself and the truth; but he was considering another way to surround this man
with evils and was seeking a fair-faced occasion to send the guiltless one
into evils and to put out of the way the proof of the truth. Accordingly,
whenever some affair fell in that needed both great counsel and a hand, and
that demanded war and a showing of a noble soul and of manhood, with danger
hanging over the attempt of it, at once there was use of Comnenus above all,
and Comnenus alone of all was put forward to preside over works so great and
full of danger: this being the wish of the scheme, so that either Comnenus,
having become obedient to what was assigned him and having attacked one of
such works nobly and manfully, as was his custom, and having gone into the
midst of swords, might be struck in a vital place and fall in the war, because
he was not even furnished with forces proportionate to the work set, or at any
rate, not obeying, nor doing so, nor yielding to what was commanded him, might
supposedly be justly accused and undergo lawful punishment. Already then those
distinguished, both Bryennius and Basilaces, men of those well born and
notable, not themselves bearing the seating of Botaneiates on the imperial
throne, inasmuch as they also knew this man as a rebel with themselves against
the emperor Michael and jointly liable in the same things, kept in form
against him also that revolt which they had first raised against Michael, and
with a multitude of soldiers and a force not to be despised they array
themselves against him. And at once Comnenus, coming to the emperor's
remembrance, is sent as antagonist in arms and opposing general against such
men, holding at that time the dignity of Grand Domestic, and he is set against
them and joins a most confident battle and clashes with them not once only,
but many times he puts forth his very soul into war, and at last he conquers
the adversaries and sets up a brilliant trophy against them and, taking them
spear-captured, leads them to the queen of cities and delivers them to the
emperor. All know these things, and the matters concerning them lie on the
lips of all, and there is no one of those of sound mind who has been ignorant
of them. p.8 But when for Comnenus, these and many other great works having
been accomplished truly manfully and nobly, there was no recompense and
return, but only a zeal not good and a study and packing against him, how a
wicked thing might be given back to him instead of good things, and how he
himself might have his eyes cut out and perish, and the porphyrogenitus too
might be crushed together with him, he no longer sets these things among what
can be borne. But seeing that the affair was coming to him for evil, and that
they had of their own will sent over the inheritance of his uncle to another
..., since none of those related by family to the one who had obtained it had
been put forward, and another, an alien, had been


brought in instead into this inheritance, and at the same time procuring
safety for himself, lest he suffer some of the terrible things which had been
planned in many forms against him, setting out from the great city he goes off
to Adrianople; and finding the military gathering there for the most part
hating the empire of Botaneiates, and attaching themselves to him and choosing
him as emperor, inasmuch as they had accomplished many deeds of manhood under
him as commander of the army, and together forcing him even unwilling toward
acclamation, he receives their counsel and recalls the inheritance of his
uncle, to which he had been entitled. And ostensibly he brings the scepters
around to himself, yet not so as to deprive the porphyrogenitus of them, who
had already come to a sufficient and worthy condition. For how could he, who
even before had not left off moving every rope so as to establish this man on
the throne of his brother? For this reason at once he brings and joins his own
daughter to the porphyrogenitus and consents that he reign with him and
jointly administer the affairs of the empire, and he even shares with him the
customary acclamation and proclamation and the red signature in writings,
betrothing the scepters to him from this point and intending finally, when the
season called, to raise him to the throne of empire, whenever he should also
be able to draw to him the good inclination of the great multitude concerning
him and to take away the former wrath. And perhaps what was being managed
would have come out into work, if a heavy disease had not first entered the
porphyrogenitus, not allowing him to step onto such a rule, and after no long
time snatched him away from things here. p.9 But even when Comnenus Alexius,
just become emperor, was stepping onto the great city, he did not find
Botaneiates opposing him in the affair and arrayed against him, but this man
too, giving way to the right which from kinship fitted Comnenus for the
empire, lays down the rule without love of strife, not wishing to take up arms
for it and to war against him, where conscience from the hearth had him
fighting him down and striking from both sides, both from his own alienness
toward the empire and from the genuineness of Comnenus Alexius toward it. For
thus Comnenus, and not in another way, recalls the imperial rule, by the
right, that is, of genuineness of blood toward the Comnenian family and of
nearness by marriage toward the Doukas family; for having fitted as partner of
his life one from the Doukai, he joined both families into one concord and
made them one plant together as a tree, and at the same time more venerable
for differing in antiquity, as they say. For this reason all, respecting both
the ancient lineage of the Comneni and that of the Doukai, and the one more
entitled than any other from this to the empire, namely Comnenus Alexius,
gladly chose to be ruled by him. For if someone should wish, as it were, to
run back up the stream, he will find the family of the Doukai as if gushing
from the first tribe of Constantine the Great, inasmuch as that first Doukas
too, being one of those who


set out from elder Rome with Constantine the Great and migrated to the
younger, was most genuinely related by blood to Constantine the Great; for he
was that man's cousin and had been entrusted by him with the dignity of doux
of Constantinople, and from this all those named from the Doukai were named
after him. p.10 Thus then the emperor Alexius, having recalled the empire and
been honored by it, and having honored it no less, by the many and great works
which he accomplished, filling the eastern lot and the western with trophies,
as the facts themselves show, at the very departure of life sends over the
scepters to his own son John, as justly preferred for rule from both, both
from the Comnenian tribe so allotted to the empire, as has been taken up
before, and from the imperial Doukas root, from which his mother too sprang, a
noble fruit. And who after the porphyrogenitus, who had left life, was most
entitled to rule? It is quite clear then that that Alexius, celebrated among
emperors, having become master of the scepters of empire, is not only not
worthy of accusation, but will already be judged by those of sound mind worthy
of encomia also, and will be set forth as a good example and archetype to
those after, so that others too, seeing their natural lords outdone and the
right that had fitted them from of old being taken away, may be jealous on
behalf of those who have suffered unjust things, and may bring all zeal toward
their vindication and toward restoring to them their inheritance from which
they have fallen, and if, the affairs perhaps opposing, they should not be
able to do this, they may at least force back the one who brought force upon
them and may drive out the one who drove them from their own inheritance, not
allowing him to enjoy his own bad turn of character, so that others too who
attempt to do injustice may not enjoy what they will deprive others of. For
those who at that time did not think thus, nor joined with Comnenus (the same
as to say with Doukas) about the restoration of their inheritance, but rather
in some way opposed them and did not throw down their arms and do homage to
their natural master, for whom rather they ought to have taken up arms, what
punishment would they not have been just to undergo, or not only as far as
money and a first-fruits of goods and every other loss, but death itself, and
this bitter and painful? And otherwise how was God going to keep these men
above maltreatment and not measure back to them with the measures with which
they themselves measured? For as they also forgot the right of nature and
dealt unsparingly with their natural lord, they would justly have obtained
unsparingness from God as well, being punished sharply, because they were also
caught as having offended against God himself. And that these things were
accomplished with the inclination from above and by God's nod, the condition
about the Roman empire presents more clearly, the affairs from that time
having taken increase toward the better and even until now advancing each day
and going fortunately toward dawn and toward the west by God's good pleasure
and grace, so that from the fortunate results the beginning too is shown to be
fortunate. p.11 This then, a prize greatest of all that we know, you have set
before us, O my most wise mind and thought, having commanded us to gather the
deeds of Alexius the Great, who, having taken over the leaderships of the
Romans in difficult seasons, when the affairs of the Romans had given out,
fallen to the ground and in danger of flowing apart, raised them completely
and brought them up to greatest glory. For joining manhood to intelligence, by
the continuity of his deeds it is not possible to say how many trophies of the
Romans he set up, so that the affairs are ...... some he drove out, some he
enslaved, and some he made under treaty to the Romans. To gather this man's
deeds then would be a laborious thing and beyond our power, and I would have
declined the work, if Heraclean force had not been forcing me toward it,
persuading me to exchange a short labor for the greatest goods. For what else
could I give back to him worthy of all that he himself has given me, if I
should pass over his deeds in silence so that they be darkened in a depth of
forgetfulness? And if the discourse should not be able to reach all of them,
let no one hiss at it; for I have not chosen to write a history, nor to weave
an encomium for him (scarcely would the force of Thucydides and the great
voice of Demosthenes have sufficed for these), but wishing to furnish some
starting point for those who wish to write his things I have set out toward
this writing; for this reason let the name Material of History also be for the
discourse. We must begin then from here. 1.t Book 1 1.1 That Manuel of great
fame, who referring his family to the Comneni, of the agreements and treaties
in between of Basil, who at that time steered the scepters of the Romans, and
of that Bardas Sclerus, who for


many years had played the tyrant and had almost overthrown the whole East, had
been appointed ambassador, having tried to join the ruptured members of the
Romans' leadership toward wholeness and indeed having still joined them very
expertly and youthfully: of this Manuel then two sons are born, of whom the
elder was called Isaac, and the younger John. And when both of these were
quite young, since the father was ill and suspected the end of life, he
resolved to commend both children to the emperor; for they had been deprived
of their mother also, his partner in life having died long ago. Having
resolved this, he brought the thing resolved to an end most excellently, and
indeed he commends them to the emperor. And he, being equitable and knowing
how to honor nobility, it is not possible to say of how much forethought he
thought the children worthy; for having set over them tutors and trainers, he
ordered some to rhythm the character of the youths, and others to teach the
things of war, to arm themselves well, and to be covered with the shield
against the enemy's shots, and to brandish a spear, and to ride skillfully,
and to send a missile at a mark, and, that I may say the most timely thing, to
practice tactics, so that they might know how one must draw up a phalanx and
enroll companies, and encamp opportunely and plant a palisade, and the other
things which tactics teach. And the monastery of Studium was assigned them as
a place of sojourn for two reasons, one that they might also be anointed
toward virtue by imitation of the best men, the other that they might have it
easy to go out of the city to go off to hunting and to exercise with arms. 1.2
And when both had passed youth, at once they were enrolled together in the
imperial companies; for this is a custom for emperors of the Romans, to enroll
together in their own service the sons of the best men and of those well born.
Not yet had much time flowed by, and the greatest of the honors had them, both
provinces and commands of phalanxes and generalships. And since there was need
of splendid marriages for those so illustrious in family, they obtain these
also; for Isaac is yoked to the elder of the daughters of Samuel, emperor of
the Bulgarians, Catherine by name; and John to the daughter of Alexius Charon,
to whom the affairs in Italy had been entrusted from the emperor, a man of
mind and intelligence and noble of hand and bold of soul, to whom the surname
also had been allotted from his manhood; for having struck one of the
adversaries, when he had shown him a corpse, he was surnamed Charon from this.
John, having married this man's daughter Anna, who drew her family on the
mother's side to the Dalasseni, those Adriani and those Theophylacti, was
shown father of excellent children. From these then five were male, Manuel,
Isaac, Alexius, Adrian, Nicephorus, and three female, Maria, Eudocia, and
Theodora, who were also preserved until his end; for upon these he ended his
life. But these things later; now let the discourse hold to the sequence. 1.3
Going forward then from glory to glory, both brothers kept concord until the
end, the younger yielding to the elder in all things and that man assigning
the fitting honor to the young one. And when Isaac also was brought up to the
imperial lookout, having honored his brother as curopalates he appointed him
commander of the armies of the West, whom of old they called Domestic of the
Schools, but now they call Grand Domestic. The things concerning Isaac then,
how he laid hold of the imperial scepters and for how long and how he steered
them, it would be superfluous to say; for it will be possible for those who
wish to read these things, meeting the histories about this man. And John,
when he obtained to rule the West, as the discourse arriving before has shown,
left an unforgettable memorial of his deeds to Thracians and Macedonians and
indeed also to Illyrians and Bulgarians, both rulers and ruled, and there was
no one who had not had experience of the man's nobility; for being equitable
and moderate if any other of men, he drew the goodwill of all, being most
sharp and intense toward benefiting, but toward


punishing blunt and hard to move. And proofs of the man's virtue are both very
many others, and not least also his putting the empire away from himself; and
how, the discourse going forward would show. 1.4 Already completing the third
year in the empire, the brother and emperor, having crossed the strait of the
Propontis, was lodging in the suburbs about the small town of Honoratus, going
out continually to hunting and exercising the body. But the exercise
contributed nothing to him; for he was seized by a disease of pleurisy, and
having endured the third day, when he perceived himself not well, having
entered the imperial dromon he was carried to the palace. Care then held him
at once of the one who would reign after him, and the brother was preferred
before all (for he knew this man to be good and sufficient for the taking up
of common affairs), and at once he had been summoned, and the emperor began
words of exhortation toward him: "Me," he said, "O dearest brother, the end of
life already having overtaken forces toward the exit, and you must take up the
common affairs and receive the tillers of empire; for I think this will be
profitable not only to those related to us by blood, but also to all those
under the Romans. Having been bound then with the diadem, while I myself am
still breathing, take up the affairs; for many, as you know, look with desire
at the empire of the Romans." Hearing these words, that man was reluctant to
run toward the taking up of the commons and was drawing back, and ready rather
to suffer all things than to take up the commons, but he was of this purpose,
while the wife, perceiving what was said, bore the refusal heavily, and
sitting beside her husband used such words toward him: "Why," she said, "my
lord, do you draw a sword against yourself and those dearest, neither pitying
their untimeliness nor caring for us? Do you not know that if another lays
hold of the rule of the Romans, he would hasten to put our whole family out of
the way, thinking that thus the affairs of the rule would be seated for him on
the safe? Why then are we so made vain, casting ourselves and those dearest
into an obvious danger, and thrusting away what is desired by all, the empire
of the Romans? What is this harmful philosophy and untimely moderation of
mind? But if you obey me at all, hasten as quickly as possible to be persuaded
by the counsels of the emperor and brother and lay hold of the affairs." 1.5
Having said these things and more than these (for she was terrible both to
speak and to act), when she did not persuade, she turned to entreaty and
offered approaches through tears and groans; and when she saw him unsoftened
and hardening toward everything, despairing of persuading she was silent,
grieving much in herself and looking sullen at this. Thus then that man had
put the empire away from himself. And the emperor, seeing the disease
stretching, since the things concerning the brother had been despaired of for
him, looked toward Doukas Constantine, a man crowned with many advantages. At
once then he called and, having spoken with him about the commons and having
commended the kinsmen to him, he crowns him with the imperial fillet, himself
first having exchanged the angelic habit for the purple. He then was led away
toward the monastery of Studium. And Doukas, having grasped the tillers of
empire, at once hastened to take away greed and to bring in moderation and
justice, and indeed this was also accomplished for him; for seeing most turned
toward doing injustice he turned to judging. And not taking a face in judgment
according to the prophet, nor indeed turning aside to the right or to the
left, but going by the royal road, to those doing injustice he appeared some
stern and terrible man, cutting out the impulses of injustice, but to those
being done injustice both beloved and most gracious, and indeed he was shown
not ungrateful toward this man who had reigned either; for when, after the
monastic life and habit, the wrapping and the leading to the monastery of
Studium, the things of the disease eased and he who had been expected to die
was shown


healthy, Doukas led this man through all honor, calling him lord and emperor
and thinking him worthy of the first seat whenever he used to go to him; and
he used to go continually. And he led not him only through honor, but also all
his kindred, wife and daughter and brother and the others; but Comnenus,
having lived a year after this man's proclamation, died. 1.6 And Doukas,
having steered the empire of the Romans lawfully for seven years in all, ended
his life also; and his partner Eudocia succeeds to the empire with Michael and
Constantius the children. And a short time after the death of Doukas the
curopalates also, having lived on, died, leaving eight children, as the
discourse arriving before has shown, five male and three female, whom the
mother, having raised all well, showed worthy of the family. But two of the
daughters, while the father still lived, were fitted to men of those well born
and crowned with wealth, the elder Maria to Michael Taronites, and the one
after her Eudocia to Nicephorus Melissenus, a man intelligent and wonderful,
who referred his family on the father's side to the Burtzes. And the last of
all, Theodora, after the father's fate, the mother later fitted to Constantine
the son of Diogenes, the father already having obtained to manage the scepters
of the Romans, a man noble and noble of hand, but not altogether praiseworthy
in manner, as the later things showed. But thus the things concerning the
girls happened. And of the male children Manuel, while this father still
lived, both began to serve in the army and, polishing himself toward imitation
of the ancestors, hastened to turn out worthy of them. And Isaac after him
followed in the footprints of the brother. And the third, Alexius, was full of
all kinds of graces; for a grace bloomed on the face such as no one had ever
seen in another, and he was always bright and full of goodness, and what could
one say about this man so as to be able to equal the words to the splendors of
the characters and the virtues of the soul? He was a thing of every kind of
natural and chosen seemliness, and as it were some most beautiful dedication
to life composed of two opposites; for being sharpest of all whom we ourselves
have seen and most active, he showed the soul so most gentle as almost to show
himself unmoved toward anger. And not yet having passed youth he forced the
mother to send him out also with the brother; accordingly he also followed
this man when he was general, and showed the things of virtue before
completeness, and carrying a shield and brandishing a spear as excellently as
possible, and he was at once on the tongue of all. Let the things concerning
this man then wait a little, and let the discourse hold to the sequence from
the beginning; for thus going forward in order it would come down to his own
deeds, having first made mention also of the remaining brothers; and these two
were Adrian and Nicephorus, for whom the mother, they being quite young,
having set teachers over them ordered them to be educated in the encyclical
education. 1.7 And just when Romanus Diogenes had laid hold of the tillers of
empire, first of the brothers Manuel was made intimate to the emperor and was
appointed protostrator by him. Not yet had much time flowed by, and he had
been put forward as general with full power of the eastern tagmata. Having
crossed the Propontis then he was lodging about Galata, the Turks already
having begun to plunder the things of the East. And who the Turks are and from
what land having set out they formerly ceased to border on the Romans later,
it is necessary to narrate from above. These obtained to dwell the northern
parts beyond Tanais and Bosporus, not very far from the Caucasian mountains;
being an autonomous nation and milk-eating from the beginning, both populous
and most warlike, they have been enslaved by no nation ever. When then the
rule of the Persians had been dissolved into those of Hagar, and the Saracen
dominion was mastering not only Persis and Media and Babylon and the
Assyrians, but already also Egypt and Libya and not the least part of


Europe, since those from Hagar, having stasied against one another, divided
the greatest rule into many, another ruling another, and the nation went into
civil wars, Mouhoumet the son of Imbrael, being then leader of Persis and of
the Chorasmii and Abritani and Media in the times of Basil the autocrator and
warring against Indians and Babylonians, when he saw the affairs going
backward for himself, knew he must send an embassy to the ruler of the Huns
and ask alliance from there. When then the embassy had been directed for him,
he sent with the ambassadors also costly gifts; the ambassadors then returned
bringing allies three thousand, having as leader Tangrolipex Moukalet the son
of Mikeel, who, having crossed the Araxes at the bridge on it that was towered
on both sides, go to Persis. Mouhoumet then, having mixed the mercenary Turks
with his own army, attacks Pisasirius the ruler of the Arabs and more easily
routs him. And having returned to his own he hastened to contend also against
the Indians at war with the allies. And when those declined and begged to be
sent out to their own, the guard at the bridge of the Araxes having been
relaxed for them, since that man pressed and intended to bring force, the
Turks, fearing they might suffer some terrible thing, go over to revolt and,
having slipped under the Carbonitis desert because they did not dare, being
few, to array themselves against so many myriads, making raids from there they
wasted and destroyed the things of the Saracens. 1.8 And Mouhoumet, bearing
what had happened with difficulty, having gathered an army of about twenty
thousand, sends it out against the Turks, having set over it ten generals of
the Saracens attested for manhood and intelligence. These then, having set
out, were hastening toward Carbonitis, but they thought it unprofitable to
enter the desert because of the scarcity of water and the lack of foods, and
having encamped nearest the mouth of the desert they were considering the
things toward the war. And Tangrolipex, lodging in the depth of the desert,
when he knew the expedition against him, having conferred with those with him
and having judged it profitable to attack the Hagarenes and Persians by night,
using swift travel for two days attacks them on the third by night as they
tented unguarded and expected no evil, and he routs them easily. From this
having become master of very much booty and having become lord of many
chariots and horses, he no longer lodged about Carbonitis, nor made his
attacks secretly as a fugitive and robber, but openly laid claim to the open
country, there flowing to him also as many as then through wickedness feared
death, both not a few slaves and those who rejoice in plunderings, so that in
a short time a very great force was gathered about him, not less than fifty
thousand. The things concerning that man were carried thus; and Mouhoumet, not
bearing what had happened moderately, blinded the ten generals who returned,
and threatened to triumph the soldiers who had run from the danger, wrapping
them in women's robes, and he himself was arming toward an opposing array. 1.9
But the soldiers, having heard his threats, go over to Tangrolipex. Such and
so great a force then having been added to that man, having gathered the whole
army he was hastening toward Mouhoumet, eager to judge the affairs by a
general battle. But that man too, having armed Saracens and Persians and
Cabeiri and Arabs and having composed an army of about five hundred thousand,
and bringing also a hundred tower-bearing elephants, hastened to stand face to

Turks, having begun the war toward the Romans, have continued until now

the emperor received both and has honored them with great gifts. =1.12= And at
the beginning of spring he campaigned against the Turks, taking Chrysoscoulus
with him; and when this man had come about Bithynia, the curopalates, seized

both took the fortress Membet and, having met detachments of Turks foraging,
both turned them and killed many, and not a few he also took alive. Lifted
