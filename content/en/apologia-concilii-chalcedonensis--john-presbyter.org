From the apologies of Eulogius concerning the same chapter

I. Everything that is contemplated commonly and in many, existing neither more
in one nor less in another, is named essence. Since therefore to each
particular man there belongs equally the being flesh ensouled with a rational
soul, which is humanity, according to this we say that humanity, being also a
mark of two essences, is one essence, as being contemplated commonly in all
men and equally. But in the case of the hypostasis of Emmanuel we do not say
that the union of the Godhead and the humanity has made one essence, since it
would then be necessary that such an essence also be contemplated in many. For
nature, which is essence, is contemplated equally in many, as has been
demonstrated. If then they say that the two have become one essence, then
surely others also have partaken of such an essence, and there will be many
equal to Emmanuel, which is not the case, nor would the present slanderers go
so far in madness, even if they have become exceedingly God-fighters and
enemies of the truth.

II. And after other things. We say that humanity, being composite from unlike
things, is of one essence, not looking to the difference of soul and body, but
looking to the common, because it is contemplated commonly in all who are
brought under the same species. For this is the definition of essence, as
Basil taught, saying: "the relation which the common has to the particular,
this essence has to the hypostasis." According to this account of commonality,
then, we say that humanity is of one essence, because commonly in all
particular men and equally there exists the being a rational mortal animal,
receptive of mind and knowledge. For this is the definition of humanity. But
when we reckon the things from which it is, and name soul, and consider body,
we would not deny the difference of nature and the properties of the essences
even after the union. For if the body and the soul are of the same essence,
and of things consubstantial there is the same account, but the body is
corruptible, then the soul also is destroyed, and the saying that the soul is
immortal will be shown false because of your senseless empty talk.

III. 1. [From the apologetic writings of Eulogius of Alexandria] against those
who say: if you say that Christ has two essences, then the holy Trinity has
certainly become incarnate as the whole of humanity.

These are the problems of the opponents. For they perhaps think that the
essence of the Godhead is divisible, and that one part of it is contemplated
in the Father, another in the Son, and another in the Holy Spirit, as if each
hypostasis were recognized from a part, and not in all the properties of the
Godhead. But we have not gone so far in impiety as to hold that there is
division and cutting in the divine essence, but we say that each
characterizing hypostasis has unfailingly the marks of the Godhead, the good,
the creative, and whatever belongs to the uncreated nature. For thus we also
say that the Trinity is consubstantial, as the same essence is recognized
wholly in three persons. For the Father has the essence of the Godhead
perfectly, likewise the Son, likewise the Holy Spirit. Whence the Father is
perfect God, the Son is perfect God, the Spirit is perfect God. With these the
herald of the truth, Paul, also agrees, saying: "For in him dwells all the
fullness of the Godhead bodily." For he does not say that the Father has
become incarnate, but that the whole Godhead exists unfailingly in the three
hypostases, in the Father, in the Son, in the Holy Spirit, existing perfectly
in each hypostasis according to the voice of the Savior which says: "All
things that the Father has are mine," that is, whatever marks belong to the
essence of the Father, these belong to God the Word even when he has become
incarnate, not as if the Father were the Son or the Son were the Father; for
these names are significant of the relation toward one another. How then
should we not say that the essence of the Godhead is unfailingly in Christ,
when we say that he is perfect <God>? And how, confessing him also to be
perfect man, shall we not further confess that the whole essence of humanity
exists in him? For he did not assume a part of it, as Apollinarius says, flesh
without a rational soul, but the whole essence, which is flesh ensouled with a
rational and intellectual soul. For this, existing perfectly and commonly in
particular men, would reasonably be called essence. For individuals are
separated from one another not by essence, but by the accompanying properties,
by size and color and, to speak simply, by the characterizing qualities of the
persons.

2. And how will they say that Christ is from two natures, unless the name of
nature is understood by them in place of essences? For they will not dare to
say it in place of characterizing hypostases, since they would then be forced
to say also from two persons. For the characterizing hypostasis gives us to
understand a person. And I say "in character," lest by the customary deceit of
the heretics they suppose hypostasis without character. For then it carries
the meaning of essence. And how do they suppose Christ after the union to be
consubstantial with the Father and consubstantial with us, and are not numbed
at confessing two consubstantial relations, while denying the two essences?

From the apologies of Eulogius of Alexandria

IV. 1. I heard one of them who thought he was discoursing nobly, that there is
no nature without a person, but that a person also accompanies the nature. If
then there are two natures, there are certainly also two persons. How would
one not pity those who put forward such things, who so clearly stumble, and
while pretending to war against Nestorius use his problems, and, so far as in
them lies, make his disease secure? For if there is no nature without a
person, but a person also accompanies the nature, and they say that the
ineffable union in Christ has come about from two natures, then according to
them Emmanuel is from two persons, and henceforth boldness is opened to those
who blaspheme to say two persons in Christ, which is full of madness. For one
would not, if he united Peter and Paul, say one hypostasis and one person. For
hypostases, which are persons, admit the union according to essence and
according to relation, not the inhypostatic union that produces one person.
According to essence, then, hypostases are united, as in the case of the holy
Trinity; for three hypostases exist of one essence. But the union according to
relation is understood in many ways: in homonymy, in equal honor, in sameness
of will, and whatever is like these. In homonymy, as Judas of James and the
Iscariot; in equal honor and sameness of will, as Peter and John; for each was
an apostle, and they wished to increase the preaching of Christ. What I said
before, then: two hypostases, that is persons, when united never produce one
hypostasis and one person. If then Emmanuel is from two natures, and nature is
a person according to them, he will therefore also be from two persons, which
do not admit the inhypostatic union. And thus according to their corrupted
account the dogma of godless division will find room, and Cyril will be cast
out, who long ago cried out such things against Nestorius: "It will in no way
profit the right account concerning the faith that it should be so, even if
some give the name of a union of persons." But neither does the truth demand
it thus, nor shall we endure it, but we shall walk the unswerving and middle
road, anathematizing those who introduce a duality of persons, yet confessing
the truth of the essences in Christ.

2. But perhaps from perplexity, trying to twist things, they have made such a
proposition. For when we say two natures united inhypostatically, they play
deaf, and scraping away from our confession the "united inhypostatically,"
they use against us a problem that is out of tune with our confession, and
they say: there is no nature without a person. What then does this clever
problem of theirs mean? If that it is not possible for natures to be
contemplated otherwise than in persons, everyone, even of those who know
little, would agree with them. For who does not know that the universal is
contemplated in particular persons, that is, in the characterizing hypostases,
as the nature of the Godhead in the Father, in the Son, in the Holy Spirit,
likewise the angelic nature in Michael, in Gabriel and the rest, and the
nature of humanity is recognized in particular persons? But if they wish to
establish this, that a particular person accompanies every nature, in the case
of natures, that is essences, not united according to hypostasis, I hold this
account also to be right; but in the case of those united in composition and
inhypostatically it stands far from the truth. For there are times when
different natures coming together according to the indivisible union produce
one person and one hypostasis, as the four elements are different essences,
yet they produce one body of this wood, say, or stone contemplated in
particularity, which is a hypostasis. But since the example seems somehow to
indicate mixture and confusion, we must take the example according to the man
who is in one hypostasis. For in each of us the essence of the soul is other
as toward the essence of the body, yet the two essences coming together in
particular individuals produce one person, say of John or Paul or someone
else, remaining two. For neither did the soul become flesh, nor did the flesh
become soul. And witness of this is Paul the herald of the truth, saying: "the
inner man and the outer," that he may signify the difference of the essences.
Following in his steps, John the bishop of Constantinople and teacher of the
whole Church writes such things in the discourse entitled On the obscurity of
the Old Testament: "For this animal, man, is double, composed from two
essences, the one sensible, the other intelligible, I mean soul and body, and
having kinship both in heaven and on earth; for through the intelligible
essence he communes with the powers above, and through the sensible he is
joined to the things on earth, being an exact bond of either creation." Thus
then in Christ there has come about a union of the two essences into one
hypostasis and one person, even if it has been shown dimly through the
examples. For the economy in Christ, as truly surpassing every union, escapes
human comprehension.

3. But again they linger in the same diseases, like a sow according to the
saying rolling in the mire. For they put forward the same things again in a
different way. For they say: There is no essence without hypostasis; how then
should it not be said in two hypostases, if indeed you say two essences? Do
you see the ignorance of these clever ones? For they have mindlessly
introduced the same proposition to us, again exchanging the names. For what is
the difference of essence and nature? And what of person toward the hypostasis
in character? Whence the same account will suffice for us also in the present.
For I would say to them: you seem to make even me, the man contemplated from
soul and body in characterizing properties, many hypostases and many men. For
you have not been born so foolish as to be ignorant that the soul is other
according to essence toward the body, and that the body holds another account
concerning essence. But if each essence according to you receives its own
characterizing hypostasis, the things of the union are empty for me and the
one has become two, that it may seem so to you and to your vain quibbles. But
if I, being composed from two different essences, am not two men, but both
have the two essences of soul and body and am contemplated in one hypostasis,
what necessity is there, when confessing the two essences also in the case of
Christ, to say two particular hypostases as well? For the divine things are
his by nature, and the human things also his through the inhypostatic union;
for not in another, but in him his own flesh subsisted, as has been said
before. For having the common of the human essence, that is the being flesh
ensouled with a rational soul, it had the particular things in God the Word
alone, that is the being flesh of God the Word and of no other. How then is
that another hypostasis which in no way subsisted by itself?

4. Besides, let them not be ignorant of this either, that hypostasis has often
been understood by the holy fathers in place of essence, as it also seems to
Cyril. For in the third anathema he says: "If anyone on the one Christ divides
the hypostases after the union." For here hypostasis signifies that which has
subsistence; for the God-bearing one, who always wars against the blasphemies
of Nestorius, would not have named hypostases on the one Christ, unless he
took them in place of essences.

5. Likewise also holy Athanasius in the letter to the Africans says: "But
hypostasis is essence and has no other meaning than the very being, which
Jeremiah names existence, saying: And they did not hear the voice of
existence. For hypostasis and essence are existence; for it is and exists."

6. If then someone according to this account should say that the essences are
inhypostatic, that is, existing, we too would not deny it. For hypostasis does
not differ from essence in being something, but in that the one is commonly, I
mean the essence, and the hypostasis particularly, whenever along with the
universals it also has something particular. We do not then say that our
essence in Christ is inhypostatic according to this, as being a characterizing
hypostasis by itself and a person, but inasmuch as it has subsistence and is.
For sometimes hypostasis signifies having subsistence, which is essence, as
has been demonstrated, whenever it is deprived of the characterizing
properties contemplated also concerning a person.

[From the apologies of Eulogius] against those who say that if you say two
essences, that is natures, united, then certainly one nature has come about;
for things that are united certainly become some one thing.

If they wish to establish the becoming some one thing according to the
characterizing hypostasis, they speak rightly and very piously and in harmony
with those who dogmatized at Chalcedon; for those also declare one hypostasis,
but two essences. The flesh remained flesh, even if it became flesh of God the
Word, that is, he assumed flesh and united it to himself indivisibly. For he
did not undergo change into flesh; for the nature of the Trinity exists as
unchangeable God. As I said, then, the two essences have remained unconfused
in Emmanuel, the one Christ the Son of God, after the incarnation. But if they
say that the two natures have become one essence, they have escaped their own
notice, deceiving themselves and supposing confusion or change, as if the one
had been changed into the other or confused. For that things united
unchangeably and unconfusedly are preserved in their own quantity, this I will
clearly set before you through the example of the three hypostases united
indivisibly and unconfusedly according to essence, I mean of the Father and of
the Son and of the Holy Spirit. For they have not become one hypostasis
because they have been united, but they are united according to essence, and
are recognized in three hypostases even while united. Thus also in the economy
of Christ two essences exist united indivisibly and unconfusedly, remaining
two essences, but producing one incarnate hypostasis of God the Word, in which
they also exist united. And let the example be set down only to exhibit the
unsound problem, since the account of the union in Christ "is the reverse of
that in the Trinity. For there it is another and another, that we may not
confuse the hypostases, but not another thing and another thing; for the three
are one and the same in the Godhead." But in Emmanuel Godhead is one thing,
humanity another, that we may not confuse the essences; for "the timeless is
not the same as that in time, nor the invisible as the visible. But not
another and another, God forbid"; for both by the union have produced one
person and one characterizing hypostasis.

VI. Let them not then give us trouble who wish to understand nothing sound,
but talk vainly at random and attempt to disturb the right and blameless
faith. For we worship one Christ, Son of God, who appeared in flesh to those
on earth. Yet we know a difference of the Godhead toward the flesh. For God
the Word is timeless, uncreated, impassible, and whatever befits the divine
majesty. But his flesh, being some one thing toward him through the extreme
and inhypostatic union, because of which also the divine John cries: "The Word
became flesh and dwelt among us," exists under time, created, passible. And we
confess that all these things of the flesh have become of God the Word. For if
the flesh is his own, it is clear that all the things of the flesh will also
be his; for he makes them his own, even if his divine nature was not subject
to these. For that all the things of his flesh after the incarnation belong to
God the Word, the divine Scripture teaches us. For it says: "We have seen the
Lord," speaking of both together; for he who has seen the flesh of Christ has
beheld the whole, even if the Godhead was by nature unseen. And again: "they
would not have crucified the Lord of glory," although he did not suffer in his
divine nature, but in his own flesh. We are taught then in all these things
that of God the Word incarnate there were both the things befitting God and
the human things; but in those in which it says that he hungered or thirsted
or was handled or was seen, I understand that he did not suffer these in the
divine nature, but in the assumed flesh, according to which he was called man.
