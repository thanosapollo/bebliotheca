Discourse to a younger woman who has been widowed
That you have received a hard blow, and that you took the dart sent from above
in a vital place, all would agree with you, and not even one of those who
philosophize intensely will contradict; but since those who are struck must
not spend all the time in mourning and tears, but must also take much care for
the healing of the wounds, so that they may not, being neglected, make the
blow greater by the tears and work a fiercer flame of the pain, it is good
both to endure the consolation that comes through words and, having held back
the fountains of tears for a little time at least, to give yourself also to
those who attempt to comfort you. For this very reason we too did not trouble
you at the very peak of the grief, nor at the same time as the thunderbolt was
brought down; but having waited this intervening time and having allowed you
to be filled with the lamentations, when at last you have been able both to
see a little out of that mist and to open your ears to those who try, then we
too, after the words of the maidservants, have added also those from
ourselves. For while the storm was still violent and much grief was blowing,
the one exhorting to abstain from sorrow would have provoked more toward the
wailings, and having gained only to be hated would have furnished much fuel to
the fire by such words, together with taking the reputation of an enemy and a
fool; but since now the squall has begun to cease and God has laid the
wildness of the waves, we shall easily spread the sails of the discourse. For
in a moderate storm perhaps the art will be able to do its own; but when the
assault of the winds is irresistible, there is no benefit from experience. For
all these reasons, having been silent the whole former time, we have scarcely
even now attempted to break voice, since we heard from your uncle that now we
must take courage; for indeed even the maidservants who are in honor dare to
stretch out long speeches about these things, and the women from outside, both
those related by birth and those otherwise somehow close.

That, receiving the words from those, you will not dishonor those from us, but
will provide them quiet and undisturbedness according to your power, we are
very confident and have believed. And otherwise the female race is somehow
more inclined toward sympathy; but when also youth is present, and untimely
widowhood, and inexperience of affairs, and a great crowd of cares, and having
been brought up the whole former time in luxury and cheerfulness and wealth,
the terrible things become many times greater, and if she who has suffered
this does not obtain the inclination from above, even a chance thought will be
able to overthrow her, which I set first and greatest as a proof of the much
care of God concerning you. For that, so many evils having suddenly run
together, you were not choked by grief nor driven out of your natural mind was
not of any human help, but of the hand that can do all things, of the
understanding of which there is no number, of the prudence of which there is
no finding out, of the Father of mercies and God of all consolation. "For he
has smitten us," he says, "and he will heal us; he will strike and he will
bind us up and he will make us well." For as long as that blessed man was with
you, you enjoyed honor and care and zeal, and you enjoyed such things as it
was likely to enjoy from a man; but since God has taken him to himself, he
himself has become to you in his place. And this is not my word, but that of
the blessed prophet David; for he says: "He will take up the orphan and the
widow." And again he calls him father of


orphans and judge of widows; and you will see him making much zeal in many
places on behalf of this race.

But that this name, being continually mentioned, may not turn your soul and
confuse the reasoning, having come upon you in the very flower of age, I wish
first to speak about this and to show that it is not a name of misfortune, but
of honor, the name of widowhood, and of the greatest honor. For do not bring
me the deceived opinion of the many as testimony, but the legislation of the
blessed Paul, or rather of Christ. For the things that one uttered, these
Christ spoke through him, as he himself also said: "Do you seek a proof of
Christ speaking in me?" What then does he say? "Let a widow be enrolled not
less than sixty years old"; and again: "But refuse younger widows"; wishing
through both to present to us the greatness of the matter. And when arranging
about bishops he nowhere sets a number of years, but here he makes much
exactness. Why then? Not because widowhood is greater than priesthood, but
because these have greater labor than those, many public and private affairs
flowing around them from many sides. For just as an unwalled city is set in
the midst for all who wish to plunder, so also a young woman living with
widowhood has many who plot from every side, not only those who desire her
money, but also those who hasten to corrupt her chastity. And not these only,
but we shall find also other occasions arising toward her fall. For servants
despising, and affairs neglected, and no longer being in the former honor, and
seeing her age-mates doing well, and often also the desire of luxury, have
persuaded them to associate with second marriages. And there are some who do
not wish to live with men by the law of marriage, but secretly and with
concealment. And they do this in order to reap the praises of widowhood; so
much does the matter seem among men not to be reproachful, but admirable and
worthy of honor, not among us the faithful only but even among the unbelievers
themselves. For I too, once when I was still young, know my sophist (and that
man was more superstitious than all men) marveling at my mother before many.
For asking of those sitting by him, as he was accustomed, whose son I was, and
someone saying that of a widow woman, he learned from me both the age of my
mother and the time of the widowhood. And when I said that being forty years
old she has now twenty since she lost my father, he was astonished and cried
out loudly, and looking toward those present: "Bah," he said, "what kind of
women there are among Christians." So much does the matter enjoy wonder and
praise not among us only but also among those outside. All which things the
blessed Paul having understood said: "Let a widow be enrolled not less than
sixty years old." And not even after so great a testimony of years does he
allow her to be ranked in this holy choir, but he adds also other things
saying: "Well reported of in good works, if she has brought up children, if
she has lodged strangers, if she has washed the feet of saints, if she has
relieved the afflicted, if she has followed every good work." Bah, of the
testing and the trial, how much virtue he demands from the widow and how he is
exact. He would not have done this if he were not about to entrust to her an
honorable and august matter. And: "But refuse younger widows," he says. Then
he adds also the cause: "For when they have grown wanton against Christ, they
wish to marry." For through this saying he hints to us that those who have
lost their husbands are joined to Christ in their place.

Then showing this joining to be gentle and reasonable, he sets this saying:
"For when they have grown wanton against Christ, they wish to marry," as of
some gentle husband and one who has not used authority over them, but who lets
them live in freedom. And he did not even stop the word at these things, but
also elsewhere again he has made much


care on her behalf saying: "But she who lives in luxury is dead while she
lives; but she who is a widow indeed and desolate has hoped in God, and
continues in supplications and prayers night and day." And writing to the
Corinthians he says: "But she is more blessed if she so remain." Did you see
how many are the praises of widowhood, and these in the New, when also the
beauty of virginity shone forth? But nevertheless not even then was the
brightness of this able to hide its rays, but it shines even so, having its
own virtue. When therefore we mention widowhood in between, do not fall, nor
think the matter a reproach; for if this is reproachful, much more is
virginity. But it is not, it is not, may it not be. For where we all marvel at
and accept those who abstain from husbands while they still live, those who
even when they have departed show the same goodwill toward them, how must we
not be astonished and praise? So then, as I said, while you were with the
blessed Therasius, you enjoyed both honor and providence such as it is likely
to enjoy from a good man; but now you have in his rank God the Master of all,
who even of old standing over you will do this more greatly and with more zeal
now; and of his much providence he has already shown us no small proof, as I
said before, having kept you sound and untouched in so great a furnace of
cares and grief, and having allowed you to suffer nothing of the unwanted. And
he who in so great a swell did not allow a shipwreck to happen will much more
in a calm keep your soul and will make the widowhood light and the things that
seem to be terrible from it. But if it is not the name of widowhood that
troubles you, but the having lost such a husband, I too agree with you that
everywhere on earth few such have been among men of worldly life,
affectionate, gentle, humble, unfeigned, intelligent, pious. But if he were
being dissolved entirely and ending into nothing, one ought to be troubled and
to grieve; but if he has sailed down into the fair-weather harbor and has
departed to his true king, one must not mourn for these things, but even
rejoice. For this death is not death, but a sojourning and a kind of transfer
from the worse to the better, from earth to heaven, from men to angels and
archangels and the Master of angels and archangels. For here on earth, serving
as a soldier to a king, it was to expect dangers and many plots from those who
envy; for as much as the things of his good repute increased, so much also
those from the enemies multiplied; but having gone there it is not possible to
suspect any of these. So that as much as you mourn that God took him being
good and virtuous, so much you ought to rejoice that he has departed with much
safety and glory, and having been released from the disturbance in the present
danger he is in much peace and quiet. For how is it not absurd to confess that
heaven is much better than earth, but to mourn those who have transferred from
this to that? For if that blessed man were of those who have lived shamefully
and contrary to what seems good to God, one ought to beat the breast and
lament, not only when he had gone but even while he lived; but since he too
happened to be one of his friends, one must rejoice with him not only living
but also having fallen asleep. You have certainly heard the blessed Paul
saying: "To depart and to be with Christ is far better." But perhaps you long
to hear his words and to enjoy friendship toward him, and you seek the
familiarity and the glory through him and the splendor and honor and safety,
and all these things having become gone trouble you and darken you.

The friendship toward him then it is possible for you to keep even now
similarly as also formerly. For such is the power of love; it embraces and
glues and binds not only those present and near us and seen, but also those
standing far off; and neither multitude of times, nor interval of roads, nor
anything else of such things could cut off a soul's friendship and


cleave it. But if you also wish to see him face to face (for this I know that
you especially long), keep the bed untouched by another man for him and hasten
to show a life equal to his, and you will certainly go away to the same place
with him, not five years as here, nor twenty and a hundred, nor a thousand and
twice so many, nor ten thousand and many times so many, but you will dwell
with him the infinite and endless ages. For the places of that rest it is not
the bodily kinships, but the equality of lives that is wont to inherit. For if
it brought Lazarus, being unknown to Abraham, into the same bosom with him,
and the unvarying of the way of life prepares many from east and west to
recline with that one, the place of ease will receive you also with the good
Therasius, if you are willing to show him the same life; then you will receive
him again not in this beauty of body, which he had when he went, but in some
other radiance and brightness shining more than the sun's rays. For this body,
even if it run in much height, is nevertheless corruptible; but the bodies of
those who have well pleased God will put on so great a glory as it is not even
possible to see with these eyes. And of these things God prepared some signs
and faint traces to be given to us both in the Old and in the New. For there
the face of Moses was then lit with so great a glory as to be unapproachable
to the eyes of the Israelites; but in the New the face of Christ shone forth
much more than this. For tell me, if someone promised to make him king of all
the earth, then commanded you to be apart twenty years' time for this, and
after these things promised to give him back to you with the diadem and the
purple and again to rank you in the same honor with him, if you bear the
separation mildly and with the fitting chastity? Would you not have welcomed
the gift and thought it a thing worthy of prayer? Bear this then also now, not
for a kingdom on the earth, but for that in the heavens, not that you may
receive him wearing a golden garment but that you may receive incorruption and
immortality and glory, such as it is likely those dwelling in the heavens to
have. But if you are very unbearably disposed toward the length of the time,
it is likely that he will also stand by you sometime through dreams, and
converse in the accustomed things, and show the longed-for face; let this be a
consolation to you instead of letters, or rather this is even clearer than
letters. For there it is possible to see letters only, but here both a form of
face and a gentle laugh and figure and gait, and to hear a sound, and to
recognize the dearest voice. And since you also lament over the safety which
you formerly possessed through him, and perhaps also because of the showing
greater hopes of good repute (for I used to hear that he would come
immediately to the throne of the prefects, which most of all, I think, twists
your soul and confuses it), consider those who were in greater dignity than he
and who ended life very pitiably.

And I will remind you: perhaps you hear of Theodore from Sicily; for he
happened to be of the very distinguished; this man, prevailing over all in
beauty and size of body and freedom of speech toward the king, and being able
as much as none of those turning inside, did not bear that prosperity mildly,
but having plotted against the king and been caught, he himself was cut off
very pitiably, and his wife, having nothing less than your nobility in
upbringing and birth and all the other things, suddenly stripped of all that
was hers and having fallen from freedom, was enrolled among the spinning-women
of the treasury, and was forced to live a life more pitiable than every
maidservant, having this much more than the others, that through the excess of
the misfortune she was wept over by all who saw her. And Artemisia too is
said, having become wife of a man of very good repute, when that one also
desired tyranny, to have come thus into this poverty and the maiming. Both


the greatness of the despondency and the multitude of the tears quenched her
eyes; and now she needs those who will lead her by the hand and those who will
take her to others' doors, that she may thus have plenty of the necessary
food. And I would have been able to speak of many other houses thus brought
down, if I did not know the piety and the intelligence of your soul, not
wishing to find consolation of her own misfortune from others' evils. And of
these of which I made mention I made mention now for no other reason, but that
you may learn that human affairs are nothing, but truly, as the prophet says:
"All the glory of man is as the flower of grass." For as much as it is lifted
up and shines, so much greater a fall it works, not only the things of those
ruled but also those of the very ones who reign. For one would not find a
private house filled with so many misfortunes as the palaces are full of
evils. For untimely orphanhoods and widowhoods and violent deaths and things
far more lawless and bitter than those in the tragedies especially strike down
upon this rule. And that we may leave aside the ancient things, of those who
have reigned in our generation, nine having come to be in all, two only ended
life by a common death; and of the others one by a tyrant, one in war, one
having been plotted against by those guarding him inside, and one by the very
one who appointed him and put the purple around him. And the women who dwelt
with these, some, as they say, died by poisons, and some by the despondency
itself.

And of those still living, one, having an orphan child, trembles and fears
lest some of those in power kill him from fear of the things to come. And
another scarcely, many having begged, returned from the exile into which the
one in power had formerly cast her. And of those dwelling with the present
kings, one, having breathed again from the former misfortunes, has much pain
mixed with the pleasure because the one in power is still very young and
inexperienced and has many who plot from many sides; and the other has died of
fear and lives more wretchedly than the condemned because her husband, from
when he bound on the diadem until today, spends time in wars and battles and
is more consumed by shame than by the misfortunes and by the reproaches from
all. For what never happened has happened now, and the barbarians, having left
their own land, have overrun countless stades of ours and many times so many,
and having burned the countries and taken the cities, they do not wish to turn
back home again, but as if dancing rather than making war they thus mock all
of ours; and they say that one of the kings among them said that he was
astonished at the shamelessness of our soldiers, who, being slaughtered more
than sheep, still expect to win and do not wish to leave their own fatherland;
for he himself said he had even taken his fill, often cutting them down.

What kind of soul then do you think both the king and his wife have, hearing
these words? And since I mentioned this war, a great swarm of widows ran upon
me, who many shone greatly from the honor of their husbands, but now all at
once, having put on mourning and black clothing, lament the whole time. For
not even this belonged to them which belonged to your honored head. For you, O
admirable one, both saw that good man lying upon the bed, and heard a last
voice, and learned what must be done concerning the affairs of the house as he
charged, and through the wills blocking every approach to the greedy and the
sycophants. And not this only, but you also often fell upon him still lying as
a corpse, and kissed eyes, and poured yourself around, and wailed, and saw him
sent forth with much honor, and you did all things toward the burial as was
fitting, and coming often to his tomb you have a consolation


of the pain not small. But these have been deprived of all these things, all
having sent their own into war as about to receive them again, but instead of
those having received the bitter message of their end. For not even did anyone
return to them bringing their bodies, but words only narrating the manner of
the end. And there are some who were not even thought worthy of this narration
nor were able to learn how they fell, they having been buried in the great
multitude of the clash. And what wonder if of the generals many thus died,
when even the king himself, having been left in some village with a few
soldiers, did not dare to go out and stand against those who came upon him,
but remaining inside, when those kindled fire, was burned together with all
those inside, not men only but also horses and walls and beams, and all became
one dust? And those who had gone to war with the king returned to the wife
bringing this message instead of the king. For nothing, nothing of the things
that happen on the stage and of the season of spring flowers have the
splendors of the world differed. For first, before they appear, they fly away;
then even if they endure to wait a little time, they immediately become easily
corruptible.

For what is more worthless than the honor and glory from the many? What fruit
does it have, what benefit? To what useful end does it arrive? And would that
this only were the terrible thing; but now, besides having nothing good to
gain from this, he who has this most harsh mistress is forced continually to
bear many painful and harmful things. For she is mistress of those who have
her, and as much as she is flattered by the slaves, so much more she is raised
against them and stretches them with harsher commands; but those who spit upon
her and overlook her she herself could no longer even avenge; so much is she
harsher than every tyrant and beast. For the tyrant and the beast are often
tamed through service, but she then especially rages when we obey her, and if
she finds one who will listen and yield in all things, there is nothing she
then declines to command. And she also has another ally whom one would not err
in addressing as her daughter. For when this too, having been nourished and
increased, is well rooted among us, then she bears madness, a thing not less
able than she to hurl down the soul of those who have it. Do you then lament
these things, tell me, that God has released you from so harsh a slavery,
that he has blocked every approach to these pestilential diseases? For while
your husband lived they had not yet ceased continually leaping upon the
reasonings of the soul, but when he died they have no whence to take hold of
your mind. This then must now be accomplished, not to lament their withdrawal
nor to seek the bitter mastery. For wherever these blow violently, they have
overturned and pulled down everything from the foundations; and just as many
of the women who prostitute, being ugly by nature and repulsive, by the paints
and the underlinings flutter the souls of the young still being tender, but
when they take them under their own rule, they use them more insolently than
every slave; so also these passions, both vainglory and madness, pollute the
souls of men more than every pollution. For this reason also wealth has seemed
to the many to be a good; at any rate, stripped of this it will not even
itself be desirable any longer. At any rate those who were able through
poverty to hit upon the glory from the many no longer chose to be rich, but
spat upon much gold given. And these you will not need to learn from me, but
you yourself know more accurately than we Epaminondas, Socrates, Aristides,
Diogenes, Crates, the one who let his own land be sheep-pasture. For the
others, from whom it was not possible to become rich, saw the glory coming to
them through poverty, and they came to this immediately; but this one even
what he had


he threw away; so all were mad toward the acquisition of this harsh beast. Let
us not then weep that God has released us from this tyranny that is shameful
and ridiculous and has many reproaches; for it has a splendid name only, but
in the deeds it disposes those who have it contrary to the appellation, and
there is no one who does not mock the one who does anything toward glory. For
that one alone will be able to become conspicuous and renowned who does not
look toward this; but he who sets as great glory that from the many and who
endures and does all things in order to hit upon this, this one most of all
will miss it and fall away and will enjoy all the contraries, laughter and
accusation, jeers and enmity and hatred. And this is accustomed to happen not
upon men only, but also upon you women, and especially upon you. For the
unfeigned one both in figure and in gait and in garments and who seeks honor
from no one all marvel at and are astonished at and bless and pray together
with her all the good things; but the vainglorious they turn from and hate,
and they flee as some wild beast and they strike with countless curses and
revilings.

And not these evils only do we escape from not admitting the glory from the
many, but we shall also gain the greatest of all in addition to what has been
said, being trained little by little to go up and fly toward heaven and to
despise all things upon the earth. For he who does not need the honor from
men, whatever good things he does, he will do all these with safety, and
neither upon the painful nor upon the good things of this life will he suffer
anything terrible. For neither can these plunge and pull him down, nor those
swell and puff him up, but in things easily falling and disturbed he himself
remains being outside all change; which I expect will quickly be also upon
your soul, and having been suddenly torn from all the worldly things, you will
show us the citizenship in the heavens, and a little later you will mock the
glory which you now lament, and you will look through to its empty and vain
mask. But if you long for the safety that has come to you through him and the
guarding of the affairs, and being plotted against by none of those who leap
upon others' misfortunes, "Cast your care upon the Lord, and he himself will
nourish you." "For look," he says, "at the ancient generations and see who
hoped in the Lord and was put to shame, or who called upon him and he
overlooked him, or who remained in his commandments and he abandoned him." For
he who carried through the misfortune so unbearable and made it to be in a
calm now, this one will also beat off the things that come; for that you will
receive no other harsher than this blow henceforth, you yourself also would
agree with us. She then who so nobly bore the things at hand even being
inexperienced, much more will easily bear the things that come, if ever, which
may it not be, some of the unwanted happen to us. For seek heaven and all
things that bear toward the life there, and nothing of the things here will be
able to harm you, not even the world-ruler of darkness himself, only let us
not harm ourselves. For even if someone take away the substance, even if he
cut the body, none of these is toward us while the soul remains sound for us.
And if you wish at all that the money also remain for you in safety and
increase again, I will show you both the manner and the place, where it is not
lawful for any of those who plot to enter. What then is the place? Heaven.
Send it to that good man, and neither thief, nor plotter, nor anything else of
the things that ruin them will be able to leap upon them. If you bury these
moneys there, you will find much fruit from them. For all things planted by us
in the heavens have both more and better the yield and such as it is likely of
them being rooted in the heavens. And if you do this, see of what good things
you will enjoy: first


of the eternal life and of the things promised to those who love God which
neither eye saw, nor ear heard, nor went up upon a man's heart, then of
dwelling with this good man the whole age, and here too you will release
yourself from cares and fears and dangers and plots and enmity and hatred.

For as long as you have them lying about you, perhaps there will be some who
will set upon them; but if you transfer them to heaven, you will live a life
without danger and safe and full of much quiet, enjoying self-sufficiency with
piety. For it is of much unreason, when wishing to buy a field, to seek the
well-bearing land, but when heaven instead of earth is set forth, it being
possible to acquire a plot there, to remain upon the earth and to endure the
labors from this and those through sins; for often it deceives us of the hope.
And since the having expected him often to come to the dignity of the
prefects, and his having been snatched away before the office, terribly turns
your soul and troubles it, first consider that even if this hope was very
clear, nevertheless it was a hope of men, often falling through; and we see
many such things happening in life, and of the things very much expected
remaining unfinished, and the things that did not even come into our mind have
often come out to an end, and this we always see happening both in offices,
and in kingdoms, and in inheritances, and in marriages, and everywhere. So
that even if the time was very near, yet many things, according to the
proverb, between cup and extreme lip. And the Scripture also says: "From
morning until evening time changes." So also: "The king who is today tomorrow
dies." And again showing the unexpected to us this same wise one says: "Many
tyrants have sat upon the ground, and the unsuspected one has worn a diadem."
And it was not entirely clear that having lived he would come to that office;
for the unclearness of the future also furnished us to suspect other things.
For whence was it clear that having lived he would come to that office? that
the contraries did not also come out, and he fall from the existing honor,
having been given over to disease, and having been struck by envy and jealousy
of those wishing to outdo him, or even having suffered some other hard thing?
But let us set, if you wish, even that it was clearly evident that having
survived he was altogether about to hit upon this summit; for as much as the
dignity is greater, so much also it was necessary to have greater the dangers
and cares and plots. And let not even these be, but let him sail through that
sea without danger and with much quiet; and what is the end, tell me? not this
which is now, or rather not this, but perhaps another unpleasant and to be
prayed against.

First the seeing heaven and the things in the heavens more slowly, which is no
small damage to those who have believed concerning the things to come; then
even if he lived very purely, nevertheless the length of the life and the
necessity of that office would not have allowed him to go away so pure as now.
And it is unclear whether also having received many changes and having
declined toward slackness he turned away at last. For now we are confident
that by the grace of God he has flown away to the place of rest, because
nothing of the things that shut out from the kingdom of the heavens has been
dared by him; but then, having spent much time in public affairs, perhaps he
would also have rubbed on much filth. For to succeed while turning in the
midst of so many evils is of the very rare, but to sin both unwilling and
willing is of the likely and always happening. But now we have been released
from this fear and we are very persuaded that on that day he will come with
much brightness, shining near the king and going before Christ with angels,
and having been clothed with the unspeakable glory, and standing by the king
as he judges, and ministering the first things to him. Therefore having left
the laments and the wailings hold to the same way of life, or rather even a
more exact, that having arrived at the equal virtue with him, you may dwell
the same tent with him and be able to be joined again to him into those


immortal ages, not this joining of marriage, but another much better. For this
is an intertwining of bodies only; but then there will be a more exact union
of soul toward soul and much sweeter and better.
