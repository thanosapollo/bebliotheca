On the saying, "Greet Priscilla and Aquila," and what follows, discourse 1.
=51.187=

αʹ. I think many of you wonder at the passage of this apostolic reading, or
rather consider this part of the letter a side-matter and superfluous, because
it has only greetings, continuous and one after another. Wherefore I myself,
though set today toward another subject, having left that theme, am preparing
to come down to this one, that you may learn that of the divine Scriptures
nothing is superfluous, nothing a side-matter, even if it be one iota or one
tittle, but even a bare greeting opens to us a great sea of thoughts. And why
do I say a bare greeting? Often even the addition of one letter has introduced
a whole power of thoughts. And this can be seen in the name of Abraham. For
how is it not absurd, when receiving a letter from a friend, not to read the
body of the letter only, but also the greeting lying below, and from there
especially to guess the disposition of the writer, yet when Paul writes, or
rather not Paul, but the grace of the Spirit dictating the letter to a whole
city and so great a people, and through them to the whole world, to think that
something of what is set down is superfluous, and to run past it simply, and
not to consider that these things have turned all things upside down? For this
is it, this very thing that has filled us with much sloth: that we do not go
through all the Scriptures, but picking out what we think is clearer, we make
no account of the rest. This also has introduced the heresies: the
unwillingness to go through the whole body, the thinking that something is
superfluous and a side-matter. Therefore all other things have been pursued by
us with zeal, not the superfluous only, but even the useless and harmful; but
experience of the Scriptures has been neglected and overlooked; and those who
are excited toward the spectacle =51.188= of the horse-race can tell with all
exactness names, and stable, and breed, and country, and training of the
horses, and years of life, and energy of courses, and who paired with whom
will snatch the victory, and which horse, let out from which starting-post,
and having which charioteer, will prevail in the race and outrun his rival.
And those who have given their leisure to the orchestra display not less than
these, but even more madness about those who behave shamefully in the
theaters, I mean mimes and dancing-girls, recounting their family, and
country, and training, and all the rest; but we, when asked how many and which
are Paul's letters, do not even know how to tell the number. And if some
should even know the number, yet when asked the cities that received the
letters, they are at a loss at the question. And a man, a eunuch and a
barbarian, dragged by a myriad cares from a myriad affairs, was so given to
books that not even at the time of travel did he rest, but sitting on his
chariot he applied himself with much exactness to the reading of the divine
Scriptures; but we, not having even the smallest part of his occupation, are
strangers even to the names of the letters, and this though we gather here
every Lord's day and enjoy a divine hearing. But lest we spend the word on
rebuke only, come, let us bring into the midst the greeting itself that seems
to be superfluous and to be a nuisance. For when it is unfolded and the profit
is shown, how great it furnishes to those who attend to it with exactness,
then the accusation will be greater



against those who neglect such =51.189= treasures, and who throw the spiritual
wealth from their hands. What then is the greeting? "Greet," he says,
"Priscilla and Aquila, my fellow-workers in the Lord." Does it not seem to be
some bare greeting, and to show us nothing great, nothing noble? Come then,
let us spend the whole discourse on this alone, or rather we shall not even
suffice today, though we strive to draw up for you all the thoughts laid in
these few words, but the abundance of the contemplations born from this little
greeting must be stored up for you unto another day as well. For I am not
preparing to go through the whole of it, but a part of it, and the beginning
and the preface only: "Greet Priscilla and Aquila."

βʹ. First one must be struck with amazement at Paul's virtue, that having the
whole world entrusted to him, both land and sea, and all the cities under the
sun, both barbarians and Greeks, and carrying about so many peoples in
himself, he took such thought for one man and one woman; and second, this must
be wondered at, how he had a soul both sleepless and full of care, remembering
not only all in common, but also each of the approved and noble privately. For
now it is no wonder that those who preside over the Churches do this, because
those tumults have been stilled, and they have taken up the care of one city
only; but then not the greatness of the dangers only, but also the distance of
the road, and the multitude of cares, and the successive waves, and the not
always visiting all continually, and many other things more than these, were
enough to cast out of memory even those who were exceedingly close. But he did
not cast these out. How then were they not cast out? Through Paul's greatness
of soul, and his warm and genuine love. For he so had them in mind that he
remembered them often even in letters. But let us see who and of what sort
these were, who so won Paul over and drew him to their own affection. Were
they some consuls and generals, and prefects, or possessed of some other
distinction, or clothed in much wealth, and among those who lead the city?
None of these can be said, but the very contrary, poor and needy, and living
from the work of their hands. For they were, he says, tentmakers by trade; and
Paul was not ashamed, nor did he think it a reproach, in a most royal city and
a people of great pride, to bid them greet those artisans, nor did he think he
insulted them by friendship toward those people; so had he trained all then to
philosophize. And yet we, often having kinsmen a little poorer, alienate
ourselves from kinship with them, and think it a reproach if we should ever be
detected as belonging to them; but Paul was not so, but even glories in the
matter, and made it plain not to those then only, but also to all after these
things, that those tentmakers held the first rank in friendship with him.
=51.190= And let no one say to me, "And what is great and wonderful, if he
himself, being of the same trade, is not ashamed of his fellow-craftsmen?"
What do you say? This very thing is greatest and wonderful. For those who can
tell of distinction of ancestors are not so ashamed of their inferiors as
those who were once in the same cheapness, then suddenly rose to some splendor
and distinction. And that nothing was more splendid than Paul, nor more
distinguished, but that he was more notable even than the kings themselves, is
surely plain to everyone. For he who commanded demons, and raised the dead,
and could both maim by command and heal those maimed, whose garments and whose
shadows loosed every kind of disease, it is quite clear that he was no longer
even thought to be a man, but some angel come down from heaven. But
nevertheless, enjoying so great glory, and everywhere



admired, and turning all wherever he appeared, he was not ashamed of the
tentmaker, nor did he think those in such dignities were made less. For it is
likely that in the Church of the Romans there were many distinguished people,
whom he compelled to greet those poor folk. For he knew, he knew clearly, that
it is not distinction of wealth, nor abundance of money, but reasonableness of
manners that is wont to make nobility; so that those deprived of this, yet
puffed up from the glory of those who begot them, deck themselves with a bare
name of nobility only, but not with the thing; or rather the name itself is
often detected, if one should go up to the higher ancestors of these nobles.
For the distinguished and splendid man, who can tell of a notable father and
grandfather, if you examine with exactness, you will often find to have had
some cheap and nameless great-grandfather; just as, if of those who seem cheap
we go up through the whole family little by little and search it out, we shall
often find their higher ancestors prefects and generals, and one would find
them to have become horse-herds and swineherds. All which things Paul knowing,
he made no great account of these, but sought nobility of soul, and trained
the others to admire this. So we reap no small fruit from this already: not to
be ashamed of any of the cheaper people, to seek virtue of soul, to think all
the things laid around us from without superfluous and useless.

γʹ. There is also another gain, not less than this, to be reaped from here,
and one which especially holds our life together when it is set right. And
what is this? Not to accuse marriage, nor to think it a hindrance and a block
on the road that leads to virtue, to have a wife, and to rear children, and to
preside over a house, and to practice a trade. See, here too there was a man
and a woman, and they presided over workshops, and they practiced a trade, and
they displayed a philosophy far more exact than those who live in monasteries.
Whence is this clear? From the titles with which Paul addressed them, or
rather not from the titles with which he addressed them, but also =51.191=
from the witness he bore after these things. For having said, "Greet Priscilla
and Aquila," he added also their dignity. What dignity then is this? He did
not say the rich, the distinguished, the well-born; but what? "My
fellow-workers in the Lord." And nothing equal to this could come to be in the
account of virtue; and not from here only, but also from his remaining with
them, not one day, two and three, but two whole years, one can see their
virtue. For just as the rulers of those outside would never choose to lodge
with cheap and lowly people, but seek splendid houses of certain notable men,
so that the cheapness of those who receive them may not spoil the greatness of
their dignity; so the apostles also did; they did not lodge with chance
people, but as those sought splendor of houses, so these sought virtue of
soul, and searching out with exactness those fit for them, they lodged with
those. For a law too from Christ lay commanding this. "Into whatever city or
house you enter," he says, "inquire who in it is worthy, and there remain." So
these were worthy of Paul; and if worthy of Paul, they were worthy of the
angels. I would boldly call that chamber both heaven and Church. For where
Paul was, there Christ also was. "Do you seek a proof of Christ speaking in
me?" he says. And where Christ was, there angels also continually resorted.
And those who even before this had shown themselves worthy of Paul's
attendance, consider what they became, dwelling with him two years, and
observing figure, and gait, and glance, and manner of dress, and goings in and
goings out, and all the rest. For in the case of the saints it is not



these words only, nor the teachings and the exhortations, but also all the
rest of the conduct of life that would suffice, for those who attend, as a
teaching of philosophy. Consider how great it was to see Paul both taking his
meal, and rebuking, and exhorting, and praying, and weeping, going out and
coming in. For if, having fourteen letters only, we carry them about
everywhere in the world, those who had the fountain of the letters, who had
the tongue of the world, who had the light of the Churches, who had the
foundation of the faith, who had the pillar and ground of the truth, what
would they not have become, living with such an angel? For if his garments
were fearful to demons, and had so great strength, how much grace of the
Spirit would his dwelling together not have drawn? For to see Paul's bed, to
see the mattress, to see the sandals, would it not have sufficed them as a
ground of continual compunction? For if the demons shuddered seeing his
garments, much more did the faithful who lived with him feel compunction
looking at them. And it is worth examining that too, why, when greeting, he
has set Priscilla before the husband. For he did not say, "Greet Aquila and
Priscilla," but "Priscilla and Aquila." Nor did he do this simply, but it
seems to me he was conscious of more piety in her than in the husband. And
that what has been said is not a guess, it is possible to learn this also from
the =51.192= Acts. For Apollos, a man eloquent and mighty in the Scriptures,
and knowing only the baptism of John, she took and instructed in the way of
God, and made him a finished teacher. For the women in the time of the
apostles did not care for these things which the women now do, how they might
put on splendid garments, and beautify their own face with paints and
underlinings, and strangling their own husbands, forcing them to buy a robe
more costly than the neighbor's and the equal in rank, and white mules, and
gold-spangled bridles, and a retinue of eunuchs, and a great swarm of
maidservants, and all the rest of the ridiculous display; but having shaken
off all these things, and having cast away worldly pride, they sought one
thing only, how they might become partners of the apostles, and share the same
hunt with them. Therefore not she only was such, but also all the rest. For
concerning a certain Persis he says, "Who labored much among us," and he
admires Mary and Tryphaena from these toils, that they labored with the
apostles and stripped for the same contests. And how, writing to Timothy, does
he say, "But I do not permit a woman to teach, nor to have authority over the
man"? When the man too is pious, and possessed of the same faith, and sharing
the same wisdom; but when he is unbelieving and led astray, he does not
deprive her of the authority of teaching. At any rate, writing to the
Corinthians he says: "And a woman who has an unbelieving husband, let her not
leave him. For what do you know, wife, whether you will save the husband?" And
how would the believing wife have saved the unbelieving husband? By
instructing him, clearly, and teaching and leading him toward the faith, just
as this Priscilla also did Apollos. And otherwise, when he says, "I do not
permit a woman to teach," he speaks of teaching on the bema, of discourse in
public, and of that according to the word of priesthood; but he did not forbid
to exhort and advise privately. For he would not, if it had been forbidden,
have praised this woman for doing this.

δʹ. Let men hear, let women also hear these things; the latter, that they may
imitate their fellow and kinswoman; the former, that they may not appear
weaker than a woman. For what defense shall we have, what pardon, when women
display such eagerness and such philosophy, and we are continually bound to
the affairs of the world?



Let rulers also learn these things, and those ruled, and priests, and those
who have the rank of the laity, that the former may not admire the rich, nor
chase distinguished houses, but seek virtue with poverty, and not be ashamed
of the poorer of the brethren, nor, running past the tentmaker, and the
tanner, and the seller of purple, and the coppersmith, pay court to those in
powers; and those ruled, that they may not think there is a hindrance to the
receiving of the saints, but considering =51.193= the widow, who received
Elijah, having only a handful of meal, and these who hosted Paul for two
years, may open their houses to those in need, and possess all things in
common with the strangers. For do not say this to me, that you have no
servants waiting. For even if you have a myriad, God commands you to gather
the fruit of hospitality through yourself. Therefore Paul, discoursing with a
widow woman, and bidding her lodge strangers, ordered this not through others,
but through herself. For having said, "If she has lodged strangers," he added,
"If she has washed the feet of saints." He did not say, if she spent money,
nor, if she commanded the servants to do this, but, if she worked this through
herself. Therefore Abraham also, having three hundred and eighteen home-born
servants, himself ran to the herd, and carried the calf, and served all the
rest, and made his wife a partner of the fruits of hospitality. Therefore our
Lord Jesus Christ also is born in a manger, and being born is nursed in a
house, and having grown up had nowhere to lay his head, that he may train you
through all things not to gape at the splendid affairs of this life, but to be
a lover of cheapness everywhere, and to pursue poverty, and to flee abundance,
and to be beautified within. "For all the glory of the king's daughter," he
says, "is within." If you have a hospitable purpose, you have the whole
treasury of hospitality, even if you possess only an obol; but if you are a
hater of men and a hater of strangers, even if you are clothed with all
affairs, your house is straitened for the strangers. She did not have
silver-bound beds, but she had much self-control; she did not have a mattress,
but she possessed a gentle and hospitable purpose; she did not have flashing
columns, but she had a beauty of soul shining out; she did not have walls
clothed in marbles, nor a floor flowered with mosaics, but she herself was a
temple of the Spirit. These things Paul praised, these he loved; for these,
remaining two years, he did not rise up from the house; for these he remembers
them continually, and composes for them a great and wonderful encomium, not
that he may make them more splendid, but that he may lead the rest to the same
zeal, and persuade them to count blessed not the rich, nor those in offices,
but the hospitable, the merciful, the lovers of men, those who display much
kindness toward the saints.

εʹ. These things then, having learned them also from this greeting, let us
display through the works themselves; and let us neither simply count the rich
blessed, nor make cheap of the poor, nor be ashamed of trades, nor think work
a reproach, but idleness, and not having anything to do. For if it were a
reproach to work, Paul would not have practiced it, he would not have thought
more highly on it, speaking thus: "For if I preach the gospel, I have no
glorying. What then is my reward? That, preaching the gospel, I may make the
Gospel of Christ without charge." And if a trade were a reproach, he would not
have commanded those who do not work not even to eat. For sin only is a
reproach; and idleness is wont to beget this, and not one =51.194= and two and
three only, but all wickedness together. Therefore a certain wise man also,
showing that idleness taught all vice, and discoursing about servants, says:
"Set him to work, that he may not be idle." For what



the bit is to the horse, this work is to our nature. If idleness were a good,
the earth would have sprouted all things unsown and untilled; but it works no
such thing. Formerly then God commanded all things to come forth untilled; but
now he has not done so, but he has also legislated for men to yoke oxen, and
to draw the plow, and to cut a furrow, and to cast down seeds, and to tend in
many other ways both vine and trees and seeds, that the occupation of work may
lead the mind of those who work away from all wickedness. In the beginning
then, that he might display his own power, he arranged that all things be
yielded without our toils; "Let the earth bring forth grass of herb," he says;
and all things were at once in bloom; but after these things not so; but he
commanded them to be brought forth from the earth even through our toils, that
you may learn that he introduced the toil for what is useful and profitable to
us. And it seems to be punishment and vengeance to hear, "In the sweat of your
face you shall eat your bread"; but the truth is, it is a certain admonition
and chastening, and a medicine of the wounds that came from sin. For these
reasons Paul also worked continually, not in the day only, but even in the
night itself; and this he cries, saying: "For working night and day, that we
might not be a burden to any of you." And he did not simply practice the work
for amusement and diversion, as many of the brethren do, but he displayed such
toil about it that he was able to help others also. "For these hands," he
says, "ministered to my needs and to those who were with me." A man commanding
demons, being teacher of the world, entrusted with all who dwell on the earth,
and all the Churches lying under the sun, and tending peoples and nations and
cities with much care, worked night and day, and did not even breathe a little
from those toils. But we, not practicing even the ten-thousandth of his care,
or rather not even able to take it into mind, continue living in idleness
continually. And what defense shall we have, or what pardon, tell me? From
this all the evils have been brought into life, that many think it a greatest
dignity not to practice their own trades, and a last accusation to be seen
knowing any such thing. And Paul was not ashamed, at once handling a knife and
sewing hides and discoursing with those in dignities, but he even gloried in
the matter, when a myriad splendid and notable men were coming to him. And not
only was he not ashamed doing these things, but as on a bronze pillar he
paraded his own occupation in the letters. What then he learned from the
beginning, this he practiced after these things also, and then after being
caught up to the third heaven, after being carried into paradise, after
sharing unspeakable =51.195= words with God; but we, not even being worthy of
his sandals, are ashamed of these things in which he exulted; and sinning
every day, we do not even turn, nor think it a reproach; but living from just
toils we flee as shameful and ridiculous. What hope of salvation then shall we
have, tell me? For the one who is ashamed ought to be ashamed of sin, and of
striking against God, and of doing something of the things that are not due,
but even to exult in trades and works. For thus we shall both easily cast out
an evil thought from the mind by the occupation of work, and we shall help
those in need, and we shall not trouble the doors of others, and we shall
fulfill the law of Christ who said: "It is more blessed to give than to
receive." For this is why we have hands, that we may help ourselves, and may
contribute from our own, according to our power, all things to those maimed in
body; so that if anyone continues idle, even if



he is in health, he is more wretched than those with fever; for the latter
have pardon from the weakness, and would meet with mercy; but these, putting
to shame the good condition of the body, would reasonably be hated by all, as
both transgressing the laws of God, and spoiling the table of the sick, and
making their own soul worse. For this is not the only terrible thing, that
though they ought to be fed from home and from themselves, they trouble the
=51.196= houses of others, but that they themselves also become worse than
all. For there is not, there is not anything of all things that is not
destroyed through idleness. For water too, that which stands, rots, but that
which runs and wanders everywhere preserves its own virtue; and iron, that
which lies in idleness, becomes both softer and worse, consumed by much rust;
but that which is in works, far more useful and more comely, gleaming no less
than any silver. And earth lying idle one would see bringing forth nothing
sound, but evil herbs and thorns and thistles and unfruitful trees, but that
which enjoys work, blooming with cultivated fruits. And each of the things
that are, to speak simply, is corrupted by idleness, and becomes more useful
by its own work. Knowing all these things then, both how great the harm from
idleness, and how great the gain from work, let us flee the one, and pursue
the other, that we may both live the present life decently, and help those in
need from what we have, and having made our own soul better may obtain the
eternal good things; which may we all attain, by the grace and love of man of
our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory and the might, together with the
Father and the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

On Aquila and Priscilla And on not speaking evil of the priests of God.
Discourse 2.

αʹ. Have you been trained to think that nothing of the things laid in the
divine Scripture is a side-matter? Have you learned to search out even titles
and names and bare greetings written in the divine Oracles? For I think that
none of the industrious any longer will bear to run past any of the words laid
in the Scriptures, even if it be a catalogue of names, even if a number of
times, even if a bare greeting to certain people. Yet that this correction may
become more secure, come, today also let us go through what remains of the
address to Priscilla and Aquila; and yet the preface of it has already helped
us not a little. For it taught us how great a good work is, and how great an
evil idleness, and what Paul's soul was, how sleepless and full of care,
making much providence not by cities and peoples and nations, but even for
each one of the faithful. It showed how poverty becomes no hindrance to
hospitality, and that not of wealth and money, but of virtue everywhere there
is need, and of a purpose that has piety, and that those who have the fear of
God are more splendid than all, even if they be brought down to last poverty.
Priscilla then and Aquila, being tentmakers and artisans, and living in
poverty, we were now counting more blessed than all the kings; and those in
dignities and powers have been silenced, but the tentmaker with =51.196=.30
his wife is sung everywhere in the world. And if here they enjoy so great
glory, consider how great a share of the recompenses and the crowns they will
be thought worthy of on that day; and before that day they



reaped even now no small pleasure and profit and glory, having lived with Paul
so long a time. For what I said before, this I say also now, and I will not
cease saying, that not the teaching only, nor the exhortation and counsel, but
even this very sight of the saints had much pleasure and profit, and even the
very array of the garments, and even the very manner of the sandals. For from
here a great profit has been brought into our life, to learn up to what point
they used the necessaries. For they not only did not overpass the measure of
need, but there are places where they did not even enjoy the need itself
entire; but they continued even in hunger and thirst and nakedness. And Paul,
commanding the disciples, said: "Having food and coverings, with these we
shall be content"; but about himself he appears saying that "Until this
present hour we both hunger, and thirst, and are naked, and are buffeted." But
the things I was saying in the midst, and which came in after these, it is
necessary to set forth into the midst, having much inquiry. And what is this?
I was saying that even =51.197= the array of the apostolic garments furnishes
us much profit; but in the midst, as I was saying these things, the law of
Christ came in, which he set for them, speaking thus: "Do not acquire gold,
nor silver, nor bronze into your belts, nor sandals, nor a staff for the
road"; yet Peter appears having sandals. When at any rate the angel woke him
sleeping, and led him out of the prison, he says: "Bind on your sandals, and
put on your garment, and follow me." And Paul too, writing to Timothy, says:
"The cloak which I left at Troas with Carpus, bring when you come, and the
books, especially the parchments." What do you say? Christ commanded not even
to have sandals, and you have a cloak, and another has sandals again? And if
they were some of the cheap people, and of those who did not always obey the
Teacher, what is said would be no inquiry; but since these are the ones who
even give their own souls, and the chiefs and first of the disciples, and they
obeyed Christ in all things, and Paul not only did the things commanded, but
even leaped beyond the scratch-lines, and when that one commanded him to live
from the Gospel, this one lived from his own hands, having done something more
than the things commanded, it is truly worth inquiring why, obeying him in all
things, they seem here to transgress his law. But they do not transgress. For
this word will be useful to us not only for this, for the inquiry on behalf of
those saints, but also for stopping the mouths of the Greeks. For many,
overturning widows' houses, stripping orphans, clothing themselves with the
goods of all, disposed no better than wolves, living from others' toils, often
seeing some of the faithful, through weakness of body, clothed with more
garments, immediately throw forward to us the law of Christ, and say these
words: "Did not Christ command you not to have two tunics, nor sandals? How
then do you transgress the law laid down about these things?" Then, having
laughed abundantly and guffawed, and put the brother to shame, they leap away.
That these things then may not happen, come, let us also bridle their
shamelessness. It would have been possible to be rid of them by saying this
only to them. And what is this? That if you think Christ to be someone
trustworthy, reasonably you throw these things forward, and you seek against
us; but if you disbelieve him, why do you throw forward the legislations? But
when you wish to accuse us, Christ seems to you to be a trustworthy lawgiver;
but when you must worship him and admire him, you have no longer any account
of the common Master of the world.

βʹ. Yet lest they think we say this through want of a defense, let us go at
last to the very solution of the things inquired. What then will the solution
be? If we see



to whom, and when, and why Christ commanded these things. For one must not
simply examine the things said by themselves, but person, and time, and cause,
and all these things must be searched out with exactness. For we shall find,
looking exactly, that these things were not commanded to all, but to the
apostles only, and to those not until the end, but until a certain defined
time. =51.198= Whence is this clear? From the things said themselves; for
having called the twelve disciples, he said to them: "Do not go into a way of
the nations, and do not enter into a city of Samaritans; but go rather to the
lost sheep of the house of Israel; heal the sick, cleanse lepers, cast out
demons; you received freely; give freely; do not acquire gold, nor silver, nor
bronze into your belts." See the Teacher's wisdom, how he made the command
light. For having first said, "Heal the sick, cleanse lepers, cast out
demons," and having given them the grace from himself abundantly, then he
commanded these things, making that poverty easy and light by the abundance of
the signs. And not from here only is it clear that these things were commanded
to them only, but also from many others. For he punished those virgins for
this reason, because they did not have oil in their lamps; and he accuses
others, that they saw him hungry, and did not feed him, thirsty, and did not
give him drink. How then was it possible for one who had no bronze, nor
sandals, but one garment only, to feed another, how to clothe the naked, how
to bring the homeless into the house? And apart from these things, this very
thing will be clear and manifest from elsewhere also. For when someone came
and said, "Teacher, what having done shall I inherit eternal life?" when he
had listed all the things of the law, and the other, searching further, said:
"All these I have kept from my youth; what do I still lack?" he says to him:
"If you wish to be perfect, go, sell your possessions and give to the poor,
and come, follow me." And yet if it were a law and a command, he ought to have
said this first from the beginning, and to have legislated, and to have set it
in the rank of a command, but not to have introduced it in counsel and
exhortation. For when he says, "Do not acquire gold, nor silver," he speaks
commanding; but when he says, "If you wish to be perfect," he speaks
counseling and exhorting. And it is not the same to counsel and to legislate.
For the one who legislates wishes the thing commanded to be done in every way;
but the one who counsels and exhorts and leaves to the judgment of the hearer
the choice of the things said, makes the hearer master of receiving and not.
For this reason then he did not simply say, "Go, sell your possessions," lest
you think what is said to be a law, but how? "If you wish to be perfect, go,
sell your possessions"; that you may learn that the matter lies in the
judgment of the hearers. That these things then were commanded to the apostles
only, is clear from here; but we have not yet found the solution. For even if
this has been legislated for them only, why, having been commanded not even to
have sandals, nor a double garment, are they found, the one possessing
sandals, the other even having a cloak? What then would we say to these
things? That he did not leave even them to be under this necessity of the laws
until the end, but when he was about to go to the saving death, he released
them from this legislation. Whence is this clear? From the Savior's own words.
For when he was about to journey to the passion, having called them he says:
"When I sent you without purse and wallet, did you lack anything?" And they
answering said: "Nothing." And he said to them: "But now let him who has a
purse take it, and a wallet. =51.199=; and he who has not, let him sell his
garment, and he will buy a sword." But perhaps someone might say that By the
things said you have freed the apostles from the charges; but what is inquired
is to say why Christ



legislated contrary things, saying at one time, "Do not acquire a wallet," and
at another time saying, "Let him who has a purse take it, and a wallet." Why
then has he done this? Worthily of his wisdom and of his providence for the
disciples. For at the beginning he commanded these things, that they might
receive the proof of his power by deed and experience, and having received it
might thereafter take courage to go out into the whole world. But when they
had now sufficiently known his power, he wished them also to display their own
virtue from themselves, and not to be carried through until the end, but to
yield in many places and to concede, and for them to endure trials, that they
might not remain idle through to the end. And just as those who teach
swimming, at the beginnings, putting under their own hands, carry their own
disciples with much exactness, but after the first and second and third day,
drawing their right hand away from them in many places, they command them to
help themselves, and somewhere they even allow them to be dipped a little, and
to receive much brine in the mouth; so Christ also did in the case of the
disciples. In the beginning and in the prefaces he allowed them to suffer
nothing small, nothing great, but was present everywhere walling them, fencing
them, arranging that all things flow to them with abundance; but when they
needed to display their own courage also, he drew in the grace a little,
commanding them to accomplish many things even through themselves. For this
reason, when they had no sandals, nor belt, nor staff, nor bronze, they lacked
nothing; "For did you lack anything?" he says. And they answering said:
"Nothing." But when he commanded them to have both a purse, and a wallet, and
sandals, they are found both hungering and thirsting and being naked. Whence
it is clear that in many places he conceded that they both run risks and be
straitened, that they might have some reward. So somewhere the birds also do
with their own nestlings; for those too, while they have the wings tender,
sitting on the nest warm them; but when they see them feathered and able to
cut the air, first they arrange for them to fly about the nest itself, then
they also lead them further around, at the beginning following and carrying
them, but after these things leaving them to help themselves. So Christ also
did, feeding the disciples as in a nest, in Palestine; but when he had taught
them to fly, being present and carrying them, at last he let them fly into the
world, commanding them in many places to help themselves also. And that this
is true, and that in order that they might learn his power he stripped them of
all things, and sent them with one tunic, and commanded them to walk without
sandals, we shall know clearly when we have heard the saying itself.

For he did not simply say to them: "Take a purse and a wallet," but he
reminded them of the former things, speaking thus: "When I sent you without
purse and wallet, did you lack anything?"; that is, Did not all things flow to
you with abundance, and did you enjoy much plenty? but now I wish you to
contend even through yourselves; I wish you to take experience of poverty
also; therefore I no longer lead you to the necessity of the former law, but I
permit you to have both a purse and a wallet, that I may not be thought to
work the things concerning you as through =51.200= lifeless instruments, but
that you too may have your own philosophy to display.

γʹ. And what, he says, would not the grace have appeared greater, if they had
finished being so throughout? But they themselves would not have become so
approved; for if they had taken experience of no affliction, not of poverty,
not of persecution, not of straitness, they would have remained idle and
sluggish; but now he wished not only the grace to shine, but also the
approvedness of those who obeyed



to be displayed, that after these things some may not have it to say that
Those men brought in nothing from themselves, but the whole came to be of
God's bias. For God was able to set them in so great abundance until the end,
but he did not wish it for many and necessary reasons, which we have often
spoken to your love; this one indeed, and another not less than this, that
they may know how to be moderate; and a third, that they may not receive a
glory greater than according to man. For these reasons then, and far more than
these, leaving them to fall in with many unexpected things, he did not wish to
leave them under the exactness of the former legislation, but he slackened and
let go the tension of that philosophy, so that the life might not become heavy
and unendurable to them, being abandoned in many places, and being forced to
keep that exact law. But since it is necessary to make the matter at hand,
which is unclear, also manifest, this too must be said. For having said, "Let
him who has a purse take it, and a wallet," he added: "And he who has not,
will sell his garment, and will buy a sword." What ever is this? Does he arm
the disciples, he who says, "If anyone slap you on the right cheek, turn to
him the other also"? he who commands to bless those who revile, to bear with
those who despitefully use, to pray for those who persecute, then does he arm
them, and through a sword only? And what account would this have? For if he
had to arm them at all, they ought not to have acquired a sword only, but also
a shield and a helmet and greaves.

And altogether, if he were going to manage such things in a human way, to how
many would the command not have been a laugh? For if they had acquired a
myriad such weapons against so great an assault and plot of peoples, tyrants,
cities, nations, what were the eleven going to appear? Was it possible for
them to hear the voice of a neighing horse? And would they not have been
struck with dismay at the mere sight of the camps, having been reared in lakes
and rivers and small boats? Why then does he say this? Wishing to indicate the
assault of the Jews, and that they are about to seize him. And this he did not
wish to say openly, but through riddles, so as not to throw them into tumult
again. As then when you hear him saying that "What you heard in the ear,
proclaim upon the housetops, and what you heard in the darkness, speak in the
light," you do not suspect this, that he commands them, leaving the alleys and
the marketplace, to proclaim upon the housetops; for neither do the disciples
appear to have done this; but the "Upon the housetops," and the "In the
light," hints at speaking with boldness; and the "Into the ear," and the "In
the darkness," this makes clear, that What you heard in a small part of the
world, and in one region of Palestine, this sound forth =51.201= everywhere on
the earth. For he was not discoursing with them in darkness, nor into the ear,
but on high mountains, and often in the synagogues. So it must be taken here
also. As then there, hearing housetops, we understood otherwise, so here also,
hearing swords, let us not think this, that he commanded them to possess
swords, but that through the swords he hints at the present plot, and that he
is about to suffer from the Jews the things which he suffered. And this is
clear from what follows. For having said, "He will buy a sword," he added:
"For the things written about me must be fulfilled, that he was reckoned among
the lawless." And when those said that "There are here two swords," and did
not understand what was spoken, he says, "It is enough." And yet it was not
enough; for if he wished them to use human help, not only two and three, but
not even a hundred swords were enough; but if he did not wish them to use
human help, even the two were superfluous. But nevertheless he did not uncover
the riddle; for in many places he appears doing this; whenever they do not
understand what was spoken, he passes on and leaves it, committing



to the outcome of the later events the understanding of the things said; which
then he did elsewhere also. For discoursing about his resurrection, he spoke
somehow thus: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it." And
yet the disciples did not know what was spoken; and that they did not know,
the evangelist marked, saying: "But when Jesus rose, then they believed his
word, and the Scripture." And again elsewhere: "For they did not yet know that
he must rise from the dead."

δʹ. But the inquiry has a sufficient solution; and let us lead the word to the
remaining part of the greeting. What then is it that is being said, and from
where did we come out into these things? We were counting Priscilla and Aquila
blessed, because they dwelt with Paul, because they learned with exactness
even the manner of his dress, and the manner of his sandals, and all the rest.
From here this inquiry was born for us. For we were inquiring why, when Christ
forbade them to have anything at all, except only a garment, they appeared
having both sandals and a cloak. Then the word showed that they used these
things not transgressing the law, but even keeping it exceedingly. And we were
saying these things, not anointing you toward abundance of money, nor
exhorting you to possess more than the need, but that you may have it to
contradict the unbelievers who mock our things. For Christ, having loosed the
former law, did not command to have houses, nor slaves, nor beds, nor silver
plate, nor any other such thing, but to have been freed from the necessity of
the things said before. And Paul also exhorted thus, saying: "Having food and
coverings, with these we shall be content." For what is beyond the need must
be spent on those in need; just as these also did, Priscilla and Aquila.
Wherefore he praises and admires them, and composes a greatest encomium of
them. For having said, "Greet Priscilla and Aquila, my fellow-workers in the
Lord," he also sets the cause of such love. What cause then is this? "Who for
my soul," he says, "laid down their own neck." Therefore for this you love
them and are fond of them, perhaps someone might say? Yes indeed; even if this
only were, it was a sufficient encomium. For he who saved the general would
have saved the soldiers; he who freed the physician from the dangers brought
the sick back to health; he who snatched the helmsman from the surge freed the
whole ship =51.202= from the waves; so also those who saved the teacher of the
world, and poured out their own blood for his salvation, were common
benefactors of the world, having saved all the disciples in their care for the
teacher. But that you may learn that they were not such toward the teacher
only, but displayed the same care also concerning the brethren, hear what
follows. For having said, "Who for my soul laid down their own neck," he
added, saying: "To whom not I only give thanks, but also all the Churches of
the nations." What do you say? To tentmakers, poor artisans, who have nothing
more than necessary food, all the Churches of the nations give thanks? And
what so great thing were these two able to help so many Churches? What
abundance of money did they have? What greatness of power? What boldness
before rulers? Abundance of money and power with those who hold rule they did
not possess; but what was greater than all these, they had a noble eagerness
and a soul drawn up toward dangers with much abundance. Therefore they became
benefactors and saviors of many. For not so would the rich and the
noise-frightened be able to help the Churches as those who live with poverty
and are great-souled. And let no one think what is said to be paradoxical; for
it is true, and it is shown from the



facts themselves. For the rich man has many holds by which he can be harmed.
He fears for house, for servants, for fields, for money, lest someone take any
of these from him. And being master of many things makes him a slave of many.
The poor man, however, being some lightly-girt person and having put off all
these holds, is a lion breathing fire, and has a noble soul, and standing up
from all he easily does all the things that can help the Churches, whether he
must refute, or rebuke, or take up a myriad burdens for Christ; and since he
has once looked down on the present life, he does all things easily and with
much facility. For what does he even fear, tell me? Lest someone take his
money from him? But this cannot be said. But lest he fall from his fatherland?
But every city under heaven is his. But lest someone cut him off from luxury
and from a bodyguard? But having said farewell to all these things also, he
has his citizenship in heaven, and he hastens toward the life to come. And if
he must give even the soul itself, and bring in the blood, he will not refuse.
From here such a one is both more powerful and more well-supplied than
tyrants, and kings, and peoples, and all.

And that you may learn that what is said is not flattery, but truly those who
possess nothing, these most of all would be able to speak freely, how many
rich were there in the time of Herod? how many potentates? who came forward
into the midst? who rebuked the tyrant? who defended the laws of God when they
were being wronged? Of the well-supplied, no one; but the poor and needy man,
who had neither bed, nor table, nor roof, the citizen of the desert, John,
this one alone and first with all boldness convicted the tyrant, and uncovered
the adulterous marriages, and with all present and hearing he brought out the
vote that condemned him. And before this again the great Elijah, possessing
nothing more than the sheepskin, alone convicted that impious and lawless
=51.203= Ahab with much courage. For nothing so prepares one to speak freely,
and persuades one to take courage in all the terrible things, and makes people
invulnerable and strong, as possessing nothing, and not having some wrapping
of affairs. So that if anyone should wish to possess much power, let him
embrace poverty, let him despise the present life, let him think death to be
nothing. This man will be able to help the Churches more, not only than the
well-supplied, nor than the rulers, but even than those who reign themselves.
For those who reign and the well-supplied, whatever they do, they do from
money; but such a one has often accomplished many and great things even from
dangers and from deaths. And by as much as blood is more precious than all
gold, by so much is this contribution better than that.
