* Praise of Bishop Diodorus
:PROPERTIES:

Encomium on Bishop Diodorus, who had spoken first and given an encomium.
=52.761=
1. This wise and noble teacher, having lately put off the weakness of the body
and gone up upon this throne, opened his discourse concerning me, calling me
John the Baptist, and a voice of the Church, and a rod of Moses, and many
other things besides these. And he for his part gave praise then, and you
cried out; but I, sitting far off, groaned bitterly. For he was praising,
showing his fatherly love; you were crying out, showing your brotherly love;
but I was groaning sic, crushed by the weight of the praises. For the
greatness of encomia is wont to sting the conscience no less than sins; and
when a man, conscious in himself of no =52.762= good, hears others saying many
and great good things about him, considering the present reputation and the
coming day, on which all things are naked and laid open; and that he who then
judges will not judge from the surmise of the many, but from the truth of the
facts themselves (For he shall not judge according to appearance, it says, nor
rebuke according to report): thinking on all these things, I am pained by the
praises and by the kindly opinion of the many, seeing how great a distance
there is between this and the coming verdict. For now we are hidden by certain
masks, the opinions of the many; but on that day, with uncovered head, these
masks taken away, standing before the tribunal =52.763=, we shall be able to
take no help from the glory here toward the verdict there, but for this very
reason we shall be punished more severely, because even after enjoying many
praises and much acclaim from the many, we have not even by this become
better.
2. Thinking on all these things, then, I groaned bitterly sic. Therefore I
also rose quickly today, so as to thrust away such a reputation before you who
heard those things. For when a crown is larger than the head of the one being
crowned, it does not bind the temples, it does not settle on the head; but
becoming too loose by its size, it slips down through the eyes and becomes a
necklace, and leaves the head uncrowned. And this is just what we have
suffered, the crown of encomia having appeared greater than the head our worth
deserves. But nevertheless, things being so, because of the present (f.
overflowing) affection in him, the father did not leave off until he had
somehow set it upon us. So also kings often do, and taking the diadem that
fits them, they set it on the heads of their children; then when they see that
the child's head is smaller than the crown, having been content with this mere
putting it on simply and as it happened, they take it thereafter and set it on
themselves.
3. Since then the father also has set upon us the crown that befits him, and
it appeared greater than our own head, he would not choose to set it on
himself; come then, taking it from ourselves, let us set it again upon the
fitting head of the father. For the name of John is with us, but the mind is
with him; and we received the appellation, but he acquired the philosophy.
Therefore he would also more justly inherit the name than we; for it is not
the sharing of names, but the kinship of deeds, that is wont to make men
namesakes, even if the names differ. For Scripture is not wont to philosophize
about these things as those who share in the outside philosophy do. For they
say that men do not become namesakes unless the sharing of names also
coincides with the substance. But Scripture does not do so; but when it sees a
great kinship of philosophy, even if other names are assigned to those who
share with one another in manner of life, it calls them by the same
appellation. And the proof of this is not far to seek, but by bringing forward
this very John, the son of Zechariah. For when the disciples asked whether
Elijah was going to come again, he answered: And that if you are willing to
receive it? This is Elijah who is to come. And yet he was called John; but
since he had the manner of Elijah =52.764=, for this reason he also gave him a
share in this name; since he acquired the spirit of Elijah, for this reason
also the name of Elijah. For both inhabited desert places; and the one was
clothed in a sheepskin, the other in a garment of hair; and their table
likewise was poor and simple. And the one became a minister of the former
coming, and the other will be of the coming that is still to be. Since then
the manner of diet and the clothing they wore, and the places they frequented,
and the ministry were one, and all things were alike for them; for this reason
he also set one name upon both, showing that even if someone has another name,
he can be namesake of the one whose manner of life he has emulated.
4. Since then this canon is undisputed, and this is the exact definition of
namesakes in the divine Scripture, come, let us show how this wise father of
ours also emulated the manner of John, that you may learn that he also would
more justly inherit that man's name. That man had no table, nor bed, nor house
upon the earth; nor did this man ever have them. And you are witnesses of
this, how he spent the whole time, showing an apostolic life, having nothing
of his own, but being fed by others, himself continuing in prayer and in the
teaching of the word. That man preached, spending his time beyond the river in
the deserts; and this man once, taking the whole city beyond the river, taught
the sound doctrine. That man dwelt in a prison and had his head cut off for
his boldness on behalf of the law; but this man also was often driven from his
fatherland for his boldness on behalf of the faith, and often had his head cut
off for this same cause, if not in the event, yet in the purpose. For the
enemies of the truth, not bearing the power of his tongue, stitched together
countless plots from every side, and the Lord delivered him from them all.
Come then, let us hear this tongue, for which he both was in danger and was
saved. Concerning which one would not err in saying this which Moses said
concerning the land [the] of promise. And what did he say concerning that? A
land flowing with milk and honey. This one can say also concerning the tongue:
a tongue of this man flowing with milk and honey. That we may then both enjoy
the milk and be filled with the honey, having here brought our own discourse
to a close, let us hear the lyre and the trumpet. For when I consider the
sweetness of the words, I call his voice a lyre; but when the power of the
thoughts, a war-trumpet, and such as the Jews had when they threw down the
walls of Jericho. For just as then the sound of the trumpets, striking the
stones more fiercely than fire, consumed and wasted all things; so also his
voice now, falling upon the strongholds of the heretics no less than that
trumpet, pulls down =52.765= reasonings, and every height that is lifted up
against the knowledge of God. But that you may learn these things not through
our tongue but through his, let us rest =52.766= our own discourse, sending up
glory to God, who has granted such teachers, for to him is the glory unto the
ages of ages. Amen.
