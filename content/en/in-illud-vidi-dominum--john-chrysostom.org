2.2 But attend with accuracy; for the reading of the Scriptures is an opening
of the heavens. "And it came to pass in the year in which Ozias the king died,
I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and lifted up." Did you see the
courtesy of a well-disposed servant? Straightway he led us in beside the royal
throne, not first leading us around through long entries, but at the same time
he opened the gates and showed the king sitting opposite. "And the Seraphim,"
he says, "stood round about him; six wings to the one and six wings to the
one; and with two they covered the faces and with two the feet and with two
they flew, and they cried one to the other and said: Holy, holy, holy, Lord of
Sabaoth." Truly holy, because he counted our nature worthy of so many and so
great mysteries, because he made us partakers of such unspeakable things. A
shudder and trembling seized me in the midst of this song. And what wonder, if
me who am of clay and from earth, when even the powers above themselves a very
great astonishment holds continually? For this reason at any rate they turn
away their faces and put forward the wings instead of a wall, not bearing the
rays from there. And yet, he says, the thing that appeared was a
condescension. How then did they not bear it? Do you say these things to me?
Say them to those who busy themselves about the unspeakable and blessed
nature, to those who dare the undared. The Seraphim were not able to see even
a condescension, but would a man dare to say, or rather would a man be able to
take it into mind that he can see the unmixed nature with accuracy and
clearly? Shudder, O heaven, be astonished, O earth; these daring deeds are
greater than those. For the things in which those men then acted impiously,
these also now act impiously. For they worship the creation similarly; but
what these have now invented, none of the men of that time dared either to say
or to hear. What do you say? The thing that appeared was a condescension? Yes,
but a condescension of God. For if Daniel, who had much boldness toward God,
did not endure to see an angel that had condescended to him, but fell and lay
prone, the joints of his body being loosed, what wonder if the Seraphim were
astonished, not bearing to see that glory? For the interval of Daniel toward
the angel is not so great as that of God toward those powers. But lest we too,
lingering longer upon these wonders, lead your souls into amazement, come, let
us turn back to the beginning of the history, entertaining you with simpler
narratives. "And it came to pass in the year in which Ozias the king died." It
is worth first inquiring for what reason the prophet marks the times for us;
for he does not do this simply nor in vain. For the mouths of the prophets are
the mouth of God; and such a mouth would say nothing as a side work. Let us
therefore not hear as a side work either. For if those who dig mines do not
pass by even the small grains, but when they lay hold of a gold-bearing vein
they examine the fibers accurately; how much more must we do this in the case
of the Scriptures? And yet in the case of the mines the of the


discovery of the things sought is very hard to catch. For since the mines too
are earth and the gold is nothing other than earth, the kinship of nature of
the things sought steals the eyes, but even so those men do not stand off, but
they display all accuracy; and yet seeing they know what is truly earth and
what is truly gold. But in the case of Scripture it is not so. For the gold is
not set forth mixed with the earth, but it is pure gold, "silver tried by
fire, proven to the earth". For the Scriptures are not mines needing working,
but they furnish a ready treasure to those who seek the wealth from them. For
it is enough only to stoop and to go away filled with all benefit; it is
enough only to open and at once to behold the gleamings of the stones. And
these things have not been said by me simply, nor have I stretched out the
word in vain, but because there are certain coarse men who, whenever they take
the divine books in hand, then find a number of times or a catalogue of names
lying there, at once pass them by and say to those who accuse them: they are
names only and have nothing useful. What do you say? God speaks and you dare
to say that nothing of the things said is useful? For if you should see only a
bare title, will you not stand with eagerness, tell me, and search out the
wealth that lies there? And why do I speak of times and names and titles?
Learn how much even the addition of one letter only can do, and cease
despising whole names. Our patriarch Abraham, for this man belongs to us more
than to the Jews, was first called Abram, which being interpreted is
passer-over; and after these things, having been renamed Abraham he became
father of all the nations, and the addition of one letter entrusted so great a
rule to the righteous man. For just as kings stretch out golden tablets to
their own governors, a symbol of the rule; so also God then gave to that
righteous man the letter as a symbol of the honor.
2.3 But concerning names I will narrate at another time; but how great is the
gain of the times when they are known, and how great the harm when they are
unknown, it is necessary to say. And first I will display it to you from
worldly affairs. Wills and the documents concerning marriages, those
concerning debts, those concerning the other contracts, if they do not have
the times of the consulship written above from the start, are stripped of all
their proper power. For this is the strong thing in those, this does away with
disputes, this sets free from law-courts and makes enemies friends. For this
reason those who write these things, like a lamp upon a lampstand, so upon the
forehead of the writings stamp the consulship, in order that all the things
written below may shine. And if you take these away, you have taken away the
light and have filled all things with darkness and much confusion. For this
reason every giving and receiving, whether it comes about toward friends, or
toward enemies, or toward servants, or toward stewards and household managers,
needs this security, and everywhere we also write down months and years and
days below. If therefore in worldly things the force of the matter is so
great, in spiritual things it is much greater and more useful. For this shows
the prophecies to be prophecies. For prophecy is never anything else but a
proclaiming beforehand of the things that are going to be. He therefore who is
ignorant of the seasons of the things said or of the things that came out, how
will he be able to show to the contentious man the dignity of the prophecy?
From this we have also the contests toward the Greeks and the victories, when
we display our things as older than those among them; from this we have also
many proofs toward the Jews concerning the truth; toward the Jews the wretched
and miserable, who through ignorance of the times fell the greatest fall. For
if they had heard the patriarch saying: "A ruler shall not fail from Judah,
nor a leader from


his thighs, until he come for whom it is laid up" and if they had observed
with accuracy the seasons of the coming, they would not, having fallen away
from Christ, have been going to fall in with the Antichrist; just as therefore
Christ himself also said this to them, hinting at it, that "I came in the name
of my Father, and you did not receive me; if another come in his own name, him
you will receive." Did you see how great a fall came about from the ignorance
of the times? Do not therefore neglect so great a benefit. For just as
boundaries and pillars in the fields do not allow the portions to be confused,
so the times and the seasons do not allow the affairs to fall together with
one another, but, parting them from one another and arranging each according
to the fitting order, they set us free from much confusion. It is worth
therefore saying to you who this Ozias was and when he reigned and over whom
he reigned and how great the time of his rule became for him and how he ended
his life; or rather it is worth being silent henceforth. For it is necessary
to send the word out into a boundless sea of histories. And those who are
going to travel such a sea must take hold of the way not when the sailors are
worn out, but when they are in their prime. For this reason both harbors and
islands have been planted everywhere in the sea, in order that both steersman
and sailor may rest, the one laying aside the oar, the other rising from the
tillers; for this reason also inns and lodging-places have been devised
everywhere on the roads, in order that both beasts of burden and travelers may
cease from the toils. For this reason also a time of silence has been
appointed for the word of teaching, in order that we may neither wear
ourselves out by the multitude of the things said, nor weary you. And Solomon
also knows these seasons, saying thus: "A time to be silent and a time to
speak." Let there therefore be for us a time to be silent, in order that there
may be a time for the teacher to speak. For our things resemble wine freshly
drawn from the vats, but his resemble wine aged and grown old, furnishing much
both of the benefit and of the strength to those who receive it; and that
thing of the gospel has come about today; for after the lesser wine the better
is brought in. And just as a vine did not then bear that wine, but the power
of Christ made it, so also this man's word no human mind pours forth, but the
grace of the Spirit. Upon therefore abundant and spiritual streams, let us
receive them with eagerness, let us keep them with security, in order that
being watered by these continually we may bear ripe fruit to God who granted
these things; with whom be glory to the Father together with the Holy
Spirit unto the ages. Amen.

3.t On the saying of Chronicles which says: "The heart of Ozias was lifted
up", and on humility, and that the virtuous man must not be confident, and how
great an evil is arrogance.
3.1 Blessed is God, and in our generation too martyrs have sprung up, we too
have been counted worthy to see men being slaughtered for Christ, dripping
holy blood, watering the whole Church, dripping blood, fearful to demons,
longed for by angels, and for us saving. We have been counted worthy to see
men wrestling for piety, being crowned. And we have been counted worthy not
only to see, but also to receive the very bodies of the athletes, and we have
the crowned ones with ourselves now. But let us leave the word concerning the
martyrs now to the zealot of the martyrs, the common teacher; and let us
ourselves now tell you the things concerning Ozias, paying down an old debt
and loosing long birth-pangs of hearing. For each of


you, I know well, is in birth-pangs to hear that history; and we have
stretched out this birth-pang, not wishing to intensify the pain for you, but
being eager to increase the desire, so that our feast may appear most sweet to
you. For the well-provided of the hosts, if they take the guests already
filled, might by the costliness of the things prepared awaken the desire in
them; but nothing so makes the table of the poor appear splendid as that those
who are going to share it come hungry. Who then is Ozias and of whom a
descendant and of whom king and how long a time he reigned and what he
accomplished, and what he missed, and how he ended his life? All these things
we will now tell you, or rather as many as it is possible to say, so as not to
bury your memory by the multitude; which also comes about in the case of
lamp-fire. For if there you drip the oil upon the wick drop by drop, you have
given the fire sufficient food; but if you pour it on all at once, you have
even quenched the flame that was. This Ozias then was a descendant of David,
and king of the Jews; and he reigned fifty-two years; and being at first well
reputed, later he fell into sin. For having thought more than his own worth,
he leapt upon the rule of the priesthood. So great an evil is arrogance; for
it also persuades each man to be ignorant of himself and after many toils
empties the whole treasure of virtue. And the other evils are naturally able
to prevail when we are lazy, but this springs upon us when we are succeeding.
For nothing so customarily begets arrogance as a good conscience, if we do not
take heed. For this reason Christ also, knowing that after the successes this
passion comes in upon us, said to the disciples: "When you have done all
things, say that we are unprofitable servants." For when the beast is going to
come in upon you, then, he says, by these words shut the doors against it. And
he did not say: When you have done all things, you are unprofitable, but: "You
say that we are unprofitable." Speak, do not fear, for I do not bring the vote
from your judgment. For if you say yourself unprofitable, I crown you as
useful. So also elsewhere he says: "Speak you your lawless deeds first, in
order that you may be justified." For in the outer law-courts after the
accusation of the one who sins there is death; but in the divine law-court
after the accusation of the sins there is a crown. Wherefore Solomon also
said: "Do not justify yourself before the Lord." But Ozias heard none of these
things, but he went into the temple and wished to burn incense and when the
priest hindered him he would not bear it. What then did God do? He sent
leprosy upon him on the forehead, punishing the shameless face and training
him that the law-court is divine and the war was not toward men. And the
things concerning Ozias are these. Come then, let us go through the history
itself from above. For this reason I myself also, taking it beforehand, have
narrated to you in brief all the things that happened, in order that when you
hear Scripture announcing these things you may follow them with accuracy. But
attend. "And Ozias," he says, "did what was right before the Lord." By these
things he bore witness to him of a great virtue. For he did not do what was
right only, but also before God, not toward display of men, as those among the
Jews who trumpet before almsgiving, who disfigure their faces in the fasts,
who make the prayers in the streets; than whom what could be more wretched,
when they endure the toils, but are robbed of all the return?
3.2 What are you doing, O man? You are going to give accounts of the things
done to another and you call another as witness of the things that come about?
You have another as judge and you seat another as spectator? Do you not see
the charioteers, who, when the whole city is seated above in the


contests of the horses, running past the whole part of the stadium, are eager
there to overturn the chariots of their rivals, wherever they see the king
sitting, and of so many sights they think one eye more trustworthy? And you,
seeing the very king of the angels acting as president of the contest for your
courses, leaving him take refuge in the sights of fellow-servants? For this
very reason after the countless wrestlings you withdraw uncrowned, after the
many sweats you go away to the president of the contest without prizes. But
Ozias was not such, but he did what was right before the Lord. How then,
living so with accuracy, was he tripped and fell? For this I too wonder and am
at a loss, or rather this would not be worthy of being at a loss; for he was a
man, a thing easy to slip toward sin and quick to incline toward vice. And
this is not the only hard thing, but also that we have been commanded to
travel a narrow and afflicted way, hemmed in by cliffs on both sides. When
therefore both ease of purpose and difficulty of way come together into the
same, do not wonder any longer about the falls? For just as in the theatres
those who practice going up and down the rope stretched from below to above,
if they look aside a little, being overturned will be carried down into the
orchestra and will perish; so also those who travel this way, if they are a
little lazy, are thrown down the cliffs. For this way is even narrower than
that rope and steeper and more uphill and much higher; for it ends above at
heaven itself and then our steps will be more slippery, when we are above and
at the very peak; for for those standing on a height the trembling is great
and one safety only is left, not to stoop down, nor to look to earth. For from
this the dizziness becomes hard. For this very reason the prophet continually
cries out to us saying: "Unto the end destroy not", checking our soul when it
is lazy and holding it up and keeping it when it is about to fall. For at the
beginning we do not need much exhortation. Why ever? Because every man, even
if he is the dullest of all, when he is about to take hold of a matter,
displays much eagerness in the preludes and, the desire being in its prime and
the power being still fresh [he easily sets out toward the thing set forth];
but when we have gone forward the greater part of the way and the things of
our desire have withered, and the things of our power have ceased, and we are
about to fall, then the prophet stands by us in season, stretching out this
refrain like some staff and saying: "Unto the end destroy not." For the devil
too then blows more violently. And just as the pirates who sail the sea, not
when they see the ships going out of the harbor, then attack (for what benefit
to them to sink the hull empty?) but when it is coming back having the full
freight, then they move every device, so also that wicked demon, when he sees
us having gathered many things, fasting, prayers, almsgiving, temperance, all
the rest of virtue, when he sees our ship full of the costly stones of piety,
then he attacks, digging through the treasure from all sides, so as to sink
the hull at the very mouths of the harbor and to send us on naked to that
harbor henceforth. For this reason the prophet commands all saying: "Unto the
end destroy not." For after such a fall the rising again is hard to call back.
"For he who has come into a depth of evils despises." And to those who have
fallen at the beginning we all grant pardon because of inexperience; but the
one tripped after many courses no one would easily count worthy of pardon or
defense; for the fall then seems to be of laziness. And this is not the only
terrible thing, but also that many are scandalized by the falls of such a man,
so that in this way again unpardonable


the sin becomes. Knowing these things then, let us hear the prophet and unto
the end let us not destroy. For this reason Ezekiel also cries saying: "If a
man become righteous, then having changed he sin, his righteousnesses shall
not be remembered for him, but in his sin he shall die." For he too has feared
for the end. And not from this only, but also through the contraries he shows
that the force of the matter is great. "For if a man become a sinner," he
says, "then having changed he become righteous, his sins shall not be
remembered; in his righteousness he shall live." Do you see him here too
making much provision of the end? For in order that neither the righteous man,
trusting in his righteousness, having declined into laziness may perish, he
frightens him through the end; nor the sinner, having despaired over the
falls, may remain continually in the fall, he raises him through the end. You
have sinned many things, he says, but do not despair; for there is a return,
if you show the end contrary to the beginning. Again toward the righteous man:
You have accomplished many things, he says, but do not be confident; for it
happens even to fall, if you do not have the equal eagerness through to the
end. Did you see how he did away with the laziness of the one, and the despair
of the other? 3.3 But Ozias heard none of these things; wherefore having been
confident he also fell a hard and incurable fall. For not every fall works an
equal wound for us, but of the sins some lie under condemnation only, and some
receive a most hard punishment. At any rate Paul, rebuking those who do not
wait for the brothers in the common meals, spoke thus: "And in commanding this
I do not praise." Do you see the sin standing as far as condemnation and
having blame as the penalty? But not so does he do, when he discourses about
fornication. But how? "If anyone destroy the temple of God, God will destroy
this man." For here there is not blame, nor condemnation, but the most hard
punishment. Solomon also knows differences of sins; at any rate comparing
theft with adultery, he says somehow thus: "It is not a wonder, if someone is
caught stealing; for he steals, in order that he may fill his soul that is
hungry; but the adulterer through want of wits procures destruction for his
own soul." This is a sin and that is, he says, but the one is lesser, the
other greater; for the one has the pretext from poverty, but this man has been
robbed of all defense. But this man too, he says, has the necessity from
natural desire. But the wife allotted to him does not let him go, but she
stands by robbing him of the pardon. For this reason marriage and lawful
enjoyment exist, in order that the husband may have none of these things to
say. For this reason the wife was given him as a helper, in order that she may
check nature when it is raging, in order that she may lay the waves of desire.
As therefore a steersman working a shipwreck in harbor would not obtain any
pardon, so also the man who after the security from marriage digs through the
marriages of another, or looks with busy curiosity at any woman whatsoever,
would not obtain any defense, not before men, not before God, even if he speak
ten thousand times of the pleasure of nature. Or rather what pleasure could
there be, where there is fear and agony and danger and expectation of so many
terrible things, where law-courts and accounts and a judge's wrath and a sword
and an executioner and a pit and a noose? Such a man trembles at and fears all
things, the shadows, the walls, the very stones, as if they were sending forth
a voice; he suspects and is suspicious of all, the servants, the neighbors,
the friends, the enemies, those who know all things, those who know nothing.
Or rather, if you wish, let even these things be taken away, and let no one
know the things dared, but only he himself with the woman who is being
insulted; how will he bear the proof from the conscience, carrying about
everywhere a bitter accuser? For just as no one would ever flee himself, so
neither the vote from that court. This law-court is not corrupted by money,
does not yield to flatteries; for it is divine and


has been seated by God in our souls. Truly "the adulterer through want of wits
procures destruction for his own soul". Nor indeed has the one who steals been
robbed of punishment, but he pays a penalty, though a lesser one. For
comparisons do not thrust the things compared out into the contrary rank, but
leaving them to remain in their own place they introduce a lessening and a
superiority. Perhaps you did not understand what was said; so it is necessary
to speak more clearly. Marriage is good, but virginity is better; not because
virginity is better, for this reason is marriage an evil, but it is less than
that, yet itself also good. So also here: theft is an evil, but adultery is
worse; not because adultery is worse, for this reason is theft not an evil,
but it is less than that, yet itself also an evil. Did you see differences of
sins? Let us see then what sort of sin this man sinned. "His heart," he says,
"was lifted up." Hard is the wound; for it is arrogance, arrogance the
fountain of all the evils. And in order that you may learn in short the
badness of the disease, hear that. The other sins turn about our nature, but
pride dragged down a bodiless power and cast it from above. For the devil, who
was not formerly a devil, this made to be a devil. And if we bring forward
Isaiah as a witness saying thus about him: "I will go up into heaven, and I
will be like the Most High", those who do not gladly receive the allegories
will set aside our testimony; but if we set Paul as accuser against him, no
one any longer will speak against. What then does Paul writing to Timothy?
that the one who has just taken hold of the preaching must not be led to the
great dignity of the episcopate, he says somehow thus: "Not a neophyte, lest
being puffed up he fall into the judgment [and snare] of the devil"; lest
having sinned the same things as that one, he says, he be punished the same
things as he.
3.4 And this is clear not from this only, but also from the things which that
wicked demon advised to the first of all men who came to be. For just as it is
the custom of the good to advise to the neighbors those things through which
they themselves have become good, so also it is the custom of the wicked to
introduce to the neighbors such things through which they themselves have
become base. For this too is one form of their wickedness and they think the
destruction of others a consolation of their own punishment. What then did the
devil advise to Adam? To take a thought greater than his own nature and to
hope for equality with God. For if this, he says, cast me out of heaven, much
more will this same thing cast this man out of paradise. For this reason
Solomon also said: "God arrays himself against the proud." He did not say that
God lets the proud go and abandons them and strips them of his own help, but
"He arrays himself against", he says, not because he needed arraying and
battle toward the proud man; for what could be weaker than a proud man? For
just as the one who has lost his eyes is set forth for all to suffer harm, so
the proud man, who does not know the Lord "for the beginning," he says, "of
pride is not to know the Lord", is easy to catch even for men, having fallen
from that light. And if he were strong, God would not have needed arraying
toward him; for he for whom the will sufficed to bring all things forth, much
more would it have sufficed also toward the taking of them away. For what
reason then, he says, does he array himself? In order that he may display the
violence of the hatred toward the proud man. That therefore the wound of pride
is hard is clear both from these things and from many other places. And if you
wish, let us learn also the cause itself, from which the sore came about. For
it is the custom of Scripture, whenever it is going to accuse someone, not to
say his sin only, but also to teach us the cause of the sin; and it does this,
making those who are healthy more secure toward not falling into the same
things. So also physicians,


going in toward those who are sick, even before the diseases track out the
sources, so as to check the evil from above; for he who, while the root
remains, cuts off the sprouts only, does nothing other than labor in vain.
Where then did Scripture say both the sin and the cause of the sin? It accuses
those who came to be before the flood over the mixings that were not fitting;
and hear how it sets the cause: "The sons of God seeing the daughters of men
that they are beautiful, took them to themselves as wives." What then, is
beauty the cause of the sin? Let it not be; for it is a work of the wisdom of
God; and a work of God would never become a cause of wickedness. But the
seeing? Not this either; for this too is a work of nature. But what? The
seeing badly; for this is of a corrupted purpose. For this reason a wise man
also exhorts saying: "Do not study out another's beauty." He did not say: Do
not see; for it happens that this come about even of itself; but "Do not study
out", he says, doing away with the comprehension from practice, the busy
sight, the viewing with lingering, that from a corrupted and desiring soul.
And what harm, he says, could come about from this? "From this," he says,
"love is kindled as fire." For just as fire, whenever it lays hold of some
grass or stubble, does not wait some time, but at the same time it has touched
the matter and has kindled a bright flame; so also the fire of the desire in
us, whenever through the eyes it touches a well-formed and bright sight, at
once sets on fire the souls of those who see. Do not therefore look at the
temporary pleasure from the viewing, but consider the continual pain from the
desire. For the one, having put in a great wound, has often leapt away; but
the wound does not leap away, but remains and destroys. And just as a deer
having received an arrow in a vital part of the body, even if it flee the
hands of the hunters, gains nothing henceforth, so also a soul having received
an arrow of desire from an unchaste and busy viewing, even if it go away
having let the arrow go, itself is corrupted and destroyed, seeing the enemy
everywhere and having him following. But as I was saying, for one must not
allow the word to make long turnings aside, that Scripture is accustomed to
say both the sins and their causes; hear at any rate here too what it says
about Ozias. For it did not teach us only that his heart was lifted up, but it
also added from where it was lifted up. From where then was it lifted up?
"When he became strong," he says, "his heart was lifted up." He did not bear
the greatness of the power, but just as from gluttony comes inflammation, and
from inflammation fever is begotten, then from there often death, so also here
from the great wrapping-about of affairs arrogance came about. For what
inflammation is in bodies, this arrogance is in souls. Then from arrogance a
desire of the things not fitting for him.
3.5 We do not lengthen these things simply, but in order that you may never
think those in powers to be enviable[, nor bless them], knowing how great is
the insecurity of the matter, in order that you may never consider those in
poverty and hardship wretched, knowing that from this the safety is greater.
Wherefore the prophet also cried saying: "It is good for me, Lord, that you
humbled me." See at any rate how great the evil became from the height. "His
heart was lifted up unto destroying," he says. What is it? "Unto destroying?"
Of wicked thoughts some do not even at all set foot upon our soul, if we fence
ourselves with much safety; and some are begotten within, and when we have
been lazy, they also sprout; but if they are anticipated, they are quickly
choked and buried. Others both are begotten and grow and go out toward the
wicked deeds and destroy all the health of our soul, when we come to be in
much laziness. This then is what he says "His heart was lifted up"; and the
arrogance did not remain within, nor


was it quenched, but it leapt out, and having come to a wicked work, destroyed
all his virtue. The blessed thing then is not even at all to receive the
wicked thought; which therefore the prophet also said: "Lord, my heart was not
lifted up." And he did not say: It was lifted up, but I checked it, but It was
not even at the beginning lifted up, that is, I kept the soul throughout
untrodden by the vice. This then is the blessed thing; and the thing after
that, having quickly thrust away the thoughts that came in and not having
allowed them to linger more, so as not to work a wicked grazing in us. And if
we should be lazy even as far as this, there is through the loving-kindness of
God even a consolation of this laziness, and many medicines have been prepared
by that unspeakable and great goodness for such wounds. But come, let us
henceforth stop the word, lest this, which we feared at the beginning, come
about now and the multitude harm your memory. Wherefore it is necessary also
to recapitulate the things said through brief words. For so also mothers do;
whenever they throw fruits or sweetmeats or something such into the child's
lap, so that by the laziness of the children something of the things given
them may not fall out, having gathered the little tunic from all sides they
put it under the security of the belt. Let us also do this, let us gather in
the word that has been stretched out into length and let us deposit it with
the guard of memory. You heard how we must do nothing toward display of men
and how great an evil laziness is, how it easily tripped even the one living
in an accurate life. You came to know how much eagerness we need, and
especially toward the very ends of life, and how the one who changes must
neither despair over the falls, nor the one who has been lazy be confident
over the successes. We discoursed to you about difference of sins, about not
gaping toward the splendors of bodies and we showed how great an evil from
this. Remember the things said to you about arrogance, the things about wicked
thoughts. Keeping these things let us withdraw home; or rather keeping these
things let us also receive the more perfect exhortation of the good teacher.
For our things, of whatever sort they may be, have the marks of youth; but
his, of whatever sort they may be, are adorned with gray thought. And our
things resemble a stream borne with a rushing sound; but his resemble a spring
sending forth rivers with much quiet, imitating the course of oil rather than
of waters. Let us therefore receive the streams, in order that there may come
to be in us "a spring of water leaping unto eternal life"; which may we all
obtain, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom
glory to the Father, together with the Holy Spirit, unto the ages of the ages.
Amen.

4.t On the saying of the prophet Isaiah which says: "It came to pass in the
year in which Ozias the king died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high
and lifted up", and a praise of the city of Antioch, and a godly demonstration
against those who hinder marriage.
4.1 Splendid has our theatre become today and bright the gathering. What ever
is the cause? The harvest today is fruit of yesterday's seeds. Yesterday we
planted and today we gather. For we do not farm soulless earth, that it may be
slow, but rational souls. It is not nature that is going to delay, but grace
that hastens. Orderly is our people, fond of hearing is the populace.
Yesterday they were called and today they are crowned. Today's obedience is
fruit of yesterday's exhortation. For this reason we also put down the seeds
with eagerness, because we see the field clean, nowhere a thorn choking, nor a
way


that is trodden, nor a barren rock, but some deep and rich land, at the same
time receiving the seeds and furnishing us the ear. These things I say and I
will not cease always saying; that it is an encomium of our city, not that it
has a senate and we can number consuls, nor that many statues, nor abundance
of wares, nor fitness of situation; but that it has a populace fond of hearing
and temples of God filled, and the Church rather feasts each day on a word
that flows and a longing that is never sated. For the city is wondered at not
from the buildings, but from the inhabitants. Do not tell me that the city of
the Romans is great by size; but show me there a people so fond of hearing.
Since Sodom also had towers, but the hut had Abraham; but the angels coming
ran past Sodom, and were brought down upon the hut. For they were not seeking
distinction of houses, but they went about virtue of soul. So then otherwise
too the desert had John, but the city Herod; for this reason a desert is more
well-disposed than a city. Why ever? Because prophecy is not in the buildings.
And I say these things, in order that we may never praise a city from things
that are being dissolved. Why do you tell me buildings and columns? These are
dissolved together with the present life. Enter into church and see the
nobility of the city. Enter and see poor men remaining from midnight until the
day, see sacred all-nights joined to day and night, see a populace that loves
Christ, fearing neither in the day the necessity of poverty, nor in the night
the tyranny of sleep. A great city and metropolis of the world. How many
bishops, how many teachers have come here and having been trained by the
people withdraw and prepare to transplant from here the inborn law? If you
tell me dignities and abundance of money, you praise the tree from the leaves
and not from the fruit. And I say these things not flattering your love, but
proclaiming your virtue. Blessed am I because of you, blessed are you because
of yourselves. Blessed is he who speaks into ears that hear; thus I became
blessed. "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness." See how
you became blessed because of yourselves. Blessed is a man who loves spiritual
words. This distinguishes us from the irrational creatures. For it is not the
proportion of the body, nor being fed, nor drinking, nor grazing, nor living;
for all these things are common to us with the irrational creatures; but in
what has a man been distinguished from the irrational creatures? By speech;
for this reason man is also a rational living thing. For just as bodies are
fed, so also the soul is fed; but the body by bread, and the soul by the word.
Tell me: If then you should see a man eating a stone, would you say he is a
man? Thus if you should see one not being fed by the word, but by
irrationality, you will say: This man has lost even being a man; for the
nurture shows the nobility of the man. Since therefore our theatre has been
filled and again the sea that is waving and full of calm and again the deep
that is storm-tossed and standing still, come, let us draw the ship, spreading
the tongue instead of the sail, calling the grace of the Spirit instead of the
west wind, using the cross as steersman instead of neck and rudder. For the
sea has salty waters, but here living water. There irrational living things,
but here rational souls; there those who sail from sea to land, but here those
who sail are moored from earth to heaven; there ships, but here spiritual
words; there planks in the ship, but here joinings of words; there a sail, but
here a tongue; there a breeze of west wind, but here a sojourning of the
Spirit; there a man as steersman, but here the steersman is Christ. For this
very reason the ship is storm-tossed, but it does not become submerged. For it
was able also to sail in a calm, but the steersman did not allow it, in order
that both of the


sailors you may see the endurance and of the one steering you may learn the
understanding accurately.
4.2 Let Greeks hear, let Jews hear our successes and the presidency of the
Church. By how many was the Church warred against, but it was never conquered?
how many tyrants? how many generals? how many kings? Augustus, Tiberius,
Gaius, Claudius, Nero, men honored in words, powerful, warred so much against
a still youthful prime, but they did not root it out; but those who warred
have been silenced and given over to forgetfulness, and the one warred against
overtops heaven. Do not look at this for me, that the Church has stood on
earth, but that it has its citizenship in heaven? From where is this clear?
The proof of the affairs shows it. Eleven disciples were warred against and
the world was warring; but those warred against conquered and those who warred
were taken away; the sheep prevailed over the wolves. Did you see a shepherd
sending the sheep in the midst of the wolves, in order that they may not even
by flight procure the salvation? What shepherd works this? But Christ did
this, in order that he may show you that the successes come about not
according to sequence of the affairs, but above nature and sequence. For the
Church has been rooted more than heaven. But perhaps the Greek condemns
arrogance in me; but let him wait for the proof of the affairs and let him
learn the force of the truth. How? It is easier for the sun to be quenched
than for the Church to be made to vanish. Who, he says, is the one proclaiming
these things? He who founded it: "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my
words will not pass away." These things he not only said, but also fulfilled;
for why did he found it greater than heaven rather? For the Church is more
honorable than heaven. Why is there heaven? Because of the Church, not the
Church because of heaven. And heaven is because of the man, not man because of
heaven. And from the things he himself did, it is clear. For Christ did not
take up a heavenly body. But lest having lengthened the word we withdraw today
again as debtors, for you know as many things as we promised yesterday, ready
to pay down today. For I put it off because of those who were left behind.
Since therefore those who were left behind have filled up their own things and
have granted us their own presence, a filled table of the expenses, come, let
us set out the dishes, dishes not stale; for even if they were of yesterday,
yet they do not become stale. Why ever? It is not meats, that they may be
corrupted, but thoughts blossoming continually. For the meats are corrupted;
for it is a body; but the thoughts remaining become more fragrant. What then
were the things we said yesterday? For we too yesterday enjoyed a table and
those who were left behind were not damaged. "And it came to pass in the year
in which Ozias the king died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and
lifted up." Who says these things? Isaiah, the beholder of the Seraphim, who
consorted with marriage and did not quench the grace. And you have attended to
the prophet, but hear the prophet today also: "Go out, you and Jasoub your
son." It is necessary not to run past these things either. "Go out, you and
your son." Did the prophet have a son? Therefore if a son, also a wife, in
order that you may learn that marriage is not a base thing, but fornication is
an evil. But since we discourse with certain many and say: Why do you not live
rightly, why do you not display the accurate life? How can I, he says, if I do
not renounce a wife, if I do not renounce children, if I do not renounce
affairs? Why? Is marriage a hindrance? the wife has been given you as a
helper, not a plotter. Did the prophet not have a wife? And marriage did not
become a hindrance of the Spirit; but he both consorted with the wife and was
a prophet. Did Moses not have a wife? But nevertheless he both split rocks and
changed air and spoke with God and checked God-sent wrath. Did Abraham not
have a wife? And he became father of many nations and of the Church; for he
had Isaac as a son. Did this man not become for him


a subject of successes? Did he not offer up the child, the fruit of the
marriage? did he not become both father and a lover of God? was it not
possible to see a priest come to be from his own inward parts? a priest and a
father? nature being conquered and reverence prevailing? inward parts being
trampled and pious successes prevailing; and the father being loosed and a
lover of God being crowned? did you not see him wholly a lover of children and
a lover of God? did marriage hinder at all? What then? was the mother of the
Maccabees not a woman? did she not give seven children, the chorus of the
saints? did she not see them bearing witness? did she not stand like a
mountain not being shaken? did she not stand at each of them bearing witness,
both a mother of martyrs and seven times she bore witness? For while those
were being tortured, she herself received the blow. For she did not bear the
things that came about so without passion; for she was a mother and the
outrage of nature displayed its own power; but she was not conquered. For she
was a sea and waves; but just as the sea
being mad is dissolved, so also nature being raised was bridled by the fear of
God. How did she anoint them? how did she nourish them? how did she present
seven temples to God, golden statues, or rather even more honorable than gold?
4.3 For gold does not shine forth such as a soul of martyrs. The tyrant stood
and being defeated by one woman he withdrew. That man besieged with weapons
and she prevailed by eagerness; that man kindled a furnace and she the virtue
of the Spirit; that man moved a camp and she was shifting toward angels; she
saw the tyrant below and thought of the one reigning above; she saw the
tortures below and counted the prizes above; she saw the present punishment
and thought of the immortality to come. [Wherefore Paul also said: "While we
look not at the things that are seen, but at the things that are not seen."]
Did marriage become any hindrance? What then of Peter, the foundation of the
Church, the mad lover of Christ, the untrained in speech and prevailing over
rhetors, the unlearned and who stopped the mouths of philosophers, who
dissolved the Greek religion like a spider's web, who ran about the world, who
netted the sea and fished the world, did this man not also have a wife? Yes,
he had. And that he had, hear the evangelist. And what does he say? "Jesus
went in toward the mother-in-law of Peter who was fevered." Where there is a
mother-in-law, there also a wife; where a wife, there also marriage. What then
of Philip? did he not have four daughters? And where there are four daughters,
there also a wife and marriage. What then of Christ? From a virgin indeed, but
he was present at a marriage and brought in a gift; "For they have not," he
says, "wine"; and he made the water wine, honoring marriage by the virginity,
wondering at the matter by the gift; in order that you may not abhor marriage,
but may hate fornication. At my own risk I guarantee your salvation, even if
you have a wife. Attend to yourself. If a wife is useful, she is a helper of
yours. What then if she is not useful? Make her useful. Did there not come to
be both good wives and bad, in order that you may not have a pretext? Of what
sort was Job's? But Sarah was good. I will show you a wife base and wicked.
Job's wife did not harm the husband; she was wicked and base, for she also
advised him to blaspheme. What then? did she shake the tower? did she bring
down the adamant? did she prevail over the rock? did she knock out the
soldier? did she pierce the hull? did she root out the tree? None of these
things; but she struck against it, and the tower became more secure; she
herself raised the waves, and the ship was not sunk, but sailed with a fair
wind; his fruit was gathered, and the tree was not shaken; the leaves fell,
and the root remained unshaken. I say these things, in order that no one may
make pretext of a wife's vice. Is she base? Correct her. But, he says, she
cast me out of paradise. But she also brought you up into heavens. The nature
is the same,


but the mind is different. But Job's was base? But Susanna was good. But the
Egyptian was unchaste? But Sarah was orderly. Did you see that one? Look at
this one too; since also among men some are base, and some zealous. Joseph was
good, but the elders unchaste. You saw everywhere vice and virtue, these
things not being judged by nature, but being divided by the mind. No pretexts
for me? But let us hasten to the debts and the payment. "And it came to pass
in the year in which Ozias the king died." I am going to say why the prophet
marks the season; for we were inquiring yesterday, why ever, when all the
prophets are accustomed to say the time of the life of the kings, and this man
himself too, here the custom was loosed; and he does not say: in the days of
Ozias, but in the end of Ozias. This I wish to loose today. For even if the
heat is much, yet the dew of the word is greater; for even if it forces the
body as it grows soft, yet the soul in its prime is gladdened. Do not tell me
heat and sweat; if you sweat in the body, you wipe your mind. The three
children were in a furnace and suffered nothing, but the furnace became dew.
When you think of sweat, think also of the wage and the return. For a diving
man dares to throw himself down into the depth of the waters for nothing other
than for pearls, the subject of the war. But I do not slander the matter, but
the unchaste mind. And you, in order that you may take an unfailing treasure
and plant a vine in your soul, do you not bear heat, nor sweat? Do you not see
those sitting in a theatre, how they sweat and receive the ray with bare head,
in order that they may become captives of death, in order that they may become
slaves of a harlot? Those men labor unto destruction, and you grow faint unto
salvation? You are an athlete and a soldier. Do not therefore grow weary
toward the dangers, but bear the contests with good courage. Who then is that
Ozias and why did he say this end of his? This Ozias was a king and a
righteous man and flourishing with many successes; but later at some time he
came into arrogance, into arrogance, the mother of the evils, into
boastfulness, the tumult of those who are sick, into pride, the destruction of
the devil. For nothing is worse than arrogance; for this reason we also spent
the whole word upon this yesterday, taking down the arrogance and teaching the
humility.
4.4 Shall I tell you how great a good humility is and how great an evil
arrogance? A sinner conquered a righteous man, the publican the Pharisee, and
words prevailed over works. How words? The publican says: "God, be merciful to
me the sinner." The Pharisee says: "I am not as the many of men, a robber or a
greedy man." But what? "I fast twice in the week, I tithe my belongings." The
Pharisee showed works of righteousness; the publican said words of humility,
and speeches prevailed over works and so great a treasure was blown away and
so great a poverty was changed into wealth. Two ships came having freight;
both went into the harbor; but the publican was moored well to the harbor, and
the Pharisee endured a shipwreck, in order that you may learn how great an
evil is arrogance. Are you righteous? Do not humble your brother. Do you
flourish with successes? Do not reproach the neighbor and cut under your
encomium. As great as you are, so much humble yourself. And attend with
accuracy to what I say, beloved. The righteous man ought rather to fear
arrogance than the sinner (this I said yesterday too, and I say today because
of those left behind yesterday) because the sinner has the conscience accusing
him and it is necessary for him to be humble-minded, but the righteous man is
lifted by successes. As therefore in the case of ships, those who have an
empty ship do not fear a band of pirates, for they do not come to pierce
through the ship that has nothing, but those who have a ship full of freight


fear pirates (for the pirate goes away there, where there is gold, where
silver, where precious stones), so also the devil does not easily insult the
sinner, but the righteous man, where there is much wealth. Since often the
arrogance is from a plot of the devil, it is necessary to be sober. As great
as you are, so much humble yourself. When you have gone up to the height, you
have need to be made secure, lest you fall. Wherefore our Lord also says:
"When you have done all things, say that We are unprofitable servants." Why do
you think great things, being a man, kin of the earth, of one essence with the
ash, not reckoning the changes in the nature, those in the mind, those in the
purpose of the affairs? Today rich, tomorrow poor; today healthy, tomorrow
sick; today rejoicing, tomorrow grieving; today in glory, tomorrow in
dishonor; today in youth, tomorrow in old age. Does any of human things stand?
but it imitates the course of river streams. For at the same time it has
appeared, and more easily than a shadow it leaves us. Why then do you think
great things, O man, as smoke or vanity? "For man was likened to vanity." "His
days are as smoke." "The grass was dried, and its flower fell out." I say
these things, not as cheapening the substance, but bridling the arrogance. For
also "a man is great, and a merciful man is honorable." But this Ozias being
king and wearing the diadem, since he was righteous, thought great things at
some time and having thought great things and greater than the worth, he went
into the sanctuary. And hear how: He went into the holy of holies, it says,
then he says, I wish to burn incense. Being a king he seizes the rule of
priesthood. I wish, he says, to burn incense, since I am righteous. But remain
within the proper bounds; other are the bounds of kingship and other the
bounds of priesthood; but this is greater than that. For a king is not
apparent from the things that appear, nor from the stones fixed to him, and
the gold he wears, ought the king to be judged. For this man has been allotted
to manage the things upon the earth; but the ordinance of the priesthood sits
above. "Whatever you bind upon the earth, shall be bound in heaven." The king
has been entrusted the things here, I the heavenly; I when I speak, I mean the
priest. Do not therefore, if you see a priest unworthy, slander the
priesthood; for one must not slander the matter, but the one who has used the
good thing badly; since Judas also became a traitor, but this is not an
accusation of the apostleship, but of that man's mind; not a charge of the
priesthood, but of the bad mind.
4.5 And you therefore do not slander the priesthood, but the one who has used
the good thing badly. Since when someone discourses with you and says: Did you
see this Christian? say: But I discourse with you not about persons, but about
matters. Since how many physicians became executioners and have given poisons
instead of medicines? But I do not slander the art, but the one who used the
art badly. How many sailors sank ships? But not the seafaring, but the bad
mind of those men. If a Christian is base, do not accuse the doctrine and the
priesthood, but blame the one who became base from laziness, or rather do not
blame, but pray and weep so that he may be changed. The king is entrusted
bodies, but the priest souls; the king remits remainders of money, but the
priest remainders of sins; that man forces, this man exhorts; [that man by
necessity, this man by purpose;] that man has perceptible weapons, this man
spiritual weapons; that man wars toward barbarians, for me the war is toward
demons. This rule is greater; for this reason the king brings the head under
the hands of the priest and everywhere in the Old Testament priests anointed
kings. But that king having gone beyond his own lists and having leapt over
the measure of the kingship attempted to add and went into the sanctuary with
authority, wishing to burn incense. What then did the priest? "It is not
permitted you, Ozias, to burn incense." See


boldness of speech, an unenslaved mind, a tongue touching heaven, an
unconquerable freedom, the body of a man and the mind of an angel, walking on
the ground and having citizenship in heaven. He saw the king and did not see
the purple; he saw the king and did not see the diadem. Do not tell me the
kingship, where there is lawlessness. "It is not permitted you, O king, to
burn incense into the holy of holies;" you go beyond the lists, you seek the
things not given; for this reason you will also lose the things you received.
"It is not permitted you to burn incense, but to the priests." This is not
yours, but mine. Did I seize your purple? Do not seize my priesthood. "It is
not permitted you to burn incense, but to the priests the sons of Aaron."
[After a long time, after the end of Aaron this came about.] And why did he
not say: To the priests, only, but he remembered the father too? Something
such happened to come about in that time. For Dathan and Abiron and Core rose
up against Aaron; the earth was opened and swallowed them; fire came from
above and burned them. Wishing therefore himself to remind of that history,
that they too once insulted the priesthood, but it was not defeated, but a
multitude came on and God defended them. "It is not permitted you to burn
incense, but to the priests the sons of Aaron." He did not say: Think what
those who did these things then endured; he did not say: Think that those who
rose up were burned, but he said Aaron who was avenged and brought him into
remembrance of the history, all but saying this: Do not dare the things of
Dathan, lest you suffer such things as in the case of Aaron. But king Ozias
did not bear it, but being inflated by the arrogance he went into the
sanctuary, he opened the holy of holies, wishing to burn incense. What then
did God? Since therefore the priest was despised not well and the word of
priesthood was being trampled and the priest was no longer able to do anything
(for it belongs to a priest only to reprove and to display boldness of speech,
not to move weapons, nor to seize shields, nor to shake a spear, nor to
stretch a bow, nor to send an arrow, but only to reprove and to display
boldness of speech), since therefore the priest reproved, and the king did not
yield, but he moved weapons and shields and spears and used his own abundance,
the priest, as if pleading his case toward God, says: I have done my own
thing, I can do nothing more, help yourself a priesthood being trampled; laws
are being wronged, ordinances are being overturned. What then did the lover of
men? He punishes the daring man, and at once leprosy blossomed out onto his
forehead. Where the shamelessness, there also the punishment. Did you see
loving-kindness of the punishment of God? He did not send a thunderbolt, he
did not shake earth, he did not throw heaven into confusion, but the leprosy
blossomed out, not in another place but upon the forehead, in order that he
may wear the trophy of the punishment, in order that it may be as letters
lying on a pillar. For it did not come about because of him, but because of
those after these things. For being able to bring on the punishment worthily,
he did not bring it on, but like a law in some high place saying: Do not do
such things, lest you suffer such things. An ensouled law went out and the
forehead sent forth a voice brighter than a trumpet. Letters were written on
the forehead, letters not able to be wiped out (for it was not ink, that
someone might wipe it out), but from nature the leprosy both made this man
unclean, in order that it might make the others clean. And just as the
condemned, whenever they receive a cord, they lead them out having the cord
upon the mouth, so also this man instead of a cord had the leprosy upon the
forehead being led away, since he insulted the priesthood. I say these things,
not slandering kings, but those drunk with arrogance and wrath, in order that
you may learn that the priesthood is greater than kingship.
4.6 For always God, when the soul sins, punishes the body. So he also did in
the case of Cain. The soul sinned having worked a murder, and his body was
paralyzed. How?


Hear: "Groaning and trembling," he says, "you shall be upon the earth." And
Cain went about discoursing with all, sending forth a voice by silence,
training by voicelessness. The tongue was silent and the members cried out,
and he discoursed with all why he groans, why he trembles: I killed a brother,
I worked a murder. Moses after these things said through writings, that man
went about saying to all through deeds: "You shall not murder." Did you see a
mouth being silent and a deed crying out? did you see an ensouled law being
carried about? did you see a pillar going around? did you see a punishment an
undoing of honor? did you see a chastisement becoming a subject of training?
did you see a soul having sinned and the flesh being punished? And very
reasonably. So then also in the case of John, the soul sinned and the tongue
was bound; for the instrument of the tongue having become unuseful henceforth,
that man who begot the voice, Zacharias, was punished. So then Ozias too was
made leprous, having sinned, in the forehead, in order that that man might be
trained. And the king went out henceforth having become an example to all and
the sanctuary was cleansed and he was cast out, no one pushing, and wishing to
take on the priesthood he also lost what he had. And he went out from the
sanctuary. In the old time it was law that every leper be cast outside the
city; but now no longer. Why? Because God legislated as for children, then
there was leprosy of the body, but now the leprosy of the soul is sought. The
king therefore went out in leprosy and they did not cast him out of the city,
perhaps respecting his diadem and the kingship, but he sat again acting
lawlessly. What then did God? Being angry at the Jews he stopped the prophecy.
All these things then because of the saying of the prophet, in order that I
may pay down the debt. But let us return to the matter set forth. The king
went out from the sanctuary having been made leprous. It being necessary then
to cast him out from the city also according to the custom, as unclean, the
people bears with him sitting within and does nothing of the things owed, not
small not great, unto a word of boldness of speech. Since therefore they let
him go, God turns away from them and stops the grace of the prophecy; and very
reasonably. For in return for their having set aside his law and having shrunk
from casting out an unclean man, he stops the prophetic gift. "And there was
not a precious word then, and there was not a saying that prophesied", that
is, God did not discourse with them through prophets; for the Spirit did not
breathe upon them through which they spoke, since they had the unclean man;
and upon the unclean the grace of the Spirit did not work. For this reason it
was not present, it did not appear to the prophets, but it is silent and
hides. And in order that what is said may become clear, I will make it
apparent upon an example. Just as a man disposed toward someone with
affection, when that man has madly grieved him over some matter, says toward
him: I will no longer see you, I do not speak to you, so also God did then.
For since they provoked him, not casting out Ozias: No longer, he says, do I
speak to your prophets, no longer do I send grace of Spirit. See a punishment
full of gentleness. For he did not send thunderbolts, nor did he shake the
city from foundations; but what? You do not wish me to be avenged? he says, I
do not consort with you. For was I not able to cast him out? But I wished to
leave the remainder to you. You do not wish? Neither do I consort with you,
nor do I move the soul of the prophets. The grace of the Spirit does not work.
Therefore from that there was silence, enmity of God and men. Since therefore
that man after these things died, the subject of the curses was also loosed.
Since therefore the prophet had a long time not having prophesied, and when
that man had ended the wrath was loosed and the prophecy returned. Henceforth
the prophet necessarily marks the season and says: "And it came to pass in the
year in which Ozias the king died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high
and lifted up." When he died, then I saw the Lord. For before they did not see
God being angry at us. For the death of the unclean man came and loosed the
wrath. For this reason, saying everywhere the life of the kings, here


he said an end of Ozias; ["It came to pass," he says, "in the year in which
Ozias the king died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and lifted
up."] But again it is possible to see here the loving-kindness of God. The
unclean man died and God was reconciled to men. Why, there not being
successes, did it come about, but by his dying? Because he is a lover of men
and does not reckon exactly in such things; he sought one thing only, that the
unclean man go out. Knowing these things then, let us chase arrogance, let us
embrace humility, let us send up the accustomed glory to the Father and to the
Son and to the Holy Spirit now and always and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.

5.t On the remaining things concerning Ozias
5.1 Come then today let us give the end to the narratives concerning Ozias and
let us put the roof henceforth upon the word, lest we too be laughed at, just
as that man, who in the gospels attempted to build the tower, but was not
able, lest some one of those present say also about us: "This man began to
build, but was not able." And in order that the things said may become clearer
to you, let us take up small things of the things said before, lest the word
enter into the spiritual theatre for us without a head, but having received
its own face it may become known to the spectators. For the same things will
be a reminder to those who have heard, and a teaching to those who have not.
The other day then we said how Ozias was pious, and how he became base and
from where and how far he slipped of the arrogance; and today it is necessary
to say how he went into the sanctuary, how he attempted to burn incense, how
the priest hindered, how that man did not yield, how he drew upon himself the
wrath of God, how he ended his life in the leprosy and for what reason the
prophet, having passed over the days of his life, remembered the end, saying
thus: "In the year in which Ozias the king died." For this is that because of
which we moved the whole history from the beginning; but attend accurately.
"And it came to pass," he says, "when Ozias the king became strong, his heart
was lifted up unto destroying, and he did wrong in the Lord his God." What is
the manner of the wrong? "He went in," he says, "into the temple of the Lord,
to burn incense upon the altar of the incense." O the daring, O the
shamelessness. He dared the very holy inner rooms, he leapt into the holy of
holies, which was a region untrodden for all, except the high priest alone,
this he attempted to profane. Such is a soul, once having despaired of its own
salvation; nowhere does it stand from the madness, but giving over the reins
of its salvation to the irrational desires it is borne everywhere and, like an
ungovernable horse, having thrown the bit out of the mouth and having cast the
rider from the back supine, it is borne more violently than every wind and is
unbearable to those who meet it, all fleeing from under, and no one daring to
hold it, so also a soul, having thrown out the fear of God that bits it and
having cast all the reasoning that drives, runs over the regions of vice,
until carrying it into the pits of destruction it throw down its own
salvation. Wherefore one must continually check it and, as with some bit, by
the reasoning of piety pull back its irrational impulse; which Ozias did not
do, but he acted lawlessly toward the very highest rule of all. For priesthood
is a more august and greater rule even than the kingship itself. For do not
tell me the purple, nor the diadem, nor the golden garments. All those things
are a shadow and cheaper than the spring flowers. "For all glory of man," he
says, "is as a flower of grass", even if you speak of the royal itself. Do not
then these things to me


tell, but if you wish to see the difference of a priest toward a king, examine
the measure of the authority given to each and you will see the priest sitting
much higher than the king. For even if the royal throne appears splendid to us
from the stones fixed to it and from the gold that binds it round, yet
nevertheless it has been allotted to manage the things upon the earth and it
has nothing more than this authority; but the throne of the priesthood is
founded in the heavens and has been entrusted to administer the things there.
Who says these things? The very king of the heavens; "For whatever you bind,"
he says, "upon the earth, shall be bound in the heavens; and whatever you
loose upon the earth, shall be loosed in the heavens." What could be equal to
this honor? Heaven takes the beginning of the judgment from the earth. Since
the judge sits on the earth, the Master follows the servant; and whatever this
man judges below, those things that one ratifies above. And the priest has
stood in the middle of God and of the nature of men, bringing down the honors
from there toward us and leading up the entreaties from us there, reconciling
him when he is angry to the common nature, snatching us who have offended from
his hands. For this reason God also bringing the very royal head puts it under
the hands of the priest, training us that this ruler is greater than that one;
"for the lesser is blessed by the better." But concerning priesthood and how
great is the greatness of the dignity we will make clear at another time; for
now let us see the greatness of the wrong of the king, or rather of the
tyrant. "He went into the temple of the Lord, Azarias the priest also went in
behind him." Did I not say in vain that the priest is greater than the king?
For not as going to drive out a king, but as a runaway and an ungrateful
servant, so he went in with violence, like some noble puppy having run at the
unclean beast, so as to lead it out of the master's house.
5.2 Did you see a soul of a priest full of much boldness of speech and of a
high mind? He did not look at the bulk of the rule, he did not think how great
it is to hold a soul drunk with desire, he did not hear Solomon saying: "The
threat of a king is like wrath of a lion"; but having looked toward the true
king of the heavens and having thought of that tribunal and the accounts, and
having fortified himself with these reasonings, so he leapt upon the tyrant.
For he knew, he knew clearly that "the threat of a king is like wrath of a
lion", but for those who look toward the earth, and for a man imagining heaven
and prepared to let the soul go within in the inner rooms, or to overlook the
holy laws being insulted, that man was cheaper than every dog. For nothing is
weaker than the one who transgresses the divine laws; as therefore nothing is
stronger than the one who avenges the divine laws. "For he who does the sin is
a slave of the sin", even if he have countless crowns upon the head; but he
who works the righteousness is more kingly even than the king himself, even if
he be last of all. Having philosophized these things toward himself that noble
man went in upon the king. Let us go in together then also, if it seems good,
and let us see what he discourses toward the king. For it is permitted; and it
is not a small thing unto a word of benefits, to see a king being reproved by
a priest. What then does the priest say? "It is not permitted you, Ozias, to
burn incense to the Lord." He did not name him king, nor did he call him from
the name of the rule, since having anticipated he had cast himself out of the
honor. Did you see boldness of speech of a priest? Therefore learn also
gentleness. For we need not boldness of speech only when we are going to
reprove, but also gentleness, and gentleness rather than boldness of speech.
For since those who sin turn away from and hate none of men so as the one who
is going to reprove, and they desire to lay hold of a pretext


so as to leap away and to flee the rebuke, one must therefore hold them by the
gentleness and by the fairness. For such a man, not only sending forth a
voice, but also being seen, will be heavy to those who sin; "For he is heavy
to us," he says, "even being seen"; because of these things one must display
much gentleness. Because of these things the word also brought before your
eyes both the one who had sinned and the one wishing to correct him. For the
wise of the physicians, when they are going to cut rotten members, or to draw
out stones wedged in the passages, or to correct some other fault of nature,
do not do this having taken the sick man into a corner, but having set him
forth in the middle of the marketplaces and having stood a theatre from those
passing by, so they bring on the cutting. And they do this, not wishing to
parade in human misfortunes, but training those who see in others' bodies to
make much provision of their own health. So also Scripture does; whenever it
takes some one of those who sin, it sets him forth upon a high proclamation,
not in the middle of marketplaces, but in the middle of the earth; and having
stood the theatre of the world, so it displays the healing, training us to be
more secure about our own salvation. Let us see then how the priest attempted
to correct this man then. He did not say: O defiled and all-defiled, you have
overturned and confused all things, you have leapt out into an extreme of
impiety; nor did he stretch out long words of accusation, but just as those
who cut are eager to do this <the hole> shortly, stealing the perception of
the pain by the speed of the cutting, so also this man by brevity of speech
cut under the inflammation of the king. For what a cutter is in the case of
wounds, this reproof is in the case of sins. And the fairness among the other
things he displays to us also through the brevity of speech. And if you wish
to see also the cutting of the words, and where he hid the iron, hear: "It is
not permitted you," he says, "to burn incense to the Lord, but to the priests,
the sons of Aaron, those who have been sanctified." Here he gave the blow.
How? I say. For why did he not say: To the priests, simply, but he added
Aaron? This Aaron became high priest first and such a daring was dared in his
times. For Dathan and Core and Abiron having stood together against him with
certain others too attempted to cast him out of the rule; but some the earth
having split swallowed, and others fire brought down from above burned up.
Wishing therefore to remind him of this history, he reminded him of Aaron who
was then wronged, so as to send this man's mind across to the misfortune of
those who had done wrong. Except nothing more came about; but not from the
priest, but from the rashness of the king. For it being necessary to praise
the things said and to confess thanks for the counsel, but he was angered, it
says, and worked the sore more hard. For sin is not so great an evil as the
shamelessness after the sin.
5.3 But David was not so; but how? After the accusation that came about by
Nathan upon Bersabee: "I have sinned against the Lord," he says. Did you see a
crushed heart? did you see a humbled soul? did you see how even the falls of
the saints are useful? For just as the splendid of bodies even in the sickness
display to us many traces of the good form, so also the souls of the saints
even in the very falls bear the symbols of their own virtue. And yet that man
was accused in the middle of the palaces by the prophet, many being present;
but this man within in the inner rooms, and he had the reproof without
witness; but not even so did he bear the rebuke. What then? did he remain
incurable? By no means, and this through the loving-kindness of God; but just
as in the case of the lunatic, the disciples not having been able to cast out
the demon, Christ


says: "Bring him to me here"; so also here, the priest not having been able to
cast out the sin, a disease harder than every demon, God himself henceforth
handles the sick man. And what does he do? He sends leprosy upon his forehead.
["For it came to pass," he says, "in his threatening the priest, leprosy rose
in his forehead."] And he went out henceforth. Just as those being led away
unto death, having a cord upon the mouth, a symbol of the condemning vote, so
also he himself carrying the symbol of the dishonor upon the forehead, not
executioners dragging him, but the leprosy itself instead of executioners
pushing upon the head. He went in to take priesthood, but he lost the kingship
too; he went in to become more august and he became more accursed. For he was
henceforth more dishonored than every private man, being unclean. So great an
evil is it not to remain upon the measures given us by God, whether one do
this upon honor, or upon knowledge. Do you not see this sea how it is
unbearable in its violences, with how many waves it is peaked? But
nevertheless being raised to a great height and going forward with much wrath,
whenever it come to the bound set for it by God, having dissolved the waves,
it returns again toward itself. And yet what could be weaker than sand? But
that is not what hinders, but the fear of the one who set it. And if this
example does not chasten you, let the things concerning Ozias train you, the
things now said to us. But since we have seen the wrath of God and the worthy
repayment, come, let us also display his loving-kindness and the much
fairness. For one must not move only the words about the wrath, but also those
about the goodness, in order that we may neither throw the hearers into
despair, nor into laziness. So Paul also does and mixes the exhortation with
both these, saying thus: "See then the goodness and the severity of God", in
order that both by the fear and by the good hopes he may raise the one who has
fallen. Did you see severity of God? See also goodness. How then shall we see
the goodness? If we learn of what things Ozias was worthy. Of what things then
was he worthy? At the same time he set foot upon the holy forecourts with so
great shamelessness, of countless thunderbolts and of the last punishment and
vengeance. For if those who first dared these things paid this penalty, those
about Dathan and Core and Abiron, much more ought this man to be punished so,
who was not even chastened by those men's misfortunes. But God did not do so,
but first he brought words full of much fairness through the priest. And what
Christ exhorted men to do, when they sin toward one another, this God did
toward the man. "For when," he says, "your brother sin against you, go,
reprove him between you and him alone." So also God reproved this king. And
Christ says: "If he do not hear you, let him be to you as the Gentile and the
publican"; but God by loving-kindness going beyond his own laws, not even so
cut him off, but although he had disobeyed and been vexed he did not throw him
away, but again he receives him and
trains by a manner that has correction rather than punishment. For he did not
send a thunderbolt from above, nor did he burn up the shameless head, but he
trained by the leprosy only. And the things concerning Ozias are such. And I,
having added one thing still only, will stop the word. And what is this? Which
we sought of old and from the beginning; for what reason in the outer affairs
and in the prophecies, when all are accustomed to mark the times of the life
of the kings, this man having left this remembers the time of the end of
Ozias, saying thus: "And it came to pass in the year in which Ozias the king
died." And yet it was possible to say the time of the one then reigning, as
was custom for the prophets; but he did not do this. For what reason then did
he not do it? There was an old law that the leper be


driven out of the city, so that both those in the city may become better and
he himself may not be set forth as a starting-point of mockeries and jeering
for those who wish to insult, but remaining outside the city may have the
solitude as a curtain of the misfortune. This this king too ought to have
endured after the leprosy; but he did not endure it, those in the city having
respected him because of the rule, but he remained in his house secretly. This
provoked God, this hindered the prophecy; and what came about in the case of
Eli: "A precious word there was and there was not a vision distinguishing."
But you, learn here too for me the loving-kindness of God. For he did not
overturn the city, nor did he destroy those dwelling in it; but what friends
do toward the equals in honor of the friends, whenever they have something
just to accuse, remaining in a passing-over in silence, this God also did
toward that nation, which was worthy of a greater punishment and vengeance.
For I cast him out, he says, of the sanctuary, but you not even of the city; I
having bound him through the leprosy, handed him over as a private man, but
you did not even so prevail, but you did not bear to cast out of the city the
one condemned by me. And yet what sort of king would have borne this gently,
but would he not have overturned the city from the foundations, seeing the one
ordered to be moved to exile spending time in the city? But God did not do
this; for he was "God, and not a man". And when he ended, with this man's life
he also dissolved together the wrath toward those men and opened the doors of
the prophecy and returned again toward them. And you, learn also from the
manner of the reconciliation the loving-kindness of God. For if someone should
examine the word of the just, one ought not even then to have been reconciled.
For what reason? Because it did not become their success to cast Ozias out.
For they themselves having taken him did not drive him away, but the end
having come on by a law of nature then cast him out of the city. But God does
not reckon exactly as far as these things toward us, but he seeks one thing
only, a form of the reconciliation toward us. For all these things then let us
give thanks to him, let us glorify his unspeakable loving-kindness; of which
may we too appear worthy, by the grace and loving-kindness of our Lord Jesus
Christ, with whom glory to the Father, together with the Holy Spirit, unto the
ages of the ages. Amen.

6.t On the saying: "And it came to pass in the year in which Ozias the king
died", and on repentance.
6.1 At last at some time we have sailed through the sea concerning Ozias, and
we have scarcely sailed through, not because of the length of the way, but
because of the love of learning of you who sail with us. So also a steersman
having ambitious passengers and desiring to see lovely cities does not finish
the way in one day, even if the interval should be of one day, but he is
forced to spend more time, mooring the hull at each harbor, allowing them to
set foot at each city, so as to grant something to the desire of those who
sail with him. This we also did, not sailing around islands, nor displaying
seaports and harbors and cities, but virtue of men who have succeeded and
laziness of those who have fallen, shamelessness of kings and boldness of
speech of priests, wrath of God and loving-kindness, both having come about
toward correction. But since henceforth we have arrived at the royal city, let
us no longer delay, but having composed ourselves, as being about to go into a
city, so let us go up into the metropolis above, Jerusalem, our mother, the
free, where the Seraphim, where the Cherubim, where thousands


of archangels, where myriads of angels, where the royal throne. Let no one
therefore be present who is profane, nor accursed (for we are going to dare
mystical narratives), let no one unclean and unworthy of this hearing; or
rather let both profane and accursed be present, every one, but having laid
aside the uncleanness and all the wickedness outside, so let him enter. For
that man who had the filthy garments, for this reason the father of the
bridegroom drove him out of the bride-chamber and of the holy bridal room, not
because he had filthy garments, but because having them he went in so. For he
did not say toward him: Why do you not have a wedding garment? but: Why not
having a wedding garment did you go in so? Upon the crossroads, he says, you
stood begging and I was not ashamed of your poverty, I did not abhor your
filth, but having set you free from all that cheapness, I led you into the
holy bride-chamber and counted you worthy of royal dinners and led to the
highest honor the one who was worthy of last punishment; but you did not even
by the benefits become better, but you remained upon the customary vice,
having insulted the wedding, and having insulted the bride-chamber. Go away
therefore henceforth and give the owed punishment of such insensibility. Let
each of us therefore look, lest somehow he hear this voice and, having thrown
away every reasoning unworthy of the spiritual teaching, so let him share the
holy table. "And it came to pass," he says, "in the year in which Ozias the
king died, I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and lifted up." How he
saw, I do not know; for that he saw, he said, but the how he was silent about;
I receive the things said, I do not busy myself about the things silenced; I
contemplate the things revealed, I do not curiously work the things covered
over; for this reason they have been covered over. The narrative of the
Scriptures is a golden veil, the warp gold, the woof gold. I do not weave in
the weavings of spiders, the weakness of my reasonings. "Do not move
landmarks," he says, "which your fathers set." To move landmarks is not safe;
and how shall we transpose the things which God set for us? Do you wish to
learn how he saw God? Become a prophet yourself too. And how is this possible,
he says, having a wife and taking care of the rearing of children? It is
possible, if you wish, beloved. For he himself also had a wife and was father
of two children, but none of these things hindered. For marriage is not a
hindrance of the going abroad toward heaven; since if it were a hindrance and
the wife were going to plot against us, God making her from the beginning
would not have called her a helper. I wished then to say what ever it is that
God sits; for God does not sit; for the posing is of bodies; but the divine is
bodiless.
6.2 I wished then to say what ever a throne of God is; for God is not enclosed
in a throne; for the divine is uncircumscribed; but I have feared lest
lingering in the teaching about these things I drag out the debt. For I see
all gaping toward the Seraphim, not today only, but also from the first day;
wherefore, like a crowd of men, the word cutting through with much rush the
multitude of the thoughts that meet, is eager toward that interpretation. "And
the Seraphim stood round about him," he says. Behold the Seraphim, which of
old you all desired to see. View them then and fill the desire, but not with
tumult, nor with a hastening reasoning, which comes about in the case of royal
goings-out and goings-in; for there this very thing happens reasonably. For
the spear-bearers do not wait for the eyes of those who see, but before all
things are seen well, they force them to run past; but here it is not so; but
the word stands the viewing for you, until you go through all things, as many
as it is possible to go through. "And the Seraphim stood round about him."
Before the dignity of the nature he taught us the dignity from the nearness
according to place. For he did not say first what [they were] the Seraphim,
but he said where


they have stood. For this dignity is greater than that. How? Because it does
not so show those powers being great, the being Seraphim, as the standing near
the royal throne. For we too think those of the spear-bearers more notable,
whom we see driving near the royal pair. So also of the bodiless powers those
are brighter, whichever are near the throne. For this reason the prophet also,
having left discoursing about the dignity of their nature, first discourses
with us about the presidency of the place, knowing that this adornment is
greater and this is the beauty of those natures; for this is the glory and the
honor and all the safety, to appear round about that throne. This it is
possible to see also in the case of the angels; for Christ wishing to show
those too as great did not say that they are angels, and was silent; but "that
their angels continually see the face of my Father who is in the heavens". For
just as there it is greater than the angelic dignity to see the face of the
Father, so than the dignity of the Seraphim it is greater to stand round about
the throne and to have him in the middle. But this great thing is possible for
you too to take, if you wish. For he is not in the middle of the Seraphim
only, but also of us ourselves, if we wish. "For where there are two or
three," he says, "gathered into my name, there I am in the middle of them";
and "The Lord is near to the crushed in heart and he saves the humble in
spirit". For this reason Paul also cries: "Think the things above, where
Christ is sitting at the right hand of God." Did you see how he stood us with
the Seraphim, having led us near the royal throne? Then he says: "Six wings to
the one and six wings to the one." What do these six wings display to us? The
high and lifted and light and swift of those natures. For this reason Gabriel
too comes down winged, not because there are wings about that bodiless power,
but because he came down from the highest regions and having left the sojourns
above has arrived. And what does the number of the wings also wish? Here there
is no need of interpretation from us; for the word itself solved itself,
having explained to us their use. "For with two," he says, "they covered their
faces", walling the eyes as with some double barrier, because of not bearing
the brightness leaping out from that glory. "And with two they covered the
feet", because of the same astonishment. For we too are accustomed, when we
are held by some amazement, to gather the body from all sides. And why do I
speak of the body, when even the soul itself having suffered this from the
extreme appearing and drawing together its activities, flees toward the depth,
gathering itself from all sides with the body as with some wrapping? But let
no one hearing astonishment and amazement think some unpleasant agony comes
about in them; for with this astonishment there is also mixed some unbearable
pleasure. "And with two they flew." And this is a sign of continually reaching
after the high things and never looking below. "And they cried one to the
other: Holy, holy, holy." And the cry again is a greatest proof of the wonder
for us; for they do not hymn simply, but with a strong cry; and not even with
a cry simply, but they also do this continually. For the splendid of bodies,
even if they are splendid with excess, then only are accustomed to astonish
us, when first we take them with the eyes; but whenever we linger more in the
viewing of them, by the custom we dissolve the wonder, our eyes having
practiced henceforth upon the bodies. For this reason having seen a royal
image just now set up and gleaming bright from the colors, we are astonished;
but after one and a second day no longer. And why do I speak of a royal


image, when even upon the very rays of the sun we have suffered this same
thing, than which no body could be brighter? Thus upon all bodies custom
dissolves the wonder; but upon the glory of God it is not so, but entirely the
contrary. For as much as these powers linger in the viewing of that glory, so
much more they are astonished and they stretch the wonder; for this very
reason also from when they came to be until now seeing that glory, they have
never ceased crying with astonishment; but what we suffer in a short time when
lightning is borne against our eyes, this those endure continually and without
pause they have the wonder with some pleasure. For they have not only cried,
but they also do this toward one another, which is a sign of the stretched-out
astonishment. So we too, when thunder is breaking down or the earth is being
shaken, not only have feared and leap up, but we also cry toward one another.
This the Seraphim also did; and for this reason one toward the other they
cried: Holy, holy, holy.
6.3 Did you then recognize this voice? is it ours or of the Seraphim? both
ours and of the Seraphim through Christ who took away the middle wall of the
fence and made peace the things in the heavens and the things upon earth,
through him who made the both one. For formerly this hymn was sung in the
heavens only; but when the Master counted it worthy to set foot upon the
earth, he also brought this melody down toward us. For this reason this great
high priest too, whenever he stands upon this holy table offering the rational
worship, offering the bloodless sacrifice, does not call us simply to this
praise, but having first spoken the Cherubim and having reminded of the
Seraphim, so he exhorts all to send up that most shuddering voice, being eager
by the memory of those who dance together to draw our mind up from the earth
toward the heavens and all but crying toward each of us and saying: You sing
with the Seraphim, stand with the Seraphim, with those spread out the wings of
the mind,

just as of the wrestlings in the Olympic contests the end is the crown, so
also of the fast the end is the pure communion; so that if we do not

6.4 For what, tell me, labor, what toil, what sweat is it for the sinner to
persuade himself that he is a sinner and to say this toward God? Do you see
how I did not say in vain that he wishes to lay hold of a small pretext from
us and he himself contributes the whole henceforth unto our salvation? Let us
therefore repent, let us weep, let us lament. Someone having often thrown away
a daughter spends the greater time of his life in laments and wailings; we
have lost a soul, and we do not lament? we have fallen out of salvation, and
we are not cut down? And why do I speak of soul and salvation? We have
provoked a Master so meek and gentle, and we do not bury ourselves? For he
goes beyond all goodwill not of a caring Master only, but also of a
child-loving father and of an affectionate mother by the care about us. "For
shall a woman forget," he says, "her child, so as not to have mercy on the
offspring of her belly? And if a woman should even forget, yet I will not
forget, says the Lord." Faithful then even before proof is the declaration;
for it is of God; except let us come, let us also furnish the proof through
the affairs. Rebecca once commanding the child to act the drama concerning the
theft of the blessings and having wrapped him well from all sides and having
put on the mask of the brother, when she saw him not even so being confident,
wishing to take out all fear of the child: "Upon me be your curse, child," she
says. Truly a word of a mother, and of one burning with love of the child. But
Christ did not say this only, but also did it. He did not promise only, but
also became. And Paul cries saying: "Christ bought us out from the curse of
the law, having become a curse for us." This one then we provoke; tell me; and
how is this not harder than Gehenna itself and than the unending worm and than
the unquenchable fire? When therefore you are about to come forward to the
holy table, think that there the king of all is also present; for he is
present truly, studying out the mind of each, and who comes forward with
fitting holiness, and who with a wicked conscience, with unclean and filthy
reasonings, with


defiled deeds. And if he find someone such, for the time he hands him over to
the law-court of the conscience; then if that man taking him whip him with the
reasonings and make him better, he receives him again; but if he remain
uncorrected, he commands the ungrateful and insensible one to be handed over
to his own hands. "It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of a living
God." I know that the words bite, but what am I to suffer? If I do not apply
the bitter medicines, the wounds will not be taken away; if I apply the
bitter, you do not bear the pain. Narrow for me from all sides; except it is
necessary to hold back the hand henceforth; for the things said are sufficient
toward correction of those who attend. But in order that they may become
useful not to you only, but also to others through you, come, let us
recapitulate them again. We discoursed about the Seraphim, we showed how great
a dignity it is to stand near the royal throne and that it is possible for men
too to possess this dignity. We spoke about the wings and the unapproachable
glory of God and about his loving-kindness that comes about toward us; we
added the cause of the cry and of the continual wonder and how in unceasing
viewing unceasing also is the doxology of the Seraphim; we reminded you into
what chorus we were initiated and with whom we hymned the common Master; we
added the words about repentance; and finally we showed how great an evil it
is to come forward to the mysteries with a wicked conscience and how it is not
possible for the one who has dared such things to flee. Let a wife learn these
things from a husband and children from a father and a servant from a master
and a neighbor from a neighbor and a friend from a friend, or rather let us
also discourse these things toward the enemies; for we are also responsible
for their salvation. For if we are commanded to raise up even their beasts of
burden that have fallen and to save and bring back those that have wandered,
much more must we bring back their soul that is wandering and raise up the one
that has fallen. If we so manage the things concerning ourselves and the
things concerning the neighbors, we shall be able to stand with boldness of
speech before the tribunal of Christ, with whom to the Father together with
the Holy Spirit be glory, now and always and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
