Of Saint Justin the Philosopher and Martyr
Hortatory Discourse to the Greeks.

As I begin the exhortation to you, O men of Greece, I pray God that I may have
what I ought to say to you, and that you, having laid aside your former love
of contention and having been delivered from the error of your ancestors, may
now choose what is profitable, thinking that no offense will be committed by
you against your ancestors if what is opposite to the things that formerly
seemed to them not well should now appear useful among you. For accurate
examination of things often shows that what seemed to be well is of another
sort, having tested the truth by a more exact trial. Since, then, the
discourse concerning true religion lies before us, than which nothing, I
think, is counted more honorable by those who have chosen to live without
danger, because of the judgment that is to be after the end of this life,
which not only our ancestors according to God proclaim, prophets and
lawgivers, but also those among you who have been thought to be wise, not
poets only but also philosophers, who profess among you to know the true and
divine knowledge, it seemed to me well first to examine the teachers of
religion, both ours and yours, who they were and how many and in what times
they lived, in order that those who formerly received from their ancestors the
falsely named religion may now at least, perceiving it, be delivered from that
old error, and that we may clearly and openly show ourselves following the
religion of the ancestors according to God.

Whom, then, O men of Greece, do you say are the teachers of your religion? The
poets? But it will not help you to say this to men who know the things of the
poets. For they know the most ridiculous theogony about the gods that is told
by them, as we can learn from Homer, the foremost among you and first of the
poets. For he says first that the generation of the gods had its beginning
from water. For he has written thus:

Ocean, origin of the gods, and mother Tethys.

Then it is necessary also to remind you of what he says about the one
considered among them the first god, whom he often names father of men and of
gods. For he said:

Zeus, who has been made dispenser of war for men.

He says, then, that he was not only dispenser of war to the army, but also
cause of perjury to the Trojans through his own daughter; Homer introduces
this one in love and complaining bitterly and wailing and plotted against by
the other gods, and at one time saying over his own son:

Woe is me, when Fate for me that Sarpedon, dearest of men,
Be vanquished by Patroclus son of Menoetius!

and at another time for Hector:

Ah me, I see with my eyes a man dear to me driven about the wall;
And my heart wails.

And what he says also about the other gods against the plot upon Zeus, they
know who meet with these verses:

When the other Olympians wished to bind him,
Hera and Poseidon and Pallas Athena.

And if the blessed gods had not feared him whom they call Briareus, Zeus would
have been bound by them. And as many things as Homer says about the erotic
incontinence of Zeus, it is necessary to remind you through the very words he
has spoken. For he said that he spoke thus to Hera:

For never yet did desire of goddess or of woman
Thus overpower my heart in my breast, pouring around it,
Not when I loved the wife of Ixion,
Nor when I loved Danae of the fair ankles, daughter of Acrisius,
Nor when I loved the daughter of far-famed Phoenix,
Nor when I loved Semele, nor Alcmene in Thebes,
Nor when I loved Demeter the fair-haired queen,
Nor when I loved glorious Leto, nor you yourself.

And what one can learn also about the other gods from Homer's poetry, and how
many things they have suffered at the hands of men, it is fitting to remind
you now. For he says that Ares and Aphrodite were wounded by Diomedes, and he
recounts the sufferings of many other gods as well. For thus we can learn from
Dione consoling her daughter. For she said to her:

Endure, my child, and bear up, though you are distressed.
For many of us who have Olympian homes have endured
From men, laying hard pains upon one another.
Ares endured, when Otus and strong Ephialtes,
Sons of Aloeus, bound him in a strong bond;
And he was bound in a bronze jar thirteen months.
Hera endured, when the strong son of Amphitryon
Struck her in the right breast with a three-barbed arrow;
Then incurable pain would have taken her.
And huge Hades among them endured a swift arrow,
When the same man, son of aegis-bearing Zeus,
Struck him in Pylos among the dead and gave him to pains.
But he went to the house of Zeus and long Olympus,
Grieving at heart, pierced with pains; and the arrow
Was driven in his sturdy shoulder, and it distressed his spirit.

And if it is also fitting to remind you of the battle of the remaining gods
opposite one another, your own poet will remind you, saying:

So great a crash arose of gods coming together in strife.
For against lord Poseidon
Stood Phoebus Apollo, having winged arrows,
And against Enyalios the goddess grey-eyed Athena.
And against Hera stood the noisy one of the golden distaff,

Artemis the arrow-pourer, sister of the far-shooter.
And against Leto stood strong helper Hermes.

These and such things Homer taught you about gods, and not Homer only but also
Hesiod. So that, if you believe your foremost poets, who also gave the
genealogies of your gods, it is necessary for you either to think them to be
such, or not to believe them to be gods at all.

And if you decline to cite the poets, because you say it is permitted them to
invent myths and to go through many things far from the truth about gods in
mythical fashion, whom else do you suppose you have as teachers of your
religion, or how do you say they have learned it? For it is impossible that
those who have not first learned from those who know should know matters so
great and divine. You will of course say the sages and philosophers; for to
these, as to a fortified wall, you are accustomed to flee whenever anyone
reports to you the opinions of the poets about gods. Therefore, since it is
fitting to begin from the ancient and first, beginning from there I will set
out the opinion of each, being much more ridiculous than the theology of the
poets. For Thales of Miletus, who first began natural philosophy, declared
water to be the principle of all things that are; for from water, he says, all
things are, and into water all things are resolved. And Anaximander after him,
starting from the same Miletus, said the infinite was the principle of all
things; for from this all things come to be and into this all things perish.
Third, Anaximenes, he too being from Miletus, says air is the principle of the
all; for from this all things come to be and into this all things are
resolved, he says. Heraclitus of Metapontum says fire is the principle of all
things; for from fire all things come to be and into fire all things end.
Anaxagoras of Clazomenae says the homoeomeries are the principles of all
things. Archelaus the Athenian, son of Apollodorus, says infinite air and the
density and rarity about it are the principle of all things. All these, having
their successions from Thales, pursued the philosophy called by them natural.

Then next from another starting-point Pythagoras the Samian, son of
Mnesarchus, calls numbers and the proportions and the harmonies in them
principles, and the elements composed from both, and moreover the monad and
the indefinite dyad. Epicurus the Athenian, son of Neocles, says the
principles of the things that are are bodies perceptible by reason, without
share of void, unbegotten, imperishable, able neither to be broken nor to take
formation from parts nor to be altered, and for this reason perceptible by
reason. Empedocles of Acragas, son of Meton, four elements, fire air water
earth, and two ruling powers, friendship and strife, of which the one is
unifying, the other dividing. You see, then, the disorder of those thought
among you to have been wise, whom you say have become your teach

ers of religion, some of them having declared water to be the principle of all
things, others air, others fire, others some other of the things already
named, and all of them using certain persuasive arguments for the construction
of the things that seemed to them not well, and attempting to show their own
dogma more honorable. These things have been said by them. How then is it
safe, O men of Greece, for those who wish to be saved to think they can learn
the true religion from these, who were not even able to persuade themselves
not to be at faction with one another nor to appear opposed to one another's
opinion?

But perhaps those who are unwilling to stand off from that ancient and old
error say they have received the account concerning religion not from those
already named, but from the philosophers thought among them to be most
renowned and most complete in virtue, Plato and Aristotle; for these, they
say, have learned the perfect and true religion. But I would first gladly ask
those who say this from whom they say these men, having learned, know; for it
is impossible that those who have not learned matters so great and divine from
some who know should either themselves know or be able to teach others
rightly. And second I think one must also examine the opinions of these; for
we shall know whether each of these also will appear to say the opposite of
the other. And if we find even these not agreeing with one another, I think it
is easy to know their ignorance clearly as well. For Plato, as if he had come
down from above and had accurately seen all things in the heavens, says the
highest God is in the fiery substance. But Aristotle, in the discourse to
Alexander the Macedonian setting out a certain brief definition of his own
philosophy, clearly and openly overthrows Plato's opinion, saying God is not
in the fiery substance; but inventing some fifth aetherial and unchangeable
body, he says he is in this. He has written at any rate thus: Not as some of
those who err concerning the divine say that God is in the fiery substance.
Then, as if not satisfied with the blasphemy against Plato, he also calls as
witness for the demonstration of the things said by him about the aetherial
body Homer, who was thrown out of the republic by him as a liar and as being
an imitator third from the images of the truth, as he himself said. For he has
written: Thus at any rate Homer also said:

And Zeus obtained the wide heaven in aether and clouds;

wishing to show his own opinion trustworthy from Homer's testimony, not
knowing that if he should use Homer as a witness for the demonstration that he
himself speaks truly, many of the things that seemed to him will appear not
true. For Thales of Miletus, who first among them began philosophy, taking the
occasion from him will reject his first opinions about principles. For
Aristotle himself having said that God and matter are principles

of all things, Thales, the oldest of all those among them, says water is the
principle of the things that are; for from water, he says, all things are, and
into water all things are resolved. And he conjectures first from the fact
that the seed of all animals, being a principle, is moist; and second that all
plants are nourished by moisture and bear fruit, and being without share of
moisture they dry up. Then, as if not satisfied with the things he
conjectures, he also cites Homer as trustworthy, speaking thus:

Ocean, who has been made origin for all.

How then will Thales not reasonably say to him: For what cause, O Aristotle,
when you wish to overthrow Plato's opinions, do you attend to Homer as
speaking truly, but when you have declared an opinion opposite to ours you
think Homer does not speak truly?

That, then, the sages so greatly wondered at among you do not even in the
other things appear to agree, it is easy to know also from these things. For
while Plato says there are three principles of the all, God and matter and
form (God the maker of all, matter that which underlies the first generation
of the things that come to be and furnishes him the occasion of the
craftsmanship, and form the pattern of each of the things that come to be),
Aristotle nowhere mentions form as a principle, but says there are two
principles, God and matter. And again, while Plato says that both the first
God and the ideas are in the first unwandering sphere of the highest heaven,
Aristotle says that after the first God there are not the ideas but certain
intelligible gods. Thus, then, they differ with one another about the things
in the heavens. So that it is fitting to know that those who were not even
able to know the things here among us, but even differed with one another
about these, will not appear trustworthy when they narrate about the things in
the heavens. That, then, not even the account concerning the human soul here
will agree for them is clear from the things said about it by each of them.
For Plato says it is three-parted, and says that one part of it is rational,
one spirited, and one appetitive; but Aristotle says the soul is not more
common, in which the perishable parts also are included, but the rational
only. And Plato has cried out saying, Every soul is immortal; but Aristotle,
naming it actuality, would have it not immortal but mortal. And the one says
it is ever-moving; but Aristotle says it is unmoved, preceding all motion.

But in these things they are convicted of thinking contrary to one another.
And if anyone should wish to look accurately at the things according to them,
they have not even chosen to remain in their own opinions. Plato at any rate
sometimes says there are three principles of the all, God and matter and form,
and sometimes four; for he also adds the universal soul. And again, having
first said matter is unbegotten, later he says it is generated; and having
first given form its own rank as a principle, and having declared it to be
given substance in itself,

later he says this very thing is in the thoughts. Moreover, having first
declared that everything that has come to be is perishable, later he says some
of the things that come to be can be indissoluble and imperishable. What,
then, is the cause that those thought among you to have been wise are at
faction not only with one another but also with themselves? Clearly, their
unwillingness to learn from those who know, but thinking themselves able by
their own human cleverness to know clearly the things in the heavens, although
they were not even able to know the things on the earth. At any rate some of
the philosophers among you say the human soul is in us, others around us; for
not even in this have they chosen to agree with one another, but, as if they
had divided ignorance in different ways, they have chosen to contend about the
soul also and to be at faction with one another. For some of them say the soul
is fire, others air, others mind, others motion, others exhalation, and
certain others a power flowing from the stars, others a moving number, others
generating water. And altogether a disordered and discordant opinion has
prevailed among them, appearing worthy of praise in one thing only to those
able to judge rightly, that they have chosen to convict one another as
wandering and not speaking the truth.

Therefore, since it is possible to learn nothing true about religion from your
teachers, who have furnished you a sufficient demonstration of their own
ignorance through their faction with one another, I think it follows to go up
to our ancestors, who both anticipated by much the times of the teachers among
you and taught us nothing from their own imagination, nor differed with one
another nor tried to overturn one another's things, but without love of
contention and without faction received the knowledge from God and teach this
to us. For neither by nature nor by human thought is it possible for men to
know things so great and divine, but by the gift that then came down from
above upon the holy men, who had no need of the art of words nor of saying
anything eristically and contentiously, but to present themselves pure to the
energy of the divine Spirit, in order that the divine plectrum itself, coming
down from heaven, using the righteous men as an instrument of some harp or
lyre, might reveal to us the knowledge of things divine and heavenly. For this
reason, then, as if from one mouth and one tongue they taught us both about
God and about the creation of the world and about the formation of man and
about the immortality of the human soul and the judgment that is to be after
this life and about all things that it is necessary for us to know,
consistently and in agreement with one another, and this though they furnished
us the divine teaching in different places and times.

I will begin, then, from the first prophet and lawgiver among us, Moses, first
setting out the times in which he lived, with all testimony trustworthy among
you,

for I try to demonstrate these things not only from the divine histories among
us, which you are not yet willing to believe because of the old error of your
ancestors, but from your own histories that have nothing to do with our
worship, in order that you may know that of all those among you, whether sages
or poets or historians or philosophers or lawgivers, by far the oldest was
Moses, the first teacher of our religion, as the histories of the Greeks show
us. For in the times of Ogyges and Inachus, whom some among you have supposed
even to have been earth-born, they mention Moses as leader and ruler of the
race of the Jews. For thus Polemon mentions him in the first of the Hellenic
histories, and Apion son of Posidonius in the book against the Jews and in the
fourth of the histories, saying that in the time of Inachus king of Argos,
when Amosis was king of the Egyptians, the Jews revolted, of whom Moses was
leader. And Ptolemy of Mendes, writing the history of the Egyptians, agrees
with all these. And those who write the history of the Athenians, Hellanicus
and Philochorus who wrote the Atthides, and Castor and Thallus and Alexander
Polyhistor, and moreover the most wise Philo and Josephus, who wrote the
history of the Jews, mention Moses as a very ancient and old ruler of the
Jews. Josephus at any rate, wishing to signify the ancient and old of the
history even by the title of the books, beginning the history has written
thus: Of Flavius Josephus, of the Jewish Archaeology; naming the oldness of
the history Archaeology. And the most renowned of the historians among you,
Diodorus, who epitomized the Libraries, having gone around Asia and Europe in
thirty whole years, as he himself has written, for much accuracy, and having
become an eyewitness of most things, has written forty whole books of his own
history; who in the first book, having said he learned from the priests in
Egypt that Moses was an ancient and first lawgiver, has written about him in
these very words: For after the ancient constitution of life in Egypt, which
is fabled to have come to be in the time of gods and heroes, they say Moses
first persuaded the multitudes to use written laws, a man remembered as great
in soul and most sufficient in life. Then having gone on a little, and wishing
to mention the ancient lawgivers, he mentions Moses first. For he spoke in
these very words thus: Among the Jews, Moses the one called god, whether
judging it to be a marvelous and wholly divine thought that was going to
benefit a multitude of men, or having taken it that the crowd would rather
obey looking to the superiority and power of those said to have found the
laws. And they say a second lawgiver of the Egyptians was Sauchis, a man
excelling in understanding. And third they say king Sesonchosis not only
accomplished the most distinguished warlike deeds

of those in Egypt, but also established the warlike nation by legislations.
And fourth they say king Bocchoris became a lawgiver, a certain wise man
excelling in shrewdness. And after him king Amasis is said to have come to the
laws, whom they relate to have arranged the things concerning the nomarchs and
the things concerning the whole administration of Egypt. And sixth it is said
that Darius, the father of Xerxes, set himself over the laws of the Egyptians.

These things, O men of Greece, those outside our religion have written,
relating the history concerning the antiquity of Moses, and having said they
learned all these things from Egyptian priests, among whom Moses was not only
born, but was also thought worthy to share in all the education of the
Egyptians, because he was adopted by a king's daughter into the place of a
son, and for the aforesaid reason was thought worthy of much attention, as the
wisest of the historians relate, who chose to record his life and deeds and
the dignity of his race, Philo and Josephus. For these, writing the history of
the deeds of the Jews, say Moses was born from the race of the Chaldeans; and
that when his ancestors migrated from Phoenicia to Egypt because of famine,
the man was born there, whom God purposed to honor because of exceeding
virtue, and thought worthy to become ruler and general and lawgiver of his own
race, when he judged it right that the multitude of the Hebrews return from
Egypt to their own land. To this man first God also furnished the divine and
prophetic gift that then came down from above upon the holy men, and prepared
him to become the first teacher of our religion, then after him the remaining
prophets, who also themselves obtained the same gift as he and taught us the
same things about the same matters. These we say have become teachers of our
worship, having taught us nothing from their own human mind, but from the gift
given them from above by God.

But you, since because of the former error of your fathers you do not think it
necessary to be persuaded by these, whom do you say have become trustworthy
teachers of religion for you? For it is impossible, as I have often said, that
those who have not first learned matters so great and divine from those who
know should either themselves know or be able to teach others rightly.
Therefore, since from what has been said the affairs of your philosophers have
been sufficiently convicted, appearing full of all ignorance and deceit,
leaving off the philosophers altogether hereafter, as formerly the poets, you
will turn to the deceit of the oracles; for thus I have heard some speaking.
Therefore I think it follows now in due season to say to you the things I
formerly heard from you speaking about them. For when someone, as you
yourselves say, asked the oracle among you what men

happened ever to have been religious, you say the oracle spoke thus:

Only Chaldeans obtained wisdom, and Hebrews too,
Worshipping purely the self-begotten lord God.

Therefore, since you think you can learn the truth from your oracles, meeting
with the histories and the things written about the life of Moses by those
outside our worship, and knowing that Moses and the remaining prophets started
from the race of the Chaldeans and Hebrews, do not think anything paradoxical
has come to be, if God purposed to honor with this great gift the man who was
from a race of religious people and had lived worthily of the religion of his
ancestors, and to show him first of all the prophets.

And I think it necessary also to consider the times in which the philosophers
among you lived, in order that you may know that the time that brought these
to you is very new and short; for thus it will be possible for you also easily
to know the antiquity of Moses. And that I may not seem, going through the
times, to drag on, using more demonstrations, I think it sufficient to show
even from these. For Socrates was teacher of Plato, and Plato of Aristotle.
And these flourished in the times of Philip and Alexander of Macedon, in which
also the orators of the Athenians, as the speeches written by Demosthenes
against Philip also show us clearly. And that Aristotle also was with
Alexander in the time of the kingship, those who have written the history of
Alexander's deeds sufficiently show. From every side, then, it is easy to know
that Moses' history happens to be by far the most ancient of all the histories
outside. And besides, it is fitting that you not be ignorant of this either,
that nothing accurate has been related in history by Greeks before the
Olympiads, nor is there any old writing signifying a deed of Greeks or
barbarians. But only the history of the first prophet Moses already existed,
which Moses wrote from divine inspiration in the letters of the Hebrews. For
those of the Greeks did not yet exist, as the teachers of letters themselves
also show, saying Cadmus first brought them from Phoenicia and gave them to
Greeks. And the first of the philosophers among you, Plato, testifies that
they were found later. For he has written in the Timaeus that Solon, the
wisest of the wise, returning from Egypt, said to Critias the things he had
heard an Egyptian priest, not very old, saying to him: O Solon, Solon, you
Greeks are always children, and there is no old Greek. Then again: You are
young, he said, in your souls, all of you; for you have in them no old opinion
nor anything old by long time. But it has escaped you because through many
generations they end mute in letters. It is fitting, then, to know that every
history happens to have been written in the later-found letters of the Greeks,
and if anyone should wish to mention ancient poets or lawgivers or historians
or philo

sophers or orators, he will find these have written their own compositions in
the letters of the Greeks.

And if anyone should say that the writing of Moses and of the other prophets
was also written in the letters of the Greeks, let him know, meeting with the
histories outside, that Ptolemy the king of Egypt, having constructed a
library in Alexandria and having gathered books from every side and filled it,
then having learned that ancient histories written in the letters of the
Hebrews happen to be preserved accurately, wishing to know the things written,
ordered seventy wise men, who knew both the Greek and the Hebrew dialect, to
interpret the books for them, having sent for them from Jerusalem. And in
order that being outside all disturbance they might interpret more quickly, he
ordered that not in the city itself, but seven stades off where the Pharos
happens to have been built, small cottages equal in number to the interpreters
should come to be, so that each by himself privately might complete the
interpretation, having ordered the servants set over them that they should
obtain all attendance, but be kept from conversation with one another, in
order that the accuracy of the interpretation might be able to be known also
through their agreement. And when he knew that the seventy men had used not
only the same mind but also the same words, and had not missed the agreement
with one another even as far as one word, but had written the same things also
about the same matters, being struck with amazement he also judged them worthy
of honor as men dear to God, and with many gifts ordered them to return to
their own fatherland, and having deified the books, as was likely, he
dedicated them there. These things we do not report to you as myths, O men of
Greece, nor as invented histories; but we ourselves having been in Alexandria
and having seen the traces of the cottages on Pharos still preserved, and
having heard from those there who had received them as ancestral things,
report these things, which you can also learn from others, and especially from
the wise and approved men who have written the history of these things, Philo
and Josephus, and many others. And if anyone of those accustomed to contradict
readily should say that these books belong not to us but to Jews, because they
are still even now preserved in their synagogues, and should say we vainly
claim to have learned religion from these, let him know from the very things
written in the books that the teaching from these belongs not to them but to
us. And that the books belonging to our religion are still even now preserved
among Jews has come to be a work of divine providence on our behalf; for in
order that we may not, producing them from the church, furnish a pretext of
fraud to those who wish to blaspheme us, we think it right that these be
produced from the synagogue of the Jews, in order that from the very books
still preserved among them, as the just things written for teaching by the
holy men belong to us,

it may appear clearly and openly.

You must, then, O men of Greece, looking forward to the things that are to be
and looking to the judgment proclaimed by all, not only by the religious but
also by those outside, not attend to the unexamined error of your ancestors,
nor think that if they themselves, having slipped, handed something down to
you, this is true, but, looking to the danger of so terrible a failure, seek
and search accurately also the things said by your own teachers, as you
yourselves say. For even they themselves, by the divine providence for men,
were forced even unwillingly to speak many things on our behalf, and
especially those who were in Egypt and were benefited from the religion of
Moses and of his ancestors. For I think it does not escape some of you, having
met at any rate somewhere with the history of Diodorus and those of the rest
who have written the history of these things, that Orpheus and Homer and
Solon, who wrote the laws for the Athenians, and Pythagoras and Plato and
certain others, having been in Egypt and having been benefited from the
history of Moses, later declared things opposite to what had formerly seemed
to them not well about gods.

Orpheus at any rate, who became, as one might say, the first teacher of your
polytheism, it is necessary to remind you what things he later proclaims
speaking to his son Musaeus and the remaining genuine hearers about one and
only God. And he spoke thus:

I will speak to those for whom it is lawful; and shut the doors, profane,
All alike. But you hear, offspring of light-bringing Moon,
Musaeus! For I will declare true things; and let not the things that formerly
Appeared in your breast rob you of the dear life.
And looking to the divine Word attend upon this,
Directing the intelligent hollow of the heart, and well step upon
The path, and look only at the lord of the world.
One he is, self-begotten; all things have been made offspring of the one;
And among them he himself goes about, and no one of mortals
Sees him, but he himself is seen of all.
And this one gives evil to mortals out of good
And chilling war and tearful pains.
And there is no other apart from the great king.
And I do not see him; for a cloud has been set around.
For all mortals have mortal pupils in their eyes,
And they are weak to see Zeus who rules all.
For this one has been set in the bronze heaven
On a golden throne, and with his feet he has stepped on the earth
And his right hand to the limit of ocean
He has stretched from every side; for around tremble the long mountains
And rivers and the depth of the grey bright sea.

And again somewhere else he speaks thus:

One Zeus, one Hades, one Sun, one Dionysus,
One God in all. Why do I tell you these things apart?

And in the Oaths also thus:

I adjure you by heaven, wise work of the great God,
I adjure you by the voice of the Father, which he first uttered,
When he established the whole world by his own counsels.

What does he mean by saying

I adjure you by the voice of the Father, which he first uttered?

Voice here he names the Word of God, through whom heaven and earth and the
whole creation came to be, as the divine prophecies of the holy men teach us,
to which he himself also in part attending in Egypt knew that by the Word of
God the whole creation came to be. Therefore also after saying:

I adjure you by the voice of the Father, which he first uttered,

he immediately joins on, saying:

When he established the whole world by his own counsels.

Here he names the Word voice because of the poetic metre. And that this is so
is clear from the fact that a little before, the metre permitting him, he
names it Word. For he said:

And looking to the divine Word attend upon this.

And what the ancient and very old Sibyl also, of whom Plato and Aristophanes
and many others make mention as a prophetess, happens to teach you through
oracles about one and only God, it is necessary to remind. And she speaks
thus:

And there is one God only, exceeding great, unbegotten,
Almighty, invisible, himself seeing all things,
But he himself is not seen by all mortal flesh.

Then somewhere else thus:

And we have wandered from the paths of the immortal,
And with foolish heart we honor works made by hands,
Idols and carved images of perished men.

And again somewhere else thus:

Blessed will those men be on the earth
As many as will love the great God, blessing
Before eating and drinking, trusting in pieties;
Who will deny all temples when they see them
And altars, vain establishments of deaf stones,
Defiled with bloods of living things and with sacrifices
Of four-footed beasts, and they will look to the great glory of one God.

These things, then, the Sibyl.

And the poet Homer, using the license of poetry and emulating the opinion of
Orpheus at the beginning about polytheism, mentions more gods in mythical
fashion, in order that he may not seem to be out of tune with Orpheus' poetry,
which he purposed so to emulate that even through the first verse of the
poetry he signified the relation toward him. For Orpheus having said

Sing, goddess, the wrath of Demeter of the splendid fruit

at the beginning of the poetry, he himself has written

Sing, goddess, the wrath of Achilles son of Peleus,

having chosen, as it seems to me, even at the beginning to fall out of

the metre according to the poetry, in order that he may not seem not to have
mentioned first the name of the gods. And a little later he also clearly and
openly sets out his own opinion about one and only God, in one place saying
through Phoenix to Achilles:

Not even if God himself should undertake for me
To scrape off old age and set me young in youth,

signifying by the pronoun the God who really is; and in another place through
Odysseus to the multitude of the Greeks speaking thus:

The rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler.

And that the rule of many is not good, but on the contrary an evil, he
purposed to show by deed, recounting their wars because of the multitude and
battles and factions and plots against one another. For monarchy happens to be
without battle. These things, then, the poet Homer.

And if we must also add testimonies from the stage about one God, hear also
Sophocles speaking thus:

There is one in truths, there is one God,
Who has made heaven and the long earth,
And the bright swell of the sea and the forces of winds.
But we many mortals, wandering in heart,
Have set up a consolation of woes,
Images of gods from stones and woods,
Or types of gold-wrought or ivory;
And making sacrifices to these and fair festivals,
We think thus to be pious.

These things, then, Sophocles.

And Pythagoras son of Mnesarchus, who set out the dogmas of his own philosophy
mystically through symbols, as those who have written his life show, himself
also appears thinking things about one God worthy of the sojourn into Egypt.
For saying the monad is principle of all things and that this is cause of all
good things, through allegory he teaches that there is one and only God. And
that this is so is clear from the things in which he said the monad and one
differ much from one another; for he says the monad is in the intelligibles,
and the one in the numbers. And if you also desire to know a clearer
demonstration of Pythagoras' opinion about one God, hear also his own opinion.
For he spoke thus: God is one, and he himself is not, as some suppose, outside
the ordering, but in himself whole in the whole circle, overseeing all
generations, being a blending of all the ages and worker of his own powers and
works, principle of all, light-bringer in heaven and father of all, mind and
animation of the wholes, movement of all circles. Thus, then, Pythagoras.

And Plato, having accepted, as it seems, the teaching of Moses and of the
other prophets about one and only God, which he came to know when he was in
Egypt, but fearing because of the things that happened to Socrates lest he
also prepare some Anytus or

Meletus to come to be against himself, accusing him among the Athenians and
saying: Plato does injustice and is a busybody, not thinking the gods whom the
city thinks; in fear of the hemlock he exercises a certain varied and figured
discourse about gods, constructing by the argument both that there are gods
for those who wish and that there are not for those to whom the opposite
seems, as it will be easy to know from the very things said by him. For having
first declared that everything that has come to be is mortal, later he says
gods have come to be. If, then, he would have God and matter to be principle
of all things, it is clear that there is every necessity to say the gods have
come to be from matter. And if from matter, from which he also said evil has
started, what sort one ought to think the gods that came to be from matter
are, he has left to those who think well to consider. For this reason he also
said matter is unbegotten, in order that he may not seem to say God is maker
of evil. And about the gods crafted by God he appears to have said these
things: Gods of gods, of whom I am craftsman. But about the God who really is
he appears to have the right opinion. For having heard in Egypt that God said
to Moses I am the one who is, when he was about to send him to the Hebrews, he
knew that God did not speak to him a proper name of himself.

For no name can be used as a proper name of God. For names lie for the showing
and distinguishing of the underlying things, many and different as they are;
but for God neither did one who sets a name exist beforehand, nor did he
himself think it necessary to name himself, being one and only, as he himself
testifies through his own prophets, saying: I am God first and I after these
things, and besides me there is no other God. For this reason, then, as I also
said before, God sending Moses to the Hebrews does not even mention any name,
but through a certain participle he mystically teaches that he himself is one
and only God. For I, he says, am the one who is, contrasting himself clearly,
the one who is, with those who are not, in order that those formerly deceived
may know that they attended not to those who are but to those who are not.
Since, then, God knew that the first men remembered the old deceit of the
ancestors, which the man-hating demon wished to deceive them with, saying to
them If you should be persuaded by me to pass by the commandment of God, you
will be as gods, naming gods those who are not, in order that men, thinking
both that there are also other gods and that they themselves could become
gods, might believe, for this reason he said to Moses I am the one who is, in
order that through the participle of being he might teach the difference of
the God who is and of those who are not. Men, then, having been persuaded by
the deceiving demon and having dared to disobey God, went out of paradise,
remembering the name of gods, but no longer being taught by God that there are
not other gods; for it was not just still to teach those who did not keep the
first commandment, which it was easy to keep, but to bring upon them just
punishment. Having been thrown out of paradise, then, and thinking they had
been thrown out

because of the disobedience alone, not knowing that they were also persuaded
that gods who are not are, they handed down the name of gods also to the men
who came to be from them after these things. This, then, is the first false
fancy about gods, having had its beginning from the father of the lie. God,
then, knowing the untrue opinion of polytheism troubling the soul of men like
some disease, wishing to take it away and overturn it, first appearing to
Moses said to him: I am the one who is. For it was necessary, I think, that
the one who was going to be ruler and general of the race of the Hebrews
should first of all know the God who is. Therefore appearing first to this
one, as it was possible for God to appear to a man, he said to him: I am the
one who is. Then, being about to send him to the Hebrews, he orders him to say
the same things to them also: The one who is has sent me to you.

These things, then, Plato having learned in Egypt, and being very pleased with
the things said about one God, thought it not safe among the Athenians to
mention the name of Moses, because he teaches one and only God, fearing the
Areopagus, but the thing well said by him he set out not as having learned
from that one, but as his own opinion, in his elaborate discourse Timaeus, in
which he also attempts to speak of God, the same thing that Moses also wrote
about God. For he said: There is, then, according to my opinion, first to be
divided, what that which always is, having no generation, and what that which
always comes to be, but never is. Does this, O men of Greece, not seem to
those able to understand to be one and the same, differing only by the
article? For Moses said the one who is, and Plato that which is. And each of
the things said appears to belong to the God who always is; for he himself
alone is the one who always is, having no generation. What, then, is the other
that is contrasted with the one who always is, about which he himself said:
And what that which always comes to be, but never is, it is fitting to
consider accurately. For we shall find him clearly and openly saying that the
unbegotten is eternal, but that the generated or crafted (as he himself said
it had been said about the same: Gods of gods, of whom I am craftsman) come to
be and perish. For thus he himself says this: There is, then, according to my
opinion, first to be divided, what that which always is, having no generation,
and what that which comes to be, but never is. The one, then, is graspable by
thought with reason, always being according to the same, but the other again
is opinable by opinion with irrational perception, coming to be and perishing,
but never being. These words proclaim to those able to understand rightly the
taking away and destruction of the gods that have come to be. And I think it
necessary to attend the mind also to this, that Plato names him not maker but
craftsman of gods, although there is much difference in these according to
Plato's own opinion; for the maker, needing nothing else, makes the thing made
from his own power and authority, but the craftsman, having taken the power of
the craftsmanship from matter,

constructs the thing that comes to be.

But perhaps some, unwilling to stand off from the dogmas of polytheism, will
say that the craftsman himself said these things to the crafted gods: Since
you have come to be, you are not immortal nor wholly indissoluble, yet you
will not be dissolved nor obtain a share of death, having obtained my will, a
still greater and stronger bond. Here Plato, fearing those who embrace
polytheism, introduces the craftsman according to him saying things contrary
to himself. For having said that he had formerly said everything that has come
to be is perishable, now he introduces him saying the opposite, not knowing
that it is in no way possible thus to escape the charge of false-speaking; for
either having formerly said everything that has come to be is perishable he
speaks falsely, or now announcing the opposite of the things formerly said
about them. For if there is every necessity that the generated be perishable
according to his former definition, how is it allowed that what is of
necessity impossible become possible? So that Plato seems in vain to grant
impossibles to the craftsman according to him, announcing that those who once,
because they have come to be from matter, became perishable and dissoluble
will again through him be imperishable and indissoluble. For the power of
matter, being unbegotten and of equal time and of the same age as the
craftsman according to his opinion, is likely to stand against his will; for
to the one who has not made there is no authority toward what has not come to
be, so that it is not even possible for it to be forced, being free of all
outside necessity. For this reason, then, Plato himself also looking to these
things has written thus: And it is not possible even for a god to be said to
force.

How, then, does Plato throw Homer out of his own republic, since in the
embassy to Achilles he has made Phoenix say to Achilles:

And even the gods themselves are turnable,

although Homer said these things not about the king and according to Plato
craftsman of the gods, but about certain gods, of the many thought among
Greeks to be, as we can learn also from Plato himself, saying gods of gods?
For Homer refers the authority and the power of all things to the one and
first God through that golden chain, and he said the remaining gods are so far
from that one's divinity that he thinks it right to name them even with men.
At any rate he introduces Odysseus saying against Hector to Achilles:

He rages terribly, trusting in Zeus, and he honors not at all
Men nor gods.

Here Homer seems to me at any rate, himself also having learned in Egypt like
Plato about one God, clearly and openly to show this, that the one who has
trusted in the God who is neglects those who are not. For thus the poet also
somewhere else, through another word of equal force, has used the pronoun, the
same as the participle said by Plato, announcing the God who is, about whom
Plato said: What that which always is, having no

generation. For it does not seem to me to have been said simply, the thing
said by Phoenix:

Not even if God himself should undertake for me
To scrape off old age and set me young in youth.

For the pronoun himself signifies the God who really is. For thus also the
oracle said to you about the Chaldeans and Hebrews signifies; for when someone
inquired what religious men ever happened to have come to be, you say he spoke
thus:

Only Chaldeans obtained wisdom, and Hebrews too,
Worshipping God himself, the self-begotten lord.

How, then, does Plato blame Homer for saying the gods are turnable, although
Homer said this because of the useful, as is clear from the very things said?
For it is proper to those who think it right to obtain philanthropy through
prayer and sacrifices to cease and to change their mind about the things in
which they sinned; for those who think the divine is unturning have in no way
chosen to stand off from sins, thinking they will have no benefit from
repentance. How, then, having condemned the poet Homer for having said

And even the gods themselves are turnable,

does the philosopher Plato himself introduce the craftsman of the gods so
easily turned, as sometimes to say gods are mortal, sometimes that the same
are immortal? And not only about them, but also about matter, from which the
crafted gods also, as he himself says, have of necessity come to be, he
sometimes says it is unbegotten, sometimes generated, not knowing that he is
convicted falling into the very things for which he blames Homer, saying the
craftsman of the gods is so easily turned, although Homer has said the
opposite about him. For he said that he spoke thus about himself:

For not mine is it to be taken back nor deceitful
Nor unfulfilled, whatever I nod with my head.

But these things Plato willingly, as it seems, fearing those who embrace
polytheism, appears to go through as strange about gods. And as many things as
he has learned from Moses and the prophets about one God he thinks he must
say, these he has chosen to say mystically, signifying his own opinion to
those who wish to be religious. For being pleased with what was said by God to
Moses I am the one who is, and having received with much contemplation the
brief saying said through the participle, he knew that God, wishing to signify
his eternity to Moses, said I am the one who is, the syllable being showing
not one time but the three, the past and the present and the future. For thus
Plato also mentions the being of the extra time, saying But never being. For
never has been said not of the past, as some think, but of the future time.
For this has been accurately treated also among those outside. For this
reason, then, as if wishing to interpret to those who do not know what was
said mystically about the eternity of God through

the participle, Plato has written in these very words thus: God indeed, as
also the old account, having beginning and end and middles of all things. Here
Plato clearly and openly names the old account the law of Moses, fearing
because of fear of the hemlock to mention the name of Moses; for he knew the
teaching of the man was hostile to Greeks; and through the oldness of the
account he signifies Moses clearly. And that the law of Moses is old and first
has been sufficiently demonstrated to us also from the histories of Diodorus
and the rest in the preceding; for Diodorus says he became first of all
lawgivers, the letters belonging to the Greeks not yet even having been found,
using which they have written their own histories.

And let no one wonder if Plato, having been persuaded by Moses about the
eternity of God, has written thus. For you will find him mystically after the
God who really is also referring the true knowledge about the things that are
to the prophets. For thus in the Timaeus discoursing about certain principles
he has written: And the principle of fire and of the other bodies we lay down,
going according to the likely account with necessity; but the principles still
of these God knows from above, and whichever of men is dear to him. And what
other men does he think are friends of God, if not Moses and the remaining
prophets? Having met with their prophecies and having learned from them the
account about judgment, in the first discourse of the Republic he thus
proclaims beforehand, saying: Whenever someone is near thinking he will die,
fear and care enter him about things that did not enter before. For the so-
called myths about those in Hades, that the one who did injustice here must
give justice there, being laughed at until then, then indeed turn his soul
lest they be true, and he himself either by the weakness of old age or also as
being nearer those there sees them more. At any rate he becomes full of
suspicion and dread, and already reckons and considers whether he did anyone
injustice. The one, then, finding many injustices of himself in life and from
sleep, like children, often waking, fears and lives with bad hope; but to the
one conscious of no injustice to himself sweet hope is always present and a
good nurse of old age, as Pindar also says. For charmingly indeed, O Socrates,
that one said this, that whoever should go through life holily and justly,

Sweet hope that nurses old age attends his heart, cherishing it,
Hope, which most of all steers the much-turning mind of mortals.

And these things he has written in the first discourse of the Republic.

And in the tenth he clearly and openly, the things he has learned from the
prophets about judgment, these not as having learned from them, because of
fear toward Greeks, but as having heard from someone, as it seemed good to him
to invent, slain in war and about to be buried on the twelfth day and lying on
the pyre,

having come to life again and narrating the things there, has written in these
very words thus: For he said he was present when one was asked by another
where the great Aridaeus was. And this Aridaeus had become tyrant in a certain
city of Pamphylia, having killed an old father and an elder brother, and
having worked many other unholy things, as was said. He said at any rate that
the one asked said: He has not come, he said, nor would he come here. For we
indeed also beheld this of the terrible sights. When we were near the mouth,
being about to go up and having suffered all the other things, we suddenly saw
him and others, almost the most of them tyrants; and there were also certain
private persons of those who had sinned greatly; whom, thinking they would
already go up, the mouth did not receive, but bellowed whenever anyone of
those so incurably in wickedness, if he had not sufficiently given justice,
attempted to go up. There wild men, fiery to look at, standing by and
perceiving the sound, took some and led them, and Aridaeus and others, having
bound hands and feet and head, having thrown them down and flayed them, they
dragged beside the road outside, carding them on thorns, and signifying to
those present for what things and that they were being led to fall into
Tartarus. Then of many and all sorts of fears, he said, that had come to be
for them, this surpassed, if the sound should not come to be for each when he
went up, so that each most gladly went up when it was silent. And the justices
and punishments were some such, and again the benefits corresponding to these.
Here Plato seems to me to have learned from the prophets not only the account
about judgment, but also that about the resurrection disbelieved among Greeks.
For to have said that the soul is judged with a body shows nothing other than
that he believed the account about the resurrection. Since how did Aridaeus
and the rest, having left the body that has head and hands and feet and skin
above earth, undergo such punishment in Hades? For they will not surely say
the soul has head and hands and feet and skin. But Plato, having met in Egypt
with the testimonies of the prophets, and having accepted the teaching about
the resurrection of the body, teaches that the soul is judged with the body.

And not Plato only, but also Homer, himself likewise having learned in Egypt,
said Tityus is likewise punished. For thus in the necromancy Odysseus narrates
to Alcinous, saying:

And I saw Tityus, glorious son of Earth,
Lying on the ground; and he lay over nine acres,
And two vultures sitting on either side tore his liver.

For liver shows not the soul but the body has it. In the same way he has also
written that Sisyphus and Tantalus undergo the punishment with a body. And
that Homer was in Egypt and transferred many of the things he learned there
into his own

poetry, Diodorus, the most renowned of the historians, sufficiently teaches
us. For he said that he, having been in Egypt, learned that the drug

Banishing grief, and without bile, causing forgetfulness of all evils,

Helen, having received from Polydamna the wife of Thon, brought to Sparta; and
Homer said Helen, using that drug, stopped the lament that came to be in the
presence of Telemachus at Menelaus'. And he named Aphrodite golden from the
history in Egypt; for he also knew a precinct of golden Aphrodite spoken of in
Egypt and a plain named of golden Aphrodite. And for the sake of what has
mention of these things come to be now? In order that we may show the poet
transferred many things also from the divine history of the prophets into his
own poetry; and first the beginning said by Moses of the making of the world.
For thus Moses has written: In the beginning God made the heaven and the
earth, then sun and moon and stars. For having learned these things in Egypt,
and being pleased with the things written by him in the generation of the
world, he prepared Hephaestus to construct on the shield of Achilles as it
were a certain image of the making of the world. For thus he has written:

On it he made earth, on it heaven, on it sea,
And unwearied sun and the moon at the full,
And on it all the stars with which heaven is crowned.

And he has made the garden of Alcinous preserve an image of paradise, showing
it through the image ever-blooming and full of fruits. For thus he has
written:

There tall trees had grown, flourishing,
Pears and pomegranates and apple trees of splendid fruit
And sweet fig trees and flourishing olives.
Of these the fruit never perishes nor fails
In winter nor in summer, year-long, but always the breeze
Of Zephyrus blowing brings some to birth, and ripens others.
Pear upon pear grows old, apple upon apple,
And grape upon grape, fig upon fig.
And there his many-fruited vineyard is rooted;
Of it one part of the drying-ground in a level place
Is dried by the sun, and others they harvest,
And others they tread; and in front are unripe grapes,
Casting flower, and others are growing dark.

Do these words not show a clear and open imitation of the things said by the
first prophet Moses about paradise? And if anyone should also wish to look to
the making of the tower, through which the men of that time thought they could
construct for themselves the ascent into heaven, he will find this also a
sufficient imitation through allegory done by the poet through Otus and
Ephialtes. For thus the poet also said about them:

Who even threatened the immortals on Olympus

To set up a battle of much-rushing war;
They were eager to set Ossa on Olympus, and on Ossa
Pelion of shaking leaves, that heaven might be climbable.

And likewise also about the enemy of humanity brought down from the heavens,
whom the divine writings call devil, having obtained this appellation from his
first slander against man; and if anyone should wish to consider accurately,
he would find the poet nowhere mentioning the name of the devil, but having
made the naming from his worst deed; for the poet, naming him Ate, says he was
taken down from heaven by the god according to them, as if accurately
remembering the words said by the prophet Isaiah about him. Thus he has
written in his own poetry:

And immediately he seized Ate by the head of shining hair,
Angry in his mind, and he swore a strong oath
That never to Olympus and starry heaven
Would Ate come again, who deceives all.
So speaking he threw her from starry heaven,
Whirling her with his hand; and quickly she came to the works of men.

And Plato also, saying that after God and matter form is a third principle,
appears having taken the occasion from no other place but from Moses, having
learned the name of form from the words of Moses, but not having then been
taught by those who know that it is possible clearly to know nothing of the
things said by Moses outside mystic contemplation. For Moses has written as
God having said to him about the tent thus: And you shall make for me
according to all things, as many as I show you on the mountain, the pattern of
the tent. And again: And you shall set up the tent according to the pattern of
all its vessels, and thus you shall make. And again a little later thus: See,
you shall make according to the type that has been shown you on the mountain.
Plato, then, meeting with these and not receiving the written words with the
fitting contemplation, thought some separate form pre-existed the perceptible,
which he also often names pattern of the things that have come to be, since
the writing of Moses signifies thus about the tent: According to the form that
was shown you on the mountain, thus you shall make it.

And likewise also about the earth and the heaven and man he appears to have
slipped; for he thinks there are ideas of these too. For since Moses has
written thus: In the beginning God made the heaven and the earth, then
immediately joins on saying: And the earth was invisible and unfurnished, he
thought that this earth about which he said And the earth was was the pre-
existing earth that was spoken of, since Moses said: And the earth was
invisible and unfurnished; and this about which he says God made the heaven
and the earth he thought he said this perceptible one that came to be by God
according to a pre-existing form. And likewise

also about the heaven that came to be; the heaven that has been made, which he
also named firmament, this is the perceptible that came to be, and there is
another intelligible, about which the prophet said: The heaven of heaven is
the Lord's, and the earth he gave to the sons of men. And about man likewise;
first Moses mentions the name of man, then after the many of the created
things he has made mention about the formation of man, saying thus: And God
made man, dust from the earth. He thought at any rate that the man first named
pre-existed the one that came to be, and that the one formed from the earth
later came to be according to the pre-existing form. And that man has been
formed from earth, Homer also, having learned from the old and divine history
that says You are earth and to earth you will go away, names the lifeless body
of Hector dumb earth. For he said somewhere against Achilles dragging the body
of Hector after death:

For you outrage dumb earth in your rage.

And again somewhere else Menelaus saying to those who did not readily receive
Hector's challenge about the single combat:

But may you all become water and earth!

through exceeding anger resolving them into the ancient formation from earth.
These things Homer and Plato, having learned from the ancient histories in
Egypt, have written in their own discourses.

Since from where else having learned does Plato say Zeus drives a winged
chariot in heaven, if not having met with the histories of the prophets? For
he knew from the words of the prophet about the Cherubim said thus: And the
glory of the Lord went out from the house and mounted upon Cherubim, and the
Cherubim took up their wings, and the wheels next to them, and the glory of
the Lord God of Israel was upon them from above. Starting from here the loud-
voiced Plato shouts with much boldness, saying: The great Zeus indeed in
heaven driving a winged chariot. Since from where else having learned has he
written these things, if not from Moses and the prophets? And from where
having taken the occasion did he also say God is in the fiery substance? Not
from the third of the history of the Kingdoms, where it is written: The Lord
was not in the wind; and after the wind an earthquake, the Lord was not in the
earthquake; and after the earthquake fire, the Lord was not in the fire; and
after the fire a voice of a thin breeze? But those things it is necessary for
the religious to understand according to a higher sense with much
contemplation; but Plato, not attending to the words with the fitting
contemplation, said God is in the fiery substance.

And if anyone should also wish to consider accurately about the gift coming
down from above from God upon the holy men, which the sacred prophets name
holy spirit, he would find this also proclaimed by Plato in the discourse to
Meno under another

name; for fearing to name the gift of God holy spirit, in order that he may
not seem, following the teaching of the prophets, to be an enemy of Greeks, he
confesses that it itself comes down from above from God, yet he thought it
right to name it not holy spirit but virtue. For thus to Meno in the discourse
about recollection, having first sought many things about virtue, whether
virtue is teachable or not teachable but attainable by practice, or neither
attainable by practice nor learnable but coming to men by nature or in some
other way, later he declares in these very words thus: And if now we in all
this discourse both sought and spoke well, virtue would be neither by nature
nor teachable, but coming by divine portion without mind to those to whom it
comes. These things, I think, Plato having clearly learned from the prophets
about the holy spirit appears transferring into the name of virtue; for
likewise as the sacred prophets say the one and the same spirit is divided
into seven spirits, so also he himself, naming one and the same virtue, says
this is divided into four virtues, nowhere wishing to mention the holy spirit,
but through a certain allegory clearly announcing the things said by the
prophets about the holy spirit. For thus toward the end of the discourse he
said to Meno: From this reasoning, then, O Meno, virtue appears to us to come
by divine portion to those to whom it comes; and we shall know this clearly
from him, in what sort of way virtue comes to men, when we first attempt to
seek it itself by itself, what virtue ever is. You see how he names the gift
coming down from above virtue only; and he still thinks it worthy of seeking
whether it is fitting to name it virtue or something else, fearing to name it
openly holy spirit, in order that he may not seem to follow the teaching of
the prophets.

And from where having learned did Plato know that time has come to be with
heaven? For thus he has written: Time, then, has come to be with heaven, in
order that having come to be together they may also be dissolved together, if
ever a dissolving of them comes to be. Has he not learned this also from the
divine history of Moses? For he knew that the generation of time took the
beginning of its constitution from days and months and years. As, then, the
first day, which came to be with heaven, having furnished the beginning of all
time (for thus Moses has written: In the beginning God made the heaven and the
earth; then immediately he joins on saying: And there came to be day one, as
signifying all time from a part), Plato names the day time, in order that he
may not seem, mentioning day, as following the words of Moses in every way, to
be accused among the Athenians. And from where having fancied also about a
dissolving of heaven has he written thus? Has he not learned this also from
the sacred prophets and thought it had been said by them thus?

And if anyone should also examine the account about the images, from where
starting the first who constructed your gods

decided that they have forms of men, he will find they learned this also from
the divine history. For the history of Moses saying from the person of God:
Let us make man according to our image and likeness, as if it had been said
thus that men are like God according to form, they began thus to construct
their gods, thinking to craft the like from the like. For the sake of what was
I led to mention these things now, O men of Greece? In order that you may know
that it is not possible to learn the true religion from these who were not
even able to write anything of their own in the things for which they were
wondered at by those outside, but have announced in their own compositions
through a certain allegory the things said by Moses and the remaining
prophets.

The time, then, has now come, O men of Greece, you having been persuaded from
the histories outside that Moses and the remaining prophets have become by far
the oldest of all those thought among you to have been wise, to stand off from
the old error of your ancestors, and to meet with the divine histories of the
prophets and to know from them the true religion, not announcing to you arts
of words, nor speaking persuasively and for belief (for this is proper to
those who wish to steal the truth), but using simply the names and words that
come to hand, and announcing to you these things which the holy spirit that
came down upon them has chosen to teach through them those who wish to learn
the true religion. Therefore having thrust aside all shame and old error of
men and the imaginary noise of the masses, through which you also think you
have all enjoyment, hold to the things profitable for you. For there will be
no offense from you even concerning your ancestors, if you now wish to turn to
the opposite of the error from those, whom it is likely now in Hades,
repenting a repentance after the season, to wail; to whom if it were possible
from there to show you the things that have happened to them after the end of
this life, you would know from how many evils they chose to deliver you. But
now since it is not possible in the present either for you to learn from those
or indeed from those here who profess to philosophize this falsely named
philosophy, it will follow for you hereafter, having thrust aside the error of
your ancestors, to meet with the prophecies of the sacred men, not asking from
them distinction of phrasing (for the affairs of our religion are not in words
but in deeds), and to learn from them the things that will be causes of
eternal life for you. For those who vainly insult the name of philosophy are
convicted of knowing nothing, as they themselves also are forced even
unwillingly to confess, through the things in which they not only are at
faction with one another, but also set out their own opinions now in one way,
now in another.

And if the finding of the true is said among them to be a certain definition
of philosophy, how are those who have not obtained the true knowledge worthy
of the name of philosophy? For if the wisest of the wise

know I do not even think I know. And let no one think Socrates, speaking

fallen out of the accuracy of the metres. For this reason, then, Plato

For why, men of Greece, do you wish as in a boxing-match to clash the

not have constitution in time, being alone without beginning and himself being
beginning of the wholes. God is spirit, not going through through the matter,
but constructor of material spirits and of the figures in it, both invisible
and intangible, himself having become father of sensibles and visibles. This
one we know through his making, and we grasp the invisible of his power by the
things made. I do not wish to worship the craftsmanship that has come to be by
him for our sake. Sun and moon have come to be through us; then how shall I
worship my servants? And how shall I declare woods and stones gods? For the
spirit that goes through the matter, being less than the more divine spirit,
and as being like a soul, is not to be honored equally with the perfect God.
But neither is the unnameable God to be bribed; for the one without need of
all is not to be slandered by us as in need. And I will set out our things
more openly.

God was in the beginning, and the beginning we have received as power of word.
For the master of the wholes, himself being the substance of the all,
according to the making not yet having come to be was alone; but inasmuch as
all power of visibles and invisibles he himself was substance with himself, he
himself and the Word, who was in him, substantiated all things with himself
through rational power. And by will of his simplicity Word springs forth; and
the Word, not having gone according to a void, becomes first-born work of the
Father. This one we know as the beginning of the world. And he has come to be
according to a partition, not according to a cutting-off; for what has been
cut off has been separated from the first, but what has been partitioned,
having taken on the division of an economy, has not made the one from whom it
has been taken in need. For as from one torch many fires are kindled, and the
light of the first torch is not lessened through the lighting of the many
torches, so also the Word having gone forth from the power of the Father has
not made the one who begot him without word. For I myself also speak, and you
hear; and I do not, I suppose, through the transfer of the word become empty
of the word as the one conversing, but putting forth my own voice I have
chosen to

order the unordered matter in you, and just as the Word having been begotten
in the beginning generated in turn the making according to us for himself,
having crafted the matter, so also I having been begotten again according to
the imitation of the Word and having made the grasp of the true, I re-rhythm
the confusion of the kindred matter. For neither is the matter without
beginning as also God, nor because of the without-beginning is it itself of
equal power with God, but generated and not having come to be by another, but
having been put forth only by the craftsman of all.

And for this reason we have also believed there will be a resurrection of

constitution will be to the complete through men alone for the sake of
judgment. And Minos and Rhadamanthys do not judge us, of whom before the end
no soul, as they mythologize, was judged, but the maker God himself becomes
examiner. For even if you think us altogether babblers and seed-pickers, it is
no care to us, since we have believed this word. For as not being before I

fleshly matter, and having come to be I who was not long ago have believed the
being through the generation; in the same way the one who has come to be and
through death no longer being and again no longer being seen I will be again,
just as not having come to be long ago then having been begotten. And if fire
should make my little flesh vanish, the world has contained the matter
evaporated; and if I should be spent in rivers or in seas or torn by beasts, I
am stored in treasuries of a rich master. And the poor and godless one does
not know the things stored, but God who is king, when he wishes, will restore
the substance visible to him alone toward the ancient.

We know differences of two spirits, of which the one is called soul, and the
other greater than the soul, and image and likeness of God; and both existed
among the first men, in order that they may be in one respect material, and in
another above the matter. And it is thus. It is possible to see all the
construction of the world and the whole making having come to be from matter,
and the matter itself having been put forth by God, in order that one part of
it may be thought without passage and unfigured before taking a distinction,
and the other ordered and well-arranged after the division in it. There is,
then, in it the heaven from matter and the stars in it; and the earth also and
everything made alive from it has the like constitution, so that there is a
common generation of all. And these being thus, there are certain differences
of the things from matter, so that one thing is more beautiful, and another
also itself beautiful, except being lessened by something better. For as the
constitution of the body is of one economy, and about it is the cause of
having come to be, and these being thus there are certain differences of glory
in it, and one thing is an eye, another an ear, another an ordering of hairs
and an economy of inwards and a compacting of marrows and bones and sinews,
and the one being different from the other according to economy is a harmony
of agreement; similarly also the world according to the power of the one who
made it, having obtained some things more bright, some unlike these, by will
of the spirit that crafted it has shared in material. And the things according
to each it is possible to understand for the one who does not vain-gloriously
reject the most divine interpretations, which having been proven through
writing according to time have made those who attend to them very dear to God.

The soul, men of Greece, is not immortal by itself, but mortal; but it is able
also not to die. For it dies and is dissolved with the body not knowing the
truth, and it rises later at the completion of the world with the body,
receiving death through punishment in immortality; and again it does not die,
even if it is dissolved for a time, having made the recognition of God. For by
itself it is darkness, and there is nothing bright in it, and this then is
what has been said: the darkness does not grasp the light. For the soul itself
did not save the spirit, but was saved by it; and the light grasped the
darkness, in which Word is the light of God, and darkness the unknowing soul.
For this reason dwelling alone it inclines down toward the matter, dying
together with the flesh, but having obtained a yoking of the divine spirit it
is not without help, but it goes up to the places to which the spirit leads
it; for of the one the dwelling is above, and of the other the generation is
from below. The spirit, then, came to be from the beginning dwelling-together
with the soul; but the spirit has left this one not wishing to follow it. And
the soul, as having obtained a spark of its power and through the separation
not being able to see the complete things clearly, seeking God according to
wandering typed many gods, following the demons who counter-sophisticate. And
the spirit of God is not with all, but dwelling with certain who constitute
themselves justly and being entwined with the soul it announced through
proclamations-beforehand to the remaining souls the hidden;

Such also are you, O Greeks, fluent in words, but having a strange judgment,

run aground into much wickedness, have deceived the isolated souls among you
through ignorances and fancies. Who die not easily, for they are without share
of flesh; but living they practice deeds of death, themselves also dying as

for them in the now, not dying like men, this whenever they are going to be
punished is bitter for them *** they will not share in eternal life instead of
death, taking a share in immortal. And as we, for whom dying turns out easy

that of the time in which they lived, according to the constitution like men
who according to judgment practiced the things which they have legislated for

the forms of the sin bloom less because of not living for a long time, but for
the aforesaid demons the offending has turned out greater because of the

And it is necessary hereafter that we seek this which having we have lost, and

soul of men, then, is many-parted and not one-parted. For it is composite, so
that it is apparent through a body; for neither would it itself ever appear
without a body nor does the flesh rise without soul. For man is not, as the
crow-voiced dogmatize, a rational animal receptive of mind and knowledge; for
according to them the irrational also will be shown receptive of mind and
knowledge; but man alone is image and likeness of God, and I say man not the
one doing things like the animals, but the one who has gone far from the
humanity toward God himself. And about this we have composed more accurately
in the On Animals, but the thing now holding must be said, of what sort the
image and likeness according to God is. The incomparable is nothing other than
the being itself, and the compared is nothing other than the similar.
Fleshless, then, is the perfect God, but man is flesh; and bond of the flesh
is soul, and holding of the soul is the flesh. And such a form of the
constitution, if it should be as a temple, God wishes to dwell in it through

And the demons who order men are not the souls of men. For how would they
become active even after dying apart, unless the man living should become
mindless and powerless, and having become dead should thereafter be believed
to take a share of a more active power? But neither is this so, as we
demonstrated in other places, and it is hard to think the immortal, being
hindered by the parts of the body, becomes more prudent whenever it migrates

And the demons are seen even by the psychic, sometimes showing themselves to

harm some as enemies, being bad friends in judgment, or may furnish the
occasions of the worship toward them to those like them. For if it were
possible for them, they would altogether have dragged down even the heaven

are unable; but with the matter below they war toward the matter like them.

they work. And how would it be good to serve adulteries? And how also serious
to help toward hating some who come by? Or how is it beautiful to attach to
