HOMILY XI.
On Envy.

God is good, and a giver of good things to those who are worthy; the devil is
wicked, and a craftsman of every kind of evil. And just as ungrudgingness
follows the good, so the evil eye follows the devil. Let us therefore guard
ourselves, brothers, from the passion of envy, lest we become partners of the
works of the adversary, and be found condemned together with him under the
same judgment. For if the one puffed up falls into the judgment of the devil,
how shall the envious man escape the punishment prepared for the devil? For no
passion more destructive than envy is implanted in the souls of men; which,
while it least of all grieves those outside, is a first and =31.373= proper
evil to the one who possesses it. For just as rust consumes iron, so envy
consumes the soul that has it. Or rather, just as they say that vipers are
born by eating through the belly that conceived them, so also envy is by
nature wont to waste the soul that is in travail with it. For envy is grief at
the neighbor's prosperity. Therefore neither distresses nor despondencies ever
fail the envious man. Has the neighbor's land borne well? Does the house
flourish with all the things of life? Do gladnesses not fail the man? All
these things are food of the disease, and an addition of the pain to the
envious man. So that he differs in nothing from a naked man being wounded by
all. Is someone brave? Does he enjoy good health? These things strike the
envious man.

Is another more graceful in form? Another blow to the envious man. Does
so-and-so surpass the many in the advantages of the soul? Is he looked to and
emulated for prudence and power of speech? Does another grow rich and contend
honorably in contributions and in sharing toward those in need, and is there
much praise for him from those who are benefited? All these things are blows
and wounds, striking the middle of his heart. And the grievous thing of the
disease is that he cannot even speak it out; but he stoops, and is downcast,
and is confused, and bewails, and has been destroyed by the evil. And being
asked the passion, he blushes to make the calamity public, that "I am envious
and bitter, and the good things of my friend wear me down, and I mourn the
cheer of my brother, and I cannot bear the sight of others' good things, but I
make the prosperity of the neighbors a calamity." For these things he would
say, if he were willing to speak the truth. Choosing to speak out none of
which, he holds the disease in the depth, smoldering his inward parts and
eating them.

Therefore he neither takes a physician of the disease, nor is he able to find
any drug that is a defense of the passion, although the Scriptures are full of
these healings; but he awaits one relief of the evil, if somehow he should see
one of those envied fallen. This is the bound of the hatred, to see the one
envied wretched from blessed, the one emulated become pitiable. Then he makes
a truce, and is a friend, when he sees him weeping, when he beholds him
mourning. And he does not rejoice together with the one who is cheerful; but
he weeps together with the one who is lamenting. And he pities the change of
life, from what things into what things he has fallen, not from love of man,
and from being sympathetic, raising the former things by speech, but in order
that he may make the calamity heavier for him.

He praises the child after death, and with countless encomia he magnifies him,
how fair to see, how apt to learn, how well-fitted toward all things; he would
not have granted him

a well-speaking tongue while he was still alive. If however he sees many
running together to the praise, changing again, he envies the dead. He admires
the wealth after the fall. The bloom of the body, or the =31.376= strength and
the good condition he praises and exalts after the diseases. And in a word, he
is an enemy of the things present, but a friend of the things being destroyed.
What then could be more destructive than this disease? Corruption of life, a
blight of nature, enmity toward the things given us by God, opposition toward
God. What maddened the first-evil demon into the war against men? Was it not
envy? through which he was also clearly convicted as a fighter against God,
being vexed at God for the great gift toward man, but taking vengeance on the
man, since he was not able against God. And Cain also is shown doing these
same things, the first disciple of the devil, and taught by him envy and
murder, the sister lawlessnesses, which Paul also yoked together, saying:
"Full of envy, of murder." What then was it that he did? He saw the honor from
God, and was kindled toward jealousy, and he killed the one honored, in order
that he might fasten upon the one who honored. For being unable toward the war
against God, he fell over into fratricide. Let us flee, brothers, a disease
that is a teacher of war against God, a mother of manslaughter, a confusion of
nature, an ignorance of kinship, a most irrational calamity. Why are you
grieved, O man, having suffered nothing terrible? Why do you war against the
one who is in certain good things, who has lessened none of your things? And
if indeed even being benefited you are indignant, do you not outright envy
your own benefit? Of such a kind was Saul, who made the excesses of the
benefits an occasion of the war against David. For first, having been
delivered from the madness by that all-harmonious and divine music, he tried
to pierce through the benefactor with a spear; then, having been saved
together with the army itself from the enemies, and having been delivered from
the shame concerning Goliath, nevertheless since in the victory songs the
women dancing attributed to David the tenfold of the cause of the things that
happened: "David has struck in tens of thousands, and Saul in his thousands";
because of this one voice, and the testimony that arose from the truth itself,
first he attempted to become a slayer with his own hand, and to kill from
ambush; then having made him a fugitive, not even so did he stop the enmity,
but at last having marched out against him with three thousand chosen men, he
searched through the wildernesses. And if he had been asked the pretext of the
war, he would have said altogether the good deeds of the man.

Who at the very time of the pursuit, having been caught sleeping, lying ready
for slaughter to the enemy, and again having been saved by the righteous man,
who guarded himself from bringing hands upon him, was not even bent by this
benefit; but again he levied an army, and again he pursued; until, having been
caught even a second time by the same man in the cave, he showed the virtue of
the one more brilliant, and made his own wickedness more manifest. Envy is a
most unmanageable kind of enmity. For good deeds make those otherwise hostile
more gentle; =31.377= but to suffer well further inflames the envious and
ill-disposed man; and by as much as he happens upon greater things, he is the
more indignant and distressed, and is displeased. For he is more vexed at the
power of the benefactor, than he has gratitude for the things coming to be
toward him. What beast do they not conquer by the harshness of their manners?
What of the untamed do they not surpass in wildness? Dogs being fed are tamed;
lions become tractable being tended; but the envious are further made wild by
the tendings. What made the noble Joseph a slave? Was it not the envy of the
brothers? Where it is also worthy to marvel at the irrationality of the
disease. For fearing the outcome of the dreams, they were making the brother a
slave, as though a slave would never be worshipped. And yet if the dreams were
true, what device is there that the things foretold should not altogether come
out? And if the visions of the dreams were false, for what do you envy the one
who is mistaken? But now by the economy of God

their cleverness is overturned. For through the things by which they thought
to hinder the prediction, through these they were shown making a way for the
outcome.

For if he had not been sold, he would not have gone into Egypt, he would not
through chastity have fallen into the plots of the unchaste woman, he would
not have been cast into prison, he would not have become familiar with the
servants of Pharaoh, nor would he have interpreted the dreams, from which he
received the rule of Egypt, and because of the famine, his brothers having
come to him, he was worshipped. Pass over with the word to the greatest envy,
and that which arose upon the greatest things, which came to be from the
madness of the Jews against the Savior. Why was he envied? Because of the
wonders. And what were the wonder-workings? Salvation of those in need; the
hungry were fed, and the one feeding was warred against; the dead were raised,
and the life-giver was envied; demons were driven out, and the one commanding
was plotted against; lepers were cleansed, and lame men walked, deaf men
heard, and blind men looked up, and the benefactor was driven into exile. And
at last they delivered to death the one who granted life, and they scourged
the liberator of men, and they condemned the judge of the world. Thus the
evils of envy went through as far as all things. And with this one weapon,
beginning from the foundation of the world until the consummation of the age,
the destroyer of our life, the devil, wounds and casts down all, the one who
rejoices at our destruction, who fell through envy, and who casts us down
together with himself through the same passion. Wise then was the one who did
not even permit dining with an envious man; speaking from the association at
the dinner concerning all together the communion according to life. For just
as it is a care to us to set combustible matter as far as possible from the
fire, so =31.380= it is necessary, as far as it is possible, to lead
friendships away from the company of the envious, making ourselves outside the
darts of envy. For it is not otherwise possible to be entangled with envy,
unless one has drawn near to it through intimacy. Since according to the word
of Solomon, "To a man the jealousy is from his companion." For so it is.

The Scythian does not envy the Egyptian, but each his fellow-countryman; and
in the same nation, however, he does not envy those unknown, but
those most familiar; and of the familiar, the neighbors and fellow-craftsmen,
and those otherwise intimates; and among these again, age-mates and kinsmen
and brothers. And in a word, just as rust-blight is a proper disease of wheat,
so envy is a sickness of friendship. That at least one might even praise of
the evil, that by as much as it is moved more vehemently, by so much it is
more grievous to the one who possesses it. For just as of darts those borne
violently, whenever they fall upon something strong and resistant, return upon
the one who sent them; so also the motions of envy, grieving the one envied in
nothing, become blows of the envious man himself. For who, being grieved, ever
lessened the good things of the neighbor? Himself, however, he further wasted,
being melted by the griefs. And already those sick with the evil eye have even
been supposed to be more destructive than the venomous creatures themselves;
if indeed those inject the venom through a blow, and little by little the
thing bitten is grazed by putrefactions; but some think that the envious even
through the eyes alone impose the harm; so that bodies in good condition, and
having flowered out from the prime according to age into the peak bloom, waste
away being bewitched by them, and the whole bulk is destroyed together all at
once, as of some destructive stream flowing off from the envious eyes, and
blighting and corrupting. But I dismiss this account, as popular and
introduced into the women's quarters by old women; but that I say, that the
demons who hate the good, whenever they find choices akin to themselves, use
them in every way toward their own wish; so as even to use the eyes of the
envious for service of their own will. Then do you not

shudder, making yourself a servant of a destructive demon, but you receive an
evil, through which you will become an enemy of those who wrong nothing, and
an enemy of God who is good and ungrudging? Let us flee an unbearable evil.

It is a teaching of a serpent, a discovery of demons, a sowing of an enemy, an
earnest of punishment, a hindrance of piety, a way upon Gehenna, a deprivation
of the kingdom. And the envious happen to be somehow clear even by the face
itself. Their eye is dry and without gleam, the cheek downcast, the brow
collapsed =31.381=, the soul confused by the passion, not having the criterion
of truth upon the things. No deed according to virtue is praiseworthy among
those, no power of speech adorned with dignity and graces, nor any other of
the things emulated and looked around at. And just as vultures are borne
toward the foul-smelling things, flying over many meadows, and many pleasant
and fragrant places; and flies run past the healthy thing, but hasten toward
the sore; so the envious do not even look at the splendors of life and the
magnitudes of the things accomplished, but they set upon the unsound things;
and if it happens that something stumbles, as many are the things of men, this
they make public, and from these they wish to make the men known; just as the
wicked of the painters, from the twisted nose, or from some scar or
mutilation, having come upon them from nature or from a passion, signifying
the forms of those being painted. And they are clever at spitting out the
praiseworthy by turnings toward the worse, and at slandering virtue from the
neighboring vice. For they call the brave man rash, and the temperate man
without feeling; the just man harsh; the prudent man a malefactor. And they
slander the magnificent man as vulgar, and the liberal man as prodigal; again
the economical man as stingy. And in a word, all the forms of virtue do not
lack among them a name transferred from the opposing vice. What then? Shall we
stop the word in the accusation of the evil? But this is as it were some half
of the healing. For to show the one who is sick the magnitude of the disease,
so as to produce in him a care of the evil worthy of it, is not useless; but
to leave it as far as this, not having led by the hand toward health, is
nothing other than to leave the one who is sick given over to the sickness.

What then? How might we not suffer the disease from the beginning, or having
been led under it might we escape? If first we should judge none of the human
things great, nor extraordinary; not the plenty among men; not the glory that
withers; not good condition of body. For we do not define the good to be in
the things that pass by, but we have been called to a participation of
eternal and true goods. So that the one who is rich is not yet to be emulated
because of the wealth; nor the ruler because of the bulk of the dignity, nor
the strong man because of the strength of the body, nor the wise man because
of the abundance of speech. For these are instruments of virtue for those who
have used them well, not having the blessedness themselves in themselves. The
one therefore who has used them badly is pitiable, as the one who with the
sword, which he received toward the defense against the enemies, with this
willingly wounds himself; if =31.384= but he handles the present things well
and according to the right reason, and is a steward of the things from God,
and does not treasure toward his own enjoyment, he is just to be praised and
loved because of the brother-loving and sharing manner. Again someone differs
in prudence, and has been honored with a word of God, and is an interpreter of
sacred oracles; do not envy such a one, nor ever wish the interpreter of the
sacred things to be silent, if by the grace of the Spirit some acceptance and
praise from those hearing follows. For the good is yours, and the gift of the
teaching was sent to you through the brother, if you are willing to receive
it. Then, no one stops up a spring that gushes; and no one covers the eyes
when the sun is shining, nor envies those things, but prays together for
himself the enjoyment; but when a spiritual word is gushing in the Church, and
a pious heart is springing from the gifts

of the Spirit, do you not hold out the ears with gladness? Do you not receive
the benefit thankfully? But the applause of those hearing bites you, and you
would have wished that neither the one being benefited should be, nor the one
praising. What defense will these things have before the judge of our hearts?
It is necessary then to consider the good of the soul to be fair by nature;
but the one flowering out with wealth, and thinking great things of power, and
of good condition of body, and using well the things he has, to love and to
tend, as having acquired the instruments of life as common, if indeed he
should handle these according to the right reason; so as by the supply of the
money to be ungrudging to those in need, and with the body to furnish service
to the weak; and to consider all the remaining abundance not more his own,
than also of anyone of those in want; but the one not so disposed toward these
things to set down as wretched rather than enviable, if he has greater
occasions toward being evil. For this is to be destroyed from a greater
preparation and business. For if wealth is a provision toward injustice, the
one who is rich is pitiable; but if a service toward virtue, the evil eye has
no room, the benefit from them being set forth common to all; except unless
someone then by the abundance of wickedness should even envy himself of the
goods. And in a word, having looked over the human things with the reasoning,
and having looked toward the truly fair and praiseworthy, you would be far
from judging any of the corruptible and earthly things blessed and to be
emulated.

And for one being thus, and not struck as at great things by the worldly
things, it is impossible that envy should ever come to be present. And if you
altogether desire glory, and wish to appear above the many, and because of
this you do not endure to be second =31.385= (for this too then is an occasion
toward envying), then transfer your love of honor, as some stream, upon the
acquisition of virtue. For do not wish altogether to be rich by every means,
nor to be well-reputed from the things of the world. For these things are not
upon you; but be just, and temperate, and prudent, and brave, and enduring in
the sufferings on behalf of piety. For thus you will both save yourself, and
upon greater goods you will have a greater distinction. For virtue is upon us,
and able to be acquired by the one who loves labor; but the surrounding of
money, and bloom of body, and bulk of dignities, are not upon us. If then
virtue is both a greater good and more lasting, and having the preference
confessed by all, this is to be pursued by us; which it is impossible to come
to be in the soul not being purified both from the other passions and most of
all from the evil eye. Do you not see how great an evil hypocrisy is? And this
too is a fruit of envy. For the doubleness of character comes to be in men
especially from envy, whenever, holding the hatred in the depth, they show the
surface dyed with love; according to the submerged rocks, which, being covered
with a little water, become an unforeseen evil to the unguarded. If then also
death has flowed upon us from there as from a spring, a fall from goods, an
alienation from God, a confusion of ordinances, and an overturning of all
together the fair things according to life; let us be persuaded by the
Apostle, and "Let us not become vainglorious, challenging one another, envying
one another"; but rather kind, tender-hearted, granting freely to one another,
as also God granted freely to us, in Christ Jesus our Lord, with whom be the
glory to the Father with the Holy Spirit unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
