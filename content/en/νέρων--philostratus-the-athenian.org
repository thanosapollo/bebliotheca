* Menecrates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

The digging of the Isthmus, Musonius, which they say you too had a hand in,
showed a Greek mind in the tyrant.

* Musonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Know, Menecrates, that Nero had even better thoughts than that. For by a
breach of twenty stades through the Isthmus he would have spared seafarers the
circuit of the Peloponnese past Malea. That would have helped trade, the
cities on the sea, and those inland as well; for even they find the fruit of
their own land enough, if the coast does well.

* Menecrates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Go through it then, Musonius. We all wish to hear, unless you mean to be busy
with something else.

* Musonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

I will go through it, since you wish it. I do not know how I could better
oblige men who have come for serious study to so grim a schoolroom. Songs,
then, had drawn Nero to Achaea, and his own fierce conviction that not even
the Muses could strike up more sweetly. He wished also to take the crown at
Olympia, the most athletic of the games, by singing. The Pythian games he
thought belonged to himself more than to Apollo, for not even Apollo would set
lyre and song against him. The Isthmus was not among the plans he had formed
from far off; but when he met the nature of the place he fell in love with a
grand work, thinking of the king of the Achaeans who once went against Troy,
how he cut Euboea from Boeotia by the Euripus at Chalcis, and of Darius too,
how the Bosporus was bridged for him against the Scythians. Perhaps even
before these he thought of the works of Xerxes, the greatest of grand works,
and besides he thought that by mixing all peoples with one another over a
short crossing Greece would feast in splendour on what came from outside. For
tyrannical natures are drunk, yet they somehow reach... even to hear this sort
of praise. Coming forward from his tent he sang a hymn to Amphitrite and
Poseidon, and a short song to Melicertes and Leucothea. When the governor of
Greece held out a golden mattock to him, he rushed at the digging amid
clapping and songs, and struck the earth three times, I think, and after
urging those entrusted with the start to take hold of the work in earnest, he
went up to Corinth thinking he had outdone all the labours of Heracles. The
men from the prisons toiled at the rocky and hard ground, the army at the
earthy and level parts. On about the seventh and fifth day after we had been
yoked to the Isthmus, an unclear report came down from Corinth that Nero had
changed his mind about the cutting. They said the Egyptians, surveying the
nature of each sea, had found them not on a level, but thought the sea from
Lechaeum higher, and feared for Aegina; for if so great a sea poured over the
island, Aegina might be swept under. But not even Thales, the wisest and most
given to natural inquiry, would have turned Nero from cutting the Isthmus; for
he loved to cut it more than to sing in public. The rising of the western
nations, and the consul who is now taking them in hand there, Vindex by name,
have drawn Nero away from Greece and the Isthmus after his cold surveying; for
I know the seas are level with the land and with each other. They say that
affairs at Rome too are already slipping and giving way under him. You
yourselves heard this yesterday from the tribune who sailed in.

* Menecrates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And the voice, Musonius, for which he is mad for the Muses and loves Olympian
and Pythian crowns, how does it stand with the tyrant? For of those who have
sailed to Lemnos some wondered at it, others laughed.

* Musonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

As for him, Menecrates, his voice is neither wonderful nor ridiculous; nature
has tuned him blamelessly and in the middle. He speaks with a hollow, heavy
sound by nature, since his throat is set deep, and the notes, with a throat so
made, boom somewhat. The pitch of the notes smooths this over, since he does
not trust himself, but the kindness of his colouring, a yielding melody, a
neat cithara-playing, and, where the moment calls, walking, standing,
shifting, and matching his nod to the tunes; the only shame is that a king
should seem to perfect these things. But if he imitates his betters, what
laughter, how much of it falls from the spectators, though countless fears
hang over anyone who laughs at him. For he nods more than is moderate,
gathering his breath, and stands apart on the tips of his feet, bent back like
men on the wheel. Naturally ruddy, he grows redder still as his face burns,
and his breath is short and not enough, I think.

* Menecrates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And those in contest against him, how do they give way, Musonius? For they
humour him by craft, I suppose.

* Musonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

By craft, yes, like wrestlers who take a fall. But think, Menecrates, of the
tragic actor, how he died at the Isthmus. There are dangers around the crafts
too, if those who work them stretch them too far.

* Menecrates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

And what is this, Musonius? I have heard nothing of the story.

* Musonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

Hear then a tale that is strange, but done before the eyes of Greeks. Though
custom at the Isthmus forbids contest in comedy or tragedy, Nero thought fit
to win as a tragic actor, and several entered this contest, but the man from
Epirus, who had the best voice, and was in repute and wonder for it, made a
show more brilliant than usual, pretending to love the crown and not to let it
go until Nero gave him ten talents for the victory. Nero grew savage and was
in a rage, for he was listening under the stage at the contest itself. When
the Greeks shouted for the Epirote, he sent his secretary bidding the man
yield to him. But he raised his voice still higher and contended as if among
equals, so Nero sent in his own actors onto the platform as if they belonged
to the business; for they had ivory tablets, two-leaved, held out like
daggers, and standing the Epirote up against the nearby pillar they broke his
throat, striking with the tablets edge-on.

* Menecrates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

And did he win in tragedy, Musonius, after working so foul a deed before the
eyes of the Greeks?

* Musonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

Child's play, this, to a young man who had killed his mother. If he killed a
tragic actor by cutting out his voice, why wonder? He even set out to block
the Pythian mouth from which the oracles breathed, so that not even Apollo
should have a voice, though the Pythian had listed him with the Oresteses and
Alcmaeons, to whom even matricide gave some claim of glory, since they avenged
their fathers. But he, quite unable to say whom he had avenged, thought
himself insulted by the god, though he heard milder things than the truth. But
in the midst of our talk, what is this ship coming on? It seems to bring some
good; for they have garlands on their heads, like a chorus of good omen, and
someone from the prow stretches out his hand bidding us take heart and
rejoice, and he shouts, unless I mishear, that Nero is gone.

* Menecrates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

He does shout it, Musonius, and more clearly the nearer he touches land. Well
done, you gods.

* Musonius
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

But let us not pray thanks. They say one must not, over the dead.
