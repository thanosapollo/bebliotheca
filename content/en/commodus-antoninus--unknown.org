* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Enough has already been said about the parents of Commodus Antoninus in the
Life of Marcus Antoninus. He himself was born at Lanuvium, together with his
twin brother Antoninus, on the day before the Kalends of September, in the
consulship of his father and his uncle, the same place where his mother's
father is also said to have been born. When Faustina was pregnant with
Commodus and his brother, she dreamed that she gave birth to snakes, and that
one of them was fiercer than the rest. After she had borne Commodus and
Antoninus, Antoninus was carried out for burial at four years old, though the
astrologers had promised that he would match Commodus in the course of the
stars. When the brother was dead, Marcus tried to train Commodus both by his
own teaching and through great and excellent men. He had Onesicrates as his
Greek tutor and Capella Antistius as his Latin; Ateius Sanctus was his teacher
of rhetoric.

But so many masters of so many studies did him no good. Such is the force of
native character, or of those who pass as tutors at court. From his earliest
boyhood he was base, dishonest, cruel, and lustful, and defiled besides in
mouth and body. He was already skilled in arts that did not belong to the
imperial station: he would mould cups, dance, sing, whistle, and show himself
a finished buffoon and gladiator. He gave a first sign of his cruelty at
Centumcellae in his twelfth year. When he had happened to bathe in water that
was too lukewarm, he ordered the bath attendant thrown into the furnace. The
tutor who had been given that order burned a ram's hide in the furnace
instead, so that the stench of the smoke would make the punishment seem real.

As a boy he was named Caesar together with his brother Verus. In his
fourteenth year he was admitted into the college of priests.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

He was co-opted among the Trossuli, the chiefs of the youth, when he took the
toga. While still in the boy's bordered toga he gave a congiarium and himself
presided in the Basilica of Trajan. He put on the toga on the Nones of July,
the day on which Romulus vanished from the earth, and at the time when Cassius
broke with Marcus. He set out with his father for Syria and Egypt, recommended
to the soldiers, and returned with him to Rome. After this, when a
dispensation from the law of years had been obtained, he was made consul, and
with his father he was named emperor on the fifth day before the Kalends of
December, in the consulship of Pollio and Aper. He also triumphed with his
father, for the Senate had decreed that too. He went with his father as well
to the German war.

He could not bear the more respectable men appointed as guardians of his life.
He kept the worst of them, and when they were removed he missed them to the
point of sickness. When they were restored through his father's softness, he
kept taverns and stews always in the Palatine house, and never spared either
shame or expense. He gambled in the palace. He gathered women of finer looks
as if they were slave prostitutes, under the show of a brothel and in mockery
of chastity. He copied the hawkers who go from market to market. He bought
himself chariot horses. He drove in a charioteer's dress, lived with
gladiators, and carried himself like a pimp's servant, so that you would have
thought him born for disgrace rather than for the station to which fortune had
raised him.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

He dismissed his father's older attendants and threw off his aged friends. He
made a vain attempt on the son of Salvius Julianus, who commanded the armies,
on a charge of unchastity, and from then on he laid traps for Julianus. He
cast out the most honorable men either by insult or by an office too base for
them. The mimes mocked him as a man who had been debauched; he suddenly
deported them so that they were no longer seen. Yielding to the enemy's terms,
he also gave up the war that his father had almost finished, and returned to
Rome.

When he came back to Rome he triumphed with his catamite Saoterus placed
behind him in the chariot, and he kissed him often in public, turning his
neck. He did the same even in the orchestra. While he drank until daylight and
squandered the strength of the Roman empire, he also ran about at evening
through taverns and brothels. He sent out as provincial governors either
partners in his crimes or men recommended by the guilty. He fell into such
hatred with the Senate that he himself raged cruelly for the ruin of that
great order, and from being despised he became cruel.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Commodus' way of life drove Quadratus and Lucilla to form plans for his
murder, not without the counsel of the praetorian prefect Tarrutenius
Paternus. The business of carrying out the killing was given to Claudius
Pompeianus, a kinsman. He went in to Commodus with a drawn sword, and though
he had the chance to do the deed, he burst out with the words "The Senate
sends you this dagger," and so the fool disclosed the crime and did not finish
it. Many shared the cause with him. After this Pompeianus was killed first,
then Quadratus, then Norbana and Norbanus and Paralius. Paralius' mother and
Lucilla were driven into exile.

Then the praetorian prefects, seeing that Commodus had fallen into such hatred
because of Saoterus, whose power the Roman people could not bear, had Saoterus
killed by the frumentarii when he had been shrewdly drawn from the Palace for
a rite and was returning to his gardens. This was harder for Commodus than a
blow against himself. As for Paternus, the author of this killing and, as it
seemed, both a party to the planned murder of Commodus and the man who had
stepped in so that the conspiracy would not be punished more widely, Commodus,
at Tigidius' urging, removed him from the prefecture by granting him the honor
of the broad stripe. A few days later he charged him with
conspiracy, saying that Paternus' daughter had been promised to Julianus' son
so that the empire might be transferred to Julianus. For that reason he killed
Paternus, Julianus, and Vitruvius Secundus, Paternus' closest friend, who had
charge of the imperial correspondence. Besides this the whole house of the
Quintilii was wiped out, because Sextus, the son of Condianus, was said to
have escaped to revolt by a pretended death. Vitrasia Faustina, Velius Rufus,
and the consular Egnatius Capito were also killed. The consuls Aemilius Iuncus
and Atilius Severus were driven into exile, and many others were treated with
various cruelty.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

After this Commodus never went out in public readily, and he would not let
anything be reported to him except what Perennis had first handled. Perennis,
knowing Commodus well, found a way to be powerful himself: he persuaded
Commodus to leave himself free for pleasures while Perennis took on the cares
of state. Commodus accepted this gladly. Living by that rule, he raged through
banquets and baths in the Palace with three hundred concubines, whom he had
gathered for beauty from a selection of matrons and whores, and with three
hundred other grown catamites, whom he had likewise collected from the commons
and the nobility by force and by price, with looks as the judge. Amid this he
offered victims in the dress of a sacrificial attendant. He fought in the
arena with wooden swords, and among his chamberlains as a gladiator, sometimes
with shining steel. Perennis, however, claimed everything for himself: he
killed whom he wished, stripped many, overturned all rights, and poured all
the plunder into his own lap. Commodus himself, after sending his sister
Lucilla to Capri, killed her. Then, after debauching his other sisters, it is
said, and joining his father's cousin to his embraces, he even gave one of his
concubines his mother's name. His wife, whom he caught in adultery and drove
out, he banished once she was driven out and later killed. He ordered his own
concubines to be debauched before his eyes, and he did not escape the infamy
of young men rushing upon him. He was defiled in every part of the body and in
the mouth toward either sex.

At that time Claudius too was killed, as if by brigands; his son had once gone
in to Commodus with a dagger. Many other senators were put to death without
trial, and rich women as well. In the provinces some who were accused by
Perennis for their wealth were stripped or even killed. Those for whom there
was no made-up charge to pin on them were reproached with this, that they had
refused to write Commodus as their heir.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

At that time Perennis put down to his own son successes in Sarmatia that other
generals had won. Yet this Perennis, who had been so powerful, was suddenly
named a public enemy, because in the British war he had set men of equestrian
rank over the soldiers after removing senators. The matter was betrayed by the
army's envoys, and he was handed over to the soldiers to be torn apart. In the
place of his power Commodus put Cleander, one of the chamberlains.

After Perennis and his son had been killed, Commodus cancelled many things as
if they had not been done by him, restoring them as if to their former state.
He could not keep even this repentance for his crimes more than thirty days,
and through Cleander he did worse than he had done through Perennis. Cleander
had succeeded Perennis in power, but Niger succeeded in the prefecture, a man
said to have been praetorian prefect for only six hours. For the praetorian
prefects were changed by hours and days, while Commodus did everything worse
than he had done before. Marcius Quartus was praetorian prefect for five days.
Their successors were kept or killed at Cleander's will. At his nod even
freedmen were enrolled in the Senate and among the patricians. Then for the
first time there were twenty-five consuls in one year, and all the provinces
were sold. Cleander sold everything for money. He adorned men recalled from
exile with offices, and he cancelled cases already judged. Through Commodus'
folly he had such power that he dragged Burrus, the husband of Commodus'
sister, who was finding fault and reporting to Commodus what was being done,
into a suspicion of aiming at the throne, and killed him, and many others who
were defending Burrus were killed with him. The prefect Aebutianus was killed
among these. In his place Cleander himself was made prefect with two others
whom he had chosen. Then for the first time there were three praetorian
prefects, and among them a freedman, who was called the Dagger.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

But a worthy end of life was at last set on Cleander too. When by his plots
Arrius Antoninus had been killed on made-up charges to please Attalus, whom
Antoninus had condemned in the proconsulate of Asia, and Commodus could not
then bear that hatred while the people raged, he was given to the commons for
punishment. Apolaustus and other court freedmen were killed with him. Among
other things Cleander had also debauched Commodus' concubines, and had sons by
them; after his death those sons were killed with their mothers.

Julianus and Regillus were put in his place, and Commodus later punished them
too. When they had been killed he put to death Servilius and Dulius Silanus
with their households, then Antius Lupus and the Petronii, Mamertinus and
Sura, and Mamertinus' son Antoninus, born of Commodus' own sister. After them,
six men of consular rank at once: Allius Fuscus, Caelius Felix, Lucceius
Torquatus, Larcius Eurupianus, Valerius Bassianus, and Pactumeius Magnus, with
their households; and in Asia the proconsul Sulpicius Crassus and Julius
Proculus with their households, the consular Claudius Lucanus, and Faustina
Annia, his father's cousin, in Achaea, and countless others. He had also meant
to kill fourteen more, when his expenses could no longer be borne by the
strength of the Roman empire.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Amid this, while the Senate mocked him, after he had designated his mother's
adulterer as consul, he was named Pius. When he had killed Perennis he was
named Felix, among the many slaughters of many citizens, as if some new Sulla.
This same Commodus, that Pius, that Felix, is said to have invented a
conspiracy against himself as well, so that he might kill many. There was no
other revolt except that of Alexander, who later killed himself and his own,
and that of his sister Lucilla. Flatterers also named Commodus Britannicus,
though the Britons had even wanted to choose an emperor against him. He was
also named the Roman Hercules, because he had killed wild beasts at Lanuvium
in the amphitheatre. For it was his custom to kill beasts at home. He had
besides this madness, that he wanted the city of Rome to be called the
Commodian colony. That frenzy is said to have been put into him among Marcia's
blandishments. He also wanted to drive four-horse chariots in the Circus. He
came out in public in a Dalmatian tunic and so gave the signal for starting
the chariots, and that at the very time when he referred to the Senate the
making of Rome into a Commodian city. Not only did the Senate accept this
gladly, in mockery, as far as can be understood, but it even called itself
Commodian, naming Commodus Hercules and a god.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

He pretended that he would go to Africa as well, so that he might exact the
travel allowance, and he exacted it and turned it to banquets and dice. He
killed Motilenus, the praetorian prefect, with poison in figs. He received
statues in the dress of Hercules, and sacrifice was offered to him as to a
god. He had prepared besides to kill many. This was betrayed by a little boy
who threw out of the bedroom a tablet on which the names of those to be killed
were written.

He celebrated the rites of Isis, so that he both shaved his head and carried
Anubis. Out of a taste for cruelty he ordered the servants of Bellona to cut
their arms in earnest. He forced the devotees of Isis to beat their breasts
with pine cones to the point of ruin. When he carried Anubis he struck the
heads of the Isiacs hard with the mouth of the image. With a club, in a
woman's dress and a lion's skin, he struck down not only lions but many men as
well. Those lame in the feet, and those who could not walk, he dressed in the
manner of giants, so that from the knees they were covered with rags and
linens as if with snakes, and he finished them with arrows. He polluted the
rites of Mithras with real homicide, though there it is usual that something
be said or pretended only for the look of fear.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Even as a boy he was both a glutton and unchaste. As a youth he disgraced
every kind of person who was with him, and he was disgraced by all. Those who
mocked him he threw to the beasts. He even ordered a man who had read
Suetonius' book containing the life of Caligula thrown to the beasts, because
he had the same birthday as Caligula. If anyone had said beforehand that he
wished to die, Commodus ordered him thrown down against his will. He was
deadly in his jokes as well. When he had seen a man whose greying hairs among
the black looked like little worms, he had a starling set on him, which
thought it was hunting worms, and by the beating of its beak he sent the man
away with a festering head. He split a fat man down the middle of the belly so
that his guts poured out at once. Those from whom he had taken one eye, or
whose one foot he had broken, he nicknamed One-eye and One-foot. He killed
many others here and there besides, some because they had met him in barbarian
dress, others because they were noble and handsome. He kept as pets men named
after the private parts of either sex, whom he was readier to kiss than
others. He also had a man whose penis stood out beyond the measure of animals,
whom he called Onos, dearest to himself, whom he both enriched and set over
the priesthood of rustic Hercules.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He is said often to have mixed human dung into the costliest foods, and not to
have kept from tasting them, while others, as he thought, were mocked. He had
two twisted hunchbacks served to himself on a silver platter, soaked in
mustard, and at once he promoted them and made them rich. He shoved his
praetorian prefect Julianus, in his toga, into a fish pond in the presence of
his staff. He also ordered him to dance naked before his concubines, shaking
cymbals with a distorted face. Because of the unbroken round of his luxury he
rarely had any kind of cooked vegetables served at a banquet. He bathed seven
and eight times a day and ate in the baths themselves. He went into the
temples of the gods defiled with debauchery and human blood. He also played
the doctor, so that he might let blood from men with deadly lancets.

The months too, in his honor, flatterers named Commodus in place of August,
Hercules in place of September, Invictus in place of October, Exsuperatorius
in place of November, and Amazonius in place of December, from his own device.
He was called Amazonius from his love of his concubine Marcia, whom he loved
painted as an Amazon, and for her sake he himself wanted to go into the Roman
arena in Amazon dress.

He also entered a gladiatorial contest and took the names of gladiators with
as much joy as if he were receiving triumphal honors. He always went into the
gladiators' school, and whenever he went in he ordered it entered in the
public records. He is said to have fought seven hundred and thirty-five times.

He was named among the Caesars on the fourth day before the Ides of October,
which he later named the Herculean Ides, in the consulship of Pudens and
Pollio. He was named Germanicus on the Herculean Ides, in the consulship of
Maximus and Orfitus.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

He was taken into all the priestly colleges as a priest on the thirteenth day
before the Invincible Kalends, in the consulship of Piso and Julianus. He set
out for Germany on the fourteenth day before the Aelian Kalends, as he later
named them. In the same consulship he received the man's toga. With his father
he was named emperor on the fifth day before the Exsuperatorian Kalends, in
the second consulship of Pollio and Aper. He triumphed on the tenth day before
the Kalends of January in the same consulship. He set out a second time on the
third day before the Commodian Nones, in the consulship of Orfitus and Rufus.
He was given in perpetuity by the army and Senate to be kept in the Commodian
Palatine house on the eleventh day before the Roman Kalends, in the second
consulship of Praesens. When he was thinking of a third departure he was held
back by the Senate and his people. Vows were made for him on the Pious Nones
in the second consulship of Fuscianus. Amid this it is put on record that he
fought under his father three hundred and sixty-five times. Later too he won
so many gladiatorial palms, either by defeating net-fighters or by killing
them, that he reached a thousand. With his own hand he killed many thousands
of various beasts, even elephants. And he did this often with the Roman people
looking on.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

He was strong for these things, otherwise weak and feeble, and with a swelling
between the groins as well, so that the Roman people knew the tumor through
his silk clothes. Many verses were written on him, of which Marius Maximus
even boasts in his own work. He had such strength for finishing beasts that he
could run an elephant through with a pike, send a spear through an oryx's
horn, and with single blows finish many thousands of huge beasts. He had such
shamelessness that, sitting in a woman's dress in the amphitheatre or the
theatre, he very often drank in public.

Yet while he lived like this, the Moors were defeated under him through his
lieutenants, the Dacians were defeated, Pannonia too was settled, and in
Britain, in Germany, and in Dacia the provincials refused his rule; all this
was quieted through the generals. Commodus himself was slow and careless in
signing, so that he subscribed many petitions with one form, and in most
letters wrote only "Farewell." Everything was done through others, who are
even said to have turned condemnations into their own purse.

* Chapter 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

Through this negligence, when those who then managed the state were also
wasting the grain supply, a huge scarcity arose at Rome, though the crops were
not lacking. Those who were wasting everything Commodus later killed and
proscribed. He himself, pretending by name a Commodian golden age, proclaimed
cheap prices, from which he made a greater want.

Many under him bought off with money both another's punishment and their own
safety. He also sold the varieties of punishments, and burials, and the
lessening of evils, and he killed some in place of others. He also sold
provinces and administrations, while those through whom he sold took a share,
and Commodus a share. To some he even sold the killing of their enemies. Under
him the freedmen also sold the outcomes of lawsuits. He did not long endure
the prefects Paternus and Perennis, yet in such a way that of the prefects he
himself had made none completed three years. He killed most of them either by
poison or by the sword, and he changed the city prefects with the same ease.

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

He killed his chamberlains readily, though he had always done everything at
their nod. Eclectus the chamberlain, when he saw him killing chamberlains so
readily, forestalled him and took part in the faction for his death.

As a spectator of gladiators he took up arms, covering his bare shoulders with
a purple cloth. He had besides this custom, that he ordered everything he did
in a base, unclean, cruel, gladiatorial, or pimping way to be entered in the
city records, as the writings of Marius Maximus attest. He also called the
Roman people Commodian, and very often, with them present, he fought as a
gladiator. Indeed, when the people had often favored him as he fought as if he
were a god, he thought himself mocked and had ordered the Roman people to be
killed in the amphitheatre by the sailors of the fleet who drew the awnings,
and he had ordered the city burned, as being his own colony. This would have
been done if Laetus, the praetorian prefect, had not frightened Commodus off.
Among his other triumphal names he was also called, six hundred and twenty
times, First Palus of the Secutores.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

These prodigies happened in his reign both in public and in private: a hairy
star appeared. Footprints of the gods were seen going out in the Forum. Before
the war of the deserters the sky caught fire. A sudden fog and darkness rose
in the Circus on the Kalends of January; and before dawn there had been
fire-bringing birds and ill-omened ones as well. He himself moved from the
Palatine to the Vectilian house on the Caelian hill, saying he could not sleep
in the Palatine. The two-faced Janus opened of his own accord, and a marble
image of Anubis was seen to move. A bronze statue of Hercules in the Minucia
sweated for several days. An owl was also caught above his bedroom, both at
Rome and at Lanuvium. He himself made no small omen against himself: for when
he had put his hand into the wound of a slain gladiator, he wiped it on his
head, and against custom he ordered the spectators to come to the show in
cloaks, not in togas, which was the custom at funerals, while he himself
presided in dark clothes. His helmet was twice carried out through the Gate of
Libitina.

He gave the people a congiarium of seven hundred and twenty-five denarii
apiece. Toward all others he was most sparing, because he had lessened the
treasury by the costs of his luxury. He added many circus games from caprice
rather than from religion, and so that he might enrich the masters of the
factions.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

Stirred by these things, though far too late, Quintus Aemilius Laetus the
prefect and Marcia his concubine formed a conspiracy to kill him. First they
gave him poison; when that worked too little, they had him strangled by an
athlete with whom he used to train.

He had a body of proper size, a rather stupid face as drunkards commonly have,
and rude speech, his hair always dyed and lit with gold dust, singeing his
hair and beard for fear of the barber.

The Senate and people demanded that his body be dragged with a hook and thrown
into the Tiber, but afterward, by order of Pertinax, it was moved into the
monument of Hadrian.

No works of his remain except the bath which Cleander had built in his name,
but the Senate erased his name, which had been cut on other men's works. He
did not finish his father's works. He established an African fleet, which was
to be a reserve if the Alexandrian grain should ever fail. He even
ridiculously named Carthage Alexandria Commodiana Togata, after he had also
named the African fleet Commodiana Herculea. He did add some ornaments to the
Colossus, all of which were later taken away. He took off the head of the
Colossus, which was Nero's, and put his own on, and he wrote a title in the
usual way, not omitting Gladiator and Effeminate. Yet Severus, a grave emperor
and a man worthy of his name, from hatred of the Senate, as it seems, placed
him among the gods, adding a flamen, whom Commodus while alive had prepared
for himself, the Herculean Commodian flamen.

He left three sisters still living. Severus established that his birthday
should be celebrated.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

The acclamations of the Senate after the death of Commodus were heavy. In
order that it may be known what the Senate's judgment of Commodus was, I have
inserted the acclamations themselves from Marius Maximus, and the opinion of
the senate's decree:

/Let the honors of the enemy of the fatherland be taken away. Let the honors
of the parricide be taken away. Let the parricide be dragged. Let the enemy
of the fatherland, the parricide, the gladiator, be mangled in the
spoliarium. Enemy of the gods, butcher of the Senate, enemy of the gods,
parricide of the Senate; enemy of the gods, enemy of the Senate: the
gladiator into the spoliarium. Let him who killed the Senate be put in the
spoliarium; let him who killed the Senate be dragged with the hook; let him
who killed the innocent be dragged with the hook. Enemy, parricide, truly,
truly. Let him who did not spare his own blood be dragged with the hook. Let
him who was going to kill you be dragged with the hook. You were afraid with
us; you were in danger with us. That we may be safe, Jupiter Best and
Greatest, keep Pertinax for us. Good fortune to the loyalty of the
praetorians, good fortune to the praetorian cohorts, good fortune to the
Roman armies, good fortune to the piety of the Senate.

Let the parricide be dragged. We ask, Augustus, let the parricide be dragged.
This we ask: let the parricide be dragged. Hear, Caesar: the informers to the
lion. Hear, Caesar: Speratus to the lion. Good fortune to the victory of the
Roman people, good fortune to the loyalty of the soldiers, good fortune to the
loyalty of the praetorians, good fortune to the praetorian cohorts.

The enemy's statues from every side, the parricide's statues from every side,
the gladiator's statues from every side: let the statues of the gladiator and
the parricide be taken down. Let the killer of citizens be dragged. Let the
parricide of citizens be dragged. Let the gladiator's statues be taken down.
With you safe we are safe and secure, truly, truly, now truly, now worthily,
now truly, now freely.

Now we are secure. Fear to the informers, that we may be secure. Fear to the
informers, that we may be safe. Informers out of the Senate; the club for the
informers. With you safe, the informers to the lion; with you ruling, the club
for the informers./

/Let the memory of the parricide gladiator be abolished; let the statues of
the parricide gladiator be taken down. Let the memory of the unclean
gladiator be abolished. The gladiator into the spoliarium. Hear, Caesar: let
the butcher be dragged with the hook. Let the butcher of the Senate be
dragged with the hook according to the custom of the ancestors. More savage
than Domitian, more unclean than Nero. As he did, so let him suffer. Let the
memories of the innocent be kept. Restore the honors of the innocent, we ask.
Let the parricide's corpse be dragged with the hook. Let the gladiator's
corpse be dragged with the hook. Let the gladiator's corpse be put in the

Informers out of the Senate, accusers out of the Senate, suborners of slaves
out of the Senate. And you were afraid with us. You know all things, and you
know the good and the bad; you know all things, correct all things. We were
afraid for you. O we happy, with you truly ruling! Bring a motion about the
parricide, bring it, put it to the vote. We ask your presence. The innocent
have not been buried. Let the parricide's corpse be dragged. The parricide dug

And when, by order of Pertinax, Livius Laurensis, procurator of the privy
purse, had given it to Fabius Cilo, consul designate, Commodus' corpse was
buried by night. The Senate cried:

/On whose authority did they bury him? Let the buried parricide be dug up; let
him be dragged./

Cincius Severus said:

/He has been buried unjustly. As pontifex I say this, and this college of
pontiffs says it. Since I have gone through the joyful things, I shall now
turn to what is necessary: I move that the statues which he who lived only
for the ruin of citizens and for his own disgrace forced to be decreed for
his honor be abolished, which are to be abolished on every side, and that his
name be erased from all private and public monuments, and that the months be
called by the names they had when that evil first settled on the state./
