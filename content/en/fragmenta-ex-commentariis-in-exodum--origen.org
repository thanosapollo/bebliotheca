FROM ORIGEN'S ON EXODUS
=12.264= On the saying: The Lord hardened the heart of Pharaoh. "And the
Lord hardened the heart of Pharaoh, and he was not willing
to send them away." The saying lying often in Exodus, "The Lord hardened the
heart of Pharaoh," and, "I will harden the heart of Pharaoh," disturbs almost
all who encounter it, both those who disbelieve it and those who say they
believe. For to those who disbelieve, among many other things this too seems
to be a cause of unbelief, that things unworthy of God are said concerning
God. And it is unworthy of God to work hardening about the heart of anyone
whatsoever, and to work hardening to the end that the one being hardened
should disobey the will of the one who hardens. And how, they say, is it not
absurd that God should work something, to the end that one should disobey
=12.265= his will? For it is clear that these words seem to be spoken as of
one not wishing to have Pharaoh obedient to the things he commands. And among
those who are thought to believe, no ordinary disagreement has arisen, because
of the saying: "The Lord hardened the heart of Pharaoh." For some, being
persuaded that there is no other God besides the Creator, think that God, as
if by lot, has mercy on whom he wills, and hardens whom he wills, there being
no cause of this one being shown mercy, and that one being hardened by him.
But others, being carried better than these, say that many other meanings of
Scripture also are hidden from them, and they are not turned aside from the
sound faith because of this; and that one of the hidden things is also the
sound account concerning this scripture. But those who assert another god
besides the Creator want him to be just and not good, being carried in a very
unlearned way and at the same time impiously, in separating justice from
goodness, and thinking that it is possible for justice to be in someone apart
from goodness, and goodness without justice. Yet even saying this they admit
things contrary to their own supposition concerning a just God, that he
hardens the heart of Pharaoh, and makes it disobedient to himself. For if he
who assigns to each according to desert is just, and giving to those who have
become better or worse from their own cause the things which he knows each is
fit to receive, how is he just, who became the cause of a worse sin to
Pharaoh? And not simply a cause, but so far as those received the words,
having cooperated toward his becoming most unjust. For referring the hardening
of Pharaoh's heart to nothing worthy of the purpose of a just god, I do not
know how they can present as a just god, even if they wish as you interpret,
the one who hardens the heart of Pharaoh. Whence they must be pressed in the
things according to the present passage, so that they either present how a
just one hardens, or dare to say that, since he hardens, the Creator is
wicked; or, neither being well supplied with proofs toward the just one being
able to harden someone, nor daring to put forth so great an impiety concerning
the Creator as wicked, they take refuge in another way, an interpretive one of
"The Lord hardened the heart of Pharaoh," departing from, by thinking they
=12.268= understand the things according to the words, destroying their own
understandings concerning a just god. And lastly, even if they should confess
to being at a loss as to whatever the argument may suggest. Let these things
then be torn apart as they are examined concerning God in the present problem.
But since those who suppose that some have come into being from their
constitution for destruction also bring these things into the place concerning
nature, saying that what they teach is made clear through "the heart of
Pharaoh having been hardened by the Lord," come let us inquire these things of
them. He who was created for destruction could never do any of the better
things, the very nature existing in him acting against him toward the good.
What need then was there for Pharaoh, being, as you say, a son of destruction,
to be hardened by God, so that he might not send the people away? For if he
were not hardened, he would have sent them away. Let them answer then
concerning this, saying what Pharaoh would have done if he had not been
hardened. For if, not having been hardened, he would have released them, he
was not of a nature that is being destroyed. But if he would not have released
them, the hardening of his heart was superfluous, for likewise he would not
have released them even not having been hardened. And what was God working
concerning his ruling part when he hardened him? And how does he accuse him
saying, "Because you disobey me, behold I will kill your firstborn, and all
the Egyptians shall know," he says, "that I am the Lord"; as if the things
thought grievous were being brought upon them for this reason, that they might
know God. And in the Maccabean books something of this sort is said: "I exhort
those who encounter this book not to shrink because of the misfortunes, but to
think that the things that happen are not toward destruction, but toward the
training of our race; for not to be left a long time while acting impiously,
but immediately to fall into penalties, is a sign of a great benefaction; for
not as upon the other nations does the Master wait, being longsuffering, so as
later to avenge them when their sins have been let go to the end; so also he
judged concerning us, but training with misfortune, he does not abandon his
own people." For if falling into penalties because of sins is a sign of a
great benefaction, Pharaoh too being penalized after his heart has been
hardened, and being punished together with his people, see whether he has not
been penalized in vain, nor for his own evil. And David, becoming as it were
an imitator of God according to what falls to the times, commands =12.269=
Solomon concerning Joab to punish him for the things sinned against Abner, son
of Ner, and to kill him for the things in which he had failed, then he adds:
"And you shall bring down his gray hairs in peace to Hades." And it is clear
that, as the Hebrew also reported to us, through being punished he would
sleep in peace, punishment and torment no longer being owed to him after the
departure from here, because he had already received it from here. Thus we too
receive every threat, both from the sacred Scriptures, and from the working
according to the greatness and order of the created things, of God who created
the things seen and the things not seen, the temporary and the eternal, as of
one and the same in all things with the Father of our Lord and Savior, good
and just and wise, and we struggle to bring the Scriptures to that aim, of
showing all things of a good God, and wise, and just, not even in a chance
matter being suspected by those who have sense of falling away; but as to
fitting, or not, the things said to his goodness and justice and wisdom,
needing God the Savior. Such things then we also suppose concerning: "The Lord
God hardened the heart of Pharaoh." The Word of God is a physician of the
soul, using ways of healing most varied and suited to those who are in a bad
way and most timely. And of the ways of healing, some produce more, some less,
toils and torments for those being led to healing; and again the helps
sometimes come in a seemingly unfitting way, and sometimes not so, and again
more quickly or more slowly; and after having been filled with the sin, or
after, so to speak, only touching it. And the whole God-inspired Scripture is
full of testimonies for each, gloomier helps having been recorded as having
happened to the people more or less, and having befallen them for conversion
and correction, in greater or lesser wars, and in famines more long-lasting or
more short. And in a seemingly unfitting way in: "I will not visit upon your
daughters when they fornicate, and upon your brides when they commit
adultery." For perhaps he leaves souls that are more given over to the things
thought bodily and pleasant among themselves, until being satiated they turn
away from the things they desire, as if wanting even to vomit them, and they
would no longer quickly fall into the same things, because they have been
filled with them more, and have been so far tormented. But more slowly those
are led to healing who would have despised falling a second time into the same
things, because they had been more quickly delivered from the evils. And the
craftsman God knows the dispositions of each, and as it falls to him, being
able alone scientifically to apply the healings, =12.272= what must be done,
and when, to each. And as in certain bodily sufferings, when the evil has, so
to speak, gone into the depth, the physician by certain drugs draws and pulls
the matter to the surface, producing severe inflammations and swellings, and
more pains than one had before he set out to be healed, as they are accustomed
to do with those bitten by rabid animals, and certain others who have suffered
things similar to these: so I think God also manages the hidden wickedness
that has gone into the depth of the soul. And as the physician says concerning
this or that one: I will make inflammations about the place of the relaxation,
and I will force such and such parts to swell, so as to work a severe abscess;
and when the physician says these things, the one hearing him more
scientifically will not blame, but will even praise the one who as it were
threatens to work these things; but someone will speak, saying that he does a
thing foreign to the profession of physicians, who ought to make healthy,
leading to inflammations and abscesses: so I think God also has said: "I will
harden the heart of Pharaoh." And these things having been written, the one
hearing them as oracles of God, keeping the dignity of the speaker, accepts
them, and seeking, everyone finds even in these things to present the goodness
of God as taking place; more openly for the salvation of the people, being
made to believe through more wonders; and second concerning the Egyptians, as
many as, being struck by the things that were happening, were going to follow
the Hebrews: "For a mixed," he says, "people, many of the Egyptians, went out
with them"; and more unspeakably and more deeply perhaps, also that benefit
might come to Pharaoh himself, no longer hiding the poison, nor holding the
habit together, but drawing it and bringing it into the open, and perhaps by
acting loosening it, so that having accomplished all the eruptions of the
wickedness existing in him, he might later have the tree that bears evils
without tension, perhaps being dried at the end, when he is sunk in the sea,
not as one might think so as to perish altogether, but so as, having cast off
the sins, to be lightened, and perhaps in peace from so great a war of the
soul to go down to Hades. But it is likely that those who will read will be
hard to persuade, taking the saying to be forced, that the hardening of his
heart has happened as beneficial to Pharaoh, and that all the things recorded
have happened for his sake, even as far as the sinking. But see if we can from
here, taking away the difficulty of being persuaded, work persuasion of the
things said. "Many are the scourges," says David, "of the sinner"; and his son
teaches that God scourges every son whom he receives. And further David
himself, prophesying a promise concerning Christ and those who believe in him,
says: =12.273= "If his sons forsake my law, and do not walk in my judgments;
if they profane my justifications, and do not keep my commandments, I will
visit their lawlessnesses with a rod, and their injustices with scourges; but
my mercy I will not scatter from them." Therefore it is a grace of God that
the lawless be visited with a rod, and the sinner with scourges. And so far as
the one sinning is not scourged, he is not yet brought under training and
correction; wherefore God also threatens, if the sins of those inhabiting
Judea become great, "No longer to visit upon their daughters when they
fornicate, and upon their brides when they commit adultery." And elsewhere he
says: "Because I have cleansed you and you were not cleansed, I will no longer
be wrathful upon you, nor will I be jealous upon you any longer." Therefore
those at whom he is not wrathful when they sin, if I may so speak, being angry
he is not wrathful. And it is to be observed also in the prophetic threats
against the many, that there is added: "They shall know that I am the Lord."
And he threatens not only Israelites, but also Egyptians, and Assyrians, and
other enemies of the people. And this at the end of many things thought
threats is also written in Exodus. Does then the one who hardens harden one
who is hard? And it is clear that a hard one is not hardened, but hardness
changes from softness into hardness; and softness of heart according to
Scripture is praiseworthy, as we have observed in many places. Let them say
therefore whether Pharaoh, being good, has become wicked, but also either God,
accusing Pharaoh, accuses in vain, or not in vain; if then in vain, how is he
still wise and just? but if not in vain, he was the cause of the sins
according to the disobedience; and if he was the cause, he was not of a nature
that is being destroyed. We must indeed also believe this, because the
Apostle, using the sayings from here, says: "So then he has mercy on whom he
wills, and whom he wills he hardens. You will say then to me, Why does he
still find fault? For who has withstood his will?" Who is the one who hardens
and has mercy? For it is not of one the hardening, and of another the having
mercy according to the apostolic voice, but of the same. Either then those who
are shown mercy in God belong to the one who hardens the heart of Pharaoh, and
another God is invented for them in vain, or a good one. According to them God
is not only merciful, but also hardening, and he would no longer be, as they
suppose, good. And all these things we have examined on purpose at more length
against those who without testing grant themselves to have understood, and who
trample on the simplicity of our people, standing against them and showing
that neither into the things they suppose =12.276= concerning God, nor into
the things they dogmatize concerning nature, does the argument now being
examined contribute to them, as they think. And we, being persuaded from many
places, think that both toil and punishment, the things brought from God, come
about never against those who suffer, but always for them. And at least the
things thought most severe of the names attached to God, fury and wrath, are
said to convict and train in: "Lord, do not convict me in your fury, nor train
me in your wrath," the one praying these things asking not to need the
conviction that is through the fury of God, and the training that is through
his wrath, as some will be convicted by the fury of God, and trained by his
wrath. And that we may the more assent to the things said, similar sayings
from the New Testament must also be used, the Savior saying: "I came to cast
fire upon the earth, and would that it were kindled"; for if the fire which he
came to cast upon the earth were not saving, and saving for men, the Son of
the good God would not have said these things. But also Peter, having killed
with the word Ananias and Sapphira, who sinned in lying not to men but to the
Lord, has taken thought not only for the building up of those who from seeing
what had happened would be more reverent toward the faith in Christ, but also
of those dying; wishing them, having been purified by the sudden and
unexpected death, to be released from the body, having also something of
justification, since they had given even the half of their possessions for the
need of those in want. And Paul also, blinding with the word the one with the
proconsul Sergius Paulus, through the toils turns him toward piety, saying to
him: "O full of all deceit and all villainy, son of the devil, enemy of all
justice, will you not cease perverting the straight ways of the Lord, and now
you shall be blind, not seeing the sun until a time." Until what time? Or the
time when, having been struck and tormented because of the sins, he was going,
repenting, to become worthy to see the sun in both ways, according to soul and
according to body, so that the divine power might be proclaimed at the
restoration of his sight; and according to soul, when believing he was going
to benefit from piety? But also Demas and Hermogenes, whom he delivered to
Satan, that they might be trained not to blaspheme, have suffered something
similar to the things said before. And the one in Corinth who had his father's
wife is himself also delivered to Satan for destruction of the flesh, that the
spirit might be saved in the day of the Lord. It is no wonder then if also the
things concerning Pharaoh being hardened, and at the end being surrounded with
such punishments, are managed from the goodness of God. Let these things be
said by us for the present, as they occurred, concerning: "The Lord hardened
the heart of Pharaoh." But if anyone, keeping piety toward God, should find
better things, and such as in no way touch impiety, with testimonies from the
divine Scriptures, those should rather be used. And in other places concerning
the same. And further I attend in the things according to the passage, lest
perhaps =12.277= just as physicians of those bitten by rabid animals, drawing
the poison to the surface, so that it may not, feeding within, destroy the
man, produce more severe conditions and inflammations: so God, by his medical
art, draws the wickedness hidden and lurking in the depths of the soul to the
outside, so that it may become manifest and more evident, in order that after
this he may bring on the next healing. And such I think are also the sayings
in Deuteronomy, having this manner: "And you shall remember all the way which
the Lord your God led you this fortieth year in the wilderness, that he might
afflict you, and try you, and the things in your heart might be discerned,
whether you will keep his commandments, or not. And he afflicted you and
starved you; and he fed you the manna, which you did not know, and your
fathers did not know; that he might announce to you that man shall not live on
bread alone, but on every word going out through the mouth of God shall man
live." And observe in these things that God afflicts and tries, that the
things in each one's heart may be discerned, as being, but laid up in the
depth, and coming into the open through the afflictions. Such also is that
which in Job is announced by the Lord through whirlwind and clouds to Job,
thus: "Do you think I have dealt with you otherwise, than that you might
appear just?" For he did not say, that you might become just, but, that you
might appear, being such even before the trials, but that you might appear in
the things that befell. And again in another tome in the same notes on Exodus.
And one of those among us was saying, comforting the question from custom,
that often kind masters, being longsuffering toward those of the household who
sin, are accustomed to say, "I have destroyed you," and, "I have made you
wicked," showing by the manner that their kindness and longsuffering seem to
have become a pretext for the greater wickedness. As then when these things
are said someone can say, slandering, that the master confessed to have made
the household servant wicked: so the things that by the goodness of God became
a pretext of the hardness of Pharaoh have been written as having hardened the
heart of Pharaoh. And this one will comfort from apostolic sayings what he has
understood to say: "Or do you despise the wealth of his kindness and of his
forbearance and longsuffering, not knowing that the kindness of God leads you
to repentance? But according to your hardness and impenitent heart you store
up for yourself wrath in a day of wrath and of revelation and of justice of
God, who will render to each according to his works." The same Apostle at
least in the letter to the Romans says: =12.280= "But if God, wishing to show
the wrath, and to make known his power, endured in much longsuffering vessels
of wrath fitted for destruction"; as of his longsuffering having endured the
vessels of wrath, and as it were having begotten them. For if by his being
longsuffering, not punishing those who sin, but having mercy, it abounded by
the pouring out, he himself somehow endured by his own longsuffering the
vessels of wrath, and so to speak, he himself has made all the vessels of
wrath, and according to this, he himself hardened their heart. For when so
many signs and wonders having happened Pharaoh is not persuaded, but after
such things still resists, how is he not shown harder and more unbelieving?
the hardness and unbelief seeming able to have come from the wonders. And
similar also is that in the Gospel: "For judgment I came into this world"; for
the Savior did not set out to come for judgment, but there followed upon his
having come, that he had come for judgment of those who after the wonders had
not believed in him; but he has also come for a fall. And after other things,
thus. For the wonders that happen, to those who receive and believe, as among
the mixed Egyptians who went out with the people, were mercy; but to those who
disobey they bring hardness upon their hearts. And from the Gospel still
besides the things said it is possible to set similar things concerning the
Savior seeming to have become a cause of evils to some: "Woe to you, region of
Zin, woe to you, Bethsaida, that if in Tyre and Sidon had happened the signs
that happened in you, long ago sitting in sackcloth and ashes they would have
repented. Nevertheless I say to you, it will be more tolerable for Tyre and
Sidon than for you," and what follows; for the Savior, being one who knows
beforehand the unbelief of those in the region of Zin, and of those in
Bethsaida, and of those in Capernaum, and that it becomes more tolerable for
the land of Sodom in the day of judgment than for those, why did he perform
the wonders in the region of Zin, and in Bethsaida, seeing that because of
these things it becomes more tolerable in the day of judgment for Tyrians and
Sidonians than for these? And after other things. "And you shall say to
Pharaoh: Thus says the Lord: Israel is my firstborn son. And I said to you,
Send my people away, that they may serve me. But if you are not willing to
send him away, see then, I will kill your son, the firstborn." It must then be
said to those who assert that these things belong to a just God, and who
suppose according to the ready meaning of the wording that the heart of
Pharaoh the king has been hardened, so that he might not send his people away,
how is he just who both hardened the heart, and threatens, if he will not
release, to kill his firstborn son? For being pressed they will confess him
wicked. Then again they will be overturned from other things, and will be led
up to not being slaves to the ready wording, which according to them cannot
save the justice of the Creator. And once they are now forced =12.281= to
examine these things in a strained way, they will go up to no longer accusing
the Creator, but asserting that he is good. We must then ask those who think
they have understood: "The Lord God hardened the heart of Pharaoh," whether
they truly believe these things are said by God, through Moses being inspired,
or falsely; for if falsely, according to them God is no longer just, nor true,
and so far as these things not even God; but if truly, let them consider
whether he does not accuse him as having free will, saying: "But if you are
not willing to send him away"; and elsewhere: "Until when are you not willing
to be shamed before me?" For "Until when are you not willing to be shamed
before me?" is said in a shaming way toward Pharaoh who is not being shamed,
not because of not being able, but because of not being willing. And the
saying by Moses to Pharaoh, "That you may know that the earth is the Lord's,
and you, and your servants, I know that you have never feared the Lord," makes
clear that they will fear; which fits toward the heterodox, concerning both
the goodness of God, and concerning there not being a nature that is being
destroyed.
