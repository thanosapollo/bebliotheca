** To His Wife, Book I.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

** Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h2
:END:

I have thought it right, dearest fellow-servant of mine in the Lord, already
now to provide what course you should follow after my departure from the
world, if I am called before you, and to commit that provision to your faith,
that you may keep it. For we are busy enough with worldly affairs, and we want
each of us provided for. If we draw up wills for such things, why should we
not much more look ahead for our posterity in things divine and heavenly, and
in a way bequeath a legacy in advance: the warning and showing of those things
that are allotted from immortal goods and from the inheritance of the heavens?
Only may God grant that you can receive this trust of my warning in full; to
him be honor, glory, brightness, dignity, and power, now and forever.

I charge you, therefore, with all the self-restraint you can, to renounce
marriage after I am gone. You will confer nothing on me by that name except
what will profit you. Besides, to Christians who have left the world no
restoration of marriage is promised on the day of the resurrection, since they
are translated into the quality and holiness of angels. So no anxiety that
comes from jealousy of the flesh will, even in the case of the woman they
wanted to have married seven brothers in succession, wound any of so many
husbands on the day of the resurrection, nor is anyone waiting to put her to
shame. The question of the Sadducees has yielded to the Lord's sentence. Do
not think that, jealous to keep your flesh entire for myself and fearing the
pain of slight, I am already now slipping you the counsel of widowhood. No
shameful pleasure will then be taken up again between us. God does not promise
his own such frivolous, such filthy things. But whether what we urge will
profit you, or any other woman who belongs to God, we may treat at length.

** Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h3
:END:

We do not deny that the joining of man and woman was called blessed by God as
the seedbed of the human race, devised for filling the earth and furnishing
the world, and therefore permitted, yet as one. For Adam was Eve's one
husband, and Eve his one wife, one woman, one rib. True, among our ancestors
and the patriarchs themselves it was lawful not only to marry but even to use
marriage in several ways. There were concubines too. But although the Church
came in figuratively in the synagogue, still, to take it simply, it was
necessary to institute things that later deserved to be cut away or tempered.
For the Law was going to come after. The grounds for fulfilling the Law had to
run ahead. Soon after, the Word of God was to succeed the Law, bringing in
spiritual circumcision. So by the loose license of that time the materials of
later corrections were supplied in advance, which the Lord by his gospel, and
then the apostle at the last stretches of the world, either cut away as
redundant or set in order as disordered.

** Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h4
:END:

But I have not premised this about the liberty of old times and the restraint
of later ones in order to lay it down that Christ came to part marriages and
abolish unions, as if I were already prescribing an end to marrying. Let those
see to it who, among the rest of their perversities, teach that the one flesh
in two should be unjoined, denying him who, having borrowed the woman from the
man, fitted together again into one, by the reckoning of marriage, the two
bodies taken from a share in the same stuff.

In short, we nowhere at all read that marriage is forbidden, as being a good,
of course. What is better than this good we learn from the apostle, who
permits marrying but prefers abstinence: the one because of the snares of
temptations, the other because of the straits of the times. When the reason of
each pronouncement is examined, it is easily seen that the power of marrying
was granted us by necessity; and what necessity grants, she herself cheapens.
And what is written, It is better to marry than to burn: what kind of good is
this, I ask you, that a comparison with an evil commends, so that marrying is
better only because burning is worse? But how much better is it neither to
marry nor to burn? Even in persecutions it is better, by permission, to flee
from town to town than, when seized and tortured, to deny. And by that measure
more blessed are those who have strength to depart with the blessed confession
of their witness. I can say: What is permitted is not good. Consider: I must
die. If I lament that, the permitted thing looks good. But if I fear what is
permitted, the cause of its permission is already suspect.

But what is better, no one has permitted, as being undoubted and clear by its
own purity. Some things are not to be sought just because they are not
forbidden; they are in a way forbidden when other things are preferred to
them. Preference of the higher is dissuasion from the lowest. A thing is not
good because it is not evil, nor is it not evil because it does no harm. What
is fully good outstrips this, in that it not only does no harm but even
profits. You ought to prefer what profits to what merely does no harm. Every
contest strives for first place; second has consolation, not victory. If we
listen to the apostle, let us forget what lies behind and stretch toward what
is ahead, and be pursuers of the better gifts. So even if he does not lay a
snare on us, he shows what is useful, saying: The unmarried woman thinks of
the Lord's things, that she may be holy in body and spirit; but the married
woman is anxious how she may please her husband. Besides, he nowhere permits
marriage in such a way that he would not rather have us strive after his own
example. Happy the man who proves like Paul.

** Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h5
:END:

But we read that the flesh is weak, and from this we flatter ourselves in some
things. Yet we also read that the spirit is strong. Both are set in one
sentence. Flesh is earthly stuff, the spirit heavenly. Why then, more ready to
excuse ourselves, do we put forward what is weak in us and not defend what is
stronger? Why should the earthly not yield to the heavenly? If the spirit is
stronger than the flesh, because it is also of nobler stock, it is our fault
if we follow the weaker. For those parted from marriage, two kinds of human
weakness make marriage necessary. The first and strongest comes from desire of
the flesh; the next from desire of the world. Both must be rejected by the
servants of God, who renounce both luxury and ambition. Desire of the flesh
pleads the duties of one's years, wants the harvest of beauty, rejoices in its
own shame. It says a husband is necessary to the sex, for authority and
comfort, or to be safe from evil rumors. Against these counsels of hers, set
the examples of our sisters whose names are with the Lord, who, when their
husbands have gone before, put no chance of beauty or of years ahead of
holiness. They prefer to marry God. They are beautiful for God, girls for God.
They live with him, they talk with him, they handle him day and night, they
assign their prayers to the Lord as dowries, and from him they receive favor
as wedding gifts whenever they wish. So they have seized for themselves an
eternal gift of the Lord, and already on earth, by not marrying, they are
counted of the angelic household. Training yourself to rivalry in
self-restraint by the examples of such women, you will by spiritual affection
bury that fleshly desire, blotting out the passing and fleeting longings of
beauty or of years by the exchange of immortal goods.

As for this worldly desire, its causes are glory, greed, ambition,
insufficiency, by which it suborns a necessity of marrying, promising heavenly
things, of course: to lord it in another's household, to brood on another's
wealth, to wring display from another's store, to cut expenses you do not
feel. Far be this from believers, who have no care of getting through life,
unless we distrust the promises of God and his care and providence: he who
clothes the lilies of the field with such grace, who feeds the birds of the
sky with no labor of theirs, who forbids worry about tomorrow's food and
clothing, promising that he knows what each of his servants needs, not indeed
weights of necklaces, not wearying clothes, not Gallic mules or German
porters, which kindle the glory of weddings, but sufficiency, which fits
moderation and modesty. Presume, I ask you, that you need nothing if you wait
upon the Lord; rather, that you have all things if you have the Lord, whose
are all things. Think on heavenly things, and you will despise earthly ones.
Nothing is necessary to widowhood sealed before God except to persevere.

** Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h6
:END:

People also add for themselves reasons for marriage from anxiety for posterity
and the bitterest pleasure of children. For us this is idle. Why should we be
eager to bear children whom, when we have them, we wish to send on ahead, in
view of the coming straits, ourselves also longing to be taken out of this
most wicked world and received with the Lord, which was even an apostle's
wish? Offspring is necessary, no doubt, to a servant of God. We are secure
enough of our own salvation that we have leisure for children! We must seek
burdens that even most of the nations avoid, that laws compel, that
child-killings fight down, burdens most inconvenient to us, and as dangerous
to the faith. Why did the Lord cry Woe to those who are with child and those
who nurse, if not because he testifies that the hindrances of children will be
a trouble on that day of setting out? Those hindrances are charged to
marriage. This will not touch widows. At the first trumpet of the angel they
will spring forward unencumbered, and will freely bear whatever pressure and
persecution, with no burden of marriage heaving in the womb, none at the
breasts.

So whether one marries for the flesh, or for the world, or for posterity, none
of these necessities belongs to the servants of God, so that I do not think it
enough to have yielded once to one of them and by one marriage to have paid
off all desire of this kind. Let us marry every day, and in marrying be caught
by that day of fear like Sodom and Gomorrah. For there they were not only
marrying and trading; when he says, They were marrying and buying, he marks
the more notable vices of the flesh and of the world, which call people
farthest from divine disciplines, the one by the pleasure of wantonness, the
other by the greed of getting. And yet that blindness then was held far from
the ends of the world. What then will happen if God now keeps us from what was
already hateful before him of old? The time, he says, is drawn in. It remains
that those who have marriages act as if they had them not.

** Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h7
:END:

If those who have wives ought to blot out what they have, how much more are
those who have not forbidden to seek again what they do not have, so that she
whose husband has gone from this scene should from then on impose rest on her
sex by abstinence from marrying, an abstinence which most women of the nations
offer to the memory of dearest husbands. When something seems hard, let us
review others who face harder things. How many there are who, right from
baptism, seal their flesh? How many likewise who by equal consent cancel
between them the debt of marriage, voluntary eunuchs for desire of the kingdom
of heaven? If abstinence is borne while marriage is still intact, how much
more when it has been taken away? I believe it is harder to leave what is
still whole than not to long for what is lost. A hard and steep enough thing,
clearly, is a holy woman's self-restraint after her husband's death for God's
sake, when the nations, for their own Satan, bear priesthoods of both
virginity and widowhood? At Rome those who tend the image of that
inextinguishable fire, keeping the omens of their own punishment with the
dragon himself, are enrolled on the ground of virginity. To Achaean Juno at
the town of Aegium a virgin is allotted, and those who rave at Delphi do not
know marriage. We know besides that widows attend African Ceres, drawn from
marriage by a most harsh forgetting. For while their husbands are still alive
they not only withdraw, but even put other women in their place for them, the
husbands surely smiling, all contact taken away even to the kiss of their
sons; and yet they persist, with lasting practice, in such a discipline of
widowhood as shuts out even the comforts of holy affection. These things the
devil has commanded his own, and he is heard. He challenges the servants of
God, no doubt, by the self-restraint of his own, as if on equal terms. Even
the priests of Gehenna are continent. For he has found how to ruin men even in
good pursuits, and it makes no difference to him to kill some by luxury,
others by self-restraint.

** Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h8
:END:

To us self-restraint has been shown by the Lord of salvation as an instrument
of eternity, as a testimony of faith, as a commendation of this flesh that is
to be presented for the coming garment of incorruption, and lastly for bearing
the will of God. Beyond this, I warn you, reflect that no one is taken out of
the world except by the will of God, if not even a leaf falls from a tree
without God's will. The same who brings us into the world must also take us
out. So when a husband has died by the will of God, the marriage also dies by
the will of God. Why restore what God has ended? Why, by repeating the slavery
of marriage, scorn the liberty offered you? You are bound, he says, to
marriage: do not seek loosing. You are loosed from marriage: do not seek
binding. For even if you do not sin by marrying again, he says pressure of the
flesh follows. Therefore let us love, as far as we can, the chance of
self-restraint; as soon as it comes, let us take it up, so that what we could
not manage in marriage we may pursue in widowhood. The occasion must be
embraced which takes away what necessity used to command. How much second
marriages take from the faith, how much they shout against holiness, the
discipline of the Church and the apostle's rule declare, when he does not let
the twice-married preside, when he does not allow a widow to be enrolled in
the order unless she has been the wife of one man. For the altar of God ought
to be set out clean. That whole white company of the Church is drawn from
holiness. Priesthood among the nations is of widowhood and of celibacy. From
the devil's rivalry, of course. For the king of the world, the chief pontiff,
to marry again is unlawful. How pleasing holiness is to God, when even his
enemy affects it! Not as akin to any good, but as affecting with insult what
pleases God the Lord.

** Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h9
:END:

For the honors of widowhood with God are summed in one saying of his through
the prophet: Do justice to the widow and the orphan, and come, let us dispute,
says the Lord. These two names, as far as they are stripped of human help, so
far are they laid open to divine mercy; the Father of all undertakes to
protect them. See how he who has done good to a widow is put on a level, of
what worth the widow herself is, whose champion will dispute with the Lord!
Not so much is given to virgins, I think. Although in them solid integrity and
whole holiness will see the face of God most nearly, still the widow has
something more laborious, because it is easy not to seek what you do not know
and to turn from what you have never desired. More glorious is the
self-restraint that feels its own right, that knows what it has seen. The
virgin may be held the happier, but the widow the more hard-worked: the one
because she has always had the good, the other because she has found the good
for herself; in the one grace is crowned, in the other virtue. For some things
are of divine bounty, some of our own working. What the Lord grants is
governed by his grace; what a human seizes is carried through by striving.
Strive therefore after the virtue of self-restraint, which looks after
modesty; after diligence, which does not make women wanderers; after
frugality, which scorns the world. Follow company and talk worthy of God,
mindful of that little verse made holy by the apostle: Bad company corrupts
good morals. Talkative, idle, wine-drinking, curious companions hurt the
purpose of widowhood most of all. Through talkativeness words hostile to
modesty creep in; through idleness they draw one from strictness; through wine
they slip in any evil; through curiosity they bring in rivalry of lust. None
of such women knows how to speak of the good of having one husband. For their
god, as the apostle has it, is the belly, and so also what is next to the
belly.

These things I commend to you already now, dearest fellow-servant, treated at
length after the apostle, indeed more than enough, but they will also be a
comfort to you, in that, if it so falls out, you will keep my memory in them.

** Book II.

** Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h2
:END:

Just now, dearest fellow-servant in the Lord, I have, as I could, set out for
you what a holy woman should pursue when marriage has been taken away by
whatever lot. Now let us turn to second counsels, in view of human weakness,
warned by the examples of certain women who, when the chance of self-restraint
was taken from them by divorce or by a husband's death, not only threw away
the opportunity of so great a good, but would not even in marrying again
remember the discipline, so as to marry above all in the Lord. So my mind is
confused, lest I, who lately urged you to perseverance in having one husband
and in widowhood, should now by mention of hasty marriages offer you a chance
of falling. If you are sound in sense, you surely know that you must keep what
is more useful. But because it is hard, and not without constraints, and this
is the highest aim of life, I have sat back a little; nor would I have had
reason to return to this with you as well, had I not already taken up a graver
care. For the greater is the self-restraint of the flesh that serves
widowhood, the more pardonable it can seem if it is not borne. Pardon of hard
things is then easy. But as far as marrying in the Lord is doable, as being in
our power, so much more blameworthy is it not to keep what you can. Add that
the apostle, concerning widows and the unmarried, advises that they remain so,
when he says, I wish all to persevere by my example; but concerning marrying
in the Lord, when he adds, Only in the Lord, he no longer advises, but plainly
commands. So in this kind especially, if we do not obey, we are in danger;
because one may neglect advice with more impunity than a command, since the
one comes from counsel and is set before the will, while the other comes down
from authority and is bound to necessity: there it looks like liberty to fail,
here like stubbornness.

** Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h3
:END:

So when in these days a certain woman took her marriage out of the Church and
joined herself to a Gentile, and I remembered that this had been done by
others before, wondering either at their own wantonness or at the
double-dealing of their advisers, because no scripture offers a license for
that deed, Do they, I said, flatter themselves from that chapter of the first
to the Corinthians, where it is written: If any of the brothers has an
unbelieving wife, and she consents to the marriage, let him not send her away;
likewise a believing woman married to an unbeliever, if she finds her husband
agreeable, let her not send him away; for the unbelieving husband is
sanctified by the believing wife, and the unbelieving wife by the believing
husband; otherwise your children would be unclean? Perhaps by taking this
warning about joined believers in a simple sense they think it is also lawful
to marry unbelievers. Far be it that he who so interprets should knowingly
cheat himself. Besides, it is clear that this scripture points to those
believers who were found by the grace of God in Gentile marriage, according to
the words themselves. If any believer, he says, has an unbelieving wife. He
does not say, takes an unbelieving wife. He shows that one already living in
marriage with an unbelieving woman, presently converted by the grace of God,
ought to continue with his wife, namely so that no one who has obtained the
faith should think he must divorce a woman now in some sense alien and
strange. He even adds a reason: that we are called in peace to the Lord God,
and that the unbeliever can be gained by the believer through the use of
marriage. The very close of the passage confirms that it is to be understood
so: As each, he says, is called by the Lord, so let him continue. But it is
Gentiles who are called, I think, not believers. If he had pronounced
absolutely about the marriage of believers only, he would have permitted the
holy to marry at large. If he had permitted it, he would never have added a
pronouncement so different and contrary to his own permission, saying: A
woman, when her husband is dead, is free; let her marry whom she will, only in
the Lord. Here certainly nothing is to be reopened; for on the point that
might have been reopened, the Spirit has spoken: lest we make ill use of what
he says, Let her marry whom she will, he added, Only in the Lord, that is, in
the name of the Lord, which is without doubt a Christian. That Holy Spirit,
then, who prefers that widows and unmarried women persevere in integrity, who
exhorts us to his own example, prescribes no other form of repeating marriage
except in the Lord; to this condition alone he grants the losses of
self-restraint: Only, he says, in the Lord. He added weight to his law: Only.
With whatever sound and manner you utter that saying, it is heavy: it both
commands and advises, both orders and exhorts, both asks and threatens. It is
a tight, ready sentence, eloquent by its own brevity. So the divine voice is
wont to speak, that you may understand at once, observe at once. For who could
fail to understand that the apostle foresaw many dangers and wounds to the
faith in marriages of this kind, which he forbids, and first of all guarded
against the defilement of holy flesh in Gentile flesh?

At this point someone says: What then is the difference between him who is
chosen by the Lord in Gentile marriage and him who was already, that is before
marriage, a believer, that they should not equally beware for their flesh,
when the one is kept from marriage with an unbeliever, the other is ordered to
continue in it? Why, if we are defiled by a Gentile, is the one not unjoined,
just as the other is not bound? I will answer, if the Spirit gives it,
alleging first of all that the Lord holds it more pleasing that a marriage not
be contracted than that it be dissolved at all: in short he forbids divorce
except for the cause of fornication, but he commends self-restraint. Let the
one therefore have the necessity of continuing; the other, further, even the
power of not marrying. Then if, according to the scripture, those who are
caught by the faith in Gentile marriage are not defiled for this reason, that
together with them others also are sanctified, without doubt those who were
sanctified before marriage, if they mix themselves with strange flesh, cannot
sanctify that in which they were not caught. The grace of God sanctifies what
it finds. So what could not be sanctified is unclean; what is unclean has no
part with the holy, except to defile it from its own and kill it.

** Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h4
:END:

If this is so, it is settled that believers who enter Gentile marriages are
guilty of fornication and are to be barred from all sharing with the
brotherhood, from the apostle's letter saying that with such a one not even
food is to be taken. Or shall we produce marriage tablets about that before
the Lord's tribunal, and plead a marriage duly contracted which he himself
forbade? I suppose what is forbidden is not adultery? not fornication? I
suppose the admission of a stranger violates the temple of God less, mixes the
members of Christ with the members of an adulteress less? As far as I know, we
are not our own, but bought with a price; and with what price? the blood of
God. So in hurting this flesh we hurt him directly. What did he mean who said
that to marry a stranger is indeed a sin, but a very small one, when
otherwise, setting aside the injury to the flesh that belongs to the Lord,
every voluntary sin against the Lord is great? For the more there was power to
avoid it, the more it is loaded with the charge of stubbornness.

Let us now review the other dangers or wounds, as I said, to the faith,
foreseen by the apostle, most grievous not to the flesh only but also to the
spirit itself. For who would doubt that faith is daily blotted out by
unbelieving intercourse? Bad talk corrupts good morals: how much more living
together and undivided use? Any believing woman must of necessity observe God.
And how can she serve two lords, the Lord and a husband, add a Gentile? For by
observing a Gentile she will exhibit Gentile things: appearance, dressing of
the hair, worldly refinements, baser blandishments, even the secrets of
marriage themselves stained, not as among the holy, where the duties of the
sex are carried out with honor to the very necessity, modestly and moderately,
as under the eyes of God.

** Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h5
:END:

But let her see how she pays her duties to her husband. To the Lord she
certainly cannot give satisfaction according to the discipline, having at her
side a servant of the devil, his lord's agent for hindering the pursuits and
duties of believers, so that if a stated fast is to be kept, the husband makes
an appointment from daybreak for the baths; if fasts are to be observed, the
husband holds a banquet that same day; if there is to be a going forth, never
does family business fall more in the way. For who would let his wife, for the
sake of visiting the brothers, go round from street to street other people's
cottages, and the poorer ones at that? Who will gladly bear her being taken
from his side by nightly gatherings, if need be? Who, lastly, will without
anxiety endure her staying out all night at the Paschal solemnities? Who will
dismiss her without some suspicion of his own to that Lord's banquet which
they defame? Who will suffer her to creep into prison to kiss a martyr's
bonds? And further, to meet any of the brothers for the kiss? to offer water
for the saints' feet? to snatch from her food, from her cup? to long for them?
to have them in mind? If a brother from abroad arrives, what hospitality in a
strange house? if something is to be given to anyone, the granary and the
store-room are shut.

** Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h6
:END:

But someone endures our practices and does not hinder. This then is the sin:
that Gentiles know our practices, that we are under the knowledge of the
unjust, that it is their kindness if we do any work. He who endures cannot be
ignorant; or if it is hidden because he does not endure, he is feared. Since
scripture commands both, to work for the Lord without another's knowledge and
without pressure on ourselves, it makes no difference in which part you sin,
whether toward the husband's knowledge, if he is patient, or toward your own
struggle, while the impatient one is avoided. Do not, he says, cast your
pearls to swine, lest they trample them and turn and overthrow you too. Your
pearls are also the marks of daily conduct. The more you take care to hide
them, the more suspect you will make them, and the more to be seized by
Gentile curiosity. Will you stay hidden when you sign your little bed, your
little body; when you blow away some uncleanness; when even by night you rise
to pray, and you will not seem to be working some magic? Will the husband not
know what you taste in secret before all food? and if he knows it is bread,
does he not believe it is that bread which it is said to be? And will anyone,
ignorant of the reason, simply endure these things? without a groan? without
suspicion whether it is bread or poison? Some do endure, but so as to trample,
so as to mock such women, whose secrets they keep in reserve for the danger
they believe in, if perhaps they are hurt; they endure those whose dowries
they make the wage of silence by throwing the name in their teeth, meaning to
go to law, of course, before some spy as arbitrator. Which most women, not
seeing ahead, have been used to find out either by their property being wrung
from them or by the loss of their faith.

** Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h7
:END:

The handmaid of God dwells among alien labors, and among them, at all the
names of demons, at all the solemnities of kings, at the beginning of the
year, at the beginning of the month, she will be shaken by the smell of
incense. And she will go out from a door wreathed with laurel and hung with
lamps, as from some new council-chamber of public lusts; she will recline with
her husband often in clubs, often in cookshops, and will sometimes serve the
unjust, once used to serving the holy, and will she not recognize from this a
prejudgment of her own damnation, attending those whom she was going to judge?
Whose hand will she long for? from whose cup will she share? What will her
husband sing to her, or she to her husband? She will hear something of God, I
suppose, while dining from the tavern. From Gehenna what mention of God? what
invocation of Christ? Where the fosterings of faith from the weaving-in of the
scriptures? Where the Spirit? Where refreshment? Where the divine blessing?
All things strange, all hostile, all damned, sent in by the evil one for the
wearing down of salvation.

** Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:

If these things can happen also to those women who, having obtained the faith
in Gentile marriage, remain in it, still they are excused, as having been
caught in these very circumstances by God, and they are ordered to continue
and are sanctified and receive a hope of gaining the other. If then a marriage
of this kind is ratified with God, why should it not also go forward well, so
as not to be so harassed by pressures and straits and hindrances and
defilements, already having in part the patronage of divine grace? For he who
has been called from the nations to some heavenly virtue, by proofs of some
marked favor, is a terror to the Gentile, so that he hinders her less, lays
snares less, spies less. He has felt mighty works, he has seen proofs, he
knows her made better; so he himself too is a candidate for God by fear. So
men of this kind, in whom the grace of God has made a familiarity, are more
easily gained. But it is another thing to go down of one's own accord and
freely into what is forbidden. What does not please the Lord surely offends
the Lord, surely is brought in by the evil one. This will be a sign: that the
Christian name pleases suitors only. Therefore men are found who do not shrink
from such women, in order to drive them out, to snatch them away, to shut them
out from the faith. You have a reason not to doubt that no marriage of this
kind runs a prosperous course, while it is brokered by the evil one, but
condemned by the Lord.

** Chapter 8

:END:

To this let us ask whether it is lawful, as if we were in truth inspectors of
divine sentences. Do not even among the nations the strictest lords and those
most tenacious of discipline forbid their slaves to marry outside? lest of
course they run into wantonness, desert their duties, give out their master's
things to strangers. Have they not further ruled that those who, after their
masters' warning, have persisted in intimacy with other men's slaves are to be
claimed for slavery? Shall earthly disciplines be held stricter than heavenly
rules, so that Gentile women joined to strangers lose their liberty, but ours
join the devil's slaves to themselves and remain in their standing? They will
deny, of course, that the same warning has been given them by the Lord through
his apostle.

What cause of this madness shall I hold to, if not the weakness of faith,
always prone to the desires of worldly joys? This indeed is found most among
the richer. For the more a woman is rich and puffed up with the name of
matron, the more capacious a house she wants for her burdens, as a field in
which ambition may run its course. Churches look mean to such. A rich person
is hard to find in the house of God, and if there is one, hard to find
unmarried. What then are they to do? From where but from the devil are they to
seek a husband fit for displaying a sedan-chair and mules and hairdressers of
outlandish height? A Christian, even a rich one, would perhaps not furnish
these. I ask you, set Gentile examples before yourself. Most, noble by birth
and wealthy in goods, join themselves indiscriminately to the ignoble and the
mean, found for luxury or mutilated for license. Some give themselves to their
own freedmen and slaves, despising the opinion of all men, provided they have
those from whom they need fear no hindrance to their own liberty. A Christian
believer is reluctant to marry a believer lesser in estate, though she will be
richer in a poor husband? For if the kingdoms of the heavens are the poor's,
they are not the rich's; the rich woman will find more in the poor man. She
will be dowered with a greater dowry from the goods of him who is rich in God.
Let her be on a level on earth, who in the heavens perhaps will not be. Is
there need to doubt and inquire and deliberate again and again whether he is
fit for the dotal goods brought in, to whom God has entrusted his own fortune?
Where shall we find enough to tell the happiness of that marriage which the
Church joins, and the offering confirms, and the blessing seals, the angels
report, the Father holds ratified? For not even on earth do children marry
rightly and lawfully without their fathers' consent. What a yoke of two
believers, of one hope, one wish, one discipline, the same service? Both
brothers, both fellow-servants, no difference of spirit or of flesh. Nay,
truly two in one flesh. Where the flesh is one, the spirit also is one.
Together they pray, together they prostrate themselves, together they keep
their fasts, teaching one another, exhorting one another, bearing one another.
Both alike in the church of God, alike at the banquet of God, alike in
straits, in persecutions, in refreshments. Neither hides from the other,
neither shuns the other, neither is a burden to the other. The sick is visited
freely, the needy is supported. Alms without torment, sacrifices without
scruple, daily diligence without hindrance; no stealthy signing, no trembling
greeting, no mute blessing. Psalms and hymns sound between the two, and they
challenge one another which shall better sing to their Lord. Such things
Christ seeing and hearing rejoices. To these he sends his peace. Where there
are two, there is he himself also; where he is, there the evil one is not.

These are the things which that voice of the apostle leaves us to understand
under its brevity. Suggest these to yourself, if need be. By these turn
yourself away from the examples of certain women. It is not lawful for
believers to marry otherwise; it is not expedient.
