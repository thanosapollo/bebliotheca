* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Pelopidas the Theban, known more to historians than to the crowd. about whose
virtues I doubt in what way I should set them forth, because I fear, if I
begin to unfold the affairs, that I may seem not to narrate his life but to
write a history, but if I only touch the sums, that to those unskilled in
Greek letters it may appear less clearly how great that man was. therefore I
will meet both things, as much as I shall be able, and I will remedy both
the satiety and the ignorance of readers.

Phoebidas the Lacedaemonian, when he was leading an army to Olynthus and
making a journey through Thebes, occupied the citadel of the town, which is
named Cadmea, at the impulse of a few Thebans who, that they might more
easily resist the opposing faction, were devoted to the affairs of the
Laconians, and he did that by his private, not public, counsel. by which
deed the Lacedaemonians removed him from the army and fined him with money,
nor any the more did they give back the citadel to the Thebans, because,
enmities having been taken up, they thought it better that they be
besieged than set free. for after the Peloponnesian war and Athens
conquered they judged they had a matter with the Thebans and that they were
the only ones who would dare to stand against them. with this mind they had
given the highest powers to their friends and of the other faction they had
partly killed the chiefs, others they had cast into exile: among whom this
Pelopidas, about whom we have begun to write, driven out, was lacking his
fatherland.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

These almost all had betaken themselves to Athens, not that they might
follow leisure, but that, at whatever place nearby chance had offered, they
might strive to recover the fatherland. therefore when the time of doing
the thing had seemed, jointly with those who at Thebes felt the same, they
chose a day for crushing the enemies and freeing the city, that on which
the highest magistrates had been accustomed to feast together. great things
have often been done with not so great forces, but surely never from so
slender a beginning have such great resources been overthrown. for twelve
young men came together from those who had been punished with exile, when
altogether there were not more than a hundred who would offer themselves to
so great a danger. by which small number the power of the Lacedaemonians
was struck down. for they at that time brought war not more on the faction
of their adversaries than on the Spartans, who were chiefs of all Greece:
the majesty of whose empire, and not so much later, by the Leuctric battle,
struck from this beginning, fell. those twelve therefore, whose leader was
Pelopidas, when they had gone out from Athens by day, that with the sky
growing evening they might be able to arrive at Thebes, went out with
hunting dogs, carrying nets, in rustic dress, that they might make the
journey with less suspicion. who when they had arrived at the very time
they had aimed at, turned aside to the house of Charon, by whom both the
time and the day had been given.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

In this place it is pleasing to insert, although it is separated from the
proposed matter, of how great a calamity excessive confidence is accustomed
to be. for it came at once to the ears of the Theban magistrates that the
exiles had come into the city. that those given to wine and feasts despised
so far that they did not even take the trouble to inquire about so great a
matter. there was added what would even more open their madness. for a
letter was brought from Athens from Archias, one of those who were over the
Eleusinian rites, to Archias, who then held the highest magistracy at
Thebes, in which all things about their setting out had been written
through. which when it had already been given to him reclining at the
banquet, just as it was sealed, putting it under the cushion he said "I put
off serious things until tomorrow." but all those, when night had already
advanced, drunken, were killed by the exiles with Pelopidas as leader.
which things having been finished, the people having been called to arms
and liberty, not only those who were in the city but also from all sides
from the fields ran together, they drove the garrison of the Lacedaemonians
from the citadel, they freed the fatherland from siege, the authors of
occupying the Cadmea they partly killed, partly they cast into exile.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

In this so turbulent time, as we have shown above, Epaminondas, as long as
it was fought with citizens, was quiet at home. therefore this praise of
the liberated Thebes is proper to Pelopidas, the rest almost common with
Epaminondas. for in the Leuctric battle, Epaminondas being commander, this
man was leader of a chosen band, which first laid low the phalanx of the
Laconians. moreover he was present at all his dangers (as, when he attacked
Sparta, he held the other wing), and that Messene might more quickly be
restored, he set out as envoy to the Persians. finally this man was the
other person at Thebes, but yet second in such a way that he was next to
Epaminondas.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

But he was also tried by adverse fortune. for both at the beginning, as we
have shown, as an exile he lacked his fatherland, and when he desired to
bring Thessaly into the power of the Thebans and thought himself
sufficiently covered by the right of embassy, which among all nations had
been accustomed to be sacred, he was seized by the tyrant Alexander of
Pherae together with Ismenias and thrown into chains. Epaminondas recovered
this man, pursuing Alexander with war. after that deed he could never be
soothed in spirit toward him by whom he had been violated. therefore he
persuaded the Thebans that they should set out as aid to Thessaly and drive
out its tyrants. of which war when the chief command had been given to him
and he had set out thither with an army, he did not hesitate, as soon as he
caught sight of the enemy, to join battle. in which battle when he noticed
Alexander, kindled with anger he spurred his horse against him and having
gone far from his own he was pierced by a throwing of weapons and fell. and
this happened with a successful victory: for already the forces of the
tyrants had been bent. by which deed all the cities of Thessaly bestowed on
Pelopidas slain golden crowns and bronze statues and on his children much
land.
