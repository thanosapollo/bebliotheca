Rom 1,5-7a For our nature had been made at war with God through
disobedience, and not keeping the virtue which the law commanded, but being
trained even unto old age, yet becoming no more better than ourselves, we
reasonably provoke the lawgiver, learning thoroughly both what must be done
and what must not be done so that not even a pretext of ignorance is left to
us, but doing least of all what is needful, and most of all what is not
needful, so as to be made liable not only for idleness of good things but
also for bad action and to owe a penalty for both, both of the things we
clung to and of the things we held off from. Of this enmity then, he says,
and of the punishment from it, it will belong after all, O Romans, for you
too to obtain the release, and to share in the peace of which the race of
men the divine grace has now deemed worthy. He therefore prays for them
first the grace of God; for through this all who believed enjoyed salvation;
then the peace through which he hints at the accomplishment of virtue for
all; for he is at peace toward God who embraces the evangelical way of life,
who is eager to serve him toward all. {Rom 1,7b } For the God who is master
of the whole showed us an unexpected adoption. What kind is this? That which
appeared through Jesus Christ our Lord; for by the name of the Father he
indicated the gift of adoption, and by the addition of the Lord Jesus Christ
he presented the one in whom first and through whom first this came to be.
And he says adoption the restoration of our nature into deathlessness and
incorruption; for such are worthy to be called sons of God, themselves also
sharing in the paternal impassibility as far as is possible. And of this we
received a share being already united through faith from here with the
firstfruits; for he himself is our peace, who made both one and broke down
the middle wall of the fence. And this was, I think, the mortality of the
body and the passionate of the flesh, by which being carried down toward sin
we were walled off from kinship toward God. 353 But now no longer, he says;
for he raised our fallen nature in Christ and seated it at his right in the
heavenly places.{ Rom 1,8} Therefore he does not say through Jesus Christ as
through some servant but i.e. the [thanksgiving] that lies especially toward
thanksgiving of God according to the master Christ, and the unexpected
economy that saved through him our race, which also captured us through the
faith upon him together with the others. And he makes them his own, treating
them as far as possible through these things, lest perhaps thinking either
that he himself has condemned something against them, or that he has come to
write to them as taking hold of Peter's tradition, then being impatient at
this they flee the reading, and be deprived of the benefit of the letter.
Beginning then from the thanksgiving and from the faith, having praised them
as it being unlying and firm and those all together strongly holding to it,
both through the word "is proclaimed" which he said very fittingly toward
the eminence of the city, and through the addition of "in the whole world"
having sufficiently honored and exalted them, he passes at once to the
recommendation of his own person.{ Rom 1 ,9} And he said "my spirit", since
he was not speaking of the hypostasis of the Spirit, but of the grace of the
Spirit given to him toward preaching the gospel, being deemed worthy of
whose cooperation he was able to accomplish the work of the apostleship.
{ Rom 1,10-12 These things he says suspecting the hearer to take the word
harshly, and as it were to meet him with "what are you saying, you are in
haste to impart a spiritual gift to you. For what is lacking to Peter's
teaching? Do you altogether blame his doctrines, as being someone yourself,
as a greater apostle than he, as having conversed more than he with the
master Christ and having been loved more than he by him." Suspecting these
things then first even in himself he indicates the aim of his presence, he
sufficiently chastened the suspicion of arrogance. Then the "to add
something" 354 and the not to have said "give"


but "impart"; for this is surely much more than that. Having lowered himself
he healed again by saying "to be established you"; for I have been eager, he
says, not as myself evangelizing something new to you besides Peter, but as
going to confirm you in the very things you have received. And still more
putting off such a suspicion, and this is, he says, to be mutually
encouraged in. you through the faith in one another of both you and me. I
said, he says, that I long for the sight of you, and wish to impart some
spiritual gift to you toward your confirmation, knowing that the gain from
this is common, not rather from me of you, or from you of me receiving
encouragement. { Rom 1,13-15} My gain was the presence toward you, he is
saying; for the nations being brought to the gospel through me will clearly
increase the wealth for me. I would not then willingly neglect my own
benefit nor voluntarily undergo so great a loss; for it would not make sense
that the one who so eagerly pursues each of the other nations because of the
gain should despise you, unless left behind by much necessity. And not only
for this but also because among Greeks and barbarians, wise and foolish, I
am a debtor; thus the according-to-me eagerness also to evangelize you who
are in Rome. For I was not put forward as apostle of some and not of others,
but I was commanded to preach Christ simply to all, so that even as a debt
from something, not giving a favor, the coming to each, I would not have
been left behind even from you, if it had been possible. Having then with
frequent and noble reasonings just now stripped off the accusation upon the
delay, and still before these having sufficiently treated them and having
taken away the slander growing upon him as of an arrogant man, he also
concludes the preface. {Rom 1,16-17} Those who war against the Christian
preaching were tearing it down, mocking it as shameful; for there is nothing
more ridiculous than a word that announces that the son has been seized and
taken by Jews, and denies neither the cross nor the death, yet again claims
not only that he himself has been raised from the dead but also has gone up
into heavens as lord of the whole and that all the rest will rise from the
dead and all the other things whatsoever the apostles were preaching.
Mocking these things those men and tearing them down thought they were
stopping the apostles' mouths. Toward this reputation of the apostles then
355 the blessed Paul also standing up thus begins the word concerning the
doctrine: for I am not ashamed of the gospel. And this too is customary for
him, not putting forward the objection often, to make the beginning of the
solution from the solution itself, and if one does not stretch the mind
accurately to the same apostolic words, wandering from their meaning he will
not easily perceive. Such a thing is also the careless "not as though the
word of God has fallen away", but this we will make clear then when we come
upon it with God, but now let us return to the matter at hand. For having
met the strongest argument of the opponents as they thought, by taking this
away he began the establishment of his own; for I am not ashamed of the
gospel i.e.: why must the eagerness run out toward all men? Someone will
say, let the cheapness of the preaching cut you off. This then is itself
what more stirs and nerves me; for what seems weak in the evangelical word,
this especially presents the power of God. In what way? For it is the power
of God, he says, unto salvation to everyone who believes. It saves you all
whoever believe him to be true, and of this the Jew has need before the
others, who boasts through the written law to have more than Greeks toward
godliness. And how does it save? For the righteousness of God is revealed in
it from faith unto faith. Through "righteousness" he says the sinlessness,
through "of God" that from divine grace, through "is revealed" that it will
be known in due time on the day of the resurrection, through "from faith"
that not from works of law but the believing in the resurrection of Christ,
through "unto


faith" that not concerning him only but from him also concerning ourselves
as going to rise to believe. The whole thought of what lies before, what is
this then? That our gospel is something very great and truly wonderful, if
one attends accurately to its power; for all men whom neither the natural
law helped nor the written added to the natural, it saves through the faith
upon Christ; for someone having been fully assured concerning the
resurrection from the dead, that he himself also will share in this, himself
also obeying him according to the Savior's own promise, is inside salvation.
And this, he says, God was making known long ago and of old through Habakkuk
the prophet, that the righteous shall live from faith. And he prefixed this
testimony both unto credibility of his own word and especially unto
refutation of the Jews' rivalry supposedly for the law. Such then in power,
he says, is our 356 gospel, and most finely from its power, because of its
being as yet unseen, he rushed upon the establishment, as if someone were
saying about a grain of wheat, that this now seen is nothing more than a
grain, but if you look at its power you will find this being both root and
stalk and ear and wheat, and likewise multiplied of itself, and the more
wonderful; before it falls into the earth and dies, none of these things
comes to be, but having been buried and having died into earth, as I said,
it does. Up to these things having as it were skirmished beforehand the
establishment of the gospel and having made it rather declaratively than
demonstratively and having said it is saving similarly to all, both Jews and
Greeks, he proceeds then to the examination, of Hellenism first and of
Judaism after this, that is of both the natural law and the written. And
natural law I do not mean such as that of the irrational animals but the
natural power of the soul to distinguish with reason the things to be done.
He examines however the natural before the written, because the natural law
is upon more, for it is common of all, and the written upon less; for of
Jews only. That one then reasonably, as having all under itself, he first
refutes, and having shown both Greeks and Jews liable to punishment; for
knowing they transgress what is needful he constructed for them as being
necessary without dispute the only remaining salvation through the faith
unto Christ. {Rom 1,18a } This is confessed, he says, among all, that both
penalties and rewards will come to each in due time analogously to the
things lived from here. And well the "is revealed" as from here God is
already indignant at these things but holding back through long-suffering,
then showing the indignation by the punishment. And the addition of "from
heaven" increased the fear for the hearer more greatly; for it indicated the
inescapable of the judge, and reasonably this as confessed of a Greek,
because also among Greeks it is believed without doubt that there will be a
judgment of all. {Rom 1,18b-19} For since these two are the most generic of
sins, the offense concerning God and that concerning the neighbor, he sets
both, and first as greater the impiety toward God, having called it that,
357 and second the injustice toward the neighbor, having named this. Then he
also establishes that our whole race will justly come under judgment, having
said "of those who hold the truth in injustice"; for it is not possible for
them to flee into ignorance. Knowing then they maltreat the truth according
to them. And having divided the sins, he examines first of them that toward
God and says that the knowledge concerning God is clear and manifest to
them, God himself having manifested himself to them. {Rom 1,a } For also
that God is powerful and eternal, and indeed also creator and provider and
all the other things whatsoever both belong to him, and are invisible to all
generated things. These through the all-wise creation in the making of this
world he granted us to behold with the mind; for having created the world he
constructed it so intelligently that being altered


and being changed it teaches that it has come to be by God, him being truly
eternal and unalterable, and standing together even in the continuous
motions and not being destroyed by them it cries out that he who made it is
powerful. And he created us of such a nature, having cast upon us mind and
reasoning, having granted us these, so that following the teaching through
this world we are able understanding to behold his invisible things. For if
we learned from its turns and alterations that it is generated, it is clear
that having come to be it has been generated by the altogether ungenerated
and unturned, who being able in excess does not even, it being in so great a
change of the elements being borne together into one another because of what
is needful for us, let it perish but still holds it existing and
indissoluble. {ROM 1,β} And the "that they may be without excuse" he says
i.e. he declines their defense; this turns away the tongue, stops the mouth,
makes to be silent. And he said it in the causal manner according to his own
idiom; for it is customary for the apostle to set the outcomes of things as
causes of the things. For God did not for this first deem men worthy of the
knowledge of himself, that they might have no defense at all, but he
manifested this in them toward their benefit in order that they might have
both his and receive of his care. Since those insulted the divine gift
through their own sloth, there followed as is likely that having fallen from
what is fitting they were deprived of all excuse. 358 Rom 1,21-23} That they
knew God to be, then, is clear; therefore they will not pay a penalty for
this, but that they did not use the knowledge as was needful, for this they
will be punished; for each was glorifying him as God, and whatever he
thought God, this also as being superior he served. But they went after the
matter mistakenly and unintelligently; for having left the road of God upon
the knowledge of him and having cut a road of their own they ran aground
upon extreme madness, having acquired the excess of what they thought their
wisdom as an addition of folly, having brought down the divine worship as
far as reptiles and lifeless things, so that their wandering concerning God
is proven against them by the knowledge concerning God existing in them, and
at once takes away all pardon from them. The sense however of "they did not
glorify as God or give thanks" is thus: of "they gave thanks", that they did
not confess him to be cause and supplier of all good things to them, of
"they glorified", that the conception they ought to have had concerning God,
this they did not have. Knowing him then from the teaching through this
world as invisible, eternal, incorruptible, almighty, they did not remain in
these, nor did they keep such a glory concerning him, but they exchanged the
glory of the incorruptible God in a likeness of an image of a corruptible
man. Likeness of an image he says the image itself, just as also he says
likeness of flesh the flesh itself, wherefore also heavily weighting the
charge with the word, he added this very thing "of a corruptible man" that
is of the flesh that is corrupted. Since also of this of the man alone, not
also of the soul, the impressions are images. He went through next
generically, yet still by name parading the error itself, having said of
birds and four-footed things and reptiles; such also is that of the psalm:
they exchanged his glory in a likeness of a calf eating grass; for here too
likeness of a calf he says the calf. What kind of calf however he means, he
made clear having said: they made a calf in Horeb and worshipped the graven
thing. {Rom 1,24-27} The apostle then having sufficiently shown that
knowledge of God has been given to men from God by the artful creation of
this world and by the construction of their nature suited toward the
learning of these things, and that those who received this corrupted it in
itself with fleshly reasonings and did not use it toward anything needful,
he says then 359 consequently of how many and of what kind of absurdities
the falling away of God from the true


knowledge became their cause. The master God then, he says, nothing of these
things of their ignorance harmed, but being without need and having a
nature, how would one say: he himself remains by nature most blessed
continually upon this? But they were glued toward every kind of vice, having
been so overpowered by the error and darkened by the ignorance of God, as
even to be carried out toward the things against nature, after the manner of
the blind falling upon whatever they happened upon, and reasonably; for if
it is not possible that those deprived of sensible light not stumble against
the things at their feet, much more is it impossible to live rightly and
without sin for those who have deprived themselves of that blessed light.
Showing this very thing to have been led off as a penalty of their offense
toward God, as before the punishment upon the sins the sinning itself is not
the most grievous punishment for those who are temperate. {Rom 1,28} He does
not then say this, that God overturned them into these abominable daring
deeds; for God is cause of this to men of nothing. May it not be. For how
[would he], who both is indignant and threatened gehenna upon these things?
But Scripture is accustomed thus to call the permission of God and the
abandonment, as "you turned aside our paths from your way", and "the Lord
hardened the heart of Pharaoh", and "why did you make us wander, Lord, from
your way? you hardened our heart not to fear you"; and "lead us not into
temptation"; he says then that of those who stood away he also stood away
and having left him he left them, so that of this counterfeit life itself
the cause is their unapproved knowledge concerning God. {Rom 1,29-32} And
with this one name of injustice having commonly taken in all their sins
toward one another, in what follows he enumerates according to each. And
wickedness he says the labor coming to be from construction toward someone
from this, and greed the harm 360 for the desire of more, and malice the
eagerness to harm the neighbor, and envy the grudging at the good things of
the neighbor, and murder the movement as far as destruction, and strife the
blameworthy rivalry, and deceit the plots and devices upon injury of the
brethren, and malignity the bad-turning, and whispering the speaking-ill of
those present under the teeth, and slander the blasphemy against those
absent by some, and haters of God not those hated by God, for this is not
now proposed for him to show, but those who hate God, and insolent the rash
and abusive, and proud those puffed up against those who do not have upon
the things they have, and boasters those who boast upon things they do not
have as having them, and inventors of evils those who innovate other evils
upon the old evils, and disobedient to parents those ungrateful even as far
as parents, and without understanding the without-conscience, for of such
nothing is more mindless, and covenant-breakers those who do not remain in
the covenants which they may make toward some, and without affection the
harsh and friendless, and implacable the unreconcilable and remembering-evil,
and unmerciful the unbending and unyielding toward mercy.
{Rom 2, 1} Or "he who judges" he says i.e. he who has been deemed worthy of
a nature discerning of the things to be done. And why is such a one without
excuse? In that you judge, he says, the other, you condemn yourself; for you
who judge do the same things. {Rom 2, 2-5} And above the things said are
taught me; for having said that we are condemned upon the things we stumble
in, again here similarly to the things above, the judgment of God being
confessed among all and without doubt, he adds: we know that the judgment of
God is according to truth upon those who do such things. And having set this
as undisputed he uses henceforth more rebukingly the words following toward
them. This being for us all, he says, clearly that an unbribed judgment
succeeds, do you expect, O man, stumbling in knowledge 361 being convicted
by yourself, not to give a penalty, but


upon the things for which you vote condemnation of certain others, upon
these to be taken off as innocent of them. And you think the true judge God
does not even make as much provision of the just as you, but because he does
not bring the punishment at your feet, giving a time unto repentance, you
will abuse this toward increase of the evil. And you will make the
long-suffering of God concerning you an occasion of all unholiness. Know
then clearly that according to your hardness you are treasuring wrath for
yourself. This whole resembles that of the psalmist: these things you did
and I was silent, you supposed lawlessness that I will be like you; I will
convict you and set your sins before your face. The "you are treasuring"
then he said from a metaphor of those who have the treasures in hidden
places, yet increase them with additions little by little. And day of wrath
he says the time of the punishment. And you then, he says, by the multitude
of the sins as in something unseen the lesser, will bring upon yourself the
punishment at the coming judgment. And he named it a day of revelation
because all the things that are completely hidden are then going to be
brought into the open upon all, and of righteous-judgment as here indeed the
just judgment being wronged in many ways, either through ignorance of those
judging or through another fleshly pretext, but there the vote being brought
out purely justly and brightly. And such as is likely the divine, he took up
however also through these the "for the wrath of God is revealed from
heaven", through the beginning and the end, having taken in the accusation
of their impiety and of the injustice by the fear of the punishment.
{Rom 3, 19} Unto this, he says, the law was of benefit, it made the sin
known to us, not only by forbidding the practice of the absurd things but
also by punishing those who disobey it; for we would not, punishment not
being brought upon the stumbles, have known of what sort the thing of sin
is, and through all these things then having established very accurately
that all are under sin he adds then, showing the surplus of his own word.
362 {Rom 3,22} Unto all the Jews, he says, as having from home and from

{Rom 4,1} The saying is hyperbaton; for it would follow thus, if somehow it

{Rom 5,12-14} Of this life of ours, he says, in this world that admits both
sinning and dying and for this also needing a law, reasonably of the one
that both indicates the sin and forbids it, Adam first began, as being both
first and having come to be and having transgressed. Of those then born from
him in successions all were dying as is likely inheriting of the nature of
the forefather; but of these who were dying some by the things by which they
also themselves were transgressing were being brought under death, and some
by the condemnation of Adam alone, such as the portion of infants. For this
now, he says, death went through unto all men, upon which all sinned, saying
the all instead of the many just as in the things after these; for if by the
trespass of the one the many died, he says the many instead of the all. And
now death reigned from Adam until Moses even upon those who did not


sin; for those who are inside neither of action nor of discerning choice,
363 how would they be liable to an offense? And the "until law sin was in
the world" is such a thing. The blessed Paul often discusses with us from
the things according to Christ, as after the manifestation of the Savior the
old constitution no longer holding but the new; in the old then, we being
fleshly and subject to passions, the giving of the law was coming to be
necessary, cutting short by the threats of punishments the impulses of our
nature toward sin, but having become new in Christ and righteous, that is no
longer admitting the aging through sin, the law is altogether surplus. For
this he says: a law is not laid down for a righteous man and you are not
under law and you were put to death to the law through the body of Christ
and if you are led by spirit, you are not under law. Through then the "for
until law sin was in the world" he says this, that as long as we were of a
fleshly and passionate nature and for this needing to be under law, we were
being overpowered by sin, it living as a citizen in the whole world, the law
however having been taken away because we have passed over having become
spiritual toward justification in Christ, sin also has been taken away
together; for it is no longer reckoned there being no law. The whole then,
as one might say in a heading, mind of this saying is this. The grace
according to Christ furnishes to us all who have firmly believed it to boast
also for this, that nothing is unlikely but even very reasonable, that this
one begin for us the deathless and righteous life in the way Adam formerly
began for us the corruptible and passionate; for if to that one having
stumbled and been condemned we all after him from him have shared in the
end, some also themselves adding sin by transgressing, and some from his
punishment alone according to kinship, how is it not indisputable for the
master Christ also who lived according to the human and succeeded and
obtained after the resurrection from the dead the better lot? For this, he
says, God highly exalted him, those being united to him through faith and
deemed worthy of the indwelling of the Spirit, of him who first raised that
one himself from the dead, to share with him in the unstained life having
become spiritual instead of fleshly. But death reigned, he says, from Adam
until Moses even upon those who did not sin upon the likeness of the
transgression of Adam, i.e. in the way death reigned of Adam having
transgressed, thus also of those from him who came after him both having
sinned and not having sinned similarly. And the "until Moses" is the same as
the "for until law"; for Moses now he says not specifically the person of
the man but the thing, calling the law from the lawgiver. Thus also in 364
the [epistle] to the Corinthians: until Moses is read, that is the law given
through Moses. Who is a type of the one to come. It was being typed then, he
says, in the things about Adam and being pre-drawn the things that would
come to be afterward, these indeed the things according to our Lord Jesus
Christ; for it was perhaps needful that the first man Adam come to be unto a
living soul, and the last Adam unto a life-giving spirit. Since then the
first man Adam is of earth earthy, and the second man the Lord from heaven,
it is necessary that according to the earthy be the earthy, and according to
the heavenly the heavenly; and as we wore the image of the earthy, so surely
we shall also wear the image of the heavenly, being raised correspondingly
from the lesser toward the superior.{Rom 5,16} For one sin, he says, was
cause of the condemnation of all, and all in succession paid the penalty of
that, but the grace took away not that only but also many others heavier
than that practiced by those later, having worked our nature henceforth
altogether unstumbling and righteous.{Rom 5,17} Having sufficiently shown
the surplus of the grace by comparison, he then syllogizes further and
constructs the


aimed-at and says: for if by the trespass of the one death reigned through
the one and the things following. For he used the conjunction now i.e.
therefore; if therefore, he says, even by a trespass of one man and through
one trespass death reigned, then much more those who obtain the more of the
grace unto righteousness in the one Christ, as has been shown, will reign in
deathless life.
{Rom 5,} And law entered beside, he says, that the trespass might abound.
This resembles that in the [epistle] to the Galatians: the law was added for
the sake of the transgressions. For the "that" here too he has set according
to the idiom, for it signifies the consequent. For to all those after Adam,
he says, or rather also to Adam himself a law having been given 365 the
trespass abounded; for by how much the commands are more, by so much also
the trespasses when they are transgressed. And sin having been increased
into a multitude by the lawlessnesses the grace that takes this away has
reasonably appeared being exceedingly great. {Rom 5,21} So that, he says, it
is indisputable that just as sin strengthened its own domination in death
and through death, so also the grace furnishing to all the justification in
Christ will have the kingdom unsuccessive in unending life.
{Rom 6,12} Having said the things preceding upon establishment of the word
concerning the grace according to Christ he perceived an objection being
born to him from these, and having set this against as from the contrary, he
works its solution most strongly and says: what then shall we say? Shall we
remain in the sin, that the grace may abound? May it not be. Will you then,
he says, from the things I said say that we must go on sinning and by this
make the grace both more and greater, if then toward the multitude of our
sins its nature also is increased? And this especially resembles the "some
say we say: let us do the evil things that the good things may come." May it
not be, he says. Meantime as unholy of every reasoning he prayed the word
away, and after this already also with undisputed reasonings he refutes its
absurdity and says: we who died to the sin, how shall we still live in it?
This, he says, those who are fleshly and unbelieving might have to say, but
for us it is of the altogether impossible to live again to the sin, we who
once died to it.
{Rom 6,3-4} You know this altogether, he says, that we say the baptism of
Christ has a type of both his death itself and of the resurrection. Those

this he says through the glory of the Father, so also we having shared in
the death with him through the baptism, we were sharers of that deathless
and unaging and all-blessed life. 366 {Rom 6,5} Likeness of his death. He
says his death itself as also the "in
 likeness of flesh of sin" and "having become in likeness of a man", or also
