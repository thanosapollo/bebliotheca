Scholia, with God, on the metres, from the voice of George Choeroboscus,
grammarian of Byzantium, of the oecumenical teaching.
1. One must know that the metrician has as his "aim" to treat of metres. The
inventor of metre is Apollo, that is the Pythia, or, as some say, Phemonoe the
priestess of the Pythia. Metres were therefore first found at the Pythia,
because the oracles and the responses were given in metre; then from the
Pythia the poets came to know them. Father and origin of metres is rhythm; for
it extends more universally, being not only in the metres but also in certain
other things; for rhythm is also accomplished from hammers in bronze-works and
from horses' feet and clapping of hands and the lyre and certain other things.
178 And the critic of metres is hearing; whence also some, defining what metre
is, say that which is composed of feet or bases, judged by the sense that is
through hearing. Feet and bases are to be understood as the same thing in
parallel. If then hearing is discriminative of metres, and the proper object
of sense for hearing is the voice, it is clear that the voice adorns the
metres. For as the voice, stretching and contracting the syllables, shapes and
adorns the sound of good rhythm, so hearing, having received it, judges the
voice itself and the rhythm of the metres. Whence often metres are found in
prose speech and escape notice because of the rhythm of the prose speech (and
conversely in metres prose speech is found and is not easily perceived),
unless perhaps hearing, judging well, makes it evident and clear. Whence also
in Demosthenes one can find metres: iambs continuously, as (Ol. 1.5) "for it
is clear to the Olynthians that"; but also a heroic verse, as (On the False
Embassy 143) "the war in Amphissa, on account of which to Elatea"; but also an
ionic a maiore in the same (Ol. 3.4) "and when many speeches and a tumult
arose among you"; similar [and] to (Sapph. 76 = Heph. 36, 17) [assarotera]
"Mnasidica of the tender Gyrinno." 179 Rhythm therefore differs from metre, in
that rhythm is in many things and in words, but metre is in the times alone.
This Aristophanes also makes clear, even if he seems to jest in bringing on
Socrates, that they differ; for he speaks thus (Nub. 638) "whether about
metres or about verses or rhythms"; so that it is clear that metre is one
thing and rhythm another. Metre is said in many ways. For metre is that which
measures and <that which> is measured, just as also a medimnus and a choenix
and a cubit. Metre is also said of due proportion, as the saying "every
measure is best" shows. Metre is also said of the simple foot, whence we also
call the verse hexameter, as having six feet. Metre is also said of the
dipody, whence we are also accustomed to call iambs trimeters, numbering them
by dipody. Metre is also said of the system of feet, as a verse or an iamb,
whence we say that this rhapsody has perhaps five hundred metres, as it were
five hundred verses. Metre is again said universally of every metrical speech
in distinction from prose diction, as it were the poem. It is also said
specifically of each metre, the dactylic or ionic or one of such. Metre is
also said of the time; whence a dactyl is tetrameter, as it were of four
times, and the heroic verse 180 is said to have 24 metres, as it were times,
as Orpheus or the Pythia shows, speaking of the epic thus: "upright, of six
parts, of four and twenty measures." (One must know that the metricians, that
is the grammarians, take the times otherwise, and the rhythmicians otherwise.
The grammarians know as a long time that which has two

times, and they are not brought down to anything greater; but the rhythmicians
say that this is longer than that, claiming that of the syllables one is of
two and a half times, another of three, another of more; for example ˉωˉς the
grammarians say is of two times, but the rhythmicians of two and a half, two
of the long ˉω, and a half-time of the ˉσ; for every consonant is said to have
a half-time.) The technician then, as we said before, has as his aim to treat
of metres. 2. The present treatise is "useful" not to all, but to those who
will compose books in metre, not to orators or simply to those who use prose
speech. 3. The present treatise is "genuine" of Hephaestion, first from the
common testimony of those who have made commentaries on it, and then also from
his mentioning this also in his other writings; for he makes a book On the
Poem, which is always found together with this book On Metres. 181 4. The
present treatise is "entitled" Encheiridion, either because, just as the
encheiridion sword is sharp and cutting, so this too is that which sharpens
and cuts the teaching; or, which is better, from its being very small (for it
makes an epitome of what he has said at length) and, as custom says, from
having it in the hands; for so also Heliodorus, having himself also made an
encheiridion on metres, says at the beginning "for those who wish to have in
their hands the most capital points of metrical theory" and the rest. One must
know that [this Heliodorus] first made 48 books on metres, then later
abbreviated them into eleven, then again into three, then further into one,
this Encheiridion. From its being small then and easily carried in the hands
it has been entitled Encheiridion. 5. It is "divided" into two; and in the
first he treats of "syllables", that every syllable is either short or long or
common, and of "concurrent pronunciation", which is also called synizesis;
then also of "feet" and of various "metres", that there is iambic, trochaic,
dactylic, ionic, and certain such. And in the second, of the "antipathetic"
ones, that this foot is antipathetic to that, for example the trochee is
opposite to the iamb and the spondee to the pyrrhic, the dactyl to the
anapaest, and certain such; then also of "asynarteta", that this 182 said
beside that is uncomposed and asynartete, and it is clear that he brings
forward the examples of each, examining them; then he inquires also about
"polyschematista" and so completes it. 6. The present treatise "ought to have
been placed first", as some say, before every metrical treatise and before the
Iliad; for first we ought to have known what metre is and of what kind and
from where it is taken, and thus to know the metres themselves, whether they
are well or ill. But we say that the matter of the present treatise is the
metrical poems of the poets; for Hephaestion regulates these and brings
forward their usages, saying that such and such a poet used such and such a
metre. But how, the matter itself being unknown, could we know the method? As,
how, there being no wood, could someone learn to carpenter? Reasonably then we
first speak the words, that is the poems that admit metre, and their
inflection through the eight parts of speech, their syntax and punctuation,
and later this present treatise treating of their metre. 7. This again, as
every technical inquiry, is "brought under" the technical organon of grammar;
for it is not, as some have wished to say, under the 183 metrical, since it
treats of metres. For the poem itself is not in metre, so that we might say it
is under the metrical, as the work circulating in iambs of Philemon the
Atticist on Attic dispute in words. In which the seven headings have been
stated. One must know that metre has been said from meiro (I divide), as from
seio seistron and from dero dertron, <that is> the skin, as also the usage
(Od. 11.579) "going within the dertron." One must know that among the
metricians the oxytone syllable is greater than the barytone, for example the
syllable ˉλˉοˉς

in kalos is greater than that in philos; for a certain slowness of the time
comes about, as is also said in the case of the rough breathing, through the

Cap. I. On the short syllable.
"A short syllable is that which has a short vowel or one shortened, not at the
end of a word, in such a way that there are not, between this vowel and the
[vowel] in the next syllable, consonants more than one simple, but either one
or none." U "A short syllable is that which has a short vowel or one
shortened, not at the end of a word, so that between it and the vowel in the
next syllable there are not more consonants than one simple, but either one or
none." K One must know that, as has been said before, writing an encheiridion
and as in the manner of an introduction, he did not begin first from the
elements, as some say, or from stating the definition of metre, or from the
genus of the syllable, as it were the syllable in general (for in general "a
syllable is," as Dionysius says in the Art, "properly a taking together of
consonants with a vowel or vowels, as Kar, bous"), but from the syllable in
species, as it were long and short and common. For the matter of metres is the
syllable, and without this a metre would not be constituted. And he himself
says, as all also agree, that there are three species of the syllable in
general, I mean the 185 long and the short and the so-called common. But of
these [that is, of each one] he states certain differences, when and how and
in how many ways a short or long or common syllable comes about. Horos,
better, taking every syllable into five, says there are five species only of
syllables, two of the short, two of the long, and one of the common. For the
short syllable, he says, is either short by nature or by position; by nature,
when it has one of those short by nature, by position, when it has one of the
dichrona. And the long likewise, by nature, when it has one of those long by
nature, both vowels I mean and diphthongs, by position, when it has a
dichronon or a short vowel lengthened through <two> consonants or one double.
And one species of the common is the being able, though short, to be taken as
long, or conversely the being able, though long, to be taken as short. The
technician then treats first of the short syllable, and gives a definition of
it that is sound and convertible with the thing defined, which is the health
of a definition; and the definition is clear and not in need of explanation.
He said well "is short", and not "becomes short", as we shall show 186 more
completely in the common syllable. And he well added "not at the end of a
word" because of the so-called common; for if it completes a part of speech,
it falls under the account of the common, for example the syllable ˉτˉα is
short, and yet it lies in the place of a long in the poet, where he says (Il.
21.352) "the fair streams around"; likewise also ˉπˉρˉοˉς in (Il. 9.147)
"toward the house of Peleus"; for we call the foot pros oi a spondee. And
again he well added "there are not to be consonants more than one simple"
because of the long by position; for if it happen that two consonants are
brought upon a short or shortened vowel, whether by combination or by
division, or one double, it becomes long by position; and what is said is
obvious. {1On the long by nature.}1 "A long syllable is that which has a
<long> vowel <or> one lengthened or one of the so-called diphthongs, in such a
way that there is next a consonant, either final of that syllable itself or
initial of the next, as thes, sos, kai; if

not, they are not outright long, but common, as will be said next." U "A long
syllable is that which has a long vowel or one lengthened or one of the
so-called diphthongs, in such a way that there is next a consonant, either
final of that syllable itself or initial of the next, as thes, thos. If not,
they are not truly long but common." K 187 First he treats of the long by
nature, and later also of that by position. He says then that long by nature
