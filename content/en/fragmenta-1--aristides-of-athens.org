1.1 I, O king, by the providence of God came into this world; and having
beheld the heaven and the earth and the sea, both sun and moon and the rest, I
marveled at the ordering of these things. 1.2 And having seen the world and
all the things in it, that it is moved according to necessity, I understood
the one who moves and holds it together to be God. For everything that moves
is stronger than that which is moved and that which holds together is stronger
than that which is held together. 1.3 Him therefore I say to be God who
composed all things and holds them together, without beginning and eternal,
immortal and in need of nothing, above all the passions and deficiencies, both
of anger and of forgetfulness and of ignorance and of the rest. 1.4 And
through him all things have been composed. He does not need sacrifice and
libation nor any of all the things that appear, but all need him.

2.1 These things having been thus said concerning God, as it was given me to
speak concerning him, let us come also to the human race, that we may see
which of them partake of the truth and which of error. 2.2 For it is clear to
us, O king, that there are three races of men in this world; of whom there are
the worshipers of the gods so called among you, and Jews, and Christians; and
they again who revere the many gods are divided into three races, both
Chaldeans and Greeks and Egyptians; for these have become leaders and teachers
to the rest of the nations of the service and worship of the many-named gods.

3.1 Let us see then which of these partake of the truth and which of error.
3.2 For the Chaldeans, who do not know God, wandered after the elements and
began to revere the creation rather than him who created them; of which having
also made certain forms they named an impression of the heaven and of the
earth and of the sea, of sun also and of moon, and of the rest of the elements
or luminaries, and having shut them up in temples they worship them calling
them gods, whom they also keep securely, lest they be stolen by robbers, and
they did not understand that everything that keeps is greater than that which
is kept and the maker is greater than that which is made. For if their gods
are unable concerning their own salvation, how will they grant salvation to
others? A great error therefore did the Chaldeans wander, revering dead and
useless images. 3.3 And it comes upon me to wonder, O king, how their
so-called philosophers did not at all understand that even the elements
themselves are perishable. And if the elements are perishable and subjected
according to necessity, how are they gods? And if the elements are not gods,
how do the images, which have come to be in honor of them, exist as gods?

4.1 Let us come then, O king, to the elements themselves, that we may
demonstrate concerning them that they are not gods, but perishable and
changing, brought from not-being by command of the God who truly is, who is
both incorruptible and unchangeable and invisible; but he himself sees all
things and as he wills he changes and transforms them. What then do I say
concerning the elements? 4.2 And those who consider the heaven to be God
wander. For we see it turning and being moved according to necessity and
composed of many things; wherefore it is also called cosmos. And cosmos is a
construction of some craftsman; and that which has been constructed has a
beginning and an end. And the heaven is moved according to necessity with its
luminaries; for the stars being carried in order and interval from sign to
sign, some set, others rise, and according to seasons they make a journey to
complete summers and winters, as it has been enjoined upon them by God, and
they do not overstep their own bounds according to the inescapable necessity
of nature with the heavenly cosmos. Whence it is clear that the heaven is not
God but a work of God. 4.3 And those who consider the earth to be a goddess
have wandered. For we see it being insulted by men and being lorded over and
being dug and being mixed and becoming useless. For if it is baked, it becomes
dead; for from the potsherd nothing grows. And further if it is soaked still
more, both it and its fruits are destroyed. And it is trampled both by men and
by the rest of the animals, it is defiled with bloods of those being slain, it
is dug through, it is filled, it becomes a tomb of dead bodies. 4.4 These
things being so, it is not possible that the earth be a goddess, but a work of
God for the use of men.

5.1 And those who consider the water to be God have wandered. For it too has
come to be for the use of men and is lorded over by them, it is defiled and
destroyed and changed being boiled and exchanging with colors and being frozen
from the frost and being polluted with bloods. 5.2 And it is brought to the
washing of all unclean things. Wherefore it is impossible that the water be
God but a work of God. 5.3 And those who consider the fire to be God wander.
For it too came to be for the use of men and is lorded over by them being
carried about from place to place for boiling and roasting of all kinds of
meats, and further also of dead bodies. And it is destroyed also in many ways
by men being quenched. Wherefore it is not possible that the fire be God but a
work of God. 5.4 And those who consider the breath of the winds to be a
goddess wander. For it is clear that it is enslaved to another and for the
sake of men it has been constructed by God for the conveyance of ships and
gatherings of grain and for their remaining needs. And it increases and ceases
according to the command of God. 5.5 Wherefore the breath of the winds is not
considered to be a goddess but a work of God.

6.1 And those who consider the sun to be God wander. For we see it being moved
according to necessity and turning and passing from sign to sign, setting and
rising to heat the plants and shoots for the use of men, and further also
having portions with the rest of the stars. 6.2 And being much less than the
heaven, and failing of the light, and having no sovereignty. Wherefore the sun
is not considered to be God but a work of God. 6.3 And those who consider the
moon to be a goddess wander. For we see it being moved according to necessity
and turning and passing from sign to sign, both setting and rising for the use
of men and being less than the sun, both waxing and waning and having
eclipses. Wherefore the moon is not considered to be a goddess but a work of
God.

7.1 And those who consider man to be God wander. For we see him being moved,
being conceived according to necessity, and being nourished and growing old,
even when he does not wish. 7.2 And sometimes he rejoices, sometimes he is
grieved, being in need of foods and drink and clothing. 7.3 And that he is
irascible and jealous, and desirous, and repenting and having many
deficiencies. And he is destroyed in many ways by elements and animals and by
the death that lies upon him. It is not possible therefore that man be God but
a work of God. 7.4 A great error therefore did the Chaldeans wander after
their desires. For they revere perishable elements and the dead images and
they do not perceive these things being deified.

8.1 Let us come then to the Greeks that we may see if they think anything
concerning God. 8.2 The Greeks therefore saying they are wise became more
foolish than the Chaldeans, introducing many gods to have come to be, some
male and some female, slaves of all kinds of passions and craftsmen of all
manner of lawless deeds, whom they themselves set forth to be adulterers and
murderers, irascible and jealous and wrathful, patricides and fratricides,
thieves and robbers, lame and maimed and sorcerers and mad, and of these some
having died, some having been struck by lightning and being cut and being
lamented and having been enslaved to men and having become fugitives and being
transformed into animals for wicked and shameful mixings. 8.3 Whence the
Greeks, O king, introduced laughable and foolish and impious words, addressing
such as are not gods according to their wicked desires, that having them as
advocates of vice they may commit adultery, plunder, murder and do all the
terrible things. 8.4 For if their gods did such things, how will not also the
men themselves who attend to them practice such things? 8.5 From these
practices of error therefore it came to pass for men to have frequent wars and
slaughters and bitter captivities.

9.1 But also if we shall wish to go through the account according to each of
their gods, you will see much absurdity. 9.2 Thus there is introduced among
them before all a god the so-called Kronos and to this one they sacrifice
their own children. 9.3 Who had many children from Rhea and having gone mad
ate his own children. 9.4 And they say that Zeus cut off his private parts and
cast them into the sea, whence Aphrodite is fabled to have been born. Zeus
therefore having bound his own father cast him into Tartarus. 9.5 You see the
error and licentiousness which they introduce against their god; is it
possible then that a god be a prisoner and cut off? O the mindlessness; who of
those having mind would say these things? 9.6 Second is introduced Zeus, whom
they say to have reigned over their gods and to be transformed into animals,
that he may commit adultery with mortal women. 9.7 For they introduce this one
being transformed into a bull toward Europa, and into gold toward Danae, and
into a swan toward Leda, and into a satyr toward Antiope, and into a
thunderbolt toward Semele; then from these many children to have come to be,
Dionysus, and Zethus and Amphion, and Heracles, and Apollo and Artemis, and
Perseus, Castor and Helen and Polydeuces, and Minos, and Rhadamanthys, and
Sarpedon, and the nine daughters whom they named Muses. Then thus they
introduce the things concerning Ganymede. 9.8 It came to pass therefore, O
king, for men to imitate all these things, and to become adulterers and mad
for males, and workers of other terrible deeds, according to imitation of
their god. 9.9 How then is it possible that a god be an adulterer or a mounter
of men or a patricide?

10.1 And with this they also introduce a certain Hephaestus to be a god and
this one lame and holding a hammer and fire-tongs and smithing for the sake of
food. 10.2 Is he then in need? Which it is not possible that a god be lame and
in need of men. 10.3 Then they introduce Hermes to be a god, desirous and
greedy and a magician and maimed and an interpreter of words. 10.4 Which it is
not possible that a god be such. 10.5 And they introduce Asclepius to be a
god, being a physician and preparing drugs and a composition of plasters for
the sake of food. For he was in need; and later to be struck by lightning by
Zeus because of Tyndareus son of Lacedaemon and to die. 10.6 And if Asclepius
being a god and having been struck by lightning was not able to help himself
how will he help others? 10.7 And Ares is introduced to be a god, a warrior
and desirous of cattle and of certain other things; and later him committing
adultery with Aphrodite to have been bound by the infant Eros and by
Hephaestus. How then is he a god who is desirous and a warrior and a prisoner
and an adulterer? 10.8 And they introduce Dionysus to be a god, conducting
nocturnal festivals and a teacher of drunkenness and dragging away the women
of neighbors and being mad and fleeing; and later him to have been slain by
the Titans. If therefore Dionysus having been slain was not able to help
himself, but was also mad and a runaway, how would he be a god? 10.9 And they
introduce Heracles to have become drunk and to have gone mad and to have slain
his own children, then to have been consumed by fire and thus to have died.
And how would he be a god, a drunkard and a child-slayer and being burned up?
Or how to help others, not having been able to help himself?

11.1 And they introduce Apollo to be a god, jealous, and further also holding
a bow and quiver, and sometimes also a lyre and flute and prophesying to men
for the sake of pay. Is he then in need? Which it is not possible that a god
be needy and jealous and a lyre-player. 11.2 And they introduce Artemis to be
his sister, being a huntress, and to have a bow with a quiver, and this one to
roam about the mountains alone with the dogs, that she may hunt a deer or a
boar. How then will such a woman be a god, both a huntress and roaming with
the dogs? 11.3 And they say Aphrodite also herself to be a goddess, being an
adulteress. For sometimes she had Ares as adulterer, sometimes Anchises,
sometimes Adonis, whose death she also weeps seeking her lover; whom they say
also to go down into Hades, that she may buy Adonis back from Persephone. Did
you see, O king, a mindlessness greater than this? to introduce as goddess the
one who commits adultery and laments and weeps? 11.4 And they introduce Adonis
to be a god, being a hunter and this one to die violently having been struck
by the wild boar and not having been able to help his own misery. How then
will he take thought of men, the adulterer and hunter and violently dead? 11.5
All these things and many such and much more shameful and more wicked the
Greeks introduced, O king, having fashioned concerning their gods, which it is
neither lawful to speak nor at all to bring to memory. Whence men taking
occasion from their gods practiced all lawlessness and licentiousness and
impiety defiling both earth and air with their terrible deeds.

12.1 And the Egyptians being more silly and more mindless than these wandered
worse than all the nations; for they were not satisfied with the objects of
reverence of the Chaldeans and Greeks, but further also they introduced
irrational animals to be gods, both land and water, and the plants and shoots
and they were defiled in all madness and licentiousness worse than all the
nations upon the earth. 12.2 For from the beginning they revered Isis, having
as brother and husband Osiris, who was slain by his brother Typhon. And for
this reason Isis flees with Horus her son to Byblos of Syria seeking Osiris,
bitterly lamenting, until Horus grew and killed Typhon. 12.3-5 Neither
therefore was Isis able to help her own brother and husband; nor was Osiris
being slain by Typhon able to succor himself; nor Typhon the fratricide,
perishing by Horus and Isis, found means to rescue himself from death. 12.6
And having been known in such misfortunes they were considered gods by the
senseless Egyptians. Who, not even in such things having been satisfied or
with the remaining objects of reverence of the nations, also introduced the
irrational animals to be gods. 12.7 For some of them revered a sheep, some a
goat, others a calf and the pig, others the raven and the hawk and the vulture
and the eagle, and others the crocodile, some the cat and the dog and the wolf
and the ape and the serpent and the asp, and others the onion and the garlic
and thorns, and the rest of the creatures. 12.8 And the wretched ones do not
perceive concerning all these that they have no power. For seeing their gods
being eaten by other men and being burned and being slaughtered and rotting,
they did not understand concerning them that they are not gods.

12,9-13.1 A great error therefore did both the Egyptians and the Chaldeans and
the Greeks wander introducing such gods and making images of them and deifying
the deaf and unfeeling idols. 12,9-13.2 And I wonder how seeing their gods
being sawn by the craftsmen and being hewn with axes and being truncated and
growing old by time and being dissolved and being melted they did not think
concerning them that they are not gods. For those who have no power concerning
their own salvation, how will they take providence of men? 12,9-13.3 But their
poets and philosophers, of both the Chaldeans and Greeks and Egyptians, having
wished by their poems and writings to dignify the gods among them, more
greatly uncovered their shame and set it naked before all. For if the body of
man being many-membered does not cast off any of its own members, but having
an unbreakable union toward all the members, is in agreement with itself, how
in the nature of God will there be so great a battle and discord? For if there
were one nature of the gods, a god ought not to pursue a god nor slay nor do
evil. 12,9-13.4 And if the gods were pursued by gods and were slain and were
seized and were struck by lightning, there is no longer one nature, but
divided minds, all evil-doing. So that none of them is a god. It is clear
therefore, O king, that all the physiology concerning the gods is error.
12,9-13.5 And how did the wise and learned of the Greeks not understand that
having set laws they are condemned by their own laws? For if the laws are
just, their gods are altogether unjust having done lawless things, mutual
killings and sorceries and adulteries and thefts and lying with males; and if
they did these things well, the laws then are unjust having been composed
against the gods; but now the laws are good and just, praising the good things
and forbidding the bad; but the deeds of their gods are lawless; lawless
therefore are their gods and all liable to death and impious those introducing
such gods, for if the histories concerning them are mythical, they are nothing
except only words; and if physical, those who did and suffered these things
are no longer gods; and if allegorical, they are myths and nothing else.
12,9-13.6 It has been demonstrated therefore, O king, that these polytheistic
objects of reverence are works of error and of destruction. For one must not
name as gods things visible and not seeing; but the invisible one who has
crafted all things one must revere as God.

14.1 Let us come then, O king, also to the Jews, that we may see what they too
think concerning God. For these, being descendants of Abraham and of Isaac and
of Jacob, sojourned into Egypt. And from there God led them out ... 14.2 For
they revere even now God the only Almighty, but not according to knowledge;
for they deny the Christ the son of God, and they are similar to the nations,
even if they seem somehow to approach the truth, from which they have
distanced themselves. These things concerning the Jews.

15.1 And the Christians are genealogized from the Lord Jesus Christ. And this
one the son of God the Most High is confessed in Holy Spirit having come down
from heaven for the salvation of men and having been born of a holy virgin
both without seed and without corruption, he took up flesh and appeared among
men, that he might call them back from the polytheistic error. 15.2 And having
completed his wondrous economy, through a cross he tasted of death by
voluntary will according to a great economy; and after three days he lived
again and went up into heavens. The glory of whose coming it is possible for
you to know, O king, from the holy evangelical scripture so called among them,
if you meet with it. This one had twelve disciples who after his ascent into
heavens went out into the provinces of the inhabited world and taught his
greatness; just as one of them went about the countries among us proclaiming
the doctrine of the truth. Whence those still serving the righteousness of
their proclamation are called Christians. 15.3 And these are they who above
all the nations of the earth have found the truth; for they know God creator
and craftsman of all things in only-begotten Son and Holy Spirit and they
revere no other god except this one. They have the commandments of the Lord
Jesus Christ himself engraved in the hearts, and they keep them, expecting
resurrection of the dead and life of the age to come. 15.4 They do not commit
adultery, they do not fornicate, they do not bear false witness, they do not
desire the things of others, they honor father and mother, and they love the
neighbors, they judge just things. 15.5 Whatsoever they do not wish to happen
to themselves they do not do to another, those who wrong them they exhort and
make them dear to themselves, they are eager to do good to the enemies, they
are meek and gentle. 15.6 From all unlawful intercourse and from all
uncleanness they refrain, 15.7 they do not overlook a widow, they do not
grieve an orphan; the one who has supplies the one who has not without
grudging; if they see a stranger they bring him under roof and they rejoice
over him as over a true brother; for they do not call themselves brothers
according to flesh but according to spirit. 15.8 They are ready to give forth
their souls on behalf of Christ; for they keep his commandments securely,
living holily and justly, as the Lord God commanded them, giving thanks to him
at every hour in every food and drink and the rest of the good things.

16-17.1 Truly therefore this is the way of the truth, which leads those
journeying it by the hand into the eternal kingdom promised by Christ in the
life to come. And that you may know, O king, that I do not say these things
from myself, having looked into the scriptures of the Christians you will find
me saying nothing outside the truth. Well therefore did your son understand
and justly was he taught to serve living God and to be saved into the age that
is going to come. For great and wondrous are the things said and done by the
Christians; for they do not speak words of men, but the things of God. But the
remaining nations wander and lead themselves astray; for journeying in
darkness they dash against themselves as those who are drunk. Unto here is my
word to you, O king, the one dictated in my mind by the truth. Wherefore let
your mindless wise men cease speaking vanities against the Lord. For it is
profitable for you to revere God creator and that you may be shown heirs of
the indestructible.
