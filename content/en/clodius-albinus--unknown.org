* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

After Pertinax, who was slain at Albinus's prompting, several men were hailed
emperor at almost one and the same time: Julianus at Rome by the senate,
Septimius Severus in Illyricum by the army, Pescennius Niger in the East, and
Clodius Albinus in Gaul. Herodian says that Clodius had been named Caesar by
Severus. But each took it ill that the other should rule, and neither the
Gauls nor the armies of Germany could bear not to have a prince of their own,
so everything on every side was thrown into confusion.

Clodius Albinus came of a noble family, though he was from Hadrumetum in
Africa. That is why he claimed for himself the oracle in which, as we said in
the Life of Pescennius, Severus was praised. He did not want it taken to mean
/the worst is the White One,/ which stood in the same verse as the praise of
Severus and the approval of Pescennius Niger. But before I speak of his life
or his death, I must also tell what made him noble.

* Chapter 2

For Commodus once, when he meant to make Albinus his successor, sent this same
man a letter ordering that he should be Caesar. I have put in a copy:

/The Emperor Commodus to Clodius Albinus. I have already sent you a public
letter about your succession and your honour, but this one is private and of
the household, written entirely, as you see, in my own hand. By it I give you
leave, if need arise, to go before the soldiers and take the name of Caesar.
For I hear that both Septimius Severus and Nonius Murcus speak ill of me among
the soldiers, hoping thereby to get for themselves the charge of the imperial
office. You will have besides, when you have done this, free power to give a
donative of up to three aurei, because I have also sent letters on this matter
to my procurators, which you yourself will receive sealed with the Amazonian
seal, and when there is need you will give them to the accountants, so that
they may not refuse to hear you when you wish to give orders about the
treasury. And indeed, that I may add for you some mark of imperial majesty,
you will have leave to use a scarlet cloak for the present, and when you are
with me you will have the purple as well, but without gold, because that is
how my great-grandfather Verus, who died as a boy, received it from Hadrian,
who adopted him./

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

When he received this letter he refused altogether to do what was ordered. He
saw that Commodus was hated for his ways, by which he had ruined the state and
disgraced himself, and would have to be struck down sooner or later, and he
feared that he himself would be killed along with him.

There is still in existence that speech of his in which, when he received the
empire, confirmed, as some say, even by Severus's will, he makes mention of
this matter. This is a copy of it:

/That I was led to empire against my will, fellow-soldiers, is proved even by
this, that I scorned Commodus when he offered me the name of Caesar. But both
your will and that of Severus Augustus must be obeyed, because I believe that
under an excellent man and a brave one the state can be well ruled./

Nor can it be denied, as Marius Maximus also says, that Severus's first
intention was that if anything happened to him he would put Pescennius Niger
and Clodius Albinus in his place. But afterwards, both from zeal for his sons,
now growing up, and from envy of the love felt for Albinus, he changed his
mind and crushed both of them in war, drawn especially by his wife's prayers.
In the end Severus even designated him consul, which he would certainly not
have done except for an excellent man, being careful in the choice of
magistrates.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

But to return to him: Albinus was, as I said, a native of Hadrumetum, but
noble among his own people and tracing his origin from Roman families, namely
the Postumii, the Albini, and the Ceionii. That family even today, most mighty
Constantine, is of the highest nobility, increased through you and still to be
increased, which grew greatly through Gallienus and the Gordiani. He himself,
however, was born in a modest home, with a very slender inheritance, of
blameless parents, his father Ceionius Postumus, his mother Aurelia Messalina,
and he was the first child of his parents. When he was taken from the womb,
unlike the usual way of boys, who when they are born tend to be red, he was of
the brightest white, and so he was called Albinus. That this is true is shown
by a letter of his father to Aelius Bassianus, then proconsul of Africa, a
kinsman, as it seems, of those very people.

Letter of Ceionius Postumus to Aelius Bassianus:

/A son was born to me on the seventh day before the Kalends of December, so
white at once over his whole body that he outdid the linen in which he was
received. Therefore I have taken him up and given him to the family of the
Albani, which is common to you and me, imposing the name Albinus. See to it
that you love the state and yourself and us, as you do./

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

He therefore spent his whole boyhood in Africa, moderately trained in Greek
and Latin letters, because even then his mind was military and proud. For he
is said to have sung very often in the schools among the little boys,

/Madly I seized my arms, though in arms there lay little reason,/

repeating

/Madly I seized my arms./

Many signs of empire are said to have been given him when he was born. For a
white ox was born with horns of a full purple colour. These, however, are said
to have been placed by him, already a tribune, in the temple of Apollo at
Cumae and to have remained there long. For when he drew a lot concerning his
fate, he is said to have been answered in these verses:

/He shall establish the power of Rome though tumult beset her,
Riding his horse he shall smite both Poeni and Galli rebellious./

And in Gaul indeed it is agreed that he subdued many nations. He himself,
however, suspected that /he shall smite the Poeni/ was foretold to
him concerning Severus, because Septimius was an African.

There was also another sign of coming empire. For since the family of the
Caesars had this special custom, that the little children of the house were
bathed in basins of tortoise-shell, when the infant was born a huge tortoise
was brought to his father as a fisherman's gift. He, being a man of letters,
took it as an omen and received the tortoise gladly and ordered it to be cared
for and dedicated to the child for the warming of the boy's baths, hoping that
even from this he would be ennobled. And since it was rare for eagles to be
seen in the places where Albinus was born, on his seventh day, at the hour of
the banquet which was assigned to the child's celebration, when they were
giving him his names, seven little eagles were brought from their nests and
set, as if in jest, around the boy's cradle. His father did not refuse this
omen either, and ordered the eagles to be fed and carefully tended. Another
omen was added: whereas the boys of his family were bound with reddish bands,
because by chance the reddish bands which the mother had prepared while
pregnant had been washed and were wet, he was bound with his mother's purple
sash. From that, in a nurse's joke, the name Porphyrius was also given him.
These and other signs of coming empire there were. Whoever wishes to know them
should read Aelius Cordus, who pursues all the trifles about omens of this
kind.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

As a young man, therefore, he went at once into military service and became
known to the Antonines through Lollius Serenus, Baebius Maecianus, and
Ceionius Postumianus, his kinsmen. As tribune he commanded the Dalmatian
horse; he also commanded the Fourth Legion and the First. He held the armies
of Bithynia faithfully at the time when Avidius was in revolt. Then he was
transferred by Commodus to Gaul, where, after routing the nations beyond the
Rhine, he made his name famous among both Romans and barbarians. Fired by
these things, Commodus offered him the name of Caesar and the power of giving
a donative and of using a scarlet cloak. From all of these he wisely
abstained, saying that Commodus was looking for men who would either perish
with him or whom he himself could kill with a pretext. He was granted the
quaestorship as a favour. When that was granted he was aedile for no more than
ten days, because he was being sent in haste to the army. Then he held a most
celebrated praetorship under Commodus. For at his games Commodus is said to
have exhibited fights both in the Forum and in the theatre. He was declared
consul by Severus at the time when Severus had prepared to put him in his own
place together with Pescennius.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

He came to the empire already well on in years and senior to Pescennius Niger,
as Severus himself says in his own Life. But after Pescennius was defeated,
since he both wished to keep the empire for his sons and saw the senate's
great love for Clodius Albinus, because he was a man of an ancient family, he
sent him through certain persons a letter of the warmest love and affection,
in which he urged that, since Pescennius Niger had been killed, Albinus should
rule the state faithfully with him. Cordus shows that this is a copy of it:

/The Emperor Severus Augustus to Clodius Albinus Caesar, most beloved and most
longed-for brother, greeting. After Pescennius was defeated we sent a letter
to Rome, which the senate, most loving of you, received gladly. I ask you to
rule the state in that spirit in which you were chosen brother of my mind,
brother of the empire. Bassianus and Geta greet you. Our Julia greets both you
and your sister. To your little child Pescennius Princus we will send gifts
worthy of his place and of yours. I would have you keep the armies for the
state and for us, my like-minded one, my dearest, my most beloved./

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

He gave this letter to most faithful attendants whom he sent, and he
instructed them to deliver it in public, but afterwards to say that they
wished to suggest many things in secret which concerned military affairs and
the secrets of the camp and loyalty at court. Then, when they had come into a
private place as if to speak their instructions, five of the strongest were to
kill him with daggers hidden under their clothes. Nor did their loyalty fail.
For when they had come to Albinus and given the letter, and after it was read
they said that certain things were to be suggested more secretly and asked for
a place removed from all witnesses, and when they would not allow anyone at
all to go with Albinus to a very long portico, on the pretext that the
instructions should not be betrayed, Albinus understood the plot. At last,
giving way to his suspicions, he put them to the torture. At first they denied
for a long time, but afterwards, overcome by necessity, they confessed what
Severus had ordered them.

Then, the facts now betrayed and the plot laid open, Albinus, seeing that what
he suspected was plain, gathered a huge army against Severus and his generals

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

and came. In the first clash, fought against Severus's generals, he had the
better. Afterwards, however, Severus himself, after he had brought it about in
the senate that Albinus should be judged an enemy, set out against him and
fought most fiercely and most bravely in Gaul, not without changes of fortune.
At last, when in anxiety he consulted the augurs, the answer given him, as
Marius Maximus says, was that Albinus would come into his power, but neither
alive nor dead. And so it happened. For when the last battle had been joined,
countless of his men slain, very many put to flight, many also surrendered,
Albinus fled and, as many say, struck himself, as others say, was struck by
his own slave, and was brought half-alive to Severus. From this the omen that
had been foretold before was confirmed. Many besides say that it was by
soldiers who sought favour from Severus by his death.

Albinus had one son, as some say, two as Maximus says. To these he first
granted pardon, but afterwards he struck them down with their mother and
ordered them thrown into the river. He had Albinus's head cut off, carried it
about on a pike, and sent it to Rome, with a letter to the senate in which he
taunted them for having loved Albinus so much that they heaped great honour on
his kinsmen and especially on his brother. Albinus's body is said to have lain
before Severus's headquarters for very many days, until it stank, and, torn by
dogs, it was thrown into the river.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Various things are said of his character. Severus himself, indeed, speaks of
him thus, calling him base, malicious, wicked, dishonest, greedy, and
luxurious. But this was in time of war or after the war, when he could no
longer be believed, speaking as of an enemy, since he himself had sent
frequent letters to him as to a dearest friend, and many thought well of
Albinus, and Severus himself had wished him to be called his Caesar and, when
he thought of a successor, had this man first before his eyes.

There are besides letters of Marcus about this same man, which bear witness
both to his virtues and to his character. There is no harm in inserting one of
them, given to the prefects concerning his name:

/Marcus Aurelius Antoninus to his prefects, greeting. To Albinus of the family
of the Ceionii, an African indeed but not having much of the African in him,
Plautillus's son-in-law, I have given two cavalry cohorts to command. He is a
practised man, severe in life, grave in character. I think he will be of use
in camp affairs; that he will certainly do no harm I know well enough. I have
decreed him a double salary, a soldier's dress plain but of his rank, and a
fourfold stipend. Do you exhort him to show himself to the state; he will have
the reward he earns./

There is also another letter in which the same Marcus wrote of this same man
in the time of Avidius Cassius, of which this is a copy:

/Albinus's firmness is to be praised, who held the armies that were gravely
falling away when they were fleeing to Avidius Cassius. And had he not been
there, all would have done it. We have therefore a man worthy of the
consulship, whom I will put in the place of Cassius Papirius, who has already
been reported to me as almost dead. I do not wish this to be made public by
you for the present, lest it reach either Papirius himself or those close to
him, and we seem to have substituted a consul in a living man's place./

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

These letters, then, show that Albinus was a steadfast man, and this above
all: he sent money to restore those cities which Niger had worn down, so that
he might more easily win their inhabitants to himself.

Cordus, who pursues such things in his volumes, says he was a glutton, and so
much so that he swallowed more than human nature can bear. For he says
that fasting he ate five hundred dried figs, which the Greeks call
callistruthiae, and a hundred Campanian peaches, and ten Ostian melons, and
twenty pounds of Labican grapes, and a hundred fig-peckers, and four hundred
oysters. He says he was certainly sparing of wine; which Severus denies, who
asserts that he was drunk even in war. He never agreed with his own people,
either because of drunkenness, as Severus says, or because of the harshness of
his character. He was most hateful to his wife, unjust to his slaves, savage
toward the soldier. For he often even raised ordinary centurions on the cross
when the nature of the case did not demand it; he certainly beat them with
rods very often and never spared offences. In dress he was most elegant, at
table most mean and intent only on plenty. He was among the foremost lovers of
women, never given to inverted lust, and a hunter of those who were; most
skilled in farming, so that he even wrote Georgics. Some say that certain
Milesian tales are his, whose fame is not obscure, though they are moderately
written.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

The senate loved him more than any emperor, especially from hatred of Severus,
whom the senators hated fiercely for his cruelty. In the end, after Albinus
was defeated, very many senators were killed by Severus who had truly been of
his party or seemed to be. And when he had killed him at Lugdunum, he at once
ordered letters to be sought out, so that he might find either those to whom
Albinus himself had written or those who had written back to him, and all
those whose letters he found he caused to be judged enemies by the senate. Nor
did he spare these, but he both killed them and put up their goods for sale
and brought them into the public treasury.

There is a letter of Severus which shows his mind, sent to the senate, of
which this is a copy:

/Nothing graver can befall me, conscript fathers, than that Albinus should
have your judgment rather than Severus. I brought grain to the state. I waged
many wars for the state. I brought the Roman people as much oil as nature
scarcely possessed. I, after Pescennius Niger was killed, freed you from the
evils of tyranny. A great return, truly, you have given me, a great thanks.
One of the Africans, and from Hadrumetum at that, pretending that he drew his
blood from the line of the Ceionii, you raised so high that you wished to have
him as prince while I was prince, and my children still
living. Was there no one, I ask, in so great a senate whom you ought to love,
who would love you?
You raised his brother with honours. From this man you hope for consulships,
from this man praetorships, from this man the badges of any magistracy you
please. You do not return me that gratitude which your ancestors showed
against the Pisonian faction, and likewise for Trajan, and lately against
Avidius Cassius. That counterfeit man, ready for every kind of lie, who even
feigned his nobility, you have set before me. Worse, Statilius Corfulenus had
to be heard in the senate, who held that honours should be decreed to Albinus
and his brother; it only remained for this noble man to decree him a triumph
over me as well. The greater pain was that most of you thought he should be
praised as a man of letters, when he, busy with certain old wives' ditties,
was growing old among the Punic Milesian tales of his Apuleius and literary
toys./

speratis P, Peter 1; sperastis Peter 2.

From this it appears with what severity he punished either the Pescennian or
the Clodian faction. All this is set down in his Life. Whoever wants a fuller
account should read Marius Maximus among the Latin writers, and Herodian among
the Greeks, who have told most of it truthfully.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

He was tall, with unbound curly hair, a broad forehead and a wonderful
whiteness, so that most think he took his name from that, with a woman's voice
and almost the sound of eunuchs, easy in movement, fierce in anger, grim in
his rages, uneven in luxury, for he often sought wine and often abstained,
thoroughly skilled in arms, so that he was not badly called the Catiline of
his time.

We think it worth showing why Clodius Albinus earned the senate's love. When
he was commanding the British armies by Commodus's order and had learned,
still falsely, that Commodus had been slain, although the name of Caesar had
been conferred on him by Commodus himself, he went before the soldiers and
used this speech:

/If the senate of the Roman people still had that old command of theirs, and
so great a matter did not rest in one man's power, the public lot would not
have fallen to men like Vitellius or Nero or Domitian. Under consular rule
those houses of ours, the Ceionii, Albini, Postumii, of whom your fathers, who
themselves had heard from their grandfathers, learned many things. And
certainly the senate added Africa to the Roman empire. The senate subdued Gaul
and the Spains. The senate gave laws to the peoples of the East. The senate
tried the Parthians; it would have subdued them, had not the fortune of the
state then named so greedy a prince to the Roman army. Caesar subdued the
Britains, a senator certainly, but not yet dictator. How much better would
this very Commodus have been, if he had feared the senate? And down to Nero,
indeed, the senate's authority held, which did not fear to condemn a filthy
and impure prince, though votes were passed against him who then held the
power of life and death and the empire. Therefore, fellow-soldiers, I do not
want the name of Caesar which Commodus conferred on me. May the gods grant
that others may not want it either. Let the senate command, let it divide the
provinces, let the senate make us consuls. And why do I say the senate? You
yourselves and your fathers; for you yourselves will be senators./

This speech, while Commodus was still alive, was brought to Rome. It
exasperated Commodus against Albinus, and he at once sent as successor Junius
Severus, one of his tent-companions. But it pleased the senate so much that
with strange acclamations it honoured him in his absence both while Commodus
lived and afterwards when he had been slain, so that some even urged Pertinax
to take him as a partner, and with Julianus his authority counted for much
concerning the killing of Pertinax. And that this may be understood as true, I
have inserted a letter of Commodus given to his praetorian prefects, in which
he made known his mind about killing Albinus:

/Aurelius Commodus to his prefects, greeting. I believe you have heard, first,
that it was a fiction that I had been killed by the counsel of my own people,
and then of the speech Clodius Albinus held before my soldiers, in which he
much commends himself to the senate, and that, as far as we see, not in vain.
For he who denies that there ought to be one prince in the state, and who
asserts that the whole state ought to be ruled by the senate, seeks empire for
himself through the senate. Beware therefore most carefully; for you already
know that the man is to be avoided by you, by the soldiers, and by the
people./
