1 Mt 1, 2 Abraham was justified through faith; and to this one who believed
God said: I will multiply your seed and I will give you the land and in you
all the tribes will be blessed. If then God speaks true things and the Jews
alone happen to be seed of Abraham, and these neither ever mastered the land
nor master it, where is the true thing of God that was spoken to Abraham? But
since it is impossible for God to lie, it is clear that God called the church
of the nations seed of Abraham. And this came to pass through Christ who was
going to be born from him, into whom the nations, having believed, were called
seed of Abraham, not by law of nature, but by grace. 2 Mt 1, 19 When Joseph
was set into perplexity of reasonings, then the angel stood by, loosing the
strife of the reasonings; for that he was at a loss is clear from what he was
considering. For he wished, it says, to send her away secretly; he would not
have wished either secretly or at all to send her out, if he had been
persuaded that the bulk of the womb was of bastard offspring; but being just
he would especially have handed her over both to law and to punishment; but
this he was not persuaded of; nor again did he know that the conception was of
divine energy; for the work was beyond human thought. Therefore he was tossed
by the reasonings and did not know what, having done, he might seem
well-pleasing to God. When he was shaken in these deliberations, then very
fittingly the angel came, fixing his mind on what it ought to be, and says: do
not fear, and so on. Joseph, having been set into perplexity about the virgin,
did not know securely what he must also do. And to him in perplexity the angel
appeared, loosing his helplessness and still leading him toward the truth; for
if he knew the conception to be of unlawful mixing, being just he would not
have wished to send her away either secretly or openly, but to do all that the
law commanded; for through this cause Joseph was at a loss and the angel
appeared who would loose his perplexity. 3 Mt 1, 20 Toward those who say how
the conception is from the Holy Spirit, for Christ is not son of the Spirit,
but of the Father, it is possible to say that the whole Godhead is called
spirit, as the Savior says: God is spirit, and those who worship him must
worship in spirit and truth. And the Holy Spirit is also called specially,
that is, the one hypostasis; therefore it is possible to understand as Holy
Spirit also the power of the Son; for the Son too is called spirit inasmuch as
he is God. And one must not wonder; for it is possible for the one who brought
forth a woman from the man alone also to bring forth a child from a woman
alone. 4 Mt 1, 22 Is "this whole thing has come to pass" a voice of an angel
or of the evangelist's wise mind? For the interpretation that has been made
unfolds the saying as a voice of an angel, but the sequence of the discourse
requires the explanation of cause to be the writer's. The saying that says
"this whole thing has come to pass," whose would it be, the angel's or the
evangelist's? In appearance the angel's, as some of the interpreters say, but
in truth the writer's. 5 Mt 1, 24 His wife, the one who did not even cast her
eye toward another man, who kept the rank of wife sound and uninnovated for
him and loosened nothing of the betrothal. Scripture calls the virgin wife of
Joseph as having kept all things that befit a chaste wife toward her own
spouse. 6 Mt 2, 13 Christ flees into Egypt, in order that he may also sanctify
this as he sanctified Babylon through the magi who came to worship him. 7 Mt
2, 15 Balaam says in Numbers: God led him out of Egypt, as the glory of a
unicorn is his. 8 Mt 3, 9 From these stones, from every dishonored and cheap
thing God is able to raise up as glorious and great those lying below in
ancestral cheapness and to show them conspicuous and notable, if only they
should choose to follow his commands, just as he made Abraham also notable
when he left the ancestral superstition; for not by glory of a father, but by
conspicuousness of his own virtue


having believed God, he shone; those therefore who believe God, even if they
come from obscure and cheap people, are raised by God and are enrolled into
the lot of Abraham's offspring, their ancestral low birth and impiety
hindering them nothing. Or also otherwise. The one who molded the first man
from earth and raised this one as father of every man is able also from the
stones to mold the humans on earth and to raise these and lift them up, so as
to become heirs of honor and glory similar to Abraham's. "From these stones"
does not mean the stones themselves, but that from incomplete and unbelieving
and poor humans he is able to make great and notable ones, if they wish, just
as Abraham, having believed God, became notable from obscure. 9 Mt 3, 12 Whose
fan is in his hand, it signifies the critical power and authority, through
which the better is distinguished from the worse; and "in his hand" makes
known the ready and unlaborious; for the discrimination is not by practice and
time and deliberation, but ready and from the instant of God willing. And the
righteous are separated from the sinners, of whom he named some wheat as
dragging nothing alien to the good seed originally sown by God in nature, and
the sinners chaff as having adulterated the whole race and ended through vice
in a monstrous nature. For just as in the other sciences there are certain
instruments that distinguish the things good according to the art and the
contrary, so also among the things farmed there is the threshing-floor and the
fan; for on the threshing-floor the wheat is distinguished from the chaff. To
which he likens both the church and the power of God that distinguishes from
the good those who are not such. 10 Mt 4, 5 8 Then the devil takes him, i.e.:
the desire is yielded and conceded to the devil, in order that he may be more
shamed; for he desired and wished Christ to go up onto the pinnacle and onto
the mountain so that he might wrestle with him by the temptations; this then
happens and our Lord goes up onto the pinnacle and onto the mountain and from
every side shames him, having thrown him down. "The devil takes him" is not
that he took him with authority, but it shows that he took him as Christ
conceded. And this economically, in order that when the devil's desire is
fulfilled he may be completely shamed and defeated. 11 Mt 5, 1 Christ sat, in
order that the crowds may believe through the wonders done by him, and the
disciples, being taught, may still be illumined and understand that he is not
only man, but also God; for he was healer not of bodies only, but also of
souls. 12 Mt 5, 13 He calls salt of the earth those who teach the divine
oracles, astringent and strengthening the mind of the hearers. 13 Mt 5, 17 I
did not come to destroy the law; for that one destroys the law who does not
fulfill the law. But the one who fulfills the law, not fulfilling it only, but
also leading it up to a more perfect exactness, how does he not rather keep
it? As therefore the one who, the form of the man typed in shadows, as
therefore the one who shapes this by insertion and application of the colors
did not destroy the types pre-drawn in it, but brought their incompleteness to
completion and perfection, so also our Master God did not destroy the gospel
way of life pre-typed, as it were, and sketched in shadow in the law, far from
it, but both manifested this and made it clear, having filled up all the
incompleteness in the law and having led it forward to unsurpassable
perfection. The one who fulfills a law and leads it to exactness is rather a
keeper of law, not indeed a destroyer; for the one who fills up the sketch of
the image through the colors would be called a perfecter of the image rather
than a destroyer of it; for the way of life previously sketched in the law
Christ, having come, both healed and perfected. 14 Mt 5, 19 One of the least
commandments, of those that seem to the many to be cheap and contemptible; for
those who lift themselves against that very lawgiver and



treating the carpenter's son as cheap clearly also thought his commandments
cheap and set at nought, but of their drunken outrage against himself and
contempt he does not yet define the penalty, showing the sea of
long-suffering, yet of the transgression of the commandments (it was working
salvation itself for them) he exacts the punishment that cannot be begged off,
declaring such a one least and dishonored and liable in the kingdom of the
heavens. You, he says, set aside my commandments as least and contemptible,
but I through the setting aside of them show you least and set at nought; and
you before humans easy to count, but I before both angels and humans; and you
in the present life, but I in the kingdom of the heavens condemn you to be
least. And it is clear that of the least there would not be another lower and
last, so that it is clear that he exacts from such a one the last punishment,
which is the outer fire. 15 Mt 5, 19 He calls a least commandment the one that
seems such to some and not in itself. And he says these things leading us up
toward the greater of the things laid down in the law; for not every
commandment has the same censure, since neither is every act equal to all, but
there are some that are even left without punishment to those who sin, which
perhaps Christ also called least. 16 Mt 5, 22 To one he brings punishment of
anger, to another pardon, and another he even deems worthy of prizes; for when
we are angry at the wicked and at the workers of wickedness, hastening to
check them from the evil acts, we store up for ourselves a great recompense;
for it is a great thing to turn a man from the way of his error. And this very
much needs a sober soul, lest under the mask of the correction of others we
appear to be serving our own enmity through the anger, as the one who shows
anger to those who have stumbled for the sake of their own salvation and does
not mix vice with the anger is blessed. The one however who is angry at the
one who has grieved him is not outside blame, if one must wait for the common
judge and commit to him the judgment concerning those who abuse, and not
himself, using anger and wrath, blind and base assessors, seek the penalty
from the neighbor. This species of anger then is not blameless, yet not far
from pardon, because he himself was angry first, having suffered badly from
another. But the one who, neither for the sake of some salvation nor defending
himself against the one who first grieved him, is carried out into anger
vainly and unreasonably and drunkenly outrages the neighbor is reasonably
liable to punishment. Which also the Master making clear said: the one who is
angry with his brother without cause will be liable to the judgment. And the
old law punishes the one who killed in vain, but the commandment of grace even
brings the one who is angry in vain under punishment, blocking the source of
murder, the wrath, from which bloodshed is accustomed to pour forth. 17 Mt 5,
22 Not all anger is liable to the judgment, but the vain and without-cause is
liable, and the one lesser than this and that has come about neither through
abundance of money nor through envy nor indeed through virtue or benefit of
soul is worthy of pardon, and the other that has come about even against those
who neglect virtue is worthy not only of pardon, but also of praise and honor
as much as possible. 18 Mt 5, 22 He calls council here that of the saints. And
the one who has said raca to his brother has not sinned against one, but
against the whole common body. Therefore he is also liable to the council as
having despised this. 19 Mt 5, 39 He calls evil here the one who unjustly
strikes someone and abuses him. And the one who resists and fights back seems
to imitate the evil one, that is, the one who struck him. If then it is bad to
grieve in return the one who grieved, it would be good not to do this, but in
every way to tend the anger and to exhort; for he will be ashamed and will
become good instead of bad. Mt 5, 39 To stand against the evil one, he says,
i.e. to contend, to act like that one and to grieve in return and to strike in
return. One must not then, he says, do likewise to the evil one. 21 Mt 5, 45
For this reason he has called us brothers though we are his slaves and made us
joint-heirs


as having become imitators of him who rains on righteous and sinners and makes
the sun rise likewise on all. 22 Mt 6, 3 4 Let not, he says, the unreason in
us know the work of the rational soul, lest it hinder it and the thing that
saves the according-to-image remain incomplete. And he will repay you in the
open, he says, in the resurrection clearly, when not only these things, but
also all the hidden things will be made manifest. 23 Mt 6, 17 18 Since being
anointed and washing was practiced among those who rejoice and are glad
according to some ancient custom and these were thought symbols of joy and
gladness, the Master, commanding us to accomplish the fast with joy and
gladness, anoint your head, he says, with oil and wash the face, legislating
from the symbols of joy and gladness that we rejoice and be glad in fasting;
for thus disposing ourselves we will both be able to escape the notice of the
others, that we are fasting, and we will make the contest of the fast
unburdensome for ourselves; for where there is eagerness and joy, there even
the toilsome is light; anoint then your head and wash your face, i.e.:
brighten yourself and prepare yourself cheerful from the good cheer and
exultation in the heart and simply in every way practice to escape notice, in
order that you may have as rewarder the overseer of hidden things. Both these,
being anointed and washing, belonged to those who rejoice. Therefore Christ
also commands the fast to be pursued with joy and gladness, so that we must
rejoice and be glad when we work the good things. 24 Mt 6, 21 23 Having said
"for where the treasure is, there also is your heart," he explains the harm
from this. And heart here he calls the governing part, as also the prophet
David "create a clean heart in me, O God." He says then that those who store
up on earth have the mind also nailed and bound there. And this being disposed
in the rank of a captive and prisoner, what then will there be for the whole
body or even for the soul itself except to have been enslaved together and
bound even more? For when the helmsman is sinking, what then will the ship and
those sailing in it suffer? For this he made clear in saying "the lamp of the
body is the eye. If then the light that is in you is darkness, how great the
darkness." And he uses the example fittingly; for as the eye in a body, so
mind in a soul. If then the mind, through which both the soul and the whole
living being is lit, has been blinded by the passion of love of money, what
would those things not suffer which were illumined through that one with the
illumination? For this reason one must in every way keep the intellectual of
the soul impassible from love of money, lest when it has been filled with the
passion from there we be unfortunate in deep darkness and miserably distressed
in our life. Heart here the Savior calls the governing mind; for so also David
named it saying: create a clean heart in me, O God. And from this the lovers
of money, having the mind bound to the money, alienate themselves from God. 25
Mt 6, 25 "Do not be anxious for the soul," not because the soul needs food.
For this reason he did not even say what the soul will eat or what it will
drink, but what you will eat and what you will drink, clearly the living being
composed from soul and body; for even if the appetitive powers go forth from
the soul as from some spring and without it it is not possible for the living
being to be fed, still it is not itself that is fed, but that. Therefore our
Lord neither assigned the being fed to it nor completely alienated it, but as
it provides the starting-points of desiring, do not be anxious, he says, for
your soul, and as not this, but the composite, eats and drinks, he added: what
you will eat and what you will drink, but not the soul clearly. And if someone
through the saying "for your soul" wishes this to be shown, that not only on
the works and toils themselves do not display the anxiety about such things,
but do not even allow this to grow in the soul and trouble you into coming to
work, this too is not alien to the gospel exhortation. He did not say: what
will the soul eat or what will it drink; for every soul


being bodiless neither eats nor drinks, but do not be anxious for the soul
what you will eat or what you will drink; for the soul also puts forth the
appetitive power, but not for its own sake, but for the provision of the body;
for this is what is fed. 26 Mt 7, 1 If you judge, you will be judged, he says,
by yourself; for if you judge sinners being a sinner, you bring your own
judgment against yourself. Leave then judging others and judge yourself only,
so that you may not, being judged from others, inherit Gehenna. 27 Mt 7, 2 For
with what judgment you judge, you will be judged, i.e.: in the very condemning
of others you come under judgment, having seized a judge's person, you who
ought, considering the rank of those judged and the multitude of your own
faults, to tremble and fear and to care about these and to be in agony, how
you may make defense, and not, overlooking these, to call others' shortcomings
to account and to condemn these for those things of which they themselves
sometimes do far worse. Whether then someone condemns one who has sinned
greater things or like things or lesser than himself, he condemns himself; for
the one who has condemned one who sinned lesser things, it is quite clear,
when he condemned himself more, himself having sinned greater things and
having added to these also the condemning of others. And the one who judges
one who has sinned greater things has also himself condemned himself, because
it was necessary to wipe out his own and to propitiate the common judge with
contrition of heart and repentance, but he, puffed up against the neighbor,
put on a judge's dignity and was lifted up to judge others, himself also
happening to be one of those judged. Likewise also the one who condemns the
one who has sinned like things issues a common vote against himself <and> the
one being condemned, or rather against himself; for by his own rashness he
even made moderate the faults that were in the other, if that one especially
should bear his drunken outrage philosophically, but for himself he made an
addition of the evils to those before, having taken on also the punishment
from condemning others. What then? Must one not, he says, judge others even if
they are shameless in the most outlandish things, stealing, robbing?
Especially he says you must not even condemn these; for laws and public
judgments punish these and such, and it befits you to share the pain and to
grieve with the condemned, looking in their suffering at your own seeming
evils, and not to trample nor to strike the one lying in the fall. What then?
Are we not even to admonish nor to exhort someone to sober up from the slip?
Yes indeed, if we first correct ourselves especially; for exhortation and
admonition and sympathy is one thing, and judgment and censure and rebuke
another, and the former are useful to all when they happen in season, but to
censure and judge and rebuke belongs only to high priests of God, when having
displayed all the rest they have made the one being treated no better. And the
one entrusted with judicial votes also judges and censures, whomsoever the
acts bring into court, and a father a child for improvement of character; and
masters are not forbidden to censure and judge and rebuke slaves humanely and
kindly. So many differences then arise both from the judgment itself and from
the judging and judged persons: one if the one who judges and rebukes does
this loving and cherishing, and not mocking and hating; a second if he does
this by law and the common just thing of the polity; a third if according to
none of these, but hating and ringing him out he does this; or even in vain,
not even having known the one judged well; or in the censures against that one
he is busy seeming to be someone exact and trying to hide his own shames by
severity and slander about others, as the one who harshly censures others on
these things would not himself be thought caught in the like. And a sixth
difference if being themselves of those judged they attempt to judge others;
for such have neither friendship nor affection for a defense. 28 Mt 7, 5 "Cast
out first the beam from your eye" and the things following he added, wishing
to cut off every pretext for condemning


ready, wishing. For lest someone should say that I have come to judging not
wishing to reproach nor to grieve the brother, but taking thought to cleanse
him of the disease, especially, he says, this is not even yours, who need to
be shepherded; for why, when it is possible to look at your own things without
danger, do you leave these and become a judge of others'? And if it is even
conceded to you to do these things for the sake of correction and care, but
not of hatred and rashness, one must first free oneself from the greater
evils, then having acquired the soul's own eye unbribed and clean, thus apply
it gentle and peaceful to the neighbor's; for a speck would thus be seen, even
the things that before seemed greatest evils. And from this either you even
cast these out wholly, so as no longer to think you still see even these on
your brother's eye, or you will prepare that one, perceiving your friendly
care and the oversight for the beneficial, both more quickly and without pain
to throw it off. Those who stumble in the great things, which are a beam, must
not then sharply see others' small sins, which are a speck, and correct them,
and simply the one who has sinned something must not set another right, before
he puts off the evil; for such a one is also a hypocrite in addition to being
a sinner. 29 Mt 7, 12 For this is the law. This commandment, he says, is not
new nor recent, to wish to find from the neighbors and to demand those things
which we ourselves also are eager to lay down for them, but this has been
arranged both in the law and in the prophets as being common and known by
nature, this ordinance. For how is it not known to all that the things each
himself thinks good are also good for the neighbor, and the things he reckons
hard for himself are also hard for the neighbor. And the one who wishes to
enjoy the good things from the neighbor must not himself deprive that one of
the like. As the one who is shameless in doing some such thing will not only
pay the penalty of the transgression of the gospel commandments, but has been
condemned both in the laws and in the prophets and in <the> self-taught and
natural conception of humans itself. This law, he says, is not recent nor
needing certain proofs and arguments that it is good, but is as it were a
precept of our nature, that is, as many things as we are eager both to suffer
from others and to demand from them, these we will be more eager to work
toward the neighbors; for this ordinance, he says, is common upon all; for as
many things as happen to be hard for you yourself, think these also such for
the others; and as many as are good and beneficial, these also such for the
others. 30 Mt 7, 13 14 Neither narrow nor afflicted is the gate that has now
come to be, but by nature broad and spacious, and by our law and choice it is
both narrow and afflicted, as the Lord says. 31 Mt 7, 13 14 The gate is narrow
not by nature nor in itself, but by contrast with the road that leads to
destruction; therefore the Lord also added "enter through the narrow gate":
because broad is the road that leads away to destruction, as if saying that I
called it narrow by contrast with the road that leads up to destruction; for
this is broad and spacious, because many are the roads of destruction, but one
of salvation. And not only by contrast with this is that one narrow, he says,
but also from the choice of those who travel them the one is thought narrow,
the other broad; for many walk the one of destruction, and many, he says, are
those who come through it; but few go through the gate of the kingdom; for
few, he says, are those who find it out by works; as those who have found it
by works will experience not a narrow nor afflicted one, but one that procures
the enjoyment of unspeakable goods and of all gladness. And "narrow the gate
and afflicted the road" is not of one denying and defining even the nature of
the gate, but as it were of one indignant and reproaching the fewness of those
who enter through the gate, through which fewness it has also come about that
it is thought narrow. In this way then


when we take it that the gate and the road that leads away to eternal life
have been called narrow, neither will "my yoke is kind and my burden is light"
be opposed to us; for there through these the nature of the thing is shown,
but here the neglect of those who ought to pursue it is reproached. 32 Mt 7,
22 23 Those who drive out demons and chase away diseases and do the like signs
have received such a gift from God. And from this they ought also to have
acquired virtues corresponding to the charisms. But since having received
charisms they did not keep themselves, but did the base things, it was said to
them: depart from me, I do not know you. For God loves in return and knows
those who know him and love him. 33 Mt 7, 23 Then, when? When those will bring
forward the things that seem just, the prophesying, the casting out many
demons, the accomplishing many powers; for from their own justifications they
rather draw the condemnation upon themselves; for those who, having enjoyed so
many charisms, ought to have been eager to acquire a corresponding life, in
order both to glorify the giver through the works and to show themselves
worthy of the gift, but they have done the opposite and have become causes of
the name of God being blasphemed (for why is the name of God blasphemed among
the nations through you), and have shown themselves unworthy of the benefits,
these, when they ought to cover themselves and be downcast, because they
insulted the Master's gift, even take pride in it and think it fit to bring
these things forward as justification: how do they not show <themselves>
liable to countless evils? Therefore the Master also says sharply to them: I
never knew you; depart from me, you who work lawlessness; for I do not now, he
says, turn away from you and abhor you, but I did not even love you at that
time, in which enjoying the charism you repaid me with ingratitude through the
wretchedness of life. But I postponed the punishment to the present time,
awaiting your turning. But since you did not repent through the whole time of
your life, now depart from me. 34 Mt 7, 24 26 It is to be noted that becoming
prudent is not without the cooperation and inspiration of God. Therefore he
says: I will liken him to a prudent man, i.e. I will help and cooperate so
that the things built by him may not fall through nor be destroyed. But upon
the foolish man no longer "I will liken," but "he will be likened," as he
himself will be cause to himself of the folly and punishment, and because the
one who pursues virtue, <indignant> at obtaining the kingdom given him by God,
himself through vice, going after it, makes himself inside the punishment.
Thus then the acquisition of the virtues is accomplished both by our eagerness
and by the grace of God, but the practice of vice comes on by wretchedness of
mind and turning aside of choice. 35 Mt 8, 1 3 Matthew then, speaking of
Christ's descent from the mountain, brings forward the leper, but Luke says
this one approached him in one of the cities, which is not of conflict, but of
truth; for the one says that as he came down from the mountain many crowds
followed, signifying his journey into the city, which Luke alone wrote. And
great is the understanding of the leper; for he did not even say, if you pray,
but if you will. For this reason he did not even say simply be cleansed, but I
will, be cleansed. That is, I grant you the cleansing upon your word; for your
voice showed the faith of the soul hot and clean, which opened for you the
spring of healing from me. I will, be cleansed: you have the healing ready,
because you have approached having a firm mind. 36 Mt 9, 5 6 Easier, said our
Lord Jesus Christ, to do forgiveness of sins than to bind up a paralyzed body,
not saying this simply; for it is clear that the healing of the body is more
easy than the therapy of the soul. He does not then say this simply, but
toward the practice of the Jews; for according to the law their high priests,
receiving gifts and sacrifices brought to them by those who had sinned,
promised the sins to be forgiven


to them, the sins for which the sacrifice, and seemed to accomplish it, but a
paralyzed man they were in no way strong enough to heal thoroughly. Therefore
it was easier for them to say "your sins are forgiven you" than "take up your
bed and walk." Or also otherwise it is possible to say and to solve, that each
of the two is of the same strength; for both are of the divine power and
beyond humans; except that since the one has the proof at the feet, that is
the healing of the body, and the other has the demonstration of the promise
unclear, reasonably the healing of the soul was said to be easier than that of
the body. And it is possible to say also otherwise: that now the tribe of the
Jews did not accuse Christ as forgiving sins, but as having said "your sins
are forgiven you"; hence one must also put a stop at "easier," but not at "to
say," so that what was said by the Master may be in a question of this sort,
which then is easier, to say: your sins are forgiven you. 37 Mt 9, 13 "I did
not come to call righteous, but sinners" he said, in order to show that I did
not come to call you who justify yourselves, but those who confess they are
sinners and who obey my commands. 38 Mt 9, 14 17 "No one puts a patch of
unfulled cloth" it is consistent to understand as said not about the
disciples, but about those very ones who brought the question, as if he said:
you did not use the question soundly; for even if you were able to imagine
nothing higher, still you ought to have remembered your teacher's voice
proclaiming me bridegroom and not to demand fasts of those who live together
with the bridegroom. And if you had the mind fitted to the grace, I would have
told you the most exact cause of their not needing fasting, that among those
with whom God as man is continuously present, associating and living and
regulating the characters and sanctifying the minds, these do not need
fasting. And otherwise the time does not yet call them to strip for the
contests and prizes over me; but if he is taken from them and the Master is
not with them bodily, and the time of the contests also stands over them, then
they will give themselves both to fasts and to the other hardships over the
