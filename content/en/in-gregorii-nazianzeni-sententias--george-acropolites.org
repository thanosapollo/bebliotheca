* On the Sentences of Gregory Nazianzen
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of the most wise great logothete kyr George Acropolites, interpretation of the
theological sayings, both of that "for even the one, in sedition against
itself, becomes many" and of that "the monad, moved into a dyad, stood as far
as the triad." 1 Many others also of the friends, O most divine and most
reverend head of mine, compelled us to come into discourses and to display
before them the ambitious zeal concerning philosophy, and hence we too have
brought forth discourses as children into the light. But in those discourses
we, being bound by friendship only and cherishing our friend, fulfilled the
things commanded; but where there is a paternal relation and an affection
raised above the others, how should we not be disposed more obediently toward
the things enjoined? Therefore, guided by your spirit, or rather by the common
and divine one, from which all true knowledge has visited the lovers of truth,
since it is permitted we go with courage about the discourses, and we have
determined to make clear to you the solutions fitting to the sayings of
Gregory the theologian, as you yourself deemed worthy.

2 There are two aporias, upon two sayings: that saying "the monad, moved into
a dyad, stood as far as the triad," and that saying "there is a time when even
the one, in sedition against itself, becomes many." But the aporia first
named, needing some little consolation, as we shall say later, we shall make
clear in few words; the other needs much and varied and, as I think, loftier
clarification. Concerning these I conversed even while still a youth with that
divinely inspired and most philosophical man Blemmydes, when I frequented him,
but he had nothing to say to me clearly; but only such things as the others
too have said who interpret the words of the Father (I mean the great in
discourses Maximus and those after him), making them clear at length or
leisurely; those very things he himself uttered toward the aporia. But since,
having myself touched the orgies of philosophy, I kept company with the most
divine Plato and with the Muse-seized Proclus, and moreover with the most
inspired men Iamblichus and Plotinus and the rest, whom it is not seasonable
to enumerate, I journeyed to the discernment of the saying. Therefore I was
led on also to make this clear, taking courage from your prayers; and may you
be gracious to us who have dared to leap beyond the things pondered, we who,
holding the ship of life in civic affairs, so untrainedly busy ourselves with
things divine and are willing to examine the things concerning us, which are
permitted to those alone who have been born above matter and have made their
dwelling beyond pleasures. But we are not so; but only using obedience we
fulfill the things commanded.

3 Now then we must begin the clarification of the sayings, and first of that
of the one saying "the monad, having from the beginning been moved into a
dyad, stood as far as the triad." To some it would seem that the one
theologizing such things speaks not reasonably, first in those things in which
he says the monad was moved into a dyad. For one might easily ask him: "Do you
then say that the monad was before in stasis, and then, having taken upon it
some kinetic power contrary to the first, so to speak static, has thus been
moved into a dyad and thereafter into a triad, and from there, having received
another power, stood? Or, being a monad and being moved continuously into a
dyad and walking as far as a triad, as with some mid-space occurring in the
dyad, has it found stasis in itself, the monad having the kinetic and the
triad, that I may say it, the static? But in both sayings certain absurdities
follow. For if there is some beginning of the monadic motion, the divine has
had a beginning, but it has also undergone an affection, the motion, and it
has had an unwilling stasis, and there has appeared to us a god as in a drama,
having taken a beginning and shared in affection and being stopped unwillingly
in the forward rush. What would be more ridiculous than this? But if, the
monad moving boundlessly, some power came upon it from outside, bridling it of
its impulse and leading it into division and number and hindering the forward
rush of the monadic, again there was found, as on a stage, another god, beheld
in the monad moving boundlessly and first and so to speak not halting the
monadic flow, at once contrary and stronger. But this too is most absurd, that
there stand two first and contrary gods."

4 That there is a god has been confessed by nearly all, I mean by the Hellenes
and the barbarians; let Epicurus be sent away from the discourse, and any
other atheist, believing in the spontaneous and perhaps some other nonsense.
But that there is a god all know; a proof that he is god neither has anyone
been able nor would he be able to make. For proofs show the particular things
from the universal, and one would never say or frame anything more universal
than the first being. For that would in every case be the first which was
first and as far as one could proceed; for where one stood and set it as
unhypothetical, there he would find the being in the first sense. For this
reason a proof constructing that there is a god is not easy to discover. But
some consolation from a method, so to speak, of induction the theologizers
have found and handed down, from the well-ordered motion of the whole and the
spherical circuit of the stars established in it, both those having their own
motion and those borne around within the vault of heaven, and they used other
such things for the construction, and not less also took the living harmony in
us into the arguments. But let us, leaving such things, walk some other way,
shorter as I think and more compelling, and demonstrate, only not with
geometrical proofs, that there is a god.

5 We must begin then here, making the things of the proof from common and
confessed things or intelligibles. There is substance, and no one seeing or in
any way perceiving will contradict; for stone and wood and horse and man
subsist together in some substance. In it also is sensation, and this everyone
will confess; for the animal touches and sees and hears and smells. There is
then substance, and above it a superior power, sensation. There is also the
knowing phantastically in some of the perceiving animals, through which those
sharing in the gnat-like faintly display it, as spiders, bees, ants; from this
memory too arises in some, as in dogs and horses and other such. There is then
together with the things said, substance and sensation, the phantastic power,
much superior to the things said. But above all these there is also a better
cognitive power, I mean that of the noetic soul, which begets also the
indwelling reason and composes the uttered one and makes arts, being by much
the superior of the things said before; for it bears in itself also the
inferior powers more excellently than in those in which they alone have grown.
Since then this is so and is confessed, it is altogether necessary that
something also be that which is superexistent, from which these composite
things too. For if a house would not come to be without a craftsman, being
fitted together from stones and woods and lime, nor would a ship ever be built
without some shipwright, how could all things here, rhythmized with surpassing
beauty into the various combinations, come to be without some maker and poet?
And since these things had a temporal beginning, it is necessary that that be
timeless; and since these things suffer corruption, that is altogether
timeless and incorruptible, being anticipative of the things that come after
it and from it, and the bringer-forth of the powers in which these partake.
For if there is here being, there is some pre-being; and if there is
sensation, there is some pre-sensing or rather hyper-sensing; and if there is
here intellect, there is some pre-intelligizing or even hyper-intelligizing.
For these reasons there is a god; for so the being in the first sense has been
named by all. Already then it has been demonstrated to us also most simply
and, one may say, sensibly that there is a god; for nothing would ever come to
be not having some beginning from which it would be substantiated and partake
of power.

6 Since then these things have been well said to us, we must go to the aporia
and dissolve it. We do not say motion, in the strict sense, in the divine
things, nor yet stasis; these things befit the things among us and fall under
sensation. For of motion, called sixfold, according to generation and
corruption, according to increase and diminution, and according to alteration
and the passage from place to place, we find none of these befitting the
pre-eternal being. For the divine things are far from generation and from the
corruption following it; nor in any way does increase increase the divine
things, nor do they suffer diminution, nor yet are they altered, for these
things are in every way unalterable, nor do they change from place to place,
for they are established above place as also above time. But the transitional
knowledge, according to which we first knew the being and then the things from
it, this we call motion; and the not passing forward we have called stasis.
For having conceived the first as a monad, then indeed having understood it
not barren, we have been moved, so to speak by this imagination, into a dyad.
Proceeding from there into a triad we stood, following the doctrines about
these things, not moving further with our thinkings the things unmoved. For
the divine alone is unmoved, and all things are moved by it; for everything
moved is moved by the willing to be moved, and if this is moved by something
moving, again it must be moved by another, and we would not stand infinitely
positing these things unless we came to something unmoved, which moving is not
moved. And this is by nature the first being, since nature, moving bodies, is
moved by another; for soul moves nature, and intellect soul, and the superior
powers among the Hellenes intellect, and those the first cause and that
unutterable and all-working power.

7 Since then we have clarified, as far as was needful, the things said about
motion and stasis in the divine nature, we must go to the other aporia, the
one saying "that there is a time when even the one, in sedition, becomes
many"; and from there, through the solution of the saying, may it become known
to us how the monad stood not as far as the dyad, nor yet went on further to
the triad. And first we must aporize thus: if there is something strictly one,
not composed from something or from somethings into some unity-like appearance
but having its substance in the one, how, remaining one as it is, would it
seditize? And not remaining one, but numbered into two or three, how would it
remain unseditious and preserve its own form forever? For the dyad prior to
the triad is a cause of stasis. The saying seems then a riddle and
enigmatical, as I think, if that numbered into two or even three will not
seditize, but that which happens to be strictly one should accept stasis,
which both preserves unseditious and saves the things fallen into number
through their participation in it, as long as they partake of it; but when
these stand out of it and seditize, they suffer corruption and the turning
away from the one.

8 There is then a first being, as has been made precise by all theologizers
and as was previously clarified to us. By all I mean Chaldaeans, Egyptians,
Indians, the Hellenes themselves, in short whoever have touched theology in
any way and have wished to discourse about god: this being and making cause
has been confessed by them. All things at any rate are from it, both the
immaterial forms and the material ones. And the immaterial stood nearest to
the same nature, even if according to the making reason as far as farthest of
all; but the things partaking of matter seem somehow to have been lodged apart
from it, having stood outside the formative simplicity and being molded by
composition; for they came to be from matter and form. For if one, beginning
from the first being to examine the natures of beings, should go down to the
things below, he will come to another simpler being, matter. By matter I mean
not that which lies subject immediately beneath the forms (for this, partaking
of form, has been named form-molded matter), but the so-called pure matter,
which has also some faint impress of entity. For the first being, happening to
be of self-form and boasting a making power of the forms, that which lies
subject over against it would be strictly named pure matter, most of all
turning out to be non-being rather than being. But the being in the first
sense bestows on this too, through the overflowing of the substance-making
power, some faint impress of entity. For the "self" fits most of all to the
form; for we say self-triangle and self-man when we behold these outside
matter.

9 This matter then the natural philosophers hypothesize to be substance not
less than the form, as receptive of all forms, and they named it therefore
all-receiving, and again maenad, as casting off forms and receiving others,
and other such things. And so, being characterized more in not-being than in
being, it has images and faint impressions of the first being, from which is
every substance both in energy and in power. And this reasonably; for things
lying diametrically over against one another project certain phantasms of
likeness. The first being is established incomprehensible to intellect;
incomprehensible the last. But something of that one, if anything at all is
comprehensible, is comprehended by its proper reasoning; for it is being. But
this last is spurious according to the philosopher from Stagira; for the first
is defined by negation as not-being because it attains to nothing of the
things that are, for it is a making cause and better than they. The last too
is defined by negation because it attains to nothing of the things that are,
but is also worse than they. Simplest the first, simplest also the last, even
if it is variegated by different forms. Because of this even some of those who
theologized among the Hellenes imagined matter co-beginning with the maker,
holding a bad and unworthy notion of his aforesaid principle. For the
entity-making and all-working power makes even matter, being known more as
not-being than being, partake of being and lie as substratum to the things
that are and become. For, taking on quantity, it constructed magnitude; and,
taking on quality, it completed a natural body.

10 They say this matter, being simplest in its completion, seditizes toward
itself because it loves to leap from form to form and is not more friendly
toward this than toward that. But it is altogether unmoved and without energy,
lying over against the first being; for that alone acting, this alone is acted
upon, and that alone moving, this alone is moved. Thus then, this being laid
as substratum and being affected immediately by quantities and qualities and
receiving hypostasis, the things perfected by it are shown to be altogether
unmoved; but when the life from there and the first lot fell upon it, it
received motion, or rather sensation and the energy according to it, and an
animal was ordered, sensing. But when intellect also frequented it, the
rational and noetic animal came to be, through which arts too have been made,
certain images of the first substance and the making nature. It is necessary
then that these things be in the first being beforehand and strictly and not
by participation. For there are life there and intellect, in order that by
life the being remain everlasting, and by intellect make and hold together the
things that are.

11 It is gathered then from the things said that the being in the first sense
ought to be ensouled and in-intellect, from which is every substance and life
and intellect, named more by participation than by form. For that is the
self-living or self-life and self-intellect, but everything beside it is more
not-being than being, since it was when the being here was not in the
coherence of it, and it remains being and lives and intelligizes. For it
partook of life as much as that one provided, and it intelligizes as much as
that one imparted; and life and intellect in the being stand not as in a
subject. Thus then also through these things the being in the first sense,
happening to live and intelligize, remains unseditious. For how would
substance seditize toward its own life which it brought forth? Or how would
that toward this, or intellect toward its own substance and life, or this
toward these?

12 From here the one being, both simplest and united, is three, and because of
this also unseditious, having a wondrous union and more wondrous division, or
unutterable this one and more unutterable that one. And that we may set forth
the things said also from more divine scriptures, that there is the One Who
Is, the Father, from whom the life and the intellect: witness the one who,
being asked by Moses what his name would be, said "I am the One Who Is." But
the Son is Life, proceeding hypostatically from the Being and returning to
him; and he testifies in the gospels, saying "I am the Life." But the Spirit
is Intellect, proceeding also from the Being as from a fount-like substance
and being shown substantially through the life and remaining in it and
returning through it to the one from whom, in order that these be both one and
three. This, the intellect of the whole, the Hellenes named, receiving from it
some faint knowledge. For this is the one adorning all things and spread wide
through all things with the making power, the substance-making and life-making
one, being a certain tenuity of the whole nature; which one of those who
theologized the things divine and ours also called the scent of the deity.

13 Now it has been demonstrated with probable arguments that the first being
must be three, one being and unseditious, because none of the aforesaid
seditizes in anything, these being preservative of one another and causes of
permanence and fellow-causes with the making cause; matter not having this
unseditiousness, being itself simplest and standing according to the arguments
we have said. But this seditizes because it does not partake of life and
intellect, being pure matter and standing far from the things that give form.

14 Thus the aporia of the theologian Father is solved for us, how the one can
seditize and how it has remained unseditious. But from here a certain
conclusion also was found for us from the things said: that man, condemned by
the transgression of the commandment to death, would by nature be able to take
from none other of the three that by which to be saved and led back up to the
first dignity, unless the first life, or rather the self-life. For since he
was condemned not to the vanishing of his substance nor to the complete
corruption of his intellect, but underwent the fall from immortality, it was
needful that the one who died bear the first life, if somehow he be
trans-elemented toward the immortal.

15 But perchance from here also appears the demonic race, not being by nature
able to undertake the return to the first order. For how, if this were
according to nature, would it not have been accomplished by the good maker,
who foresees to the utmost his own creations, and moves with tenderness of
heart toward the good of each and its completion? But even in these things
neither has their substance been corrupted so as to slide into not-being nor
yet their life; for they are and live, even if not a blessed life. But their
intellect has been upset and corrupted and, so to speak, advanced into some
other form; for it has not suffered alteration but some other form-giving; for
the healing from intellect had to come to be for them. But this was of the
first intellect, and for intellect to receive intellect and correct itself was
impossible; and besides, how could the partless have run together with the
partless into union? For these reasons, as I think, the nature of the demons
has remained unreceived by the first intellect, and because of this also
uncorrected. Let this also be to you as some other conclusion, appearing from
the things said.

16 Behold, the things wondered at have been clarified for you as was possible
for us: both that "the monad, moved into a dyad, stood as far as the triad"
and that "there is a time when even the one, in sedition against itself,
becomes many." For matter, as we demonstrated, being one and altogether
simplest, seditizes toward itself in the changes of the forms; but the one
such as we have said remains altogether unseditious and indissoluble, raised
above every union and division. For it is not one, but above the one, and
because of this three; nor does it fall under any division, for it is above
division, and because of this one. For the union is above union of this one
and the division above division; which one would more excellently name the
hyperunited and the hyperdivided. For from it are every union and every
division, as things partaken from partakables, as derived from substance and
substantiated through the infinite nature of this one, the fruitful and the
substance-making power, the life-making energy and the intellect-making
completion.
