SECOND HOMILY OF THE SAME JOHN THE PRESBYTER

1. What we discussed in the preceding for the refutation of the godless error
of the Manichaeans would have sufficed. But lest anyone, being pierced by
other thorns of impiety, should have his mind wounded, I will not hesitate to
mention other inquiries as well, showing both what evil is and whence it is;
for using such inquiries they attempt to turn aside the simpler. First then
one must distinguish concerning the good and the beautiful, what each of these
signifies; for the beautiful signifies one thing, and the good another. And
beautiful is that which is well-fitted by proportion of parts and is without
lack toward that for which it has been generated by the one who created it.
Wherefore it has been written: "Behold, very beautiful," concerning the
substance of the creatures; for God has made all things beautiful, both
sensible and intelligible. For concerning all things he adds, saying: "And God
saw all things that he had made, and behold, they were very beautiful,"
including with the sensible things the making of the incorporeals. For after
the creation of man, which consists of intellectual soul and body, Moses says
this.

2. But the good is twofold in signification. And in the case of God the good
happens to be substance; for God, being ungenerated, has all things that he
possesses substantially, and nothing later comes upon him which was not always
in him. For this reason, since God was without shape, he will always be
without shape; and since he was without quality, he will always be without
quality; and since he is almighty, he was always almighty; and since he is
creator, he always exists as creator, even if the things created and ruled by
him should not be. For God has substantially all things that he is also named,
and he does not have what he is relatively. For relations are about
[substance], not being substances. God therefore is unrelated; for it is not
possible for him to have any relation toward any of the generated things. For
the divine is self-sufficient and completing of itself, having its being in no
relation toward the things outside, lest we be compelled to say that something
of the things accomplished by him is completing of his power.

3. And also in the case of all things, not in the case of God only, the same
argument. For the powers that complete the substances do not have their being
in the relation toward the things outside, but they inhere in the things
unrelatedly, being contemplated in themselves. Consider the argument thus: the
visual power in the eye or the light in the sun is completing of the substance
of the eye and of the sun. And if someone should suppose that none of the
things contemplated by the eye exists, or of the things outside that partake
of the light-like power of the sun, neither the sun nor the eye will in truth
be supposed incomplete according to substance by those who judge rightly. Thus
then, even if the example is dim, before the world was created God is by
nature and unrelatedly creator, not becoming maker by the relation toward the
things outside. For this is nothing other than to insult God and to give God
an acquired relation, such as those advancing from unbelief toward knowledge
have toward one another, and to equalize with the things that inhere
substantially | the things that have later come upon them.

4. And matter then, as I said (for one must take up again from where the
argument departed), if it was ungenerated, being without shape and without
quality, how then will it be created, being ungenerated and not able to take
on what was not always in it? But if they say that matter has shape and
quality and quantity, it is not ungenerated, as has been demonstrated, but
rather generated from things that are not, brought forth by God together with
the shapes and qualities. For quality and quantity are proper to generated
things.

5. What I said, then: the good in the case of God happens to be substance. But
in the case of generated things it is a certain energy coming to be about the
outside, and it is an accident, accomplished through action or through
intelligible contemplation and attention. And through action men, living with
flesh, accomplish the good, but the intellectual powers through contemplation
and attention. Since therefore the good is an accident in the case of
generated things, in these the being altogether also precedes being good; for
the underlying thing must be, in order that thus the accident may be
contemplated in it, whether it be separable or inseparable. But in the case of
the ungenerated God, God always being, he also always is good; for he does not
have being as one thing and goodness as something else, but by the very being
he has the goodness. Through the things said, then, it is clear to all that
the evil is not contrary to God, who is good as substance; for nothing at all
is opposed to substance. But to the good according to accident, which is
contemplated in generated things, the evil is opposed; for coming to be
through a base action it is opposed to that which comes to be through a good
action.

6. These things having been thus demonstrated, how are the Manichaeans not to
be abhorred who say: There was God and matter, good and evil, extremely
contrary in all things, so as to share with one another in nothing, both
ungenerated and living. One must say then to them: How is your argument not
monstrous? For if according to your babblings God and matter are extremely
contrary, so that the one shares nothing with the other, you declaring as it
happens according to what seems good to you, how will they be contrary to one
another at all, sharing according to nothing? For it is proper to contraries
to share with one another according to the genus, as black and white share in
that each is a color, and again hot and cold in that each is a quality, which
you are not willing to grant in the case of the matter mythologized by you and
of God, saying mindlessly both that they are contrary and that the one shares
nothing with the other. Besides, the contraries are destructive of one another
and cannot according to energy come about together in the same thing according
to the same under the same time. How then in the same creature, as in the
burning and illuminating fire or the nourishing and drowning water, according
to you God and matter exist together according to energy, through the whole
according to the same, as you yourselves mythologize, saying that things that
happen to be in no way contrary are contrary?

7. Besides, the contraries are either immediate or mediate. The immediate do
not admit mixture, but rather are destructive of one another, as order and
disorder, even number and odd. But the mediate have also a middle other than
the extremes, as black and white; and of these the grey is the middle. If then
matter and God are immediate, how do they admit mixture and complete the world
according to your mythology? For the immediate are not by nature mixed. But if
they shall say they are mediate, there will be something through the middle of
these other than these, and then a third ungenerated will be numbered with the
two.

8. But perhaps they will say: and how is it possible for something to have
come to be from things that are not? For nothing is seen coming to be from
non-beings. To whom one may say: You err, not knowing the power of God, but
looking only at the things that come to be from men you have supposed the
divine to be such. For a man works from things that are, shaping woods and
stones as he wishes and putting into them through his own art the shape that
was not in them at some time; for man is creator of shapes only, putting the
shape from things that are not through art into the thing created. And nature
likewise works from things that are, but the work fashioned through nature has
a greater working than the art of man. For nature is not creator of shapes
only, working mixture and composition of the elements, but it also brings to
subsistence the different substances and the forms of the things, which were
not before. For from elements it works the form of bone and of flesh, of veins
and of nerves, and the countless kinds of plants, and the varied differences
of colors and of juices and of metals.

9. If then nature so far surpasses the art of men, how is it not necessary
that the creation of God also have incomparably surpassed even the working of
nature? Therefore if already nature brings forth the forms that are not, yet
it creates these in an underlying thing that pre-exists, because it is unable
otherwise to display the creative power to them without some pre-underlying
thing, it is entirely necessary, if indeed God also creates and brings the
things that are into being, that his creation have something more than nature;
but it would have nothing more, if it were not able to bring the things into
being from that which is nowhere and in no way, but he too should need, as the
craftsman man and nature, some pre-existing underlying thing, in which he will
display his own creative energy, and he would not even so far surpass nature
as nature surpasses art, being creator not of shapes only according to the art
of men, but also of different substances.

10. Besides, it is full of all absurdity to make the demonstrations of the
whole through the particular things and those that come to be in each case.
For if looking at the particular created things they say: "6Since no creator
makes without matter, for this reason God also will not make without matter"6,
let them know that the matter underlying every craftsman underlies having been
created from a prior providence or art, as the woods and iron and stones,
underlying craftsmen men toward being shaped only, have sprung from a
technical | nature from earth, and nature also, creating from things that are,
creates having found from some providence a pre-existing matter. If then the
matter underlies even him who creates the whole, God, let them say by what art
this was previously brought to subsistence, and thus their cleverness will be
refuted; for they will altogether say that some such divine art previously
brought it to subsistence. But indeed I will also refute from the things
confessed among them their strong argument, as they think, that one must not
make the demonstration of the whole from the particulars; for they pretend to
embrace astronomy and they suppose a motion of the all. If then they make the
demonstration of the whole from the particulars and say: Since each of the
particulars is seen coming to be from things that are, thus also the all must
have come to be by God from something that was before, it is reasonable for
them to suppose that what follows for the particulars also happens to the all.
And since each of the particulars is moved and the all also is moved,
therefore as the particular is moved the all also will be moved. But the
motion of these is not similar; for the particulars change place entirely, but
the whole does not remove from its own place, but goes around about its own
center, since there is not another region into which it might ever remove.

11. And they too, attempting to regulate the generation of the all from the
particulars in relation to one another, and saying that as the particulars
come to be from things that are, so also the all has come to be from things
that are, one must say the same things that the argument has gone through
toward the refutation of the Manichaeans. And one must add that the things of
the particulars must also be contemplated in the case of the whole; since the
particulars are contemplated in generation and corruption, therefore the whole
also will be subject to generation and corruption, and the world will no
longer be ungenerated and incorruptible according to the wise of the Hellenes,
but generated and at the same time corruptible; which things they do not
endure to confess. And by these arguments it has been demonstrated that matter
is not ungenerated.

12. And one must know that neither will art operate without nature and God,
nor nature without God; for it is not lawful for the second things to do what
they do otherwise than with the things that lie above, art with nature and God
(for without God not even being moved belongs to men), and nature with God.
For even if some of the products come to be through certain intermediates, yet
it is clear that the cause of these also is that which supplies even to the
intermediate causes the power of being causes. And God has given to art and to
nature the power of being causes. God therefore is first cause of the things
that are, and as many things as he brings immediately into being he creates
without matter from things that are not, at the same time also putting nature
into the things that have come to be toward continuance and the sequence of
the things that will be next from them, but as many as through intermediate
art or nature, these he works from things that are.

13. But he says: If the world has come to be from things that are not, it will
again be dissolved into that which in no way is. For the things that come to
be from things that are not are dissolved into non-being, as the shapes put by
the craftsman upon the things created and the enmattered forms brought forth
by nature; for these, not being before, but having come to be again, will go
into non-existence, having been dissolved in being separated from the matter.
| Thus then, if the world has come to be from things that are not, it will
again go into that which in no way is, having undergone corruption. But those
who put forward these things do not know that in the case of the things that
come to be by nature or by the art of men this argument is true, but in the
case of those that have received being immediately from God it is not true;
for of those whose generation is above nature and art, having been constituted
by the infinite power of God, of these also the continuance will receive that
which is above nature, being held together from God through all. Thus also the
intellectual powers will not be dissolved into not-being, even these having
been created from things that are not by the God who always is. Thus also the
world; and even matter itself, having come to be immediately from God, will
never go into that which nowhere and in no way <is>; for we look for new
heavens and a new earth; but indeed also man, even he having received
subsistence according to the flesh from the pre-underlying matter of the
earth, since he has been generated immediately of God who is sufficient of
himself; for taking dust from the earth God fashioned man, not having
permitted nature to create as in the case of the generation of the others,
saying "Let the earth bring forth a living soul" and "Let the waters bring
forth," but himself working through himself the whole man both with hands and
with inbreathing, man will not have the alteration ever into not-being, but he
will be altered toward the better. For this corruptible must put on
incorruption. And that the matter which the Manichaeans say is also evil by
nature is not ungenerated, I think the things said suffice.

14. From here then remaining there arises what is rumored among the godless:
If nothing is outside the creation of God, and God is good, clearly the things
that have come to be through him are good. Whence then the evils? If, having
supposed evil to be a substance, they inquire this, the question is full of
folly. For evil has not come to subsistence; for it is a certain energy. And
every energy has its being in the coming-to-be, neither being before the
motion or action of the one who operates, nor remaining after the motion. And
only the thing completed often leaves behind a memory of the action. It is
therefore either an action or an energy; and every action is an action of some
substance and without substance it is not. How is that a substance which in no
way has come to subsistence by itself, but needs another for being? Since
therefore evil is not a substance, as has been demonstrated, even if the
Manichaeans, being hostile toward the truth, should contend, one must
distinguish concerning this thus.

15. Evil has a twofold signification; for it signifies at one time the
affliction, at another the sin, and properly evil is the sin, but by
catachresis the affliction is named evil. For the affliction is not altogether
evil; and often it even becomes a cause of salvation, either some natural
disease having come upon us or also an afflicting accident from outside having
happened to us. But if these were properly evils and not rather educative
things devised from the providence of God toward the chastening of men, they
would not become a cause of some good. But now often even the expectation of
these both constrains even those who are very prone toward sin | and makes
them fear God and ward off the outrage from these, <and> stirs us to
supplicate God, whether the thing expected be a wild beast or a creeping thing
or war or plague or famine or some weakness, or slander and a plot devised
from a base choice of men, which is properly evil to the one who does it,
being sin, but to the one who suffers it not properly evil, yet afflicting;
and the afflicting things are not altogether evils, but even for those who are
brave according to God they become causes of greatest good repute among men,
and they treasure up for these benefactions and eternal recompenses with God.

16. Let no one then be deceived, naming the afflicting things properly evils.
For if the afflicting is evil and not rather educative and calling forth
toward justice and every virtue, let no one strike the son who sins, let no
one praise the one entrusted with some rule who cuts down a robber with the
sword or who confirms by a judicial vote that he be given over to beasts for
food, let no one praise the physician who with iron cuts away a putrefied
affection; for he afflicts the one being healed. Let no one permit theriacs to
be composed from vipers, if indeed the creeping things are evils, as the
Manichaeans say. But those who rail at these things without experience do not
know that beautiful is everything which is without lack toward that for which
it has been created by God. But if, as has been demonstrated, these have been
devised toward our chastening and are by the providence of God, how are these
not very beautiful, even if some of them do not happen to be substances, but
only supervene from natural weakness.

17. And of the natural things some are accomplished by necessity of nature.
And I say that these are accomplished by necessity of nature, as many as come
to be automatically and against our will by nature, as dying and the living
thing growing until the appointed age. But some come to be by sequence of
nature, yet it is also possible that they not come to be, as begetting
children through mixing; for this is of the things possible; for childbearing
does not altogether follow upon the mixing. All these things then, as many as
come to be by necessity of nature, are accomplished without sin even among the
men who are governed by reason. But as many as it is possible both to come to
be and not to come to be, in the case of irrational nature they happen to be
not accountable, whatever energy they may have; for they do not have reason by
nature examining the things to be done; but in the case of men, inasmuch as
they are regulated by reason, these are sometimes properly evils, whenever
indeed right reason does not lead the action, having been weakened from our
slackness or even altogether inflamed by the desires of the pleasures. For sin
is the irrational choice and use by the rational of the things that according
to nature are possible both to come to be and not to come to be; and virtue of
man is to be able to do something, and to be stirred by the natural motion,
but if it should be absurd coming to be in such a way or even coming to be at
all, to abstain from the action of this, using reason as a helmsman, and to
trample the bait of pleasure.

18. But they say: Why then | has God not made us incapable of sinning, in
order that we might not even willingly sin? And those who put forward such
things seem to be indignant that God has not made them stones and woods, and
to blame the benefactor because he worked men rational and not rather unmoved
toward the choice of good and of evil. But if they say: We ought to have been
made rational and to know good and evil, but to stand unchangeably in the
action of the good, they seem to me to seek nothing other than that we ought
not to have become men, but to be of the same nature with God; for God alone
is unable toward doing the truly evil. Whence the apostle says, since it is
impossible for God to lie. For vice is a certain weakness contemplated in
generated things; but in the ungenerated what trace of the weakness will there
be? But if we were of the same nature with God, we would no longer be his
created things, being gods by nature, just as that one is God. But since no
one is so mad as to exalt himself and to demand that he himself be God, being
a man (for to think such things is proper to the lawless Manichaeans, who
suppose our souls to be co-eternal with God and like), one must examine the
measure of the rational nature and thus go through the argument concerning
sinning.

19. We say then that for every one of the rational creatures a certain measure
has been appointed toward virtue, and it is and is known as perfect, to which
nothing is lacking of its own nature toward perfection; and to overstep such a
thing is impossible, but to arrive at the appointed measure is complete
virtue. One must not therefore demand of men who are still mortal and
perishable an angelic life, nor indeed of angels to imitate the power of God
which is above all mind and comprehension. For to demand of each what is above
the power of his own power happens to be marks of a deranged reasoning. What
measure then has been appointed for a man pressing toward virtue? Clearly they
will say the accomplishment of temperance, of justice, of courage, and as many
such things as there are. And it was not possible to distinguish these, if God
had not previously sown seeds of prudence in the nature, so that we
distinguish the nature of the contraries; and just as the carpenters with a
cord straighten the crooked of the woods, cutting off the excess and what is
outside the straight line, so also men the difference of contrary actions,
using reason as with a certain plumb-line, cleanse away what is not coming to
be as it ought as rejectable, setting it far from their own work.

20. And this is the argument of prudence <and> through what cause God
impressed this upon the rational souls; but of temperance and of justice and
together also of courage God put in us a power toward accomplishment, not
having taken away the power toward the contraries. For he wishes through the
juxtaposition of the opposed things in authority that men be shown of good
repute. For if the virtues existed by themselves, they would not be known; for
the seemliness of virtue is not known from inability to do the contraries. For
who will praise a stone being temperate, to which the contrary also is not
present toward working? Likewise who will praise a wood not slandering, or a
horse or an ass not lying? And in the case of the others likewise the same
argument is preserved among all. Virtue then is known | from being able to do
also the contraries of the praised actions. How then would the temperate be
known, if licentiousness were not set forth according to authority and he
being able to do this if he should wish? And how the brave, if the things that
frighten were not rising up? And how also just, if greed also does not have
room to come to be from him?

21. Not that vice must of necessity be operative, in order that virtue may be
shown through the juxtaposition, away with the blasphemy, but in order that
the vice may not come to be, the one who acts having it in his authority to
work this. For come, if some truth should be said by someone, was not such a
one able, having hidden this, to publish a falsehood instead of the truth, as
having each of the two in his authority? But if he hid the falsehood through
virtue, not on this account was he unable to do the working of the contrary?
For he had strength to do this, if indeed he wished. For the nature of men is
weak and, being weak, happens to be prone toward falsehood and toward the
other vices, unless right reason, having strengthened it, should beat it
toward the impulse of the better. Behold, what is rumored has also been
demonstrated, both that the gift of self-determination is necessary toward
virtue for men and that the things properly evils exist from choice and
self-determination alone; but the others, as many as are evils, coming to be
from God, are educative of men, training them rather toward virtue. Wherefore
these are not even to be called properly evils; for how will the evil be a
cause of some good?

22. But perhaps they say: Let it be that sin comes to be according to choice
and does not come to be, and that each of the two is of the things possible.
Whence then do the thoughts of the base actions rise up against us, if some
evil power were not suggesting these to us? To whom one may say briefly that
it is proper to the nature of the rational to have knowledge of the things
that have contrariety and difference toward one another, as has already been
demonstrated through many things. For prudence is nothing other than a
rational power, discriminative of the things that are not similarly. It is
necessary then that the mind instantaneously apprehend through thought those
things of which we have received the science toward their distinction. For the
knowledge is necessary of thoroughly knowing the nature of the contraries, and
the things known are examined, the mind moving these in itself through
thought; for the thought of sin is not sown from outside and impressed upon
the mind by the devil, but from the motions in us he brings the trial to us,
using as a fellow-worker toward this the motion of nature.

23. But the motion of the flesh is not sin by itself, as has been
demonstrated; for the flesh is irrational, and nothing that comes to be by an
irrational is sin. But by the conjunction of the soul toward the flesh the sin
of both together comes to be, the worse prevailing over the better, not by
inability of the better, but we putting all zeal about the good condition of
the body, and overlooking the rational and better portion. But if someone
through exercise | of virtue, caring for little of the flesh and as much as is
necessary, assigns the more to the governing reason, this one will steer
through the life without stumbling, as far as is attainable for a man,
regulating the appetites of the flesh toward what is well and well-pleasing to
God; so that the motion of the flesh is not sin, but the irrational action of
both together, whenever the reason that stands over should give over the reins
to spirit and desire, having bowed itself to the irrational pair, having
chosen not to lead according to dignity, but rather to be led.

24. But toward this they say: Why then has God made the flesh to have motion
at all? I, widening the argument for you who inquire concerning this, will
make clear as far as possible what was willed. God has made us rational,
having adorned us with reason as a greatest gift. Wishing this then to be
operative in us, he has made very many of the things that appear to be
contrary toward themselves, just as the night toward the day, and the turnings
of the year toward themselves and the qualities of the elements, in order that
in these a certain exercise of the rational may come to us, we being able to
make the diagnosis of them according to juxtaposition. Thus indeed also
through the bodily creation of the remaining things God created not one form,
but very many differences toward the exercise of reason. For some of the
bodies he adorned with bulk and shape and variety of colors; and others
besides the things said he has made to have being nourished, drawing the
nearby moisture of the earth, and to grow and to bear fruits and to sprout
plants of the same kind. Others again he makes to have gone up above the
nature of these, having added irrational sense, such as the kinds of oysters
and of sponges happen to be and as many such things. He was not content with
so many differences, but he also bestowed as a further honor upon others both
appetitive and locomotive motion, displaying his all-wise power.

25. And of these things said none obtained the better honor not having first
enjoyed the lesser. For it must first be and thus enjoy the next gift and
after this the still better. Since therefore it was necessary also that this
sensible world have been adorned with a rational living thing through the
goodness of God and that a rational nature have been mixed with the sensible
of the things seen, as many as happen to be most beautiful he created in the
bodily organ of the soul; for it is nourished and grows, and it generates and
has sense and both appetitive and locomotive motion, all which the rational
and intellectual in us has been entrusted by the creator to steer through. For
it was absurd that the body obtain the better, I mean the rational soul, not
having first acquired the good lot of the lesser things. For who of those who
think well, not to say indeed God, bestowing the greatest things, would grudge
the | small? You have the solution of the difficulty, through what cause God
has made the flesh to have motion.

26. Since therefore God has thus created man as being a boundary of both
rational and irrational, of both mortal and immortal, upon the soul he
impressed the impression of the beautiful, and he has made it to overlook the
perishable and those that in no way stand, and to reach after the beautiful
things that are eternal and correspondingly have toward the nature of the
soul; for it, happening to be immortal, loves the immortal things. But to the
body he has given to incline toward the earthly things, those that in no way
have stood, but admit now one change now another; for that which has been
created from earth longs for the alteration of the kindred, and as sensitive
it is eager to enjoy the smooth and the things pleasant to sense, and as
perishable it holds to the temporary things.

27. Man then existing as composite from soul and body, both having received a
beginning of being through the all-wise creation of God, is divided toward
each nature, being pulled against toward earth and heaven; for he has
similarly by nature with soul and with body toward each. From here there is no
one of men who, by being rational, does not know the beautiful, whatever it
is; and even if he should wish to wrong another, he himself does not endure to
be wronged, having from here as a refutation the knowledge of the better. For
even if the natural sharpness of the energy of the soul is blunted, it being
yoked with the sluggishness of the body, yet it does not cast off its own
powers completely, but as imparting life to the body it itself does not cease
from living, so also sharing the remaining things with it it does not lose the
experience. But indeed also the inclination toward the worse things, which has
come upon us from body, the difficulty shows, with how much those who wish are
accustomed to accomplish virtue. Each then, both soul and body, has a greatest
power toward that for which it has come to be by nature, neither the
inclination about the worse having ever been able to take away from the soul
the learning and knowledge of the beautiful, nor indeed the knowledge of the
beautiful that is in us having taken away the inclination about the contrary.
For if along with the learning of the beautiful God had also made the whole
inclination toward the beautiful to be in us, the action of the beautiful
would have come to us without exercise and without hesitation, or rather
irrationally. But since in the body there is in us the inclination toward the
contrary, and by the soul we are taught the beautiful, God having crafted this
wisely, in order that the man fashioned from earth, having displayed with
sweat and toil, may also have to be proud in the accomplishments from his own,
being dragged down by the nature of the body toward the pleasant and dear to
the temporary and kindred of the earth, but with the soul following the things
commanded by God, having learned both what is to be done and from what action
one must abstain, he may remain unconquered, with the reason of the soul
constraining the natural | appetites of the body that are possible both to
come to be and not to come to be, or cutting them off; so that for the sake of
exercise God has made the motions of the flesh, in order that <the> man may
not become uncrowned, as having most easily accomplished virtue without anyone
wrestling against. And this shows the vice not ungenerated, nor indeed from a
power opposed to God, but rather a natural motion of the things possible to
come to be and not to come to be, which by itself displays no trace of sin.
But if the soul should be dragged down and, having left its own order, should
irrationally give itself over to the motions of the body, then what has come
to be happens to be sin of a soul serving the decrees of the worse, and as one
living thing the man sinning is punished both in soul and in body.
