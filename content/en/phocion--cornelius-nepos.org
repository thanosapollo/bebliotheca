* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Phocion the Athenian, although he was often set over armies and took the highest
magistracies, yet the integrity of his life is much more known than the labor of
military affairs. Therefore of this there is no memory, but of that a great
fame, from which he was called by the surname Good. For he was perpetually poor,
although he could have been most rich because of frequent honors brought and
highest powers, which were given him by the people. This man, when he repudiated
gifts of great money from king Philip and the ambassadors exhorted him to
receive and at the same time admonished that if he himself easily lacked them,
yet he should look out for his own children, for whom it would be hard in
highest poverty for a father to guard so great a glory, to them he said "if they
will be like me, this same little field will nourish them, which led me to this
dignity; but if they are going to be unlike, I do not wish their luxury to be
nourished and increased at my expenses."

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
The same man, when he had come near to the eightieth year with a prosperous
fortune, in the last times came into a great hatred of his own citizens, first
because with Demades he had agreed concerning handing over the city to Antipater
and by his counsel Demosthenes with the rest, who were thought well merited
concerning the republic, had been expelled into exile by a decree of the people.
Nor in that alone had he offended, that he had badly consulted for the
fatherland, but also that he had not furnished the faith of friendship. For
increased and helped by Demosthenes he had climbed the grade which he held, when
he suborned him against Chares: by the same in judgments, when he pleaded a
capital case, having been defended several times, he had departed freed. This
man he not only did not defend in dangers, but even betrayed. And he fell most
by one charge, that, when with him was the highest command by order of the
people and he was warned by Dercylus that Nicanor, prefect of Cassander, was
ambushing the Piraeus of the Athenians and the same demanded that he provide
lest the city be deprived of supplies, to this man with the people hearing
Phocion denied there was danger and promised he would be a hostage of that
thing. And not so much later Nicanor obtained the Piraeus. To recover which when
the people had run together armed, he not only called no one to arms, but did
not even wish to be set over the armed. Without which Athens cannot be at all.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
There were at that time at Athens two factions, of which one did the cause of
the people, the other of the optimates. In this was Phocion and Demetrius of
Phalerum. Each of these used the patronages of the Macedonians: for the popular
favored Polyperchon, the optimates felt with Cassander. Meanwhile Cassander was
driven from Macedonia by Polyperchon. By which deed the people having been made
superior immediately condemned the leaders of the adversary faction on a capital
charge and drove them from the fatherland, among them Phocion and Demetrius of
Phalerum, and concerning that thing sent ambassadors to Polyperchon, who would
ask from him that he confirm their decrees. To this same place Phocion set out.
Where as he came, he was ordered to plead the case before king Philip in word,
in the thing itself indeed before Polyperchon: for he then was set over the
affairs of the king. This man, accused by Agnon that he had betrayed the Piraeus
to Nicanor, from the judgment of a council was thrown into custody and led down
to Athens, that there a judgment concerning him might be made by the laws.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
Here as it was arrived, when because of age he was no longer strong in the feet
and was carried in a vehicle, great runnings-together were made, when some,
remembering the old fame, pitied the age, but very many were sharpened by anger
because of the suspicion of a betrayal of the Piraeus and most that he had stood
against the goods of the people in old age. Wherefore not even the faculty of
perorating was given him saying the case in the judgment: certain legal things
having been completed he was condemned and handed over to the eleven, to whom
those publicly condemned are accustomed to be handed over for punishment by
Athenian custom. This man, when he was led to death, Euphiletus met him, with
whom he had been familiarly used. He, when weeping he had said "O what unworthy
things you undergo, Phocion!" to this man he said "but not unexpected: for this
end most famous Athenian men have had." In this there was so great a hatred of
the multitude that no one dared to bury him free. Therefore he was buried by
slaves.
