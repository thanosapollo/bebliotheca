* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

It is said that there are kinds of orators, as there are of poets.
That is otherwise, for the other class is manifold. For of poetry
there are the tragic, the comic, the epic, the lyric, and even the
dithyrambic, which has been treated more by the Latins; each has
its own character, different from the rest. And so the comic is a
fault in tragedy, and the tragic is a disgrace in comedy; and in
the others each has its own fixed sound and a certain voice known
to those who understand.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

But if anyone counts several kinds of orators in this way, so as
to think some grand or grave or copious, others thin or subtle or
brief, others set between these and as it were in the middle, he
is saying something about men, little about the matter. For in
the matter the question is what is best; in the man, what he is is
stated. And so one may say both that Ennius is the greatest epic
poet, if that is how someone sees it, and Pacuvius the greatest
tragic poet, and Caecilius perhaps the greatest comic poet.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

I do not divide the orator by kind, for I am seeking the perfect
one. There is, however, one kind of the perfect orator; those who
fall short of it do not differ in kind, as Terence does from
Accius, but they are not equals in the same kind. For the best
orator is he who by speaking both instructs and delights and
moves the minds of his hearers. To instruct is a duty, to delight
a gift of honor, to move a necessity.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

That one man does these things better than another must be
granted; but that happens not by kind but by degree. The best,
indeed, is one, and next to it is what is most like it. From this
it is clear that what is most unlike the best is the worst.
For since eloquence consists of words and of thoughts, we must
bring it about that, speaking purely and correctly, which is to
speak Latin, we also pursue elegance in words both proper and
transferred: in proper words, that we choose the choicest; in
transferred, that, following likeness, we use what is another's
with restraint.

* Section 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Of thoughts, moreover, there are as many kinds as I said there
are of merits. For those of instructing are sharp, those of
delighting as it were witty, those of moving grave. But there is
also a certain structure of words that produces two things,
rhythm and smoothness, and thoughts have their own composition,
and an order suited to proving the case. But of all these things,
as of buildings, memory is as it were the foundation, delivery
the light.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

The man, then, in whom all these things will be at their height
will be the most perfect orator; he in whom they are middling, a
middling one; he in whom they are least, the worst. And all will
be called orators, as even bad painters are called painters, and
they will differ among themselves not in kinds but in abilities.
And so there is no orator who would not wish to be like
Demosthenes; but Menander did not wish to be like Homer, for the
kind was different. That is not so among orators; or even if it
is, so that one man, pursuing gravity, flees subtlety, and
another wants himself keener rather than more ornate, even if
that is in a tolerable kind, it is certainly not in the best, if
indeed that which has every merit is the best.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

I have said this more briefly than the subject asked, but for
what we are doing there was no need to speak at greater length;
for since there is one kind, we ask of what sort it is. It is
such as flourished at Athens; from this the very force of the
Attic orators is unknown, their glory known. For many have seen
the one point, that there is nothing faulty in them; few have
seen the other, that there is much worthy of praise. For there is
a fault in thought if anything is absurd or foreign or not sharp
or rather tasteless; in words, if it is unclean, if mean, if not
fitting, if harsh, if far-fetched.

* Section 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Almost all who are either counted Attic or speak in the Attic
manner have avoided these things. But those who have been strong
only so far should be regarded as sound and dry at least, but
like men of the palaestra: let them be allowed to stroll in the
covered walk, not seek a crown from the Olympic games. Those who,
though free of every fault, are not content with what is as it
were good health, but seek strength, muscle, blood, even a
certain sweetness of color: let us imitate them if we can; if
not, rather those who have uncorrupted soundness, which is proper
to the Attics, than those whose abundance is faulty, of the sort
Asia has produced in numbers. When we do this:

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

if only we attain that itself (for it is a very great thing), let
us imitate, if we can, Lysias, and his thinness above all; for in
many places he is grander, but because he wrote mostly private
cases, and those themselves for others, and little cases of small
matters, he seems rather meagre, since he himself deliberately
filed himself down to the kinds of minute cases.
Anyone who will do this in such a way that, if he wished to be
richer, he could not, may indeed be counted an orator, but among
the lesser ones; yet even a great orator must often speak in that
manner in such a kind of cases.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

So it comes about that Demosthenes can certainly speak in a low
register, while Lysias perhaps cannot speak in a lofty one. But
if they think it would have been proper to speak for Milo, when
an army had been stationed in the forum and in all the temples
around the forum, in the same way as if we were speaking of a
private matter before a single judge, they measure the force of
eloquence by their own ability, not by the nature of the case.

* Section 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

Therefore, since the talk of some people has now grown common,
some saying that they themselves speak in the Attic manner,
others that none of us does, let us ignore the former; for the
fact itself answers them well enough, when they are either not
called in to cases or, if called in, are laughed down; for if
they were merely laughed at, that itself would belong to the
Attics. But those who want us to speak in the Attic manner, yet
themselves confess they are not orators, if they have refined
ears and intelligent judgment, are employed as even those
ignorant of making a picture are employed to judge one, when they
have some skill in judging;

* Section 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

but if they place intelligence in fastidiousness of hearing, and
nothing lofty and magnificent delights them, let them say they
want something subtle and polished and scorn what is grand and
ornate; but let them stop saying that those who speak subtly are
the only ones who speak in the Attic manner, that is, as it were
dryly and purely. To speak fully and ornately and copiously with
the same purity also belongs to the Attics. What? Is it doubtful
whether we wish our speech to be only tolerable, or even
admirable? For we are no longer asking what it is to speak in the
Attic manner, but what it is to speak in the best manner.

* Section 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

From this it is understood that, since the most outstanding of
the Greek orators are those who were at Athens, and of these
Demosthenes is easily the chief, if anyone imitates him, he will
speak both in the Attic manner and in the best manner, so that,
since the Attics have been set before us for imitation, to speak
well is to speak in the Attic manner.

But since there was a great error as to what that kind of
speaking was, I thought I should take on a labor useful to
students, though not necessary for me myself.

* Section 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

For I have translated from the Attics the most famous speeches of
the two most eloquent men, opposed to each other, those of
Aeschines and of Demosthenes; and I have not translated as an
interpreter, but as an orator, with the same thoughts and their
forms, as it were figures, in words suited to our usage. In these
I did not hold it necessary to render word for word, but I kept
the whole kind of the words and their force. For I did not think
I ought to count them out to the reader, but as it were to weigh
them out.

* Section 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

This labor of mine will achieve this: that our people may
understand what they should demand from those who wish to be
Attic, and to what formula of speaking, as it were, they should
recall them. "But Thucydides will arise; for some admire his
eloquence." That indeed is right; but it has nothing to do with
the orator we are seeking. For it is one thing to unfold events
by narrating, another to accuse by arguing or to dissolve an
accusation; one thing for a narrator to hold a hearer, another to
stir him. "But he speaks beautifully."

* Section 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Surely not better than Plato? Yet the orator we seek must unfold
forensic controversies in a kind of speaking suited to
instructing, to delighting, to moving.
Therefore if there will be anyone who professes that he will
plead cases in the forum in the Thucydidean manner, he will be
far even from the suspicion of that which is employed in civil
and forensic business; but if he will praise Thucydides, let him
add our opinion to his own.

* Section 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

Indeed even Isocrates himself, whom the divine author Plato,
almost his equal in age, had Socrates praise wonderfully in the
Phaedrus, and whom all learned men have said is the greatest
orator, I still do not put in this number. For his oratory is not
employed in the battle-line nor with the sword, but as it were
plays with wooden foils. But I, to compare the smallest things
with the greatest, bring on the most famous pair of gladiators:
Aeschines, like Aeserninus, as Lucilius says, not a filthy man,
but keen and skilled
/
cum PacideianoPacideiano Cratander: Pacidiano GP
he is matched here, by far the best
since men were born.
/

For I think nothing more divine can be conceived than that
orator.

* Section 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

Two kinds of criticism are opposed to this labor of ours. One is
this: "But the Greeks are better." Let it be asked of him whether
they themselves can do anything better in Latin. The other: "Why
should I read those rather than the Greek ones?" The same people
read the Andria and the Synephebi, and Terence and Caecilius no
less than Menander, and they do not accept a Latin Andromache or
Antiope or Epigoni; yet they still read Ennius and Pacuvius and
Accius rather than Euripides and Sophocles. What, then, is their
fastidiousness in speeches translated from Greek, when there is
none in verses?

* Section 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

But let us now approach what we have undertaken, if I first set
out what case was brought to trial. Since there was a law at
Athens, THAT NO ONE SHOULD MAKE A DECREE OF THE PEOPLE THAT ANYONE
BE GIVEN A CROWN IN OFFICE BEFORE HE HAD RENDERED HIS ACCOUNTS;
and another law, THAT THOSE WHO WERE GIVEN A GIFT BY THE PEOPLE
OUGHT TO BE GIVEN IT IN THE ASSEMBLY; THOSE BY THE SENATE, IN THE
SENATE, Demosthenes was commissioner for repairing the walls and
repaired them with his own money; about him, then, Ctesiphon made
a decree, though no accounts had been rendered by him, that he be
given a golden crown and that this gift be made in the theatre
with the people called together, which place is not that of a
lawful assembly, and that it be proclaimed thus, THAT HE IS GIVEN
IT FOR HIS VIRTUE AND FOR THE GOODWILL WHICH HE HAD TOWARD THE
ATHENIAN PEOPLE.

* Section 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

This Ctesiphon, then, Aeschines brought to trial, because he had
written contrary to the laws, both that a crown should be given
when accounts had not been rendered and that it should be in the
theatre, and because he had written false things about his virtue
and goodwill, since Demosthenes was neither a good man nor well
deserving of the state. The case itself is indeed far from the
formula of our custom, but it is a great one. For it has both a
fairly sharp interpretation of the laws on each side and a truly
grave contest over services to the republic.

* Section 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

And so Aeschines had a reason, since he himself had been accused
on a capital charge by Demosthenes of having lied about his
embassy, that, for the sake of avenging an enemy, a trial should
be held in Ctesiphon's name about the deeds and reputation of
Demosthenes. For he did not say so much about the accounts not
being rendered as about this, that a wicked citizen had been
praised as the best.

* Section 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

This penalty Aeschines sought from Ctesiphon four years before
the death of Philip of Macedon; but the trial was held some years
later, when Alexander already held Asia; to that trial a
gathering from all Greece is said to have been made. For what was
so much to be seen or heard as the careful contention, inflamed
by enmities, of the greatest orators in a most grave case?

* Section 23
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h23
:END:

If I, as I hope, so express their speeches, using all their
virtues, that is, the thoughts and their figures and the order of
the matter, following the words so far that they not be foreign
to our custom (and if they will not all have been translated from
the Greek, still we have worked that they be of the same kind),
there will be a rule by which the speeches of those who wish to
speak in the Attic manner may be directed. But enough about us.
For at last let us hear Aeschines himself speaking in Latin.
