Of Saint Eustathius, Archbishop of Antioch, a diagnostic treatise against
Origen on the theorem of the belly-myther} 1.1 I always admire the notable
zeal of your godly way of life, O most distinguished preacher of orthodoxy,
Eutropius; and being struck in many ways by the mark of your piety, not least
have I especially marveled at this part, that you clearly wish to ascertain
how I would stand in judgment concerning the belly-myther who is narrated in
the first of Kingdoms; 1.2 for you say you are not pleased with what Origen
published on this subject. And indeed I too know well that not a few others
blame him for what he determined offhand, but there are many who are stung in
their souls, grieving not moderately; 1.3 yet sometimes some are led by what
that man wrote, more readily committing themselves to his prior
reputation-seeking, and attending to names rather than to the facts as they
ought. 1.4 So that I may not seem to introduce a forensic contest on my own, I
think it not unfitting to yoke his whole interpretation together with this
dictation of the writing, and through each to make what is clear plain, so
that neither some may suppose that we slander those who have been led to hold
whatever opinions, nor again that the opinions of each side are in dispute;
1.5 for it is possible from the parallel comparison to examine how each of the
two parts stands in opinion, and for the lovers of letters to choose the
better judgment from both. 1.6 At any rate a racehorse is not judged approved
when it runs well-legged by itself, not even if being exceedingly light it
displays movements as if winged, nor again any of the athletes able to run
most swiftly in the stadium, nor anyone else, neither wrestling supplely, nor
pancrating most roughly or boxing, or beating the air with empty
shadow-boxing; 1.7 but it is fitting that the fighting sides stand opposed in
close combat to their adversaries, so that from one or the other the superior
may be discerned. Therefore the Origenian things must be set first, and in a
separate order one must also be eager to examine the mind that collides with
those. 2.1 Come then, let us consider the very writing of the history as far
as possible; one must begin from somewhere here. When Saul was standing most
excellently over the multitude of the people, he removed all the
belly-mythers, it said, and those so called by name knowers, as being most
hateful lurking destroyers. 2.2 But when, turned toward the worst things, he
was bitterly driven by the accursed demon, he went again to the apparitions of
their divination, building up what he had a little before pulled down, and
through this too proving himself a transgressor. 2.3 For when the surrounding
aliens with one-minded zeal proclaimed the birth of war, gathering as much as
possible in full number they drew up the phalanxes opposite, and baring
weapons they went out stoutly to battle. But wretched Saul feared, seeing with
his own eyes the very fortified outwork of the camp, and to say the whole he
"was beside himself" struck with amazement, as the divine Scripture declares.
2.4 And after this, it says, he was asking through the Lord, longing clearly
to know what must be done; but the Lord did not answer him at all because of
the excesses of injustice, neither "in dreams" nor "in clear things" nor "in
the prophets", but indeed the divine was in no way giving oracles to that man,
who had done unlawful things. 2.5 And what does he do after these things,
stripped of the highest succor? Instead of rather propitiating with more
frequent petition and steadfast soul-service, on the contrary leaping away he
increases the practices of apostasy, and he was ordering his servants to seek
a belly-myther woman, so that he might come to her


to inquire as at an oracle. 2.6 And when the worst servants at once pointed
out the madwoman, Saul without delay disguised himself and, changing the
garments of his clothing, went off at a run, leaving the army. And when he
came to her by night together with two men, he was then offering requests to
her, saying "Divine for me by the belly-myther and bring up for me," he says,
"whom I shall tell you." 2.7 Therefore, if Saul was asking to divine through
the belly-myther, someone might say that that man's words are true. But is
there anyone who does not know that that man was being driven by the demon
with savage passions, turned aside into incurable divinations and diabolical
operations of myths? 2.8 What then? Is not God more trustworthy, who put the
words into the mouth of Balaam, and permitted Moses to write expressly in
Numbers: "for there is no omen in Jacob, nor divination in Israel"? 2.9 If
then such things have been established as things to be forbidden, as being
abominable pollutions and causes of the most hateful idolatry, how would the
witness be reliable who attempted most unholy to make use of these? But these
things we will take up a little later and speak of more completely; but one
must go back again to the history of the writing. 3.1 So when the woman saw
mindless Saul, pretending not to know the ruler she was answering that he
himself would know better what Saul had done to those skilled in these things,
"how he utterly destroyed the belly-mythers," she said, "and those who cry out
from the earth." 3.2 And on this account she was saying that her soul had been
trapped by him unto death. But when he confirmed by oath and affirmed that she
would suffer nothing grim, the woman, strengthened again in wickedness,
answers more boldly, I suppose: "Whom," she says, "shall I bring up for you?"
3.3 Of what sort and of what kind and who was this ill-starred old woman, that
she should promise to bring Samuel up from the dead? And indeed, if one must
speak setting the dignity aside, she was not able in any way to bring up a
prophetic soul, nor of any of the chance people at all, nor even of an ant or
a flea. For the demons do not have authority over spirits and souls, but God
who is master of all together; so that to the divine nature alone must it be
given to send for souls from Hades and to call them back again. 3.4 But the
clever Origen, wishing to wheel instruments of idolatry and inventions of
necromancy into the chorus of the church, lies against the divine writing. For
though the Scripture itself cries out openly that the woman said "Whom shall I
bring up for you?", this man turning it the opposite way said that the person
of the Holy Spirit had said these things. 3.5 Then he was swept aside so far
that he does not blush to attach to the Holy Spirit the words of the possessed
woman; and blaspheming such things with a more naked tongue, he refers the
whole to the Holy Spirit, trying to shame the hearers with a reputable name.
3.6 So the woman asked, as I said, and as if having authority to send for the
dead she cries out: "Whom," she says, "shall I bring up for you?" And when the
one seeking an oracle said that Samuel should be brought up, she adds again:
"And the woman saw Samuel," it said, "and cried out with a great voice and
said" to the ruler "Why have you deceived me? And you are Saul." 3.7 And when
the king said to her "Fear not" and adds besides "What have you seen?", the
pythoness answering again said: "I have seen gods coming up from the earth."
And when the ruler asked the last thing too, saying "What have you known?", at
once again she answered to the word "a man standing upright coming up from the
earth, and he is wrapped in a double cloak." 3.8 What then does the divine
Scripture declare upon these things? "And Saul knew that this is Samuel, and
he stooped with his face upon the earth and worshiped him." It appears then
that nowhere at all did the account of the divine writing say that Samuel had
been brought up through the


belly-myther; 3.9 but she herself first, through the demon that was making her
rave, pronounces that she must bring him up; then, boasting that she also sees
gods coming up, she was giving the signs of the man by deceit for an ambush,
and the deranged man "Saul knew" from what he had heard that this very one is
Samuel. 4.1 And if one must believe either demon-possessed Saul as having
accurately attained a true grasp of knowledge, or a demon boasting and
promising to send for souls of the righteous from Hades, let us judge among
ourselves of how much blasphemy it is full to think these and such things, O
best of men, Eutropius; 4.2 for Origen, as it seems, has erred far from the
truth, although our master Jesus has said expressly concerning the devil "that
one was a murderer from the beginning, and has not stood in the truth, because
truth is not in him. When he speaks the lie, he speaks from his own things,
because he is a liar and the father of it." 4.3 What then do you say, O Origen
(for one must ask you)? Are these things true which the Savior uttered, or
will you answer the opposite? If then the words of the Lord happen to be true,
then the demon lied in his usual way as he grew bold; but if you determine
that the words of the demon are without falsehood, you attempt to declare the
Lord a liar. 4.4 Do you see into how great an absurdity the definition
dogmatized among you has fallen? For sometimes the demons are forced
unwillingly to speak the truth, being racked by pains, yet they would not say
anything without falsehood willingly. And if someone will also determine at
once that this one brings the righteous up from Hades, how would it not be
clear that he introduces the lie by deceit, wishing to throw someone headlong?
4.5 Yes, he says, but the demon did not say these things, but "the narrative
voice of the writer; and the writer of the words is believed to be the Holy
Spirit, but not a human being." Then does he not understand how he shows
things contrary to the account of the narrative? 4.6 Those who have more
leisureably associated with all kinds of words know better at any rate that
the narrative discourse of the writer published these things which the
belly-myther seemed to be doing or saying to Saul who was seeking an oracle;
certainly the writer, relating about that woman and setting out her words
verbatim, said "And the woman said: Whom shall I bring up for you?" 4.7 And
who is so simple as to pretend not to understand that these voices are not the
writer's, but belong to the demon-possessed woman, whose name he also set
first? Yet nowhere does he confirm her words as true, nor would there be
anyone able to show this in any way. 4.8 But indeed, if one must speak as a
lover of truth, the narrative <voice> said this only, that "the woman saw
Samuel." And he wrote these things thus as speaking to those who know about a
demon-possessed woman. For it is not possible to dispute that a demon does not
bring up anyone's soul, who when exorcised by pious human beings is driven, is
burned, is whipped and flees, leaving the tent. 4.9 But the deranged woman
saw, as was fitting for her, the figure of the fantasized sight. At any rate
the beast is accustomed to change himself into many-faced forms, and that I
may speak according to the holy-appearing Paul I would say: "for Satan himself
transforms himself into an angel of light; no great thing then, if his
servants also transform themselves as servants of righteousness." =4.10= And
the dogmatist Origen, as discoursing toward adversaries with forensic
cleverness, also arranged this passage, thinking from this to circumscribe
those who introduce the truth instead, and seeming to overturn it by lack of
art rather than by art he was adding; 5.1 "And why," he says, "has it not been
said: the woman saw a demon that was pretending to be Samuel?" But he said
that besides this it has been written "Saul knew that it is Samuel"; then
again going on he adds: "If it were not Samuel, it ought to have been written:
and Saul was thinking that he was Samuel. 5.2 But now it has been written:
\"Saul knew\". And no one knew that which


is not; \"Saul knew\" then \"that it is Samuel and he fell upon his face upon
the earth and worshiped him.\"" And so that I may not digress upon other
examples, I will make the question from the very things he wrote here, and
then I will proceed to the remaining examples. 5.3 And tell me, by God: what
do you say, O Origen? Did the woman bring Samuel up for him in the body
itself, or some figure wrapped in a form of shadow-painting? If then she
brought him up bodiless, she did not raise Samuel, but a form of a spirit; for
the one fitted from soul and body, this is Samuel, the human being who has a
mixture from both in due proportion. 5.4 But if she brought the man up whole
in body, how did Saul not see him? For if he had seen him with his own eyes,
he would not, as if unseeing, have been examining the woman, saying verbatim
"What have you seen?" Therefore if he was invisible, it is undisputed that he
was bodiless; but if he was appearing in a bodiless figure, why then was the
pythoness saying the opposite, that she had seen "a man standing upright
coming up from the earth", and "wrapped in a double cloak" according to the
former custom? 5.5 So then the facts themselves fight against your words,
Origen; Saul, having seen nothing at all, as discoursing about an invisible
phantom was inquiring, saying "What have you seen?", but she on the contrary
as having seen the human being himself has cried "a man standing upright
coming up from the earth", and in order to persuade that he is a human being
himself, she also gives him a sign, adding "and he is wrapped in a double
cloak." 5.6 What then, O counselor of unlawful divination, if he happened to
be a man, did Saul not see him? Unless perhaps you would say that the
sense-organ of the eye was also blind in him, just as indeed also the very
intelligible organ of thought. 6.1 But perhaps, being caught easily, you will
say: the very instrument of the soul was being shaped in human form at that
hour, so that appearing it might declare the future by a prophetic message,
just as sometimes in sleep spirits and souls stand over human beings,
displaying the human characters in all their limbs, and not only so but also
wearing various and diverse garments and carrying indications of scars or
marks or weals and of blows or wounds. 6.2 If then you determine that a soul
only was brought up, but not a human being in the body itself (for not even if
you wish does the writing grant it to you), you do not perceive that you are
prophesying things that fight against themselves, and someone answering this
same thing will say to you: why then did the woman not say: I saw a prophetic
soul, but on the contrary "a man standing upright"? 6.3 For in the way you
said, saying "And why has it not been said: the woman saw a demon that was
pretending to be Samuel?", by this argument I myself would answer: for what
reason, when a soul had been brought up, did she not say she had seen a soul,
if it was so, but from the opposites "a man" and this one "standing upright",
which is a mark of bodily good condition? 6.4 For the madwoman was not saying
she saw a double-humped nor a stooping old man, but upright, taut,
straightened for war, always growing young about the risings of wickedness;
and baiting the mindless man by deceit, she was persuading him by a sign that
the one coming up from the earth is a human being, and she was declaring that
he had been wrapped in "a double cloak." 6.5 What then (someone might say), if
he happened to be a man coming forward in the body itself, did Saul then not
see him, but wishes to learn from another what sort he is, as if struck with
blindness? Therefore the facts themselves have cried that he was not a human
being, but some shadow, as it seems, unseen; for he would not have been
invisible. 6.6 And if you think, Origen, that it was the soul of Samuel there,
why was the phantom seen wrapped in a double cloak? At any rate it is shown by
an obvious indication that all the garments and coverings of clothing fit
bodies more, but not souls; 6.7 for I do not think you will say, playing the
man of the world with hypocrisy, that the double cloak has remained upon the
tomb for so many years until this day, so that the soul of the prophet may
wear it, lest somehow it should spend its time going about naked.


And yet someone might say that no one seems to be ignorant that incorruptible
garments from heaven are present to the saints, shining with light-casting
gleams, but not easily corruptible weavings of varied clothing. 6.8 And if you
determine that the thing seen is the soul of Samuel, yet you say that a
priestly garment was seen according to thought, so that the manner of the
priestly service might be evident (and both of these are proper to
intelligible things), it is established that the madwoman reported nothing
sound nor true; 6.9 for she was saying other things besides the things that
are, bringing forward the names of sense-objects and pointing out the
intelligible things. At any rate, naming "a man standing upright" and a woven
hand-made garment of wrapping, she was introducing from the opposites an
invisible fantasizing spirit. 7.1 Tell then, O best dogmatist, how Saul knew
that this very one happened to be Samuel, when he did not see even a portion
of a shadow, let alone the form of a man? But you do not blush at having said:
"It ought to have been written: and Saul was thinking that he was Samuel. But
now it has been written: \"Saul knew.\"" 7.2 Then that the demon-possessed man
knew, having heard from a demon-possessed woman, you say up and down; but that
he did not see at all, you hide the writing in silence. 7.3 And nowhere do you
bring the refutation into the midst, thinking to deceive simple human beings,
and fortifying an invention of impious divination for all; for the knowledge
of the demon-possessed man was worthy of the proclamation of that woman who
was also serving the words to him. 7.4 But you might say with science and
understanding that he knew without having seen, but did not think; at any rate
each of human beings, having received a thing by hearing rather than by sight,
thinks and supposes, not knowing what is clear, especially if he should also
hear from a demon-possessed person; for only the words of God are confirmed
toward accurate grasp and strength of faith. 7.5 And indeed if the lyric
writer said concerning human beings: "I said in my amazement: every human
being is a liar," how much more, if someone should relate concerning a
mindless woman? Then you say that "no one knew that which is not," as speaking
straight to youths. 7.6 Tell then how he knew that which is, if he himself did
not see when he was inquiring, not the man standing upright, not the ephod
that surrounded him, not the high-priestly garment? But the one seeing nothing
from anywhere was being fanned by empty words, just as indeed you too wing the
unstable with light talk. 7.7 But the historian, having first set the name of
a belly-myther, showed what sort the person is, raving. Then thus he described
her as having seen the vision, as it befits those of sound mind to think
concerning the deranged; for no one would suppose that a mind-injured person
sees the things that ought to be seen. 7.8 And in like manner he also said
that mindless Saul knew while seeking an oracle, as it would be worthy to
think concerning that man who for so many cycles of years was being bitterly
driven by the demon. 8.1 But indeed that these things have this manner the
facts themselves send forth clear cries. And so that I may also establish the
like things from another example, come, let us bring forward the history upon
Elijah the prophet. 8.2 When then Elijah with godly zeal was refuting the
false prophets, being eight hundred toward fifty in number, he was asking that
the order of each be examined against the other in a sacrifice, and that no
one bring fire, but that a bringing of fire be brought by prayers, so that
from the vaunted height from heaven the God able to hear by a sending of
flaming fire might be evident to all, and from this it may be discerned what
the authority of the stronger is, and the better might be clear to all. 8.3 So
when he was ordering those men to perform first, they, having chosen a little
ox, were cutting it up as usual, and calling upon the four-formed practice of
the one called Baal they were doing nothing less, nothing in any way. And
indeed "they also were cutting themselves according to the custom


of them with swords," it said, "and with spears until the pouring out of
blood." 8.4 Then upon these things the narrative cry of the writer adds: "And
they were prophesying until the afternoon passed." Since then the writing did
not distinguish whether they were prophesying true things or false, would
someone say on this account that those men are true prophets? 8.5 For one
ought to have said according to Origen's declaration: they were prophesying as
they thought in seeming, saying nothing true (since it is not lawful <to say>
that foreknowledge dwells in them), but not to proclaim with unentangled and
pure diction "and they were prophesying until the afternoon passed." 8.6 Is it
possible then, correspondingly, from the same history of the writing to see
that these things resemble those; for if the writer of both is the same, he
draws up a like mind from both. 8.7 But just as here, having set first the
name of the idolaters, he showed according to thought that one must suppose
concerning such people that they are false prophets lacking foreknowledge, so
there too, having set first demon-possessed persons and dramas of unlawful
divination, by aposiopesis he indicated what those of sound mind ought to
opine concerning the mindless. 9.1 But if I should bring forward such
testimonies, perhaps time will fail us as we speak; and having added one other
image of an example I will be content. Therefore in the heading of Exodus it
has been marked how the Lord himself was ordering those about Moses and Aaron
to throw the rod down to the ground opposite Pharaoh, so that having changed
its form into a serpent it might strike the Egyptians with a fearful sign. 9.2
And when this was happening contrary to expectation, the ruler, being cheated
by mindless folly, called together "the sophists and the sorcerers of Egypt."
"And the enchanters of the Egyptians also did," it said, "likewise with their
sorceries, and each threw his own rod, and they became dragons; and the rod of
Aaron swallowed their rods." 9.3 And it is still reported that "the enchanters
of the Egyptians likewise made" both blood and frogs. Since then the narrative
voice does not distinguish even here the orders of each, and said likewise
that the enchanters also had done it, would someone say on this account that
the things done by the sorcerers are like the things that came to be through
Moses? 9.4 Not at all, someone would say, nor would it be lawful to say that
the curious and wailing things are made like the heavenly imitations. As many
things as were done through Moses were showing the truth from the things being
done, but as many as were cobbled together through the enchanters were
fantasizing the sights in seeming; for the lie was bringing a self-complete
refutation from its powerlessness, since how did the one rod suddenly swallow
those many? 9.5 Nor is it lawful to think that those men were able from the
lifeless rods to complete living dragons all at once; for those able to ensoul
dead woods would perhaps be better than we. And perhaps Moses would have been
doing nothing contrary to expectation, if he were working things similar to
these. 9.6 And yet how is it not just to show them powerless through the
scriptural indication itself? For if God, punishing the Egyptians, was
commanding all the streams of the waters to be changed into blood for the sake
of vengeance, and they, being tortured by such blows, likewise change the
channels into a form of blood, devising to increase the suffering, for what
reason, being pressed by thirst, do they ask and need that the waters
throughout all Egypt be cleansed of the blood-bearing mixture? 9.7 Or again,
having suddenly seen frogs without number, why then, declining their crowds,
were they again fashioning others, if they were not taking thought of those
appearing as in a shadow and a form of fantasy, but were terribly injured by
the others shown in deed? 9.8 For no one would choose to add another
innumerable multitude to the enemy multitude instead of enlisting newly chosen
spear-bearing allies for alliance, nor to raise fever upon fever longing to be
rid of the first, nor, by force of unbearable blows


being whipped, to bring additional blows upon himself, while he persists with
petition by a pure release of these and by embassy and by request; 9.9 nor, of
things being sold at home, instead of sprinkling quenching instruments, on the
contrary pouring oil or pitch, does he raise the flame to an immense height,
and as it burns more he increases the suffering and waters fire upon fire
tastelessly. =9.10= Thus then, through the things the sorcerers were ambitious
to do, through these they were bringing out clear proofs that they thought
they must lead souls by a fantasizing conceit. At any rate it is not hard to
see from this itself that even now the jugglers in the theaters customarily do
many more and greater things than these. =9.11= But nevertheless, though these
are caught straightway as stale by an improvised power, the narrative voice no
less writes beforehand "and the enchanters of the Egyptians did likewise with
their sorceries." One ought then, as the dogmatist Origen legislated, to say:
and the enchanters of the Egyptians did the similar things in seeming, but not
to say "likewise", lest someone should suppose that it starts from a like
power. =9.12= For if they were able to do things like those about Moses, then
they would likewise have been able to defend themselves against the
adversaries with equal things, so that the contests from each side would be
matched. But if they were harming no one through what they were fashioning,
then he has used the word loosely, and the impressions of the lifeless fantasy
are obvious. =9.13= Just as here too the writer, having set first the name of
the enchanters and the practices of sorcery, showed that there would be no
need at all to dispute that each thing is accomplished by the magicians with
wailing curiosity. =9.14= Such also are the dramas copied from this history;
for he himself, having named a belly-myther, then also refuting impious
inventions of mantic art, and having shown that the person seeking the oracle
is that of a demon-possessed man, showed self-completely that each thing was
being accomplished by counterfeit fantasizings. But these things must be
thought by us thus. 10.1 And let us go again unrolling upon the tracks of the
words. What then? When Saul asked the belly-myther, saying "What have you
seen?", first the deranged woman answering cries: "I have seen gods coming up
from the earth." 10.2 For since the devil, having snatched the man, wished in
every way to throw him headlong, he was trying to show clearly that the demon
had in his authority not only to bring up one soul of a righteous man, but
also all the souls of the holy men together. And he, having armed the army of
the demons in the same place from the opposites and having constituted an
assembly in one moment of an hour, was boasting, wishing through this too to
persuade all that he himself is God the gatherer of the others. 10.3 And that
then, using his own madness, he is accustomed to make himself a god, Isaiah
refuting him face to face proclaims with prophetic holy-voice: "But you said
in your heart: I will go up into heaven, I will set my throne above the stars,
I will sit on a high mountain upon the high mountains toward the north, I will
go up above the clouds, I will be like the Most High." 10.4 And the Lord
himself speaking through the prophet Ezekiel testifies things like these
straightway: "Because your heart was lifted up, and you said: I am a god, I
will dwell in the heart of the sea, but you are a human being and not a god."
Instead of this, how many punishments he threatens him with, it is easier to
see from the things that lie under; and that he leans upon such apparitions
boasting each time has been shown by a divine voting. 10.5 Therefore, if the
demon was able to bring up those called gods by name, is it not necessary to
think that the one bringing up is greater than those being brought up? And if
this one is superior to the holy ones, it is consistent to suppose that the
arch-demon himself is again even of this one. 10.6 And if someone should grant
these things, he establishes by undisputed power that he himself is God of
gods who assigns to his own servants such authority, so as to send for spirits
and souls of the righteous out of


Hades. But no one of those of sound mind will ever determine these things at
all, lest he vote things contrary to the holy testimonies. 10.7 And when the
deranged man asked the second time and joining said to the woman "What have
you known?", at once again answering toward the question she said: I saw "a
man standing upright coming up from the earth, and he is wrapped in a double
cloak." 10.8 And having seen no one at all, when he became a hearer of the
words, he "knew" according to his own thought that this one is "Samuel",
inasmuch as he was being besieged from within by the demon; and when he was
snatched by the naming of the signs, then having bent his face upon the earth
he "worshiped him." 10.9 First then someone would say most rightly: if that
one happened to be Samuel, and not the many-turning serpent transformed, he
would have answered and spoken with pious reasoning: "You shall worship the
Lord your God and him only shall you serve." =10.10= And second again I would
say: when Samuel was ruling the whole people and happened to be a chosen
prophet, Saul, still a private man and going off seeking his father's asses,
was going to him to ask him as a prophet on account of the lost beasts of
burden, and nowhere does he appear to have worshiped him, although needing him
and clearly being subject as to a leader of a people. =10.11= How then did he
who then as a private man did not worship a ruler, now on the contrary as king
worship a private man? And third upon these I have to say: why then, having
heard gods coming up, did he worship none of these, but when he heard the man
standing upright had come up, then having worshiped he stooped? =10.12= And
indeed, as Origen declared boldly, if some of these were angels or choruses of
prophets, he ought to have fallen first before the better and more numerous,
and not to have attended the one and left the more numerous by folly of
contempt. =10.13= For must one not know that the flame-like dignities of the
angels are always far better than those of human beings? Therefore from all
the works together it is easy to see that Saul was blinding the leading mind,
being driven by the demon, and the devil was changing himself into various
forms, working to be worshiped by the ruler, so that from this he might
secretly deceive as many as possible to stoop to him. =10.14= And what is
strange to say, where even having seen the person of Christ he was in deed
seeing from within and by action God and by nature genuine Son of God, and
watching a pure human being, unstained, spotless, lying about him outermost,
and a most beautiful affair of a temple, consecrated, inviolable, nevertheless
he was coming forward at once to test him, fighting against God as usual?
=10.15= And when, having challenged the first and the second things against
himself, he was defeated, wretchedly broken, again having changed to another
character he was attempting to show him the whole circuit of this world and
the kingdoms of it, and growing bold with madness and false-speaking he was
boasting "all these things I will give you, if falling down you worship me."
=10.16= Such voices then the impious one burst forth here, thinking to call
out the habits of forbearance into anger, and thinking through such a thing to
take hold. But the Lord silenced the accursed one in a manner befitting God by
long-suffering; for it would be proper to God to bear all things without
resentment. =10.17= If then he counted it as nothing to bring such words even
to the Lord himself, how is it not clear that wishing to be worshiped equal to
God he was accomplishing these things? =10.18= This, I think, is obvious to
all those of sound mind, that through a raving woman he deceived the mindless
ruler into stooping to him beyond what was due. 11.1 And one must still take
up in order the things that lie under these. So when Saul had bent down to the
phantom having worshiped, again the deceitful one pretends to have suffered
force, having changed himself into the figure of Samuel; 11.2 then with irony
he answers more gloomily, saying "Why have you troubled me to come up?".


And fabricating these things indefinitely he wished to signify that the demon
was able to send even for the prophet unwilling, and to have such authority
against him. 11.3 Then some are so dim-sighted as not to be able to understand
that all who sincerely think the things of Christ drive the demons into exile
in the divine name, and not the opposite, that as subject to these they come
up unwilling from Hades, being sent for by them? 11.4 And if someone thinks
that the demon of the belly-myther is never driven into exile, being superior
to all the others together, let him run back to the Acts of the Apostles, and
having entered upon the very passages let him see how the traveling
holy-preacher Paul came to Philippi; 11.5 and when he had enlightened the
so-called Lydia with knowledge of piety, then he went to offer the prayers,
"and it happened that a certain servant-girl met them," it says, "having a
spirit of python, who was providing much work to her masters by divining."
Therefore she, having followed Paul and those about him, was crying aloud:
"These human beings are servants of the God Most High, who proclaim to you a
way of salvation"; and she was doing this for many days. 11.6 But the demon,
being racked by an invisible whip, was being forced unwilling to send forth
such voices, and Paul, having thought most rightly that that one's testimony
is not trustworthy, being worn out said to the spirit, saying "I charge you in
the name of Jesus Christ to come out from her." Then the writer relating the
matter adds, saying "and it came out at that very hour." 11.7 So the masters
of the servant-girl, seeing that the hope of their work had gone out of the
way, "having seized Paul and Silas dragged them into the marketplace upon the
rulers" and became causes of insults and blows, raising factions by slander.
11.8 If then Paul, having rebuked the spirit, suddenly drove out the pythoness
in that very moment, and the spirit fled by the authority of the word, not
being able to withstand the sound of the tongue, let the one who stands with
such a definition come forward into the open and say by what virtue of power
the demon, calling unwilling Samuel of like mind with Paul, brought him up
from Hades. 11.9 But he would not be able to show any of these things true; so
that through these too it is very easy to see that the deceitful one, having
transformed himself, was addressing Saul, having said "Why have you troubled
me?" For he was hinting that he was coming up unwilling against his will, not
wishing, but by necessity and force, as if ordered to be sent for by the
stronger himself. =11.10= And the ruler answering said: "I am greatly
distressed, and the aliens are warring in me, and God has departed," he says,
"from me and has not heard me still, neither by the hand of the prophets nor
in dreams; and now I have called you to make known to me what I shall do."
Again then one must answer: if Samuel happened to be the notable prophet,
would he not have made the man sound by admonishing? =11.11= Would he not have
said to him first: tell me, most wretched of all human beings, if the aliens
have stood around drawn up opposite for war in close combat, if God has
departed from you and has not heard you at all, and neither prophets serving
as ambassadors for you have prevailed, nor in dream nor waking has what is due
been revealed to you, ought you not rather by this to have been bent down
propitiating God and by alms of the poor to have washed off the causes of the
charges, and not indeed to take refuge with a belly-myther seer and to
exchange the symbols of piety for raving mantic art and to increase the
suffering against himself twofold? =11.12= For must one not think that God
expressly forbade such pollutions, saying in Leviticus through Moses: "You
shall not follow after the belly-mythers, and you shall not cling to the
enchanters to be defiled in them; I am the Lord your God"? But do you not
think these abominations impious, attempting to stage things contrary to God?
=11.13= Hear also after other things God giving an oracle upon such a daring:
"And a soul," he says, "whoever


follows after the belly-mythers or enchanters so as to fornicate after them";
and having added of what sort and what things it will suffer, he declared
going on: "I will set my face," saying, "upon that soul and I will destroy it
from its people." =11.14= If then God from the beginning having legislated
sent off with austere headings those who wallow in such enchantments or
divinations, and threatens grim and shuddering chastisements against those
caught, fearfully upon the judgment, ought not the one dedicated from tender
nails to God himself and a firm guardian of the law to have accused first
concerning these things? =11.15= But the one learned in the law from youth and
able to the highest degree to make accurate the legal letters would not have
sought out the fault, lest somehow through this some be dragged out into mad
divinations, robbed wretchedly by deceit? =11.16= But of these things he
neither said nor sought anything (for it was not Samuel, but the one who from
of old has been cooking up such dramas), and upon these he answers, having put
on the person of the prophet, saying "Why do you ask me, and the Lord has
departed from you and has come to be with your neighbor? And the Lord has done
to you as he spoke by my hand." =11.17= These things then, as an enemy and at
the same time shaping vengeance, he reads as introducing the memoranda of the
accusation. 12.1 And one must consider how, having omitted the very binding
charges, he pretends by hypocrisy to utter more demonstrative things, and
having said nothing worthy of foreknowledge he artfully repeats those things
which Samuel while still living was saying beforehand would come together for
Saul, which he was announcing beforehand as already having come to be,
prophesying. 12.2 But indeed the one who said to him "Why do you ask me?"
expressly confesses that he has said these things even before; and he
tautologizes, confirming the first things through the later on a pretext in a
wailing way. 12.3 But Origen on the contrary, having taken verbatim the very
words announced beforehand of old by the prophet, determines them as fresh and
new utterances of prophecy, and on this account he wishes Samuel to have been
brought up, although the Scripture refutes him straightway, as even he himself
agrees in part locally. 12.4 But what after these things was the phantom
adding again, when it was discoursing with the ruler? "The Lord will tear the
kingdom from your hand and will give it to your neighbor David. Because you
did not hear the voice of the Lord and did not fill the wrath of his anger in
Amalek, for this word the Lord has done this to you on this day and the Lord
will give Israel with you into the hands of aliens." 12.5 And if someone
should wish to make the clear thing accurate, let him go back a little before
upon the history of the writing, and having unfolded the very passage where,
having defeated Amalek, he appropriated the strongest of the spoils and
thought he must offer whole-burnt offerings from these as firstfruits. 12.6
And seeking with clearer exactness scientifically he would find that Samuel
said all these words beforehand verbatim at that time; for he said that the
Lord also tore the kingdom from his hand, when the ruler, having taken hold of
the wing of the double cloak, also split it. 12.7 And he was saying to him
besides these that the kingdom was about to be given to his neighbor, the one
better than him. And he was prophesying also in what manner Israel must on
this account be split and no longer return. And having said these things he
was confirming the things to be said together with the deeds; for not long
after, sent by divine decision, he anointed David king. 12.8 Did then the one
said to have been brought up through the belly-myther report nothing strange,
but was fashioning the things said before by Samuel as his own, personifying
by deceit, and by persuasive artifice tautologizing he was pretending to
converse prophetically? 12.9 But just as mountebanks <and> seers, hastening to
go upon the doors of the rich, secretly track down the affairs of life that
have already passed by, asking many questions, and being suspected of saying
offhand the things that came to be long before, at once


they astound the hearers, and leading them into simple faith they fashion
concerning the future whatever they wish, so then also the phantom of the
belly-myther, transforming itself, was declaring the very prophetic utterances
of Samuel, and in seeming it thought it was prophesying though knowing
nothing, but it was setting the things of another first as its own and was
striking the accursed man with amazement, and through these it was easily
stealing and was filching that man's mind. =12.10= What else then was it
adding after these things? "And you," it says, "tomorrow, and your son
Jonathan with me, and the Lord will give the camp of Israel into the hands of
aliens." But these things the demon seemed to be saying as his own. 13.1 And
one must seek whether he was saying true things beforehand, as he thought, and
whether he was reporting from a guess and coincidence, and besides these
whether he was able to have foreknown any of these. First then Saul does not
appear to have been destroyed on the next day, as the phantom said speaking.
13.2 For if having heard the words from the extreme despondency "he did not
eat bread that whole day and that whole night," it says, as the divine
Scripture declares, and after this the woman advising again was urging him to
touch food, a way lying ahead, it is obvious that on the second day, I
suppose, after that all-night and all-day fasting she persuaded him, thrown
upon the ground slackly, to rise and sit more decently upon the seat, and the
sucking calf which she had she takes at once and slaughters, and preparing
cooked food and baking the unleavened bread, she was making foods ready. 13.3
And when she was offering to him and those about him, having prepared
breakfast eagerly, he took of the food there, having been urged; and one must
think that he came to other night hours, if he went off having set out from
there by night. So that in this the demon is self-completely convicted of
having lied; for it did not, as he said ("tomorrow you will be with me"), come
to be thus, when he spent the next day there without food, then on the second
having touched the food he walked to the camp. 13.4 And he is caught not only
having said this contrary to what came to be, but he also said that Jonathan
alone together with the father ended toward him; and from the opposites the
narrative voice of the writing declares that Saul was slain together with
three sons, but not with one only, as that one said. 13.5 Therefore by lack of
foreknowledge the demon uttered nothing true (for he would not have reported
things contrary to the things that came to be), but having filched the things
said before by Samuel, as I said, he was adding a few things to these, weaving
like things from probabilities. 13.6 Thus then he was also threatening death
to the ruler, making use of that man's voices. But still he was also promising
to give over the camp of Israel, because the prophet had said weightily that
the people would be split. 13.7 So seeing engines of war fortified and the
leader of the people very fearful, and besides also left behind by God himself
(through which the ruler was confessing, guessing that the prophet's
proclamation is being fulfilled here), he pretended to cry out toward the
present movements. 13.8 Yet as many things as he added as his own by
themselves, he was clearly caught having lied; for he was able neither to say
beforehand the day of the death, nor the killings of the sons of Saul, of whom
the Scripture made mention by name. And from these it would not be hard to
think that the spirit reported nothing of its own nor true, but as many words
of Samuel as it was filching, these it thought it was prophesying,
appropriating them. 13.9 But most of all it is not possible to say that it was
prophesying this without falsehood; and if it was also proclaiming a birth of
war and the incurable streams of bloodshed, who is the cause of all wars?
=13.10= Is it not the devil? Did he not ask for Job? Did he not raise up
against him a war


suddenly undeclared? Transforming himself otherwise elsewhere, was he not
becoming a self-messenger and at once a witness and a war-leader, having
slipped into the inwards of human beings? 14.1 But such a figure has been
thought unclear to the many; one must go then correspondingly into the tracks
of the third of Kingdoms. It appears then how the prophet Micaiah is asked by
Ahab; but since he was being forced to speak the true things unveiled, he was
saying he had seen the Lord sitting upon a throne, then also heavenly armies
standing about him from the right and at once from the left; 14.2 and together
with the history of the sight he said he had also heard a voice of some such
kind: "Who will deceive Ahab and he will go up to Remmoth Gilead and will fall
there?", and then he says (so that having left out the things in the middle I
may say what is sought) "a spirit came out and stood before the Lord and said:
I will deceive him." 14.3 And when the Lord answering spoke "In what?", he
answers again: "I will go out and I will be a false spirit in the mouth of all
his prophets." Toward these things then the stronger has said: "You will
deceive, and indeed you will be able; and now behold," he says, "go out and do
thus." And the prophet relating these very things to the king immediately
adds: "God has given a false spirit in the mouth of all these your prophets,
and the Lord has spoken evils upon you." 14.4 Therefore if it is necessary to
think that this spirit, the cause of false-speaking, is not good but wicked,
it is consistent to suppose that this very thing is a diabolical instrument.
And if this is shown strictly to be so, then he had heard beforehand how the
polluted Ahab, going off to war, was about to die; 14.5 for he himself asked
to throw in the lie instead of the truth, so that he might himself work a
birth of war and destroy the unjust man more shamefully. So that through these
works too it is shown that the devil has learned beforehand the evils brought
upon the unjust, since he himself is the dramatist of all the most hateful
things. 14.6 Yet in the case of Saul, as many things as he added from himself
and yoked to the utterances of Samuel, he not only lied persuasively, but also
blasphemed impiously. From somewhere here the first blasphemy is refuted;
first, that in imitation, as the Lord said to the robber who confessed the
power of his kingdom, saying "today you will be with me in paradise," this
same thing the deceitful one also said to Saul who had fulfilled his mad
divinations: "tomorrow you will be with me, and Jonathan your son." 14.7 And
second he wishes to show through these that the righteous man differs in
nothing from the unjust, devising to cut out the eagerness of the pious. And
no one seems to be ignorant that there exists no communion of a faithful with
an unfaithful, or again how the one who lived in luxury with insatiable wealth
was separated far from the diet of Lazarus, although he had done nothing like
that man. 14.8 But this one never gave anything to the poor man, nothing at
all, neither of superfluities nor of crumbs thrown to the ground, but he did
not injure the man with blows, nor having tortured him with whips did he drive
him further somewhere of earth. 14.9 But nevertheless, when both had changed
and migrated from here, the one ended into the bosoms of the forefather
Abraham, and the other was given to the sleepless flames of the fire. So when
being tortured in Hades he was burning, from afar he sees the forefather, and
he also sees "Lazarus in his bosoms"; =14.10= and being fired by the flame, he
was asking Abraham earnestly that he send the poor man to him; for he was
requesting that Lazarus, having come, dip the tip of the finger in water, and
cool his tongue, drawing up a moisture of wetness; so being pained with
fire-bearing pains the wretched one was making such embassy with supplication.
=14.11= And when the forefather answering to the word was showing that each in
each part had received the worthy exchanges for the sake of a prize, then
having added he declared: "And upon all these things between us and you a
chasm," he says, "has been fixed great, so that


those wishing to cross from here toward you may not be able, nor may those
from there cross over toward us." =14.12= If then there is a form of a chasm
dividing between righteous and unjust, so that those from here are not able to
ferry across there nor those from there to come here, it is established that
Saul being unjust is not with the prophet Samuel; =14.13= for if the rich man
is not able to be with the poor man, and this though having wronged him in
nothing nor having persecuted him, but not having given him readily from what
he had, how much more is the wretched man unworthy to enjoy the communion of
the saints? 15.1 But must one not think how for the sake of shameful gain he
sets aside the divine command, and struck by a demon he is driven incurably by
envy, he persecutes the holy-seeming David with no fitting order, although by
prophetic psalmody he was taming the savagery of the demon raving in him, 15.2
he slaughters the priests of the Lord, being three hundred toward fifty in
number, he departs from God seeking an oracle impiously? And if he
accomplished these things and the things brother to these, how is it possible
to think that he ended with the wonder-working Samuel, who in the season of
summer asked that rain come to be, and at that very thing an immense multitude
of waters was scattered down? 15.3 And if it is not possible to say that Saul
is with Samuel, the one who said to him "tomorrow you will be with me" lied.
Therefore if the one who has said these things is a liar, it is obvious that
this one is not Samuel; for the prophet of the Lord would not have lied so
mindlessly, so that such words, being spit apart, show naked the many-faced
beast hidden most inwardly. 15.4 At any rate he himself is the one who dragged
down into the chasm of Tartarus also raving Saul; whence having overpowered
him he was addressing him fittingly: "tomorrow you will be with me." 15.5 And
trying to establish in a double manner that a righteous man never differs from
an unjust, he was also agreeing that Jonathan together with the father carried
off the same lot as that man, although he had not come together with this
one's cruelty. 15.6 And who of those who are pretends not to understand that
the lover of the prophet David, having lived most excellently, but not having
attempted to persecute a righteous man unjustly, does not carry off the same
pay as the one who persecuted? 15.7 For a son will not die, to say the whole,
on behalf of an unjust father. But indeed the false-stitcher is also convicted
through this indication. 16.1 And the writer having added with unomitted
sequence of order said that "and Saul hastened and fell standing upon the
earth and feared greatly," saying "from the words of Samuel." 16.2 For he
knows those to be the words of Samuel, which he prophesied being present in
body itself and tongue, and the phantom collected these words and was bringing
them forward as its own; of which the ruler, having been reminded, and seeing
the symbols of the war established straight opposite, had become very fearful,
thinking of their end. 16.3 But Origen, fashioning dogmas for himself, knowing
that as many as possible happen to be those who vote these things, and
discoursing toward those who contradict him, did not take care with the owed
caution to make accurate what is due; but predicting that the drama of the
belly-myther touches all and calling the truth as necessary into the
examination, he fights the truth itself openly. 16.4 Then going through
praises of Samuel, as many things as the divine scriptures speak about him,
then again declaring him under the authority of the demon he was declaring in
the very words and in the very syllables: "Then <if> one so great was under
the earth and the belly-myther brought him up, does a demon have authority
over a prophetic soul?" 16.5 But these things he is sometimes suspected among
some of saying as raising a difficulty or perhaps asking; and so that through
the following epilogues he might establish that these things are so, he was
adding in whiter phrasing, crying "What shall I say?" "These things have been
written," he says


"are they true or are they not true? 16.6 To say that they are not true urges
toward unbelief (it will go upon the heads, saying, of those who say it), but
that they are true provides us a seeking and a difficulty." 16.7 And after
this again confirming unexamined what he announces, and setting out the
judgments of those who opine against him he adds: "And indeed we know some of
our brothers who have looked against the Scripture and say: I do not believe
the belly-myther; the belly-myther says she has seen Samuel; she lies" and
many other things besides these. 16.8 And through these he unveiledly rebukes
those who declare the oracles of the belly-myther false, and cursing he refers
it upon the heads of those who disbelieve her. Yet wishing most easily to
persuade the facile he attaches the narrative to the Scripture itself, saying
"Have these things been written or have they not been written?" 16.9 And using
syllogistic cleverness he adds repeating "Are these things true or are they
not true?", although that the soul of the prophet has not been brought up
"provides us," he said, "a difficulty and a seeking." But that to grant such
things to the demon is full of how much impiety, he no longer says seeking nor
applies his mind, struck with unmixed mindlessness. =16.10= For it belongs to
God alone to command with such authority, so as to send for souls from Hades
and to call them. One must answer here again then: these things have been
taken up in the sacred scriptures, yet the writer was not saying beforehand
that such a writing had been accomplished from his own person; for the book
would not have been assembling a church, crying against the prophetic voices,
as we both showed before and will say a little later. =16.11= But indeed he
was marking out the words of the belly-myther and the raving knowledge of the
ruler shown by deeds of ignorance to be full; for if it will thus fit to
impose upon the holy scriptures, then, since they say that the devil also
proclaims himself God straightway, will one need on this account to believe
him, committing the whole to the narrative account of the writing, as Origen
introduces? =16.12= For this too has been written into the scriptures
themselves. Or again, whenever using measureless arrogance he should say that
the whole circuit of this world, or even the kingdoms of it, are his own,
would someone say on this account that it is fitting to think that he himself
is creator of all? =16.13= Not at all, someone would say; for the narrative
voice marked out that one's boastings, yet it does not declare them true, nor
is there anyone able to show this in any way. But there are countless such
things besides these to see, and it is not possible to bring all forward, lest
we seem by long-speaking to be knocked away from the question. 17.1 So when he
seemed to those thinking most rightly to rebuke not moderately, again having
filched he leapt away upon another thought, and he was making it terrible as a
demagogue that they say besides the other things that it would be shuddering
to suppose that the well-famed Samuel has come to be in Hades, who has been
honored with a chosen dignity of prophecy. 17.2 This then he tautologizes up
and down in an old-womanish way, devising by deceit to handle the hearers from
other things into other things. But we do not differ about these things, about
which it has been established that all must agree, but about those on account
of which we have decided to dispute strictly; for one must not seek whether
Samuel has come to be, but whether a demon has such authority as to call up
souls of the righteous from Hades and again to send them away. 17.3 Since then
having been caught in the act he was convicted of pouring blasphemy as torture
upon the hearings of the hearers, he turns aside by a deceitful art, and takes
refuge supposedly upon the person of Christ, so that having compared him
against the holy men he might show that Christ himself also went down into
Hades together with all the others. 17.4 But as he was showing this and indeed
also advising man by man he was declaring, saying "Why do you fear to say that
every place has need" of him "of Christ and of the prophets?", arranging a few
things through the middle he weaves in another blasphemy, showing that Christ
even happening to be below


"that I may so speak," he says, "being in the place below, was above by
choice." 17.5 And as conversing about a human being only and no longer aiming
also at his divine nature, he was saying that he had arrived among the lowest
things, and by choice, I suppose, had gone above, just as indeed also the
other human beings. 17.6 "Thus," he said, "also the prophets and Samuel, even
if they go down where the souls," he says, "the ones below, are, they are able
to be in the place below, but they are not below by choice." And having
sounded the world about with boundless light talk, he does not even understand
this, that every one of human beings, even if he is exceedingly wretched,
having gone down in Hades has been established above by choice. 17.7 At any
rate there would be no one of the righteous nor of the unjust who does not
long each time to hold to the highest sojourns, and to have been rid of the
underground places. If then all together who have been led down into Hades
love the upper bearings by choice, even if some have not cared for a righteous
life at all, of what sort of dignity of the prophetic chorus was he making
clear? 17.8 And what was the surplus he was reporting of the Lord, declaring
him like to all? But he sounded out the voice so ignorantly, not having
thought that being God the Word, not rather by choice, but by virtue of the
godhead he is present everywhere all at once. 17.9 And if especially he
permitted his own chosen temple to be loosed, at once again he raised it in a
new manner in three days, and the soul of this human tent, having gone down
into the lowest parts of the earth, opened the gates there in a sudden moment
and released the souls shut up there; =17.10= and it has been so strengthened
with divine power through the association of God and Word, as even to have
all-bearing authority. 18.1 But indeed I myself am persuaded that there is
also a clear token of this, when he was arriving into the underground places,
and at the same time was also bringing the soul of the robber on that very day
into paradise. 18.2 For if through one human being salvation has belonged to
all human beings, it is obvious that the soul redeems the kindred souls, at
once going down into the underground parts of the chaos, and at once also
restoring again to the most ancient pasture of paradise the one who slipped
under the power of the unconquered kingdom. 18.3 And corresponding things even
before these the child of God was testifying, saying beforehand that "and no
one has gone up into heaven except the one who came down from heaven, the son
of man who is in heaven." 18.4 Therefore if he was saying that the one
starting from the human race had gone up into heaven most alone of all, and
from there again had come down here, and having gone here visits in heaven, it
is established that the human being was doing these things by virtue of soul
in a chosen way. 18.5 For the holy soul of Christ, dwelling with God and Word,
goes about all things in sum, and has gone into the very highest heaven, into
which no other of human beings has gone up. But these things have been hung
upon the very human form, which God and Word wore. 18.6 And that the child of
God is present everywhere all at once, not least also concerning this John has
stood as a witness, being a hearer of Christ himself, and he cries aloud
verbatim "no one has ever seen God; the only-begotten son who is in the bosom
of the Father, that one has declared him." 18.7 And indeed if Christ being
present here was visiting bodily, when he was reporting these words to John,
and the one who received his sayings was proclaiming him to be in the bosoms
of the Father, while being entertained upon earth in the body itself, how must
one not think that he had also gone upon the heavens at that time, and as
dwelling inside bosoms he was also sojourning upon the earth in a manner
befitting God and was present to all together


as God? 19.1 But if someone, sick with Jewish blindness, does not accept the
evangelic voices, one must bring upon him the utterances of Solomon and speak
somehow thus: "For while a quiet silence was holding all things around and
night was in mid-course in its own swiftness, your all-powerful Word from
heaven from royal thrones, bearing as a sharp sword your unhypocritical
command, having stood filled all things with death; and it was touching
heaven, but had gone upon earth." 19.2 If then the only-begotten son of God
himself is proclaimed Word and God by like-minded symphony, through whom have
come to be angels and heavens and earth and seas and depths and heaven-running
lights and, to say it in sum, the whole constitution of generated things, and
thus by just judgment disciplining all he holds from heaven a throne of the
everlasting kingdom, and by sharp justice he punishes the unjust (but indeed
being all-powerful in work and action, inasmuch as God and God Word, "it was
touching heaven, but had gone upon earth" at the same time), clearly by virtue
of godhead he fills all things in every way. 19.3 Therefore if he has taken
hold of such a nature, those who think by folly that the divine is
circumscribed by a place and a stated portion of a region act impiously, and
Origen speaks offhand by ignorance rather than by orderly thought that Christ,
having gone down into the lowest parts of the earth, was spending time above
by choice; 19.4 for he did not think that being God he was not present above
in seeming by so much eagerness of judgment [by desire], as by energy of
divinity he fills all things in every way as much as possible for all. 20.1
But it fits that man to opine such things concerning Christ, who determines
that prophetic souls are brought up by a demon; and the pythoness, when she
saw the demons of the same belly as herself, lifting as some same-womb
brothers the same-mother kinds, cries: "I have seen gods coming up from the
earth." 20.2 But the boastful Origen did not fear to say that the demon
brought up not only the soul of the prophet, but even "holy souls of
prophets," he said, "have come up together"; and not being sated with such
drunken outrage he also determined that the angels had come up together with
their spirits; for he has said that they were coming up here for the sake of
the salvation of those in need. 20.3 Great hopes then, as it seems, Origen
hypothesizes for the angels and prophets or even for the whole chorus of the
saints together, first introducing that they are under a demon's authority,
then indicating that they were brought up here through a mad woman, led
forward as prisoners to heal those who suffer. 20.4 It does not seem to me at
all that he speaks less mind-injured than that woman; for in deed he so
pitiably cheapened the holy men, nor was he ashamed to slander even the order
of the heaven-falling angels; 20.5 or let the one who stands with such a
definition show clearly from where he took it that he said the angels
entertained in the heavens come up from the earth; for these have not come
down into Hades bringing releasing dismissals for the captives, but Christ,
since this one also set up a victory-bearing trophy against the enemy, and
having torn away the spoils of the captives went up with the body itself aloft
into heavens. 20.6 And angels stand by God each time, hastening to complete
the owed services, but they do not inhabit Hades. 21.1 But, as it seems,
Origen pretends not to understand, so that he may prepare an ill-starred
divination; then having attempted to allegorize all the scriptures he does not
blush to accept this alone upon the letter, prophesying by hypocrisy, although
not even attending to the body itself fair-mindedly. 21.2 But indeed
discoursing about the paradise which God planted in Eden, then wishing also to
relate in what manner he made the fruit-bearing woods spring up he was adding
among the other things: "When reading we go up from the myths," he said, "and
from the according-to-the-letter


interpretation <and> we seek what the woods," he says, "are those which God
farms, we say that there are not sense-perceptible woods in the place." 21.3
And tropologizing these things he does not shudder to name myths as many
things as God is related to have created, and Moses the most faithful servant
of God wrote; but from the opposites, the things which the myth, being
fashioned unseen in a belly, sounds under, these he confirms by a dogmatic
heading, showing them true. 21.4 The words of the belly-myther, establishing
that they have been said through the Holy Spirit, he thinks fit to remain
unshaken, having committed them in seeming to the Scripture; but the very
indications of God published through Moses, having named them myths, he
exchanges the thought, not thinking it just to remain in the account of the
letter. 21.5 But does he not allegorize the wells dug by Abraham and those
about him, and having stretched so great a swarm of words he destroys their
whole treatment together, having transferred it into another mind, although
the wells still even now appear to sight upon the land? 21.6 Did he not
tropologize the affairs of Isaac and of Rebecca, having said that the earrings
and bracelets are golden words, and having forced that whole subject and
having taken it upon the intelligible he slandered it? 21.7 And having
conversed about much-enduring Job, instead of marveling at the endurance,
praising the toils, eulogizing the prowess, accepting the faith, describing
the examples of stoutness of soul, urging through this the newly chosen toward
virtue, arming the contestants with good courage and strength of thought to
contend manfully on behalf of piety, having left that man, having started
aloft upon the names of the daughters he wore himself out in an old-womanish
way. But of what sort of nonsense he talked into the name of Day and Cassia or
even the horn of Amaltheia, it is not even possible to say; for they are full
of all kinds of mockery. 21.8 And writing about Lazarus, instead of glorifying
the great work of Christ and through these showing clearly that he is God who
sent for the stinking dead man from the tombs by authority and by the release
of the word ensouled the swollen bodies, he says nothing on this account, but
Lazarus the friend of the Lord, whom he loved not unfittingly through virtue,
he brought up by allegorizing into the one weak and dead in sins. 21.9 And no
one said or wrote these things against the righteous man; for the Lord would
not have loved him so choicely, if he had not taken hold of a divine way of
life. =21.10= And no less also considering the drama of the stoning (since he
attempts to tropologize this too) he lies against the evangelic writing,
saying "we did not quite find, having sought in the things before this, that
the Jews took up stones so that they might stone him"; then after a few he
says: "For if \"they took up again\", they took up before." =21.11= And up and
down he wishes to construct that another like account does not also precede,
so that he might establish that they took up words, but not stones, at once
against him, although the evangelist has said before "they then took up stones
so that they might throw them upon him." =21.12= And when he had written these
things beforehand, in the middle having passed over some five and thirty
toward a hundred lines, he adds besides: "The Jews then took up stones again,
so that they might stone him." But these things have been written in two
passages, and they appear to have been done at different times from not having
the same interweavings or yokes of the words; but Origen who thinks he knows
all the scriptures did not read this, and this while writing upon the whole
gospel together verbatim. 22.1 And if someone supposes that we fashion these
things, having met with the evangelic declarations themselves and with the
studies worked out by him about this, he will find that we have said nothing
false. 22.2 But what need is there to speak further? Practically all things,
allegorizing from the names, he destroys the subjects of the facts, not even
thinking this, that there are many homonyms of righteous and unjust, and it is
not possible


for the unlike ways of life to be judged from the one name likewise. 22.3 At
any rate there is Judas the betrayer and the other Judas an apostle, or again
Zechariah a most unjust king and another Zechariah the prophet, and indeed
also Ananias who was thrown in the furnace of the fire, and still besides this
another Ananias who persecutes a church instead of being persecuted, and
orders the apostle to be struck, to whom also Paul answered face to face to
the word: "God is about to strike you, you whitened wall. And you sit to judge
me according to the law, and transgressing the law you command me to be
struck?" 22.4 Therefore, if the facts are judged by a name, let those who lean
upon such tropologies say by what method they think fit to interpret the
unlike sciences or the anagoges of ways of life from the homonymy. Certainly
many still even today, wrapping themselves in names of forefathers or
prophets, Jews do unlawful things, and many also among Greeks, being called
Peters and Pauls, do incurable things. 22.5 The empty things then of the words
of Origen are caught not least also through such light talk. And as many
things as he introduces ill-opining on account of the resurrection, it is not
possible to make clear for the present. For Methodius, worthy of holy memory,
wrote sufficiently upon this theorem, and he showed openly that he gave a
passage to the heretics mindlessly, having determined the resurrection upon a
form, but not upon a body itself. 22.6 But that he overturned all things by
tropologies and sowed the seeds of ill-opinion everywhere it is easier to see,
and tautologizing up and down he filled the inhabited world with measureless
nonsense. 22.7 And by such a method customarily allegorizing all things in
every way, he was not able to tropologize only the words of the belly-myther,
nor again to relate what is clear from the sequence itself. 23.1 But what
besides these did he introduce, fashioning escapes by a disclaimer? Since the
phantom seemed to have said to Saul: "The Lord will tear the kingdom from your
hand," toward these things arguing persuasively he was answering that there
would be no need to think that "a demon prophesies about an Israelite
kingdom." 23.2 And already even before we said that the demon stole these
words said before by Samuel, so that having deceived mindless Saul he might
seem to say his own things. But this man does not pretend to know such dramas
of the devil. So when he seemed to say beforehand that the kingdom of the
deranged man would be torn, then he was adding: "and he will give it to your
neighbor David." But the much-learned Origen, moved here, said: "And a demon,"
saying, "is not able to know the kingdom of David, the one voted by the Lord."
23.3 But it comes upon me to marvel how this man carries off a reputation
still for good counsel among the many, as of sound mind and at once best of
judgment. If then the kingdom was unclear, it would perhaps have been possible
for someone pretending to play ignorance; 23.4 but if it had been testified by
God, and had been anointed upon these things by Samuel, and always being best
in the wars had been shown son-in-law of the king, so that to this one the
dancing women assigned the myriads, and to Saul they rendered the thousands,
and if these and such things had been established as clear, what was strange
or what contrary to expectation, if the demon was recognizing the kingdom that
is and has been voted by the Lord through the prophet? 23.5 For must one not
think (if indeed one must say by excess that he knows only the bodily things)
that the anointing of the oil has a sense-perceptible bringing of the touch?
But no one will dispute that these things have been established as obvious to
all; and so that I may say the exceeding thing, adding even if such things had
not been established as clear: which would be greater toward comparison, to
know the kingdom of David, or to bring up prophetic souls from Hades? 23.6 And
yet this has been shown also upon the person of the stronger; for also upon
Christ, being racked and unwilling, the demons cry out, verbatim "what to us
and


to you," having cried, "son of God? Have you come before the time to destroy
us?" But to send for souls from Hades and to call together choruses of
heaven-falling angels at the same time, God alone has authority to accomplish,
and his most divine child, and no one else at all. 23.7 If then they knew the
invisible kingdom of God anointed by Holy Spirit, were they not able much more
to recognize that of David, shown through a sense-perceptible anointing and a
visible pouring? But the accursed one did not know the things done and laid up
in scriptures about David, who even having come forward to the Lord wrapping
himself in the body said boldly: "If you are son of God, throw yourself down;
for it has been written that to his angels," he says, "he will command
concerning you and they will lift you upon hands, lest you ever strike your
foot against a stone"? 23.8 Therefore if he is shaped from the holy scriptures
to converse, then he knows the things written in an ill-learned way; and if he
has been established in knowledge of these, then he was not ignorant of the
kingdom of David, which has been engraved in the sacred letters; so that the
demon, saying the things written, prophesied nothing in any way. 24.1 But the
much-famed Origen, finding nothing at all to say, took refuge in this
artfully, adding elsewhere besides these: "And I," he says, "am not able to
give a demon so great a power, that it may prophesy about Saul and the people
of God, and prophesy about the kingdom of David that he is about to reign."
24.2 Then, someone might say, O most mindless of men, you do not give him to
prophesy, but you permit him to bring up prophets from Hades and suddenly to
send for angelic choruses, which is greater and superior and holding of a more
divine authority? Has anyone then understood so weakly or poorly? But most of
all I myself would say answering that the demon prophesied nothing, as we also
showed before; 24.3 and if he happened even to have discoursed things worthy
of prophecy, not even thus ought one to suppose that the demon brought up
spirits and souls from Hades; for also Caiaphas upon the conspiracy against
Christ prophesied, having said that "it is expedient for us that one human
being die on behalf of the people and the whole nation not perish." 24.4 These
things he did not say from himself, as the most wise John published, but
happening to be high priest of that year he prophesied. 24.5 And if this one
who took up unjust counsels against the Lord nevertheless does not prophesy
from himself, but being moved, I suppose, by another makes the refutation
against himself, not even if some other then does some such thing must one
attend that on this account he has taken hold of a more divine power, nor must
one endure if someone should say that by demonic divination he seems to bring
up souls from Hades and armies of angels as God. 24.6 For one must listen to
what sort of things Moses, prophesying in Deuteronomy, declares about such
prophets, saying "And if a prophet should arise in you or a dreamer of a dream
and should give you a sign or a wonder, and the sign or the wonder should come
which he spoke toward you saying: 24.7 let us go and let us serve other gods
whom you have not known, you shall not hear the words of that prophet," he
says, "or of the one dreaming that dream; because the Lord your God is testing
you, to see if you love the Lord your God." 24.8 But here presenting the dyad
of Father and of the only-begotten son, he was naming one the Lord who tests,
and another besides this to be the Lord and God who is loved, so that from a
dyad he might show the one godhead and the true god-begetting. 24.9 And if
that prophet who promised the sign or to show the wonder is altogether to be
rejected because he introduces the unlawful idolatry, even if the power of the
sign operating runs ahead, how much more must one not believe a demon
promising to send for souls from Hades and spirits of the righteous? =24.10=
But nor would someone speaking impiously say that while those were seeking an
oracle God raised Samuel to test the unjust man


wishing, so that he might inscribe the drama upon the promise of the demon and
through this pretext destroy some by deceit; for it is necessary from all to
guard the pollution of the divination, as the lawgiver ordained. =24.11= But
does someone not think the causes of omen-watching polluting, and the oracles
of the belly-myther and symbols of omens? Let him hear how a little before
having passed over he dogmatized again: "And if," he says, "you should enter
into the land which the Lord your God gives you, you shall not learn to do
according to the abominations of those nations. =24.12= There shall not be
found in you one who purifies his son and his daughter in fire, and one
seeking an oracle by divination, and one taking omens and watching birds with
drugs of enchanters, an enchantment, a belly-myther and a wonder-watcher, one
asking the dead. For everyone who does these things is an abomination to the
Lord." =24.13= And after this indicating that also to the aliens on account of
these things he threatens a birth of death and destruction, he added
immediately: "For on account of these abominations the Lord will utterly
destroy them from you." =24.14= It appears then that the divination of the
belly-myther has been yoked together with all the causes of idolatry together,
as if with a fever of dungs lying within, raisers of sickness, and miserable
kindlings, accustomed to give birth by swelling and destruction to the
pestilential disease. =24.15= And if indeed he also named the unsmiling
visions, as many as are related through the belly-mythers, wonder-watchings,
he established that there is nothing true in them; =24.16= from here, I think,
again in Leviticus he brought out a more grim declaration against them, adding
in these words: "And a man or a woman," saying, "<who> becomes a belly-myther
or an enchanter among you, they shall be put to death with death; you shall
stone them with stones, they are liable." 25.1 What then do those say who set
it down that the reputation-seekings of Origen are to be embraced? Does he
order the belly-mythers to be destroyed as causes of most hateful practices,
or on the contrary as counselors of good works? If then they are self-workers
of impious dramas, justly the lawgiver drives out the accursed, who announce
nothing true. 25.2 But if they are inventors of most beautiful actions,
superfluously the law voted against them. For if they bring up souls from
Hades, and those arriving here proclaim the future, they furnish those in need
beneficially; not justly then did the lawgiver ordain that the benefactors be
stoned. 25.3 So that of two things one is necessary, either to make these
voices useless, or to slander the words of the belly-myther as stale and
false. But what shall we say? Do the divine scriptures opine against
themselves? And does the one vote fight the many? And if there is also some
fight, the many prevail. 25.4 And indeed from the opposites thus with
like-voiced symphony they vote against the belly-mythers as doing unholy
things. No less still also in the fourth of Kingdoms it has been taken up that
Manasseh by excess of impiety, besides the things in which he was killing the
just and was worshiping all kinds of idol-formed materials, "made for
himself," it says, "belly-mythers and knowers." 25.5 And conversely again king
Josiah who was distinguished by virtue of justice pulled down all the
hand-made establishments together, and having reduced them to ash with fire he
made them vanish; and indeed besides these "he removed," it said, "the
belly-mythers and the knowers and the teraphim and the idols and the
carsaein"; then also after a few he says that he also "burned them," as
unlawful things clearly. 25.6 Therefore if the writer of each of the two
writings is the same, and here he biographies two persons of kings, then of
these he introduces the one as unjust, and the other as just, but he blames
the one furnished with unmixed injustice, wherefore besides the other things
he was also holding to the belly-mythers, and he praises the one awakened with
divine zeal because he also removed the belly-mythers besides all the enemies;
and if these things are so, it is established that he was not publishing
voices fighting against himself. 25.7 Nor again did the


Holy Spirit say that Samuel had come up, but it was relating the words of the
belly-myther, as she thought and in seeming was boasting, winging the ruler
with unclear fantasizings; for it would not have been fighting against itself
and all the other scriptures. 25.8 But not only do these sayings refute these
things, but still also another notable witness votes with them, the prophet
Isaiah; in one place forbidding such pollutions, and in another advising most
rightly he sounds to those obedient to him: "And if they say toward you: Seek
the belly-mythers, and those who voice from the earth, the empty-talkers who
voice from the belly, is not a nation toward God?" 25.9 Then what after these
things having added does he add? "Why do they seek," he said, "the dead about
the living? For he has given a law for help, so that they may speak not as
this word, about which there are no gifts to give." =25.10= With an obvious
holy-voice he proclaimed away the race of the belly-mythers; and if he
declared such a council empty-talking, he showed without dispute that no one
of these ever says anything at all true, but they fashion the deceit with
empty false-speaking, and with stale empty-talk they cheat the ignorant.
=25.11= Whence correspondingly he was mocking those who ask the dead about the
living; for they themselves, being wholly dead and maimed, the empty-talkers,
are shaped to serve the voices of the dead. And the prophet was testifying
crying that an unbreakable law lies out about these things, so that they may
not be led according to those who utter with stale light-talkings; =25.12= for
the highest guard of law, I think, has been established to call the help from
heaven, but not to collect the succor from the earthly things, looking into
the very underground things. 26.1 But Origen who thought he interpreted all
the scriptures does not pretend to know these testimonies, nor does he set
them as he ought, but he wheels in those that differ in nothing in nothing
from the question, and he passes over those that belong by nature itself; for
either he seems to do this by ignorance mindlessly, or to work it by ill-will
boldly. 26.2 But also as if amphibolizing having said that "What then does a
belly-myther do here? What does a belly-myther do about the bringing up of the
soul of the righteous?", by lack of argument he fled aloft, attributing to
another this which he himself did; and having committed the whole to the
Scripture itself, he is strong that Samuel has been brought up, but he no
longer dares to repeat the "who brought him up." 26.3 And I think that also
later being refuted by those who think most rightly always, answering again by
a second interpretation, he said that the place of the belly-myther has been
kept; then he says also that "it has been written that the belly-myther
brought someone up", and "that Saul said to Samuel has been recorded"; 26.4
and having tautologized many such frequent things with much nonsense, he adds
again: "But it has not said," he says, "whether he has come up willingly; for
you do not have it lying," he said, "whether the belly-myther brought him up;
since let someone refute me having read the Scripture." 26.5 Therefore being
refuted face to face he denied white what he opined before by folly; for there
he determined that the Scripture itself rather, but not the woman, had said
the "Whom shall I bring up for you?"; but here being caught clearly he took
care to flee the charge by forgetfulness. 26.6 Thus everywhere he sets out
opinions fighting against himself nakedly, just as indeed here too having
fought in knowledge he was caught, having been hurried to run from the
criminal judgment. Since then being dragged unwilling he confessed that the
woman did not bring up the soul of the prophet, let him say who the one who
brought this up is (for he remained unmoved having determined only that it has
been brought up). 26.7 At any rate the pythoness herself happened to be alone,
and Saul who was asking that woman to bring up the prophet. But this one would
not be the self-worker of the drama; for he would not have needed the
belly-myther, arriving to her by night. And if the action has come to be, the
thing done must not be attributed to some other. So that of two things one is
necessary to say, either


that this has never come to be in any way or that through the belly-myther and
the demon the man standing upright has been brought up. 26.8 Does he then,
seeming by a disclaimer to deny his own voices, by another method introduce
the same reputation-seeking deceitfully? But through the things by which he
seems to defend himself, through these he introduces the lie as truth. 26.9
And the so many and such legislative votings do not shame him, nor agreeing
prophetic testimonies, nor the remaining like-minded symphonies of the
history-writing; but having reasoned falsely past the all-holy utterances, by
choice he confirms the words of the belly-myther, prophesying newer
instruments of idolatry, and wheeling in an impious divination of demons.
=26.10= Nor does even the compound name persuade him of what sort of conduct
it has; for the belly-myther is interpreted from showing a myth fashioned in a
belly, and the composition of the myth has been shaped, being tent-pitched
persuasively inside a belly, and it voices out not truth but the lie
straightway. At any rate those who have associated with various references of
words know far better of what sort its race is. 27.1 And even if especially
fevering with Greek superstition he introduces a divination secretly,
nevertheless I think one must not decline from here too to refute his
mindlessness through a few things, as is due. 27.2 For the rhetorical
art-writings show clearly that a myth is a fashioning composed with
soul-leading toward some of the things in life that differ by use; and a
fashioning, I suppose, has been said from its having been fashioned, they say,
by common agreement; for it would no longer be thought a myth, if it had come
to be according to truth. 27.3 And if myth-making is a composition of an
offhand fashioning, it has been convicted in deed as far from truth, and in
word it images materials of facts while falling short in the action; for it
seems to argue persuasively the things that are not as things that are, and by
a narrative it introduces a fashioned imitation; it therefore gives form to
unsubstantial facts, no firm seat at all lying under as far as possible. 27.4
And the fashion-working of myth seems to me to differ in nothing from a
lifeless shadow-painting; for according to a likeness, I think, of the tablet
it is marked out by the line itself; with a similar resemblance at any rate it
personifies a subject, looking toward some part, and it shapes 27.5 the facts
by the imitation with taut lines. It therefore keeps a disagreement upon each
especially of the rhythms, and it writes a peak of war and an end, like a
base, and speaking lips and properties of voices, and laughters and weepings,
or ruling public-speakings or drinking-parties and revels or bacchic rites or
initiations or unchaste drunkennesses or erotic outrages or robber seizures or
incurable destructions or indiscriminate slaughters or hypocrisies of
poverties or inflows of wealths. 27.6 With probability-talk then it
characterizes all things, and with resembling personification it declares
powerless actions; for the myth-makers, gathering the flowery words,
themselves work various forms of colors and weave them with the
personification; then having tinted them with sweet words, they gather the
whole narrative of the myth, and as animal-painters they image the season of
the sight, wax-painting with red and flowery colors; 27.7 and hastening to
make melody with words and figures material characters, they fall short in
deed of the truth itself. But these men variegate in such a manner the images
of the myths; and from here some musical harmony is constituted and an
invention of poetic conceit. 28.1 At any rate Plato, being asked, determines
the words of music to be [of words]; "And of words there is a double form," he
said, "the one true, and the other false; and one must educate in both, but
first in the false." For he said first that the children are charmed by an
enchantment of myths. "And this somewhere, to say it as the whole, is false,
but there is also true in it. 28.2 Therefore a beginning of every work is a
greatest thing, especially


also for anyone young and tender? For then most of all it is fashioned and a
type sinks in, which whoever wishes may stamp in upon each." And through such
sayings he tries to show that there would be no need to pour unmixed
false-speakings upon the hearings of the newly come, since the types of
ill-opinion being stamped into youths just now love to become unmoved and hard
to wash out; 28.3 wherefore correspondingly he adds again: "Shall we then so
easily permit," he said, "the children to hear myths fashioned by the chance
people under the chance people and to take in their souls for the most part
opinions contrary to those which when they have been completed we shall think
they must have? Not in any way at all," he has said that "we shall permit it.
28.4 First then, as it seems, we must superintend the myth-makers, and
whichever beautiful myth they make must be accepted, and whichever not must be
rejected. And we shall persuade the nurses and mothers to tell the accepted
ones to the children, and to fashion their souls with the myths much more than
the bodies with the hands. And of those they now tell, the many must be thrown
out." 28.5 Then since, being asked about the corresponding difference of each,
he was saying "in the greater myths the lesser also are recognized," when
Adeimantus asked again, showing that he did not know even these, answering
again he said: "Those which Hesiod, I said, and Homer were telling us; for
these somewhere composing false myths for human beings both were telling and
tell." 29.1 If then he declares the verses of Homer and of Hesiod false myths,
which most of all also for the sake of education lead the hearers in soul, and
indeed also by beauty of diction awaken the thought toward complete eloquence,
how much more will someone determine that as many words as the demon-possessed
old woman uttered are false myth-makings, when even the name of the
belly-myther dictates this mind? 29.2 For if children of Greeks, who also make
accurate the name-makings of each thing, name the myths false fashionings in
the Greek voice, it is consistent from the name to suspect the thing against
which it has been strictly arranged. 29.3 But the word-makings of Aesop know
such fashionings of myths, as even the youths in many ways playing urbanely in
the games play, and Corybant-raving old women sing with babbling words
(sometimes they do these things wetting the throat with much wine, and
attending to the cup and drink they talk nonsense measurelessly). 29.4 Yet
those who think they honor habits of philosophy were also slandering the
musical poems as false forms of myths, as is due. 30.1 If then the name itself
has been arranged rightly against the thing, the belly-myther seems to fashion
a myth in a belly; for she does not voice from the natural mind soundly, but
the demon lurking in the most inward parts feeds upon her and injures the
thought, and from the belly he sounds out mythical fashionings putting them
together, and transforming himself into various forms he leads the soul by
diverse appearances. 30.2 And since he is changed with all kinds of figures
