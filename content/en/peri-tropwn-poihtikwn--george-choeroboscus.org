OF GEORGE CHOEROBOSCUS, ON POETIC TROPES.

There are twenty-seven poetic tropes of all old and new writing: allegory,
metaphor, catachresis, metalepsis, hyperbaton, anastrophe, synecdoche,
syllepsis, onomatopoeia, the coined, antonomasia, metonymy, antiphrasis,
periphrasis, ellipsis, pleonasm, epanalepsis, preeminence, hyperbole, enigma,
parable, antapodosis, personification, example, irony, schema and
hysterologia. And they are called poetic tropes because they abound among the
poets, going beyond common usage. {1αʹ. ALLEGORY.}1 Allegory is an expression
saying one thing and presenting another meaning, as the saying in the divine
Scripture, in which God says to the serpent, "Cursed are you from all the
cattle"; for the speech is toward the serpent, but we take it analogously of
the devil, that is, allegorically. And this is employed both for dignity and
for reverence: for dignity, as in the matters concerning Hera, in which Zeus
says to her, Do you not remember when you hung from on high; and from your two
feet I let down two anvils; and about your hands I cast a golden bond? 245
whose meaning is this, at the limit of the air both earth and water are
joined, which he also calls anvils as heavier than the other elements, and a
golden bond the brightness poured about the air from the solar light, with
which the air is united. And for reverence, as "he loosed her maiden girdle,"
instead of he corrupted her; as also in Callimachus, "and you kindled the
fire, until it has gone forth with much flame," and again "hold back the
raving horses from the course, lest you turn a second time, lest they smash
the chariot at the turning-post, and you tumble headlong." These things have
not been said in the proper sense; for his speech is neither about fire nor
about horse-racing, but as if ashamed to say what he wished, he used allegory.
{1βʹ. METAPHOR.}1 Metaphor is an expression transferred from one thing to
another, or transferred from what is said in the proper sense for the sake of
likeness or of emphasis, and it has four kinds. For it is either carried over
from animate things to animate, or from inanimate to inanimate, or from
inanimate to animate, or from animate to inanimate. And from animate to
animate, as when someone calls the king a shepherd of peoples; for
shepherding, being of animate things, has again been transferred to animate
things, since just as the flocks are subject to the shepherds, so also those
under the hand to the kings; for a shepherd in the proper sense is called the
herder of sheep; both then are animate, both the king and the herder of sheep.
And from inanimate to inanimate, as when someone, having hidden a coal of fire
in ash, says he has preserved a seed of fire. For seed, said in the proper
sense of the fruits that are sown, has now been taken of the heap of coals; or
if he should say much flame has been poured upon the wood; for pouring belongs
to 246 liquids. And from inanimate to animate, as when Odysseus says to
Ajax, for such a tower has perished for them; for the tower, being inanimate,
has been said of Ajax, who is animate. And "the sea saw and fled"; for seeing
is said in the proper sense of animate things, but the sea is inanimate. And
from animate to inanimate, as when someone calls the ridge of the mountain a
peak or a head; for peak and head are said of animate things. And again,
"and the spear-point darted through, raging." And again, and many in the space
between, before they tasted fair flesh, stood in the earth, longing to glut
themselves with flesh; for raging and longing and to glut, being animate, have
been said of inanimate spears. Likewise also "on the peaks of a mountain," and
"the feet of many-fountained Ida," when one ought to have said ridge and
foothill to say. And there is also a fifth kind of metaphor, as from action
to action, for example already perhaps that man was weaving a device in his
heart. For weaving, said of woven fabrics, has now been transferred to the
fashioning of counsel. {1γʹ. CATACHRESIS.}1 Catachresis is a phrase
transferred from what was first named in the proper and etymological sense to
the unnamed, as when we call a flat surface of bronze a pyxis; for a pyxis is
called that which has been made from boxwood, but since those constructed from
any other material whatsoever are unnamed, we call these too pyxides, using
the name by catachresis. And a man-slayer in the proper sense is one who has
slain a man, but by catachresis it is said also of a woman. And a trierarch in
the proper sense is the commander of a trireme, 247 but by catachresis also
that of a bireme and of a pentereme. Of this sort also is "he poured nectar as
wine"; for pouring wine, said in the proper sense of wine, has now been said
of nectar. Likewise also the neck of a jar and the knee of a reed and the
belly of a ship and the tongue of a shoe and such things. And to calculate in
the proper sense is to measure with pebbles, but by catachresis also with the
fingers. For these things, said of those properly named, are said by
catachresis also of the unnamed. And catachresis differs from metaphor, in
that metaphor is said from a named thing to a named thing again, but
catachresis from a named thing to what is wholly unnamed, as when someone
should call one catching fish in a lake or in rivers a sea-fisher; for in the
proper sense a sea-fisher is said of the one upon the sea, but since the one
catching in the rivers is unnamed, we call this one too a sea-fisher, using
the name by catachresis. {1Δʹ. METALEPSIS.}1 Metalepsis is a phrase taking
over the homonymy from what is so in the proper sense, or making clear the
homonym through the synonymy, as when someone should call one running swiftly
sharp at the course, or should call the quickly-affecting humor a sharp
humor; for sharp in the proper sense is said of the whetted sword, whence also
"he sharpened" is employed of a knife or of some other sword. Or as in Homer,
and from there again I sent it forth among the swift islands. For with the
swift, the sharp is synonymous according to motion, and the sharp is
homonymous according to shape. Of this sort also is, And Teucer using
spareness of bows; that is, accuracy and good aim; for with spareness the
accuracy according to giving is also synonymous, and with it the accuracy
according to art is homonymous, that is, good aim. 248 {1εʹ. HYPERBATON.}1
Hyperbaton is an expression going over from what precedes to what follows
itself, as when someone, instead of saying I call upon the Lord, should say
upon the Lord I am called. {1 ʹ. ANASTROPHE.}1 Anastrophe is an expression
turning back from the subject to what lies before, with no part of speech
falling between, as when someone, instead of saying until coming to us, should
say us until coming.
{1ζʹ. SYNECDOCHE.}1 Synecdoche is an expression making clear together through
one thing another thought as well, as when, peace existing, instead of saying
there is no war one should say, there are no arms now, or arms are idle now.
Or synecdoche is an expression or phrase not uttered according to the full
sense, but needing some thought from outside, and it has four differences: for
either from the whole it makes clear the part, as dried oxen instead of
shields from ox-hides, and hide is a part of the whole; and a bronze spear;
for bronze is a part of the whole spear. Or from the part the all, as the
white-armed goddess Hera; for it hints from the part of the hands that she is
white as a whole. And silver-footed Thetis. Or from the material the result,
as and he himself put on gold about his flesh; instead of a golden panoply. Or
from the symbol the proper thing, as he gave you a scepter to be honored above
all. Instead of kingship, of which the scepter is a symbol.
{1ηʹ. SYLLEPSIS.}1 Syllepsis is a phrase taken from some one proper thing
according to 249 two or even more; or a phrase drawing what was done by
one upon another, as Boreas and Zephyrus, who blow from Thrace; for Boreas
alone blows from Thrace, but he says sylleptically that Zephyrus also blows
from there; and again and the two went limping, attendants of Ares, the
war-abiding son of Tydeus and godlike Odysseus; for Diomedes alone was
limping, having been wounded in the flat of the foot, but Odysseus had been
struck in the side. And he says sylleptically that this one too is limping.
{1θʹ. ONOMATOPOEIA.}1 Onomatopoeia is an expression that has come about
according to imitation and a certain likeness of what is signified, as when
someone names the indistinct noises voices, which are of fire, of wood, of
stone and of the like; and as the divine Scripture says, "the clouds gave a
voice," making clear the thunder, naming this a voice on account of the
ringing about us from above. And a voice in the proper sense is said to be
that which proceeds from mind, since voice is also etymologized as light of
mind. Or onomatopoeia is an expression or part of speech coined according to
imitation of the sounds produced or of a voice; of sounds, as the
flashing-eyed helmet hummed as it fell to the ground. And again so his eye
sizzled about the olive-wood stake. And of voice these, and they all held up
their hands to Athena with a cry of ololuge; and again and I heard the lowing
of cattle stalled in the courtyard. {1ιʹ. THE COINED.}1 The coined is an
expression said according to a certain likeness of that from which it is said,
as when we should say of one who has looked askance at us with 250 anger,
so-and-so looked askance at us as a lion. {1ιαʹ. ANTONOMASIA.}1 Antonomasia is
an expression making clear the proper name itself through epithets or tokens,
as when of two or even more men known to us and having the same proper name,
we should wish to make mention of one of them to one another, and we should
not say the proper name because of the homonymy, but should name him from his
accidents, saying perhaps the bronze-worker or the carpenter, and if he also
has bodily afflictions, saying the lame, it may be, or the bald.
{1ιβʹ. METONYMY.}1 Metonymy is when from the containing things we rename the
contained, according to the divine Scripture, which says, "Be instructed, all
you who judge the earth," instead of those in the earth; or from the
inhabitants the inhabited places, as when we should call wantonness a Sodomite
work; for from the wanton inhabitants at that time Sodom has the reproach
brought upon it; or metonymy is an expression making clear the synonym through
the homonymy, as for having pierced the inwards they held them over
Hephaestus; for Hephaestus among the Greeks is both the daimon and the fire,
which, inasmuch as they seem to have a certain dim difference, are homonyms;
for they call the daimon more immaterial and more divine, but inasmuch as this
mythical Hephaestus is in the proper sense nothing else than the fire, they
are called synonyms. Of this sort also is then mighty Ares, sent forth, 251
here; instead of the iron. And when the wine is called Dionysus, as wine
persuaded me, highest of daimons. {1ιγʹ. ANTIPHRASIS.}1 Antiphrasis is an
expression signifying the contrary through contraries, as when someone should
call the blind much-seeing, or vinegar a little sweet, or he is white as an
Ethiopian. {1ιδʹ. PERIPHRASIS.}1 Periphrasis is a superfluous phrase
signifying one thing through more words, as when instead of saying by God
someone should say by the fearful day of God. For he signified nothing more
through these many words except God. And phrase, periphrasis, metaphrasis,
ecphrasis, antiphrasis and symphrasis differ. For phrase is simply called the
expression, but periphrasis the superfluous phrase, as call for me the might
of Heracles, instead of Heracles, and metaphrasis the change of the words
according to quantity, either of more or of fewer, taking place with
rhetorical beauty, as the Metaphrast shows us in the metaphrases; and
paraphrase the change of the words according to the quantity of the same, as
sing, goddess, the wrath, paraphrasing he said, tell the anger, O Muse. And
ecphrasis is the detailed narration, which vividly and almost bringing before
our sight what is narrated, how it has of arrangement and of beauty, as the
ecphrasis of the shrine of Alexandria or of certain other cities. And
antiphrasis that signifying the contrary through contrary words, as a silver
Ethiopian. And symphrasis the following-along of the speech or a composition
of words, as nobilissimus supremus. 252 {1ιεʹ. ELLIPSIS.}1 Ellipsis is a
speech not uttered according to the full sense, but granting us to understand
what follows, as when someone mourning and lamenting should say, I beat my
breast with both, namely hands, or as in the poet "striking with both," that
is, hands. {1ι ʹ. PLEONASM.}1 Pleonasm is when an expression abounds
signifying nothing more, except the same, as is opposite contrary, and before
over-against. {1ιζʹ. EPANALEPSIS.}1 Epanalepsis is an expression taken twice
for the sake of intensification, that is, of increase, as when someone,
confirming to us the knowledge of a known matter, should say, I know the
matter, I know, saying I know twice; or should say it is an evil to sin, an
evil. {1ιηʹ. PREEMINENCE.}1 Preeminence is that which, after the common
signification, is assigned particularly by way of superiority to the one who
owes it, as what was said in the Gospel by the Lord, "Tell my disciples and
Peter." For the name of Peter has been taken here by way of preeminence, since
he was also included through Tell my disciples; for he too is one of the
disciples. {1ιθʹ. HYPERBOLE.}1 Hyperbole is a phrase going beyond the truth
for the sake of increase, as when someone should say of one running swiftly,
that he runs as the wind; or hyperbole is a speech raising itself above the
truth for the sake of emphasis or of likeness. Of emphasis, as they ran upon
the fruit of the ears of corn and did not break them; 253 and "brighter than
the gleam of fire," and "whiter than snow"; for they make clear the being very
much aloft in running and the being altogether luminous and the being very
white. And of likeness, as "like to the winds in running," and he was
adamantine as to the body, a phoenix as to the straightness of the body, and
if anything similar. {1κʹ. ENIGMA.}1 An enigma is a speech having in itself
the thought dark and veiled, as the question put by Samson to the foreigners,
in which he says, out of the eater came forth food, signifying the lion which
he killed, and what was found in its mouth from a honeycomb of bees; and as
that which says, not to step over a yoke, instead of just, and not to keep
hook-clawed ones, that is, to flee robbers; not to eat the black-tail, that
is, not to send forth a lie; for this is later darkened; not to stir the fire
with a sword, instead of not to provoke one who is angry. And it is also
called an enigma even from the contrary, as a man and not a man, as the eunuch
because of not being able to beget one like himself, and a bird and not a
bird, the bat because of being leather-winged and toothed; sitting and not
sitting; for she was on her back; a stone and not a stone, the pumice clearly,
because of floating upon the water; he threw and did not throw, because of
throwing indeed, but missing. And it also comes about according to accident,
as there are two sisters, of whom the one bears the other, and this one who
bore her is in turn born of her; and day and night are so called. And the
enigma differs from allegory, in that that is taken for the sake of
exhortation or dissuasion or dignity or reverence, but this for the sake of
unclarity alone, and in that that is much clearer than this. 254
{1καʹ. PARABLE.}1 A parable is a phrase bringing the thought before the sight
through like and known things, as is the Lord's parable concerning the
prodigal, in which he shows the loving-kindness of God and the goodness of his
own father, and how he receives those who repent. {1κβʹ. ANTAPODOSIS.}1
Antapodosis is a further teaching of the matters laid down in the parable, as
that we should say, in the manner of speech which the father of the prodigal
used, in such a manner God also is fitted to men, and we should show,
comparing the matters of the parable, the likeness. {1κγʹ. PERSONIFICATION.}1
Personification is, as when someone attaches to inanimate things persons and
at times also fitting words, as "the birds recount the glory of God."
{1κδʹ. EXAMPLE.}1 An example is a speech having toward some other thing the
force of a comparative showing, as what was said to Solomon, "Imitate the ant,
O sluggard, and emulate his ways," exhorting us to emulate his laboriousness,
not his nature. {1κεʹ. IRONY.}1 Irony is a diminutive speech making clear the
contrary through the contrary. And of irony there are four kinds, mockery,
sneering, sarcasm, urbanity. Mockery is a speech coming forth with a smile, as
when someone, laughing, should call the one who has thrown away his shield a
brave warrior. And sneering is a tearing-down speech taking place with a
snorting of the nostrils, as when, reproaching one caught in an evil, we
should say, bringing forth breath through the nostrils, you have done a fine
255 work, O companion, and a necessary one and of a prudent man. And sarcasm
is a speech bringing forth the truth through kind words, as when, jeering at
one who has fallen into evils in a presumption of honor and is for this
dishonored, we should say, you have brought yourself into great glory and
honor, O companion. And urbanity is a speech tearing down from itself, as when
we should say to one who understands nothing, you, O companion, are the glory
of words. {1κ ʹ. SCHEMA.}1 Schema is a solecism having an apology, as that we
should say, the lord John, whom God will have mercy on, is a good man.
{1κζʹ. HYSTEROLOGIA.}1 Hysterologia is a first speech said later and for this
reason named prothysteron, as when we should say, so-and-so was well perfected
and lived well, instead of saying he lived well and was well perfected; for
first one lives and later is perfected. {1[FILLING-OUT.}1 A filling-out is an
expression taken from what is superfluous for the sake of ornament or of
meter. Of ornament, as "and someone would speak thus"; for the "ke" is
superfluous, and without it the meter is also preserved; and of meter, as and
the leaders of the ships I will tell, and all the ships beforehand. For here
the preposition pro was becoming necessary because of the meter. And again
Pandarus, to whom Apollo himself also gave the bow. The conjunction "and" here
too having been taken because of the meter. These conjunctions, though
superfluous here too, were nevertheless taken in order that the meter be
preserved. 256 {1WITTICISM.}1 What is a witticism? An urbane speech, in which
both the speaker and the hearer are cheered. {1EPEXEGESIS.}1 Epexegesis is a
clarification of an unclear expression or speech; of an expression, as take an
akinakes, which is a Persian sword; of a speech, as the house is not firmly
made one's own; I mean, that those remaining in this quickly pass on.
{1APO KOINOU.}1 Apo koinou is an expression said once, and taken from outside,
as going he asked the men, and especially Charidemus and Hipparchus; and Homer
he was begging all the Achaeans, and the sons of Atreus especially. For apo
koinou, upon those the "he asked," and upon this the "he was begging."
{1HETEROGENES.}1 Heterogenes is a shift from gender to gender toward what is
thought, as the things about the sea are fearful, being deserts; and the
Theologian, "and putting in from himself a breath, which the Word knows as a
noetic soul." {1HETEROPROSOPON.}1 Heteroprosopon is when we put forward
another person saying what we were about to say, as all the Egyptians cry out
our injustice; or when we ourselves speak, but address ourselves to another
and not to the one who did the wrong, as and I, knowing someone harming his
neighbor, said to him, that you wrong your neighbor.]
