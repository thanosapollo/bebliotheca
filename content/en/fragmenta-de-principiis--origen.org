Menam (p. 10, 1–4 Schw.): That the creatures are co-eternal with God, from the
first discourse of the book On First Principles. (And how is it not absurd
that God, not having something of the things fitting to him, has come into
having it? But if there is not when he was not almighty, these things must
always be, through which he is almighty, and they always were being ruled by
him, using him as ruler.) 6 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 10, 1–6 Schw.): From the
same (scil. first) discourse. (Thus then I think it would also be well said
concerning the Savior that he is "image of the goodness of God", but not
self-good. And perhaps the Son also is good, but not as simply good. And just
as "he is image of the invisible God" and according to this God, but not
concerning whom Christ himself says "that they may know you the only true
God", so "image of the goodness", but not as the Father unvaryingly good.) 7
Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 10, 7–10 Schw.): That having said the Holy Spirit also
with the Son is a creature he numbered them with the other creatures,
wherefore he also calls them ministerial living beings, from the first
discourse of the book On First Principles. (That therefore everything
whatsoever besides the Father and God of the universe is generated, we are
persuaded from the sequence.) 8 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 10, 10–14 Schw.): and
after some things: (And the Hebrew said that the two six-winged Seraphim in
Isaiah, crying one to the other and saying: "holy holy holy Lord Sabaoth" are
the only-begotten of God and the Holy Spirit. But we think that also that in
the ode of Habakkuk: "in the midst of two living beings you shall be known"
has been said concerning Christ and Holy Spirit.) 9 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 08,
6–3 Schw.): From the first discourse of the book On First Principles. (That
God and Father holding together all things reaches unto each of the beings,
imparting to each from his own the being which it is, but less than the Father
the Son reaching upon the rational things alone (for he is second of the
Father), and still less the Holy Spirit extending upon the holy ones alone; so
that according to this the power of the Father is greater than the Son and the
Holy Spirit, and that of the Son more than the Holy Spirit, and again the
power of the Holy Spirit differing more than the other holy things.) 10 Just.
Ep. ad Menam(p. 10, 5–7 Schw.): From the same (scil. first) discourse. (All
the genera and the species always were, but another will say also that
according to one in number; except that in either way it is shown that God did
not begin to create having been idle at some time.) 11 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p.
10, 8–211, 7 Schw.): Concerning the preexistence of the souls, from the first
discourse of the book On First Principles. (From their own cause of those not
attending to themselves watchfully there come to be more quickly or more
slowly falls, and unto more or unto less, so that from this cause, a divine
judgment measuring along with the better or worse movements of each also the
according to worth, one will have in the coming arrangement an angelic rank or
a ruling power or an authority over certain ones or a throne over those being
ruled as kings or a lordship over slaves; but those not having fallen very
much will have the economy and help under those mentioned. And thus according
to the most part from those under the principalities and the authorities and
the thrones and the lordships, and perhaps sometimes even from them
themselves, the race of men will be constituted in the world according to each
one.) 12 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 211, 8–12 Schw.): From the same (scil. first)
discourse, that if the demons should change toward the better, they will at
some time fill up humanity. (And I think that from those subjected to the
worse principalities and authorities and world-rulers according to each world
or certain worlds, some more quickly, <and some more slowly> being benefited
and wishing from themselves to change, they will at some time fill up
humanity.) 13 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 1, 0–3 Schw.): From the first discourse of
the same book, that the sun and the moon and the stars are ensouled. (And that
the soul of the sun is older than its binding into the body, after reasoning
from a comparison of man toward it I think I can also from here demonstrate
from the scriptures.) 14 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 1, 4–30 Schw.): From the same
(scil. first) discourse. ("Better to depart and to be with Christ, for it is
much rather better." For I think that the sun would say that "better to depart
and to be with Christ, for it is much rather better". And Paul: "but to remain
in the flesh is more necessary on account of you", but the sun: "to remain in
this heavenly body is more necessary on account of the revelation of the
children of God". And the same things must be said also concerning the moon
and the remaining stars.) 17b Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 11, 17–3 Schw.): That the
soul sometimes also chooses the watery life, from the second discourse of the
book On First Principles. (The soul flowing away from the good and inclining
toward evil and coming to be in it unto more, if it should not turn back, is
made brutish by folly and is made beast-like by wickedness.) And after a few
things: (and it chooses toward being made irrational also the watery, that I
may so speak, life; and perhaps according to the worth of the fall unto more
of the evil it puts on a body of this or that irrational living being.) 18
Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 11, 13–16 Schw.): Concerning the leading down of those
from above into a body. (The world then being so most varied and containing so
many different rational beings, what else must one say has become the cause of
its coming to subsist except the variety of the falling away of those not
similarly flowing away from the unity?) 19 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 11, 4–7
Schw.): From the same (scil. second) discourse, that there will be a complete
putting-off of the bodies. (And if those subjected to Christ will at the end
be subjected also to God, all will put off the bodies. And I think that then
there will be a dissolving of the nature of the bodies into the non-being, it
being about to subsist a second time if again rational beings should sink
down.) 220 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 10, 15–0 Schw.): From the second discourse of
the book On First Principles, that he calls the Lord a mere man. For this
reason Christ also became man, having obtained this from a deed of virtue, as
the prophet witnesses saying: "You loved righteousness and hated lawlessness;
for this reason God, your God, anointed you with oil of gladness beyond your
fellows". And it was fitting that the one never having been separated from the
only-begotten should share the name with the only-begotten and be glorified
together with him. 21 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 21,2 9–14 Schw.): From the same
(scil. second) discourse concerning the same. (Just as the Savior "came to
save the lost", yet when the lost is saved, it is no longer lost; so the soul
which he came to save, as to save the lost, the saved soul no longer remains a
soul. It must still be examined whether just as the lost was when it had not
been lost and there will sometime be when it will not be lost, so also the
soul was when it was not a soul and there will be when it will not be a soul.)
23b Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 212, 3–8 Schw.): From the second discourse of the
book On First Principles, how mind became soul and soul having been purified
becomes mind. (From the falling away and the cooling from living by the spirit
the now so-called soul has come to be, being also receptive of the return unto
what it was in the beginning. Which I think is said by the prophet in:
"Return, my soul, unto your rest"; so that this whole is mind.) 24 Just. Ep.
ad Menam(p. 190, 7–14 Schw. [† i] and p. 209, 1–6 Schw. [† ii]): i And he adds
also this to his own blasphemies in the first discourse of his On First
Principles, having spoken on the very wording thus: (In the beginning as
conceived God caused to subsist by his will so great a number of intelligent
essences as he was able to suffice for; for one must say that even the power
of God is limited and one must not on a pretext of praise take away its
circumscription. For if the divine power is infinite, it is necessary that it
not even know itself; for by nature the infinite is incomprehensible. He has
made therefore so many things as he was able to grasp and to have them under
hands and to hold together under his providence; just as he also prepared so
much matter as he was able to order.) ii That the power of God and Father is
limited, from the second discourse of the same book. (For to be limited ...
one must not take away.) And after a few things: (He has made therefore ... as
much as he was able to order.) 27 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 211, 28–212, Schw.):
From the third discourse of the same book concerning the same (scil. that
there will be a complete putting-off of the bodies). (God being said "to
become all things in all", just as we cannot leave remaining evil, when God
"becomes all things in all", nor irrational living beings, lest God also come
to be in evil and in irrational living beings, but not even soulless things,
lest God also in them, when "he becomes all things", so neither bodies, which
by their own nature are soulless.) 29 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 2213, 8–10 Schw.):
From the same (scil. fourth) discourse. (Who having a mind will define that a
first and second and third day, both evening and morning, have come to be
without sun and moon and stars?) 30 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 13, 1–7 Schw.): From
the fourth discourse of the same book, that Christ must be crucified also for
demons, and this many times unto the ages that will be. (But even if someone
should inquire as far as the passion, he will seem to do a daring thing
seeking it concerning the heavenly place. But if there are "spiritual things
of wickedness in the heavenlies", see whether just as here we are not ashamed
to confess him crucified for the taking down of those things which he took
down through having suffered, so also there granting that the similar comes to
be and unto the next "until the completion of the whole age" we shall not
fear.) 31 Marcell. Anc. in Euseb. C. Marcell. I 4 (p. 21, 16–22 Kl.): ... from
the sayings of Origen ... and the sayings are these: (It is time, having taken
up again concerning Father and Son and Holy Spirit, to go through a few of the
things then left aside; concerning the Father, that being undivided and
unparted he becomes Father of a Son, not projecting him, as some think. For if
the Son is a projection of the Father and an offspring from him, such as the
offspring of living beings, it is necessary that the one projecting and the
one projected be a body.) 32 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 209, 11–15 Schw.): That the
Son is a creature and generated, from the same fourth discourse. (This Son
then was generated from the will of the Father, "who is image of the invisible
God" and "radiance of his glory and impress of his hypostasis", "first-born of
all creation", a creature, wisdom; for wisdom herself says: "God created me
beginning of his ways for his works".) 33 Athan. De decr. Nic. syn. 27, 1–2
(p. 23, 17–30 Opitz): And concerning the Word being eternally together with
the Father and not of another essence or hypostasis, but being own <offspring>
of the Father, as those in the synod have said, let it be permitted for you
again to hear also from the industrious Origen. For the things which he wrote
as inquiring and exercising, let no one receive these as of him holding them,
but of those contending in strife in the inquiry; but the things which he
declares as defining, this is the mind of the industrious one. (2) after the
things said as in exercise toward the heretics at any rate he himself
immediately brings on his own things saying thus: (If he is "image of the
invisible God", an invisible image; and I, having dared, would add, that also
being "likeness" of the Father there is not when he was not. For when did God
who according to John is called "light" (for "God is light") not have
"radiance" of his own "glory", so that someone having dared might give a
beginning of the Son who formerly was not? And when was the "image" of the
unspeakable and unnameable and unutterable hypostasis of the Father, the
"impress", <the> Word who "knows the Father" not? For let him consider who
dares and says: "there was once when the Son was not", that he will say also
this: wisdom once was not and Word was not and life was not.) 34 Athan. De
decr. Nic. syn. 27, 3 (p. 23, 30–24, 3 Opitz): And again in other places he
says thus: (But it is not lawful nor without danger on account of our weakness
that as far as lies with us God be deprived of the only-begotten Word always
being together with him, being wisdom, "in which he rejoiced"; for thus he
will not even be thought always rejoicing.) 36 Theoph. Al. Ep. pasch. II in
Theodoret Eranistes, dial. II (PG 83, 197 C): ... for he writes thus: (For
surely the soul being "troubled" and "exceedingly sorrowful" was not the
"only-begotten" and "first-born of all creation". For the God Word, as being
better than the soul, the Son himself says: "I have authority to lay it down,
and I have authority to take it".) If then the Son is better than his own
soul, as indeed he has also been confessed better, how is the soul of this one
"equal to God" and "in his form"? For saying that it is the one that "emptied
itself" and "took a form of a slave", he became more notable than the other
heretics by the excesses of the impieties, as we have noted. For if the Word
"exists in the form of God" and he thinks the soul of the Savior "equal to
God", having dared to write thus, how is the equal better? 37 Theoph. Al., at
the same place (PG 83, 197 AB): ... and saying in these very words thus: (Just
as "the Son and Father are one", so also the soul which the Son has taken and
he himself are one.) 38 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 209, 7–10 Schw.): From the
fourth discourse of the same book. (Let no one stumble at the word, if we set
measures also upon the power of God. For to comprehend infinite things is by
nature impossible. And once those things of which God himself grasps being
limited, it is necessary that there be a bound until how many limited things
he suffices.) 39 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 209, 16–23 Schw.): That the Son also is
encompassed with the other creatures by the Father, and in all things the
Father is greater than the Son, and is invisible to him, from the same fourth
discourse of On First Principles. (And if the Father encompasses all things,
and of all things is the Son, it is clear that also the Son. But another will
inquire whether it is true that God is known by himself similarly to his being
known by the only-begotten, and will declare that what has been said "the
Father who sent me is greater than I" is true in all things; so that also in
the thinking the Father is greater, as he is also known more clearly and more
perfectly by himself than by the Son.) 40 Just. Ep. ad Menam(p. 212, 15–19
Schw.): From the fourth discourse. (It is necessary that the nature of the
bodies not be primary, but subsist from intervals on account of certain
accidents coming to be concerning the rational beings needing bodies, and
again when the correction has come to be perfectly these are dissolved into
not-being, so that this always comes to be.) Max. Conf. Schol. in lib. De
eccl. hier. 6, 6 (PG 4, 173): Let not then someone of the initiates of Origen
think the present saying advocates his ill-believing opinion, claiming that
there always comes to be a fall and a reclining and a change of the heavenly
minds, as Origen says in the first discourse of On First Principles thus: (The
argument then, I think, every one shows, that every rational whatsoever is
able to come to be from every rational whatsoever.) And after a short while he
adds saying: (After the end upon all things again a flowing-away and a
falling-down comes to be.) Epiph. Haer. 64, 4, 6: (for we call it soul, he
says, for this reason, on account of its having been cooled from above.) 3
Epiph. Haer. 64, 4, 7–8: (for the saying, he says (scil. ορ.), of the prophet
"before I was humbled I sinned", the word, he says, is from the soul itself,
as it sinned above in heaven, before it was humbled in the body;) and (the
saying "return, my soul, unto your rest", as of the one who has done a deed of
virtue here in good-working returning unto the rest above through the
just-acting of his work.)
