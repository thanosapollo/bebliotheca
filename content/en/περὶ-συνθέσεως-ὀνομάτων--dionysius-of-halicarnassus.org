/This gift I too give you, dear child/, as Helen in Homer says when
entertaining Telemachus, on this first birthday from which you have come into
a man's age, the sweetest and most honored of festivals for me; except that I
send you neither a work of my hands, as she says giving the robe to the youth,
nor something fit only for the season of marriage and a grace well placed for
a wife, but a poem and offspring of my education and of my soul, and for you
the same thing both a possession and an instrument useful toward all the needs
in life that come about through speeches, the most necessary of all
instruments, if I too happen to think what is needed, alike for all who
practice political speeches, in whatever age and condition they happen to be;
but most of all for you youths and those newly taking hold of the study, Rufus
Metilius, son of a good father and to me the most honored of friends.

For there being a twofold practice concerning nearly all speeches, that
concerning thoughts and that concerning words, of which the one would seem
rather to touch the pragmatic place, and the other the lexical, and all who
aim at speaking well being eager equally about both these theories of speech,
the science that leads us to the affairs and the prudence in them is slow and
hard for the young, or rather impossible to fall into the age of the unbearded
and of youths; for the grasp of these things is more proper to an intelligence
already in its prime and an age seasoned with grey hairs, growing together
with much history of speeches and deeds, and with much experience and
misfortune of sufferings both one's own and others'; but the love of beauty
about diction is by nature also to bloom together with young ages. For every
soul of a youth is fluttered about the adornment of expression, taking some
irrational and as it were enthusiastic impulses toward this; for whom there is
need of a very great and intelligent first oversight and leading, if they are
going to speak not every /word that may come upon an untimely tongue/ nor join
at random the things that happen to meet one another, but both use a selection
of words that are at once pure and noble and adorn these with a composition
that has the pleasant mixed with the solemn. Into this part, then, which the
young must practice first, /I contribute to you a song toward love/, the
treatise on the composition of words, which has come to the mind of few of
those of the ancients who wrote rhetorical or dialectical arts, and has been
worked out by none exactly or sufficiently up to the present, as I am
persuaded. And if leisure is born for me, I will also bring out for you
another writing on the selection of words, so that you may have the lexical
place worked out completely. That treatise, then, expect again next year at
the same seasons, the gods keeping us unharmed and without disease, if indeed
it is ever fated for us to obtain this firmly; but now receive the treatise
which the divine has led to my mind.

And the heads of it which I set before myself to show are these, what the
nature of composition is and what force it has, and at what things it aims and
how it hits them, and what its most generic differences are and what the
character of each is and which of them I am persuaded is strongest, and still
besides these, what that poetic and well tongued and honey sweet thing in the
ears is, which is by nature attendant on the composition of prose diction, and
of a poetic construction imitating unpoetic speech and succeeding strongly in
the imitation, well what the power of each of them is and through what
practice each would come to be. Such then are some things, to take them in
outline, about which I am going to speak. And the treatise begins from here.

Composition is, as the name itself also makes clear, a certain placing of the
parts of speech beside one another, which some also call elements of diction.
And Theodectes and Aristotle and those who philosophized in those times
brought these as far as three, making nouns and verbs and conjunctions first
parts of diction. And those who came after them, and especially the leaders of
the Stoic school, advanced them as far as four, separating the articles from
the conjunctions. Then those later, dividing the appellatives from the
nominals, declared the first parts to be five. And others, unyoking the
pronouns also from the nouns, made this a sixth element. And some, dividing
the adverbs also from the verbs and the prepositions from the conjunctions and
the participles from the appellatives, and some bringing in also some other
cuts, made the first parts of diction many; about which the account would not
be small. Except that the weaving and placing beside of the first parts,
whether they are three or four or however many they ever are, makes the
so-called cola, then the harmony of these fills out the so-called periods, and
these complete the whole speech. The works of composition, then, are both to
place the words beside one another fittingly and to give the cola the harmony
that belongs to them and to divide the speech well by periods.

And being a second portion of the theorems about the lexical place, at least
according to the order (for the selection of words leads and preexists this by
nature), it has pleasure and persuasion and power in speeches not a little
stronger than that. And let no one think it paradoxical, if, there being many
and great theorems about selection, about which there was much speech among
philosophers and political men, composition, having second place in order and
having obtained speeches not even nearly equal to that, has such force and
power as to surpass all the works of that and to prevail, considering that
also in the other arts, as many as taking different materials make the end
collected from these, as building and carpentry and embroidery and as many as
are of like kind with these, the composing powers are second in order to the
selecting ones, but first in power; so that if the same thing has also
happened to speech, it must not be thought strange. And nothing prevents also
furnishing proofs of what is set forth, lest we seem to take from the ready
things among speeches that have dispute.

Every diction, then, by which we signify thoughts is either in meter or
without meter; of which each, when it has obtained a beautiful harmony, is
able to make both the meter and the speech beautiful, but when thrown without
attention and as it happens it also destroys along with itself even the useful
thing in the thought. Many at any rate, both poets and prose writers,
philosophers and orators, having selected with care dictions very beautiful
and fitting to the subjects, but having given them a random and unmusical
harmony, enjoyed nothing useful from that labor. And others, having taken
contemptible and low words, but having composed them pleasantly and with
surplus, put much charm around the speech. And composition would seem to have
suffered something almost analogous toward selection, which words suffer
toward thoughts. For as there is no benefit of a useful thought, if someone
will not give it the adornment of beautiful naming, so here too it is no
advantage to find diction that is pure and fair-speaking, if someone will not
also put around it the fitting adornment of harmony.

And that I may not seem to speak an undemonstrated assertion, from the things
by which I was persuaded that composition is a stronger and more complete
exercise than selection, I will try to show in deed, taking in hand a few
first-fruits of metrical and of prose speeches. And let Homer be taken of
poets, and Herodotus of prose writers; for it is enough from these to
conjecture also about the others. There is then in Homer Odysseus lodging with
the swineherd, about to breakfast at the morning hour, as was the custom for
the ancients; then Telemachus appearing to them from the journey abroad to the
Peloponnese; some small affairs of life interpreted exceedingly well. And
where the virtue of the interpretation is, the poems themselves set beside
will show;
/
Then in the huts Odysseus and the noble swineherd
were making ready breakfast at dawn, having kindled a fire,
and they sent out the herdsmen with the swine gathered together.
And the dogs of barking fate fawned around Telemachus
and did not bark as he came on. And noble Odysseus noticed
the dogs fawning, and the stamp of feet came under;
and at once he spoke to Eumaeus being near:
Eumaeus, surely some companion will come here to you
or also some other acquaintance, since the dogs are not barking,
but they fawn around; and I hear a thud of feet under.
Not yet was the whole word said, when his dear son
stood in the doorway. And the swineherd started up amazed;
and from his hands the vessels fell, with which he was laboring
mixing the dark wine. And he ran opposite his lord;
and he kissed him on the head and both fair eyes
and both hands; and a swelling tear fell from him. / 
that these things draw and charm the ears and have a portion inferior to none
of the poems that are very sweetest, all I well know would testify. And where
their persuasion is and why they are such, whether through the selection of
the words or through the composition? No one would say through the selection,
as I am persuaded; for the whole diction has been woven through the cheapest
and lowest words, which even a farmer and a seaman and a handicraftsman and
everyone who makes no care of speaking well would take from the ready and use.
If at any rate the meter is dissolved, these same things will appear paltry
and unenviable; for there are in them neither some noble metaphors nor
substitutions nor catachreses nor any other tropical dialect, nor indeed some
ancient glosses nor foreign or invented words. What then is left but to charge
the composition with the beauty of the interpretation? And such things in the
poet are countless, as I well know that all know; and for me speaking for the
sake of reminder it is enough that these alone have been said.

Come then, let us already pass over also to prose dialect and let us examine
whether that passion has happened to it too, so that from small and paltry
affairs and words composed well great pleasures and graces come to be. There
is then in Herodotus a certain king of the Lydians, whom that man calls
Candaules, and says he is called Myrsilus by Greeks, being in love with his
own wife, then thinking it right that one of his companions see the woman
naked, and the other fighting off not to be forced, and when he was not
persuading, both submitting and looking at her: an affair not only not solemn
or fit to be spoken beautifully, but even childish and dangerous and nearer to
the shameful than to the beautiful; but it has been said very cleverly, and it
has become better to be heard being told than to be seen happening. And that
no one may suppose the dialect to be the cause of the pleasure for the
diction, changing its character into the Attic tongue and having elaborated
nothing else I will thus bring out the dialogue.

/Gyges, for I do not think you are persuaded by me speaking about the form of
the woman; for ears happen to be more untrustworthy for humans than eyes; make
it so that you look at her naked. And he crying out said: master, what
unhealthy speech are you speaking, bidding me look at my own mistress naked?
And together with a tunic being stripped a woman is stripped also of her
shame. And long ago the beautiful things have been found out for humans, from
which one must learn; among which this one is, that each look at his own. And
I am persuaded that she is the most beautiful of all women, and I ask you not
to ask unlawful things. He then speaking these things was fighting off ...,
and the other answered with these: take heart Gyges, and do not fear either
me, as if I say this speech making trial of you, or my wife, lest some harm
come to you from her. For I will contrive a beginning thus, so that she will
not even learn that she has been seen by you. For having led you into the room
in which we sleep, I will set you behind the opening door; and after I have
gone in my wife also will be present to bed. And a chair lies near the
entrance; on this she will put the garments one by one as she strips, and with
much quiet it will be present for you to look. And when she goes from the
chair to the bed and you come at her back, let it be your care from there that
she not see you going away through the doors. He then, as he was not able to
escape, was ready to do these things/ .

One would not have even here to say that the dignity and the solemnity of the
words has made the phrasing well formed; for they are unstudied and
unselected, such symbols as nature has set for the affairs; for it was perhaps
not even fitting to use other stronger ones. And there is surely some
necessity, whenever the thoughts are brought out in the most proper and
nearest words, that they be no more solemn than those are. And that there is
nothing solemn or surplus in them, whoever wishes will know, changing nothing
except the harmony. And many such things are also in this man, from which one
might infer that the persuasion of the interpretation was not in the beauty of
the words, but in the coupling. And about these things these are enough.

And that someone may perceive much more how much strength the composing power
has both in poems and in speeches, I will take some dictions that seem to be
well, whose harmonies I will change and will make both the meters and the
speeches appear of another kind. And let these first be taken from the Homeric
things;
/
but she held as a true woman spinning for hire the balances,
who holding the weight and the wool draws it up around
equalizing, that she may win unseemly wage for her children. / 
This meter is a complete six-foot heroic, walking according to a dactyl foot.
And I, having moved the composition of these words, will make the same verses
tetrameters instead of hexameters, and prosodiac instead of heroic, in this
manner;
/
but she held as a woman spinning for hire true balances,
who some wool around and holding the weight draws up
equalizing, that unseemly wage for children she might win. / 
Such are the priapeia, and by some called ithyphallia, these;
/
I am not uninitiated, O celebrants of the new Dionysus ...
and I too have come initiated from a good deed. / 
Again taking other Homeric verses, having neither added anything to them nor
taken away, but having changed the composition only, I will give back another
kind, the so-called Ionic tetrameter;
/
so he lay stretched before the horses and the chariot,
bellowing, clutching bloody dust.
so he lay stretched before the horses and the chariot,
clutching bloody dust, bellowing. / 
Such are these Sotadean things;
/
then they lay corpses on the topmost pyres
on foreign earth, having left orphan walls
of holy Hellas and the recess of the ancestral hearth,
and lovely youth and the fair face of the sun. / 
And I could still show many forms of meters and different ones falling into
the heroic verse, and show that the same thing has happened also to the other
meters and rhythms almost all, so that while the selection of the words
remains the same, and the composition alone has fallen over, both the meters
are re-rhythmed and with them the figures, the colors, the characters, the
passions, the whole dignity of the poems change over; but I will be forced to
touch more theorems, some of which are known to very few. And upon many things
and not least upon such it would be well to bring in these Euripidean things;
/
do not
touch slender myths, soul;
why do you think surplus things? unless you are going
to be solemn among likes. /
These things, then, I think I will let go for the present. And that prose
diction too can suffer the same thing as the metrical, the words remaining,
but the composition being changed, it is present for whoever wishes to
examine. And I will take from the diction of Herodotus the beginning of the
history, since it is also known to the many, having changed only the character
of the dialect. /Croesus was a Lydian by race, and a child of Alyattes, and
tyrant of the nations within the river Halys; which flowing from the south
between Syrians and Paphlagonians issues toward the north wind into the sea
called Euxine/ . I change the harmony of this diction, and the mold will no
longer be narrative for me nor historical, but rather upright and agonistic:
/Croesus was a son of Alyattes, and Lydian by race, and tyrant of the nations
within the river Halys; which flowing from the south between Syrians and
Paphlagonians issues into the sea called Euxine toward the north wind./ This
character would seem not to be far from these of Thucydides: /Epidamnus is a
city on the right as one sails into the Ionian gulf; and Taulantian barbarians
dwell by it, an Illyrian nation./ And again having changed the same diction I
will give it another form in this manner: /Of Alyattes a son was Croesus, and
Lydian by race, and of the nations within the river Halys tyrant; which
flowing from the south between Syrians and Paphlagonians issues toward the
north wind into the sea called Euxine/ . Hegesias-like is this figure of
composition, petty-clever, ignoble, soft; for that man was priest of these
trifles, writing such things: /from a good festival we lead another good./ /I
am from Magnesia the great, a Sipylean./ /for Dionysus did not spit a small
thing into the water of Thebans; for it is sweet, but it makes one mad./

Let there be enough of examples. For I think I have made sufficiently clear
what was set before me, that composition has greater force than selection. And
it seems to me one would not miss the mark likening it to Homeric Athena; for
that goddess made Odysseus, being the same, appear at another time of another
kind, at one time small and wrinkled and ugly
/
like a wretched beggar and an old man, / 
and at another time having touched him again with the same wand
/
she made him greater to look on and thicker to be seen,
and down from the head
she sent woolly hair like the hyacinth flower. / 
And this, taking the same words, at one time makes the thoughts appear
formless and low and beggarly, and at another time high and rich and stout and
beautiful. And this was almost that by which a poet differs most from a poet
and an orator from an orator, composing the words cleverly. Among the ancients
then almost all there was much practice of it, and by this both their meters
and their songs and their speeches are beautiful; but among those later no
longer except few; and later in time it was neglected altogether and no one
thought it must be necessary nor contribute anything to the beauty of
speeches; therefore they left such compositions as no one endures to go
through as far as the coronis, I mean Phylarchus and Duris and Polybius and
Psaon and Demetrius of Callatis and Hieronymus and Antigonus and Heracleides
and Hegesianax and countless others; of all of whom if I should wish to say
the names, /the time of the day will fail me/. And why must one wonder at
these, when even those who profess philosophy and bring out the dialectical
arts are so wretched about the composition of words that one is ashamed even
to speak? And it is enough to use as a proof of the speech Chrysippus the
Stoic (for they would not go further); for of this man no one either made the
dialectical arts more exact or brought out speeches composed in a worse
harmony, at least of those thought worthy of a name and reputation. And yet
some of them pretended to be eager also about this part as being necessary to
speech and even wrote some arts about the arrangement of the parts of speech;
but all wandered very far from the truth and did not even see in a dream what
it is that makes composition pleasant and beautiful. I at least, when I knew
that this subject was being arranged, sought whether anything had been said
about it by those before and especially by the philosophers from the Stoa,
knowing that the men make no small care of the lexical place; for one must
bear witness of the true things to them. And seeing nowhere anything said by
anyone, at least of those thought worthy of a name, neither greater nor lesser
into the treatise which I have chosen, but the compositions which Chrysippus
has left, two having the title /On the Arrangement of the Parts of Speech/,
not having a rhetorical theory but a dialectical one, as those who have read
the books know, about the arrangement of propositions true and false and
possible and impossible and contingent and changing-over and ambiguous and
some other such things, contributing neither any benefit nor use to political
speeches toward at least pleasure and beauty of interpretation, which
composition must aim at; from this treatise I stood off, and I was examining,
having come to be by myself, whether I could find some natural starting-point,
since this seems to be the strongest beginning of every affair and of every
inquiry. And having touched some theorems and thinking the affair was going
for me by a road, when I learned that this road was leading me somewhere else,
not where I was traveling and it was necessary to go, I stood off. And nothing
will perhaps prevent touching that theory also and saying the causes through
which I left it, in order that no one may think I passed it by through
ignorance but by choice.

It seemed to me then that we must, following nature most of all, fit the parts
of speech as that wishes. For example I thought it right to range nouns before
verbs (for the one shows the substance, and the other the accident, and the
substance is prior by nature to the accidents), as these Homeric things have
it;
/
Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns / 
and
/
Sing, goddess, the wrath / 
and
/
And the sun sprang up leaving / 
and the things near to these; for in these the nouns lead, and the verbs
follow. The account was persuasive, but it did not seem to me to be true. At
any rate one might furnish other examples lying in the same poet arranged
oppositely than these have been arranged, but no less beautiful and
persuasive. Such as these are;
/
Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Unwearied / 
and
/
Tell me now, Muses having Olympian houses ...
remember your father, Achilles like to the gods. / 
For in these the verbs lead, and the nouns are ranged under; and no one would
blame this arrangement as unpleasant. Still besides these I thought it better
to put verbs before adverbs, since that which does or suffers is prior by
nature to the things sitting with them, I mean manner and place and time and
the like, which we call adverbs, using these examples;
/
and he struck turning about, and of them an unseemly groan arose ...
and he fell backward, and gasped out his soul ...
and he leaned the other way, and the cup fell from his hand. / 
For in all these the adverbs have been ranged later together with the verbs.
And this too is persuasive like the first, but not true as that is not either.
For these things have been said oppositely in the same poet;
/
and they fly in clusters upon spring flowers ...
today a man into the light birth-laboring Eileithyia
will bring forth. / 
Have the poems then become at all worse when the verbs have been ranged under
the adverbs? No one would say. Still I thought I must also watch this not as a
side-work, that the things prior in times and in order be taken prior; such as
these are;
/
they first drew back and slaughtered and flayed / 
and
/
the bow twanged, and the string cried greatly, and the arrow leaped / 
and
/
then the queen threw a ball toward a handmaid;
she missed the handmaid, and threw it into a deep eddy. / 
Yes by Zeus, someone would say, if there were not also many other poems not so
arranged no less beautiful than these;

And having raised himself he struck with a cleft of oak, which he left
splitting, for surely the raising up is prior to the striking. And still
/
he drove standing near, and the axe cut off the tendons
of the neck, / 
for surely it belonged first to the one about to send the axe into the tendons
of the bull to stand near him. Still besides these I thought it right to put
the nominals before the epithets, and the appellatives before the nominals,
and the pronouns before the appellatives, and in the verbs to watch that the
upright lead the inflected and the indicative the infinitives, and many other
such things. And experience shook all these and showed them worth nothing. For
at one time from these and things like them the composition became pleasant
and beautiful, and at another time from things not such but opposite. For
these causes then I stood off from such a theory. And I mentioned them also
now not as worthy of seriousness, and I set beside the dialectical arts not as
necessary, but in order that no one thinking them to have something useful for
the present theory may make much of knowing them, hunted by the titles of the
treatises having some likeness and by the reputation of those who arranged
them.

I will go back then to the hypothesis from the beginning from which I went out
into these things, that there was much forethought among the ancients both
poets and prose writers, philosophers and orators, of this form, and they
thought they must join neither the words to the words nor the cola to the cola
nor the periods to one another at random, but there was some art among them
and theorems using which they composed well. And what these theorems were, I
will try to teach, as I am able, as many as power came to be for me to find
out together, not saying all but the most necessary themselves.

It seems to me that there are three works of the composing science; one, to
see what fitted with what is by nature going to take a beautiful and pleasant
coupling; another, to know of the things about to be fitted toward one another
how each, being figured, would make the harmony appear stronger; and third, if
anything of the things taken needs restyling, I mean subtraction and addition
and alteration, both to know and to work it out fittingly toward the coming
use. And what each of these can do, I will say more clearly using some images
of the craftsman arts which all know, I mean building and shipbuilding and the
like; for the builder, whenever he has provided the material from which he is
going to construct the house, stones and woods and tile and all the rest,
already composes the work from these, working these three things, with what
stone and wood and brick one must fit what stone or wood or brick, then how
each of the things being fitted and on what side to seat it, and third, if
anything is ill-seated, to strike it off and cut around and make this very
thing well-seated; and the shipbuilder works these same things. The like
things then I say those also must do who are going to compose well the parts
of speech, first examining what noun or verb or some one of the other parts,
arranged with what, will lie fittingly and how well or better (for not all
things placed with all are by nature to dispose the ears similarly); then
distinguishing how the noun or the verb or whatever else of the others, being
figured, will be seated more gracefully and more becomingly toward the
subjects; and I mean, upon nouns, whether being taken in the singular or in
the plural they will take a stronger coupling, and whether being brought out
according to the upright case or according to some of the oblique, and if some
are by nature to become feminines from masculines or masculines from feminines
or neuters from these, how it would be figured better, and all such things;
and upon verbs, which will be stronger being taken, the upright or the supine,
and according to what moods being brought out, which some call verbal cases,
it will take the strongest seat, and indicating what differences of times, and
if some other things are by nature to attend the verbs (and these same things
must be watched also upon the other parts of speech, that I may not speak
according to each one); and upon these, to distinguish the things taken, if
some noun or verb needs restyling, how it would become more in-harmony and
better-seated; this element is more abundant in poetry, and rarer in prose
speeches; except that it does come to be also in these as far as it may be
allowed; for the one saying /into this here contest/ has added some letter to
the pronoun aiming at the composition; for it was complete to say /into this
contest/; and again the one saying /having looked down on Neoptolemus the
actor/ has increased the word by the preposition, for /having seen/ was
enough; and the one writing /that I have come for the sake of no private
enmity/ has lessened the parts of speech by the elisions and has struck off
some of the letters; and the one saying /he made/ instead of he made without
the n and saying /he wrote/ instead of he wrote and /I will take away/ instead
of I will be taken away and all such things, and the one saying /he
place-loved/ for he loved-the-place and /it will have been loosed/ for it will
be loosed and things of that sort restyles the dictions, in order that they
may become more beautiful and more fit to be fitted for him.

One theory then of the composing science is this about the first parts
themselves and elements of diction; and another, as I also said at the
beginning, is that about the so-called cola, needing both a more varied
treatise and a greater, about which I will try at once to speak as I have
judgment. For one must also fit these toward one another so as to appear akin
and dear and figure them as strongest as may be allowed and prepare them
beforehand, if anywhere something should be needed, by lessening and surplus
and if the cola receive some other restyling; and each of these experience
itself teaches; for often this colon put before this or put upon this shows
some good-mouthing and solemnity, but having taken some other coupling it
appears graceless and unsolemn. And what I say will be clearer, if someone
should see it upon an example. There is then some diction in Thucydides in the
demegory of the Plataeans composed very gracefully and full of passion, this:
/and you, O Lacedaemonians, the only hope, we fear lest you be not firm./ Come
then, let someone, having loosed this coupling, re-fit the cola thus: /and
you, O Lacedaemonians, we fear lest you be not firm, the only hope./ Does the
same grace still remain with the cola fitted in this manner, or the same
passion? No one would say. And what if this diction of Demosthenes /the taking
then the things given, agreeing that it is lawful, the giving back grace for

Beginnings then of the human and articulate voice, no longer receiving
division, are what we call elements and letters; letters because they are
signified by some lines, and elements because every voice takes its generation
from these first and makes its dissolution into these last. Of the elements
and letters then there is not one nature of all, but a first difference of
them, as Aristoxenus the musician declares, according to which some complete
voices, and some noises; voices the so-called vowels, and noises all the rest.
And a second according to which of the not-vowels some are by nature to
complete some noises of whatever kind by themselves, indicative of a hiss or a
sibilant or a moan or some other such sounds; and some are without share of
all voice and noise and not such as to be sounded by themselves; wherefore
some have called these voiceless, and the others half-voiced. And some, having
divided the first and elemental powers of the voice in three, called vowels as
many as both are voiced by themselves and with others and are self-complete;
and half-voiced as many as with vowels are brought out better than themselves,
but by themselves worse and not self-completely; and voiceless as many as have
neither the complete nor the half-complete voices by themselves, but are
voiced out with others.

And what their number is, it is not easy to say exactly, since the affair has
furnished much difficulty also to those before us; for some thought the
elements of the voice were thirteen in all, and the rest had been constructed
from these; and some even more than the twenty-four which we now use. The
theory about these then is more proper to grammar and metrics, and if someone
wishes, also to philosophy; and it is enough for us, having hypothesized the
beginnings of the voice to be neither fewer than 24 nor more, to say the
things attending them, having made the beginning from the vowels.

These then are seven in number, two short, both e and o, and two long, both
eta and omega, and three two-timed, both a and i and y, for these are both
stretched and contracted; and some call them two-timed, as I said, and some
changeable. And all these are voiced with the windpipe sounding together with
the breath and the mouth having been figured simply and the tongue working
nothing but being at rest. Except that the long and of the two-timed those

Of the austere harmony then the character is of this sort: it wishes the words
to be planted securely and to take strong standings, so that each word is seen
from conspicuousness, and the parts stand off from one another noteworthy
distances, being fenced by perceptible times; and it differs nothing to it to
use in many places rough and resistant joinings, such as come to be of stones
composed by picking in buildings, the bases not right-angled and not polished
together, but some idle and offhand; and it loves to be lengthened for the
most part with great words that have stepped through into breadth; for to be
gathered into short syllables is hostile to it, except if sometime necessity
should force.

In the words then it tries to pursue these things and clings to these; and in
the cola it practices these similarly and the dignified and magnificent
rhythms, and it wishes the cola neither to be equal-sided to one another nor
like-ending nor enslaved to necessity, but following and noble and bright and
free, and it wishes them themselves to be like to nature rather than to art,
and to be spoken according to passion rather than according to character. And
to compose periods completing the thought for themselves it does not even wish
for the most part; and if sometime it should be carried down upon this
automatically, it wishes to show the unpracticed and simple, using neither
some additions of words, in order that the circle may be filled out, helping
the thought nothing, nor having seriousness that their bases may become some
theatrical or polished, nor measuring them very much that they may be
self-sufficient for the breath of the speaker, nor having any other such
practice of working. Still these too are private of such a harmony: it is
counterpoised about the cases, varied about the figurings, few-conjunctioned,
article-less, in many things disdainful of sequence, least flowery,
great-minded, each-itself, unpetty, having the archaism and the antique bloom

And about the in-melody and in-meter composition that has much likeness toward
prose diction I have some such things to say, that a first cause here too in
the same manner as upon the unmetered poetic is the fitting of the words
themselves, and second the composition of the cola, and third the symmetry of
the periods. The one wishing then to succeed in this part must both turn the
parts of diction many-formedly and fit them together and make the cola in
intervals symmetrically, not completing together with the verses but cutting
through the meter, and make them both unequal and unlike, and often also
gather them into commas shorter than cola, and work the periods neither
equal-sized nor like-figured, at least those lying beside one another; for the
wandered about the rhythms and the meters appears nearest to speeches. For
those constructing the epics and the iambics and the other like-formed meters
it is not allowed to divide the poems with many meters or rhythms, but there
is necessity always to remain upon the same figure; and for the song-makers it
is allowed to throw many meters and rhythms into one period; so that those
composing the single-meters, whenever they dissolve the verses dividing them
with the cola at another time otherwise, pour out and make disappear the
exactness of the meter, and whenever they make the periods varied in both size
and figure, they throw us into forgetfulness of the meter; and the song-makers
working the strophes many-metered and making the divisions unlike and unequal,
the cola each time again being both unequal and unlike one another, and
through both these not allowing us to take a grasp of a like-formed rhythm,
construct much the likeness toward speeches in the songs, and it is present
even with tropical and foreign and gloss-like and the other poetic words
remaining in the poems that they appear no less near to speech.
