** Panegyric on the Water at Pergamon
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

To Homer it has been said of the meeting of the winter-torrents /and far off
in the mountains a shepherd heard their roar;/ and he says that, shuddering,
he drives the sheep into the cave. But I, although now so far from you, when I
heard of the inrush of the water and how much adornment has been added to the
city, was not able to keep quiet for pleasure, but I uttered what I uttered
and felt my body lighter, and nothing of joy was lacking, two days before I
heard. For perhaps it is no worse to tell you; for you will hear gladly, for
the sake of the god who showed it beforehand and at the same time of the good
omen. A vision of dreams that came to me showed the city as if doubled, a
certain place having been furnished by an addition continuously adjoining it,
and public adornments having been added most nearly somehow like those about
the Friendly [god]. Because of these things, then, I rejoiced in the dream,
and when I rose I took it for good both for the city and for myself. And on
the third day after these things a message arrives from a man among my
friends, reporting that indeed all Asia is celebrating with you the good
fortune in all things. For the water is both greatest in quantity and most
beautiful in beauty of all that cities have obtained as their lot. So I kept
not merely a spring day, but such as it is fitting to keep when Zeus of Good
Tidings and Asclepius the Savior honor everywhere. And I rejoiced with the
city for what had been added, and with myself that I was deemed worthy to hear
it beforehand, it being clear that the city belongs to me no less than to
anyone. After this I reasoned that rejoicing is common to all, both men and
little children and women, inasmuch as the sight procures the pleasure, but to
adorn with speech the gift of the Nymphs would perhaps belong to someone of
those who have spent time about Paean and of those appointed among speeches.
And I recalled the poets, that they somehow always bring Nymphs and Muses
together. And they always address Hermes as chorus-leader of the Nymphs; and
again Apollo as chorus-leader of the Muses; and this same god of yours had the
appellation of Fair-Child, on account of the father. Everywhere, then, it
appeared both fitting and not unseasonable to blend with the grace of the
Nymphs that which comes from the art of the Muses. For one would be doing what
is proper to all the gods I named. And from the beginning, as it seems, the
fairest things were given to the city both from gods and from men. This, as I
have it: the eldest of daimones are said to have been born here, the Cabeiri,
and rites for them and mysteries, which are believed to have such power that
of extraordinary storms ......
