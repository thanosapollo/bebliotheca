* On Composite Nature
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of our holy father John of Damascus concerning composite nature against the
Acephali.
1 A composite nature comes to be from different natures, when, natures being
united, something other besides the natures that were united is completed. And
that which is completed neither is this properly nor is called, nor this, but
other. As from the four elements, I mean fire and air and water and earth, a
body is constituted, the four being united and mixed together, and the body
that is completed neither is fire nor is called, nor air nor water nor earth,
but from these something other besides these. Likewise also from horse and ass
a mule comes to be, and neither is a horse nor an ass nor is called, but
something other besides these. But our Lord Jesus Christ, being from Godhead
and humanity, both is in Godhead and in humanity, and is God and is called
perfect, and perfect man, and whole God and whole man, which upon the
composite nature it is not possible to find; for the whole body is not fire
nor whole air, likewise neither earth nor water, nor is the whole mule a horse
nor whole ass nor a perfect horse nor a perfect ass, but Christ, as I said, is
perfect God and perfect man and whole God and whole man. Wherefore it is not
one composite nature, but one hypostasis known in two natures and two natures
in one composite hypostasis.
2 Let us ask the heretics then: Saying Christ from two natures, of what
natures do you say? They will say then: From Godhead and humanity. Then let us
say: And how, saying Christ in Godhead and humanity, do you not say him in two
natures? For they cannot say that Christ is not in Godhead and humanity. Again
let us ask them: Is there one composite nature of Christ, or two? And they
will say: One composite. Then we say: Is the simple and the composite
consubstantial? And they cannot say that the simple and the composite are
consubstantial. Then we say: If then Christ is of one composite nature from
Godhead and humanity, he is not consubstantial with the Father; for the Father
is not of composite nature from Godhead and humanity, but of simple. But
neither will he be consubstantial with the Mother; for neither is the Mother
composed from Godhead and humanity. But we, saying Christ one hypostasis in
two natures, in Godhead and humanity, according to the Godhead we say him
consubstantial with the Father, and according to the humanity consubstantial
with the Mother, and neither do we divide him saying one hypostasis, nor do we
confuse, confessing two natures united. And again we say thus: If there is one
nature of the Godhead of Christ and of his flesh, and one nature of the Father
and of Christ, there will be one nature of the Father and of the flesh. And
again: How do you confess the difference of the natures? For the difference
introduces at least two things that differ, either natures or hypostases, or
one hypostasis being altered and changed according to the separable accident.
That then you do not say a difference of hypostases, is clear upon Christ. And
that neither concerning the separable accident, this too is clear; for you do
not say him sometimes immortal, and sometimes mortal, having been turned from
immortality into mortality, as we from sickness into health. It remains then
for you to say the difference of the natures, as you also confess, and,
confessing the difference of the natures, first to confess the natures, of
which the difference is; for if the essences are not, neither will the
essential differences be. And if there is one nature just as also one
hypostasis upon Christ. Why saying a difference of natures do you not also say
a difference of hypostases upon him? And why, saying Christ from two natures,
do you not also say him from two hypostases? If Christ is of one composite
nature, he is not consubstantial with the Father; for the Father is of simple
nature.
And the simple and composite are not consubstantial. If according to the same
essence Christ is consubstantial with the Father and consubstantial with us,
the Father and we will be of one essence. If Christ is of like nature with the
Father and consubstantial, the Father being of simple nature, and Christ also
has a composite nature, he will have two natures, one simple and one
composite. If nature and hypostasis are the same according to you, either,
there being three hypostases of the holy Godhead, there will be also three
natures of it, or, there being one nature, there will be also one hypostasis.
If you say the common nature other and the nature contemplated in the
individual other, which you also name particular, you will be forced to
confess not only Christ, but also the Father and the Holy Spirit to have two
natures, one common and one composite. If the Godhead is uncreated and without
beginning, but the humanity created and begun, how will the without-beginning
and the begun be of one nature?
3 And since they bring forward to us the saying of saint Athanasius and of
Cyril the most holy, that says "one incarnate nature of God the Word," we say
that equal to this is also the saying of saint Gregory the Theologian, which
asserts: "Of which the one deified, and the other was deified." For just as
that which is being fired does not indicate a change of that which is being
fired, but a union toward the fire, so also deification indicates a union
toward Godhead and incarnation a union toward flesh. We know then one nature
of God the Word, that is of his Godhead incarnate, that is united to flesh,
and one nature of the flesh of the Word deified, that is united to Godhead. So
that there are two natures united to one another. For if he had said one
nature of God the Word and of him incarnate, he would have indicated without
contradiction one nature, the both-together. But having said "one nature of
the Word" and having added the "incarnate," by saying "incarnate" he indicated
the essence of the flesh, just as he also, interpreting himself in the letter
to Succensus, says: "For if having said 'one nature of God the Word' we had
been silent, not bringing in the 'incarnate,' their argument would then also
not have been unpersuasive, pretending to ask: If the whole is one nature,
where is the perfect in humanity? But since through the 'incarnate' the
indication of the flesh was introduced, let them cease, having set up for
themselves a reed staff." Behold, he clearly did away with saying "one nature"
of the whole, that is the both-together; and he said that through the
"incarnate" the indication of the flesh was introduced. We have then "the one
nature of God the Word," and through the "incarnate" we have the flesh; and
the flesh is not without essence. The nature of the Word then and the flesh
that has essence will be two essences.
4 And why, confessing a nature of Godhead and a nature of flesh upon Christ
and saying another and another, do you not say two natures, fearing there a
fear where there is no fear? And they say that number is a beginning of
division, and fleeing the division we do not say the natures of Christ two. To
whom we say that every number is significant of the quantity of the things
numbered, not of the relation; for saying three men we do not signify three
divided natures, and saying three hypostases upon the Godhead we do not
confess these divided. Thus also asserting two natures upon Christ and
signifying the number we do not admit the division; for just as upon the holy
Trinity the three hypostases have been united indivisibly together and
unconfusedly (indivisibly on the one hand both through the uniqueness of the
nature and the unconfused perichoresis in one another, and unconfusedly
through the hypostatic difference, that is the manner of existence, I mean the
unbegotten and the begotten and the proceeding), and the number of the
hypostases does not introduce division, so also upon the incarnation of God
the Word the two natures have been united indivisibly together and
unconfusedly (indivisibly on the one hand both through the uniqueness of the
hypostasis and the unconfused perichoresis of the natures in one another, and
unconfusedly through the essential difference, I mean the created and the
uncreated, the mortal and the immortal, the circumscribed and uncircumscribed
and such things), and the number of the natures does not introduce division.
5 And since they seem to puzzle us, speaking unstable things, that, if there
is not anhypostatic essence nor nature without person, saying two essences and
natures upon Christ you will of necessity say two hypostases and persons, we
say that there is not anhypostatic essence nor nature without person (for in
the hypostases and persons the essence and the nature is contemplated), yet
the essence is not hypostasis nor the nature person. For if the essence is
hypostasis and the nature person, it is necessary upon the holy Godhead
either, saying one essence and nature, to confess one hypostasis and one
person, or, confessing three hypostases and persons, to confess together three
essences and natures, likewise also upon the humanity either to confess one
nature and one hypostasis or to say infinite essences just as also the
hypostases, but also, saying Christ from two natures, to confess him from two
hypostases.
6 We say then that the essence is contemplated in hypostases, wherefore it is
also enhypostatic, but not hypostasis. There is no necessity then for the
enhypostatic to be called hypostasis. For both hypostasis and the enhypostatic
are said in two ways. For hypostasis sometimes indicates existence simply,
according to which signification it does not signify essence simply, but also
the accident, and sometimes the individual, that is the person, which is
called hypostasis in itself, which indicates Peter, Paul, this horse and such
things. And the enhypostatic sometimes signifies the essence as contemplated
in a hypostasis and being self-existent, and sometimes each of the things
coming together into the composition of one hypostasis, as it holds upon soul
and body. The Godhead then also and the humanity of Christ is enhypostatic;
for each has in common his one composite hypostasis, the Godhead pre-eternally
and everlastingly, and the ensouled and intellectual flesh in the last of the
times assumed by it and having existed in it and having had it as hypostasis.
7 Still they say that the holy fathers used the example of man upon the
mystery according to Christ; and there is one nature of men, one then also
will be the nature of Christ. Let them hear then: The name of nature is also
assigned upon the species. All men then are said to be of one nature as being
ranked under one species, that of humanity. But man, being from soul and body,
is not of one nature; for the bodiless and invisible soul is not
consubstantial with the visible body. When then man is compared toward man,
they are called consubstantial as belonging under one species and as all
composed from soul and body and as all bearing two natures; but when man is
examined as to nature, two natures are contemplated upon him, I mean of soul
and of body, composed in one hypostasis. For in the comparison of the soul
toward the body who is so mindless as to say one nature of both? But upon
Christ there is not a species; for there are not many Christs composed from
Godhead and humanity, that all, being ranked under the same species, might be
said of one nature, but there is one Christ known from two and in two natures.
For Gregory the Theologian says: "For natures are two, God and man, since also
soul and body, but not two sons."
8 Still: How will one and the same nature have the contrary essential
differences? For if the essence is receptive of the extra-essential
differences by turns and not according to the same, how of the essential? For
it is clear that it is impossible for the contrary essential differences to be
found in the same essence; for the same essence will not be rational and
irrational or created and uncreated. Still: The constitutive differences of
the essences constitute the essences; wherefore they are also called essential
and constitutive and they subsist in the essences. If then you confess the
constitutive differences of the essences upon Christ, it is necessary also to
confess the essences (for of what will they be?), and not constituting
essences they will be superfluous. Still: You who say one nature of the
Godhead and of the humanity of Christ, tell us, in what nature did Christ
suffer? In the composite, clearly, you will say. For saying one you will of
necessity attach also the passion to it, and according to you both the Godhead
and the humanity of Christ will be passible. Still: If there is one composite
nature of Christ, it will be either passible only or impassible only or
passible-impassible and according to the same passible and impassible, or
changeable, and sometimes passible, sometimes impassible, or a part of it
passible and a part of it impassible. Whichever then of the impieties you
wish, choose. For if passible only, Christ is not God. And if impassible only,
Christ is not man. And if sometimes passible, sometimes impassible, it will
not be passible by nature nor impassible by nature, but according to accident;
for that which is by nature naturally so does not change. And if also a part
of it is passible and a part of it impassible, and each is by nature that
which it is, the parts will not be of the same nature, and thus Christ will be
of two natures, who is not a nature, but a hypostasis. For it is impossible
for the same nature to be passible together and impassible; for one and the
same essence cannot be receptive of the contrary essential differences
together.
9 Still tell us this: Does Christ have a divine nature? Yes, you will say
altogether. And does he also have a human? This too you will confess together,
if at least you should not wish to be impious with bare head. A divine nature
then and a human nature, one nature or two? But if you should say one, the
Father will be consubstantial with us. And if two and not one, why, having
cast far away the conceit together with the begetter of this, I mean the
devil, will you not confess with us one Christ, one Son, one Lord, one
hypostasis from two natures and two natures and in two natures after the
union? For if you never confess Christ of two natures, why in vain do you say
him of one nature after the union? And if asserting him of two natures before
the union you confess him of one after the union, you fight openly against the
truth. For before the union, that is the divine incarnation, being one
hypostasis of one simple nature, that is the divine, he was not even Christ
nor was named, unless by prophetic word the future was proclaimed beforehand.
Either then you will altogether do away with the two natures upon Christ, or
you will say Christ two-natured before the incarnation from the Virgin, not
enduring to give him the two natures that are properly his after the union.
But we do not think thus, nor is this the portion of Jacob, but standing in
the definitions of piety we say from Godhead and humanity one composite
hypostasis; for this pre-eternal hypostasis of God the Word, having
tabernacled in the womb of the holy Ever-Virgin, hypostasized in itself an
ensouled rational flesh, itself having become hypostasis to the ensouled and
rational flesh, and there came to be from perfect Godhead and perfect humanity
one Christ, one Son the same of God and of man, one Lord the same, perfect God
and perfect man, whole God and whole man, the natures having been united to
one another unconfusedly and unchangeably and indivisibly. Unconfusedly on the
one hand: for each nature keeps its own difference, the uncreated the Godhead,
the created the humanity, the impassible the Godhead, the passible the
humanity and such things. And unchangeably, because each nature has remained
unalterable, not having been turned into the other nor having become one
composite nature, but being two and remaining through the ages. And
indivisibly, because they have been united to one another according to
hypostasis, having one hypostasis, the one pre-eternally bodiless and simple,
and in the last of the times embodied unchangeably from the holy Ever-Virgin;
for it exists this and that, bodiless on the one hand by the without-beginning
Godhead, embodied on the other by the begun, that is by the assumed flesh. And
this is the pre-eternal Son and Word of God, who himself both is bodiless as
God and embodied as having become man in the last times. The natures then have
been united according to one hypostasis and they interpenetrate.
