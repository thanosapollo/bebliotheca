ON THE LIFE OF MOSES THE LAWGIVER OR ON PERFECTION ACCORDING TO VIRTUE 1.1
Something of the sort that the spectators of horse-races suffer, who, toward
those they are eager for in the contests of the courses, even if they leave
nothing of eagerness wanting toward speed, nevertheless from zeal concerning
victory shout from above, carrying the eye along with the course, and urge on,
as they suppose, the charioteer into a sharper rush, crouching together with
the horses and stretching out and shaking the hand against them in place of a
whip, not because the things done contribute anything toward the victory, but
by goodwill toward those competing signaling the zeal from eagerness by voice
and by gesture: some such thing I too seem to do, most honored of my friends
and brothers, in that, while you contend well in the stadium of virtue in the
divine course and stretch toward the prize of the upward calling with dense
and light leaps, I shout along and hurry you on and bid you intensify the
speed by zeal. And I do these things not being carried toward this by some
irrational eagerness, but granting the things according to pleasure as to a
beloved child. 1.2 For since the letter which you sent the other day announced
this request, that some counsel from us toward the perfect life come to be for
you, I thought it fitting to furnish it; perhaps nothing else useful for you
from the things said, but this itself at any rate not unuseful, that there
come to be for you an example of compliance. For if we who have been set in
the rank of fathers over so many souls think it fitting for this gray hair to
receive a command of a youth practicing self-control, much more is it likely
that the achievement of compliance be strengthened in you, your youth having
been trained by us toward willing obedience. 1.3 And these things to this
point; but one must now attempt the matter at hand, having made God the guide
of the discourse. You sought, O dear head, that it be sketched for you in a
type by us what the perfect life is, looking toward this, that if the thing
sought should be found by the discourse, you might transfer into your own life
the grace indicated by the discourse. And I am equally at a loss in both; for
both to encompass perfection by discourse and to show in the life whatever the
discourse may understand, I say each of these is beyond my power; and perhaps
not I alone, but many even of the great and those excelling in virtue will
confess that such a thing is unattainable for them. 1.4 And that I may not
seem, to speak according to the psalmist, to fear a fear there where there is
no fear, I will set before you more clearly what I wish. 1.5 Perfection, in
all other things that are measured by sense, is taken in certain defined
limits, as in quantity, both the continuous and the discrete. For every
measure in quantity is contained by certain proper bounds; and he who looks to
the cubit or to the decad of number knows that which has begun from something
and ended in something, in which it is to have the perfect. But in virtue we
have learned from the Apostle one bound of perfection, that it not have a
bound; for that great one and lofty in understanding, the divine Apostle,
always running through virtue never ceased stretching forward to the things in
front; for neither was a standing of the course safe for him; why? because
every good by its own nature has no bound, but is defined by juxtaposition of
the contrary, as life by death and light by darkness; and every good
altogether ends into all the things conceived for goods from the contrary; as
therefore the end of life is beginning of death, so also the standing of the
course according to virtue becomes beginning of the course according to vice.
1.6 Therefore it has not


been false for us, the discourse saying that the encompassing of perfection is
impossible in virtue; for it has been shown that what is taken in limits is
not virtue. And since I also said that it is impossible for those pursuing the
life according to virtue to set foot on perfection, the discourse will be made
clear also concerning this. 1.7 The first and properly good, whose nature is
goodness, is the Divine itself, whatever it is conceived to be by nature, this
it both is and is named. Since then no bound of virtue except vice has been
shown, and the Divine is unreceptive of the contrary, the divine nature is
therefore grasped as unbounded and infinite; but indeed he who pursues true
virtue partakes of nothing else than of God, because He himself is the
all-complete virtue; since then what is by nature beautiful is altogether
desirable for participation to those who know it, and this has no bound,
necessarily also the desire of the one partaking, stretching along with the
unbounded, has no standing. 1.8 Therefore it is altogether without resource to
obtain the perfect, because perfection, as has been said, is not taken in
bounds, and the one bound of virtue is the unbounded; how then would someone
arrive at the sought limit, not finding the limit? 1.9 Yet not because the
discourse has shown the thing sought to be wholly ungraspable should one
neglect the commandment of the Lord, which says: Be perfect, as your heavenly
Father is perfect. For in the things beautiful by nature, even if it is not
possible to obtain the whole, not to miss even the part would be a great gain,
at least for those who have understanding. =1.10= Therefore every zeal must be
shown not to fall entirely out of the possible perfection, but to acquire from
it as much as we may have room for within the thing sought; for perhaps to be
thus, as always to wish to have more in the good, is the perfection of human
nature. =1.11= And it seems to me well to use Scripture as counselor
concerning this; for the divine voice says somewhere through the prophecy of
Isaiah: Look to Abraham your father and to Sarah who was in labor with you.
For these things the word altogether commands to those wandering outside
virtue, so that, just as those at sea who have been carried past the straight
of the harbor bring themselves back according to the appeared sign of the
wandering, either having seen a beacon raised from a height or some peak of a
ridge having appeared, in the same way it may again direct those wandering
with unsteered understanding over the sea of life by the example according to
Sarah and Abraham toward the harbor of the divine will. =1.12= For since human
nature has been divided toward the female and the male and the choice toward
virtue and vice lies equally in the power of both, for this reason the fitting
example of virtue has been shown beforehand by the divine voice to each
section, so that each looking toward the kindred, the men toward Abraham, and
the other part toward Sarah, both might be directed through their own examples
toward the life according to virtue. =1.13= Perhaps then even for us the
memory of some one of those well-reputed in life would suffice to fulfill the
need of the beacon and to show how it is possible to bring the soul to harbor
at the unbuffeted harbor of virtue, nowhere storm-tossed by the squalls of
life nor shipwrecked in the depth of vice according to the successive
triple-waves of the passions. For perhaps also for this reason the manner of
life of those lofty ones has been accurately related, so that through
imitation of those who have succeeded before the life that follows may be
directed toward the good. =1.14= What then, someone will say, if I am neither
a Chaldean, as Abraham is remembered, nor a nursling of the daughter of the
Egyptian, as the account holds concerning Moses, nor have I at all in such
things anything corresponding in life toward some one of the ancients, how
shall I set myself in the same rank with one of these, not having how I shall
imitate one so far removed through the practices? Toward whom we will say,
that we judge being Chaldean neither vice nor virtue, nor by the life in Egypt
nor by the sojourn of life in Babylon


is anyone made a stranger to that according to virtue, nor again in Judea only
does God become known to the worthy, nor is Zion according to the ready notion
the divine dwelling-place, but we need some more subtle understanding and
sharper sight, so as to discern from the history of what sort of Chaldeans or
Egyptians having become far, and from what sort of Babylonian captivity having
been released, we shall set foot on the blessed life. =1.15= Let Moses then be
set before us by the discourse as an example of life, whose life having first
gone through in a summary, as we have learned from the divine Scripture, thus
we shall seek the understanding suitable to the history as counsel of virtue,
through which we shall come to know the perfect life as among men. =1.16=
Moses then is said to have been born when the law of the tyrant forbade the
male among those being born to be kept alive, and to have anticipated by grace
all the contribution that comes by time; and at once from swaddling-clothes
having been seen well-formed, to have caused hesitation in those who begot him
to make such a one vanish by death; =1.17= then, as the threat of the tyrant
prevailed, not simply to have been thrown to the stream of the Nile, but
having been exposed in a certain ark, smeared at the joints with asphalt and
pitch, thus to have been given to the current (for this those who have related
the things concerning him with care have narrated); and a certain divine power
steering the ark, to have been brought to harbor on a certain bank along the
side, the ark having been automatically waved out by the carrying of the
waters at that part; and the daughter of the king coming upon the meadows of
that bank where the ark happened to have run aground, to have become a find
for the princess, having cried out in infant fashion in the chest; and having
been seen with the grace appearing upon him, at once to have made the princess
his own through goodwill and to have been taken up into the rank of a son; and
having naturally turned away from the foreign teat, by a certain device of
those of his own kin to have been nursed at the maternal breast. =1.18= And
having already come out of the age of children, in the royal nurture and
having been educated in the outside education, not to have chosen the things
that were thought glory among those outside nor still to have accepted to
confess that sophisticated mother by whom he had been adopted into the rank of
a son, but to have returned again to that according to nature and to have
mixed himself among those of the same race; and a fight having arisen for a
certain Hebrew against an Egyptian, both to have fought with his own and to
have slain the foreigner; then two certain Hebrews having grappled with one
another, to have tried to settle the rivalry for them, having advised that it
is well that those who are brothers not make anger the arbiter of the
disputes, but nature. =1.19= And having been thrust away by the one looking
toward injustice, to have made this dishonor an occasion of the greater
philosophy and, having become far from the living-together with the many, to
have made the life after these things private, having married a daughter of
one of the foreigners, a man seeing through to the better and practiced in
judging both characters and life of men, who through one act, I mean the rush
against the shepherds, having seen in the young man the virtue, how not
looking toward a private gain he fought for the just, but judging the just
itself honorable by its own nature, he punished the injustice of the shepherds
who had done no wrong to him, having admired the young man for these things
and having judged his virtue in the apparent poverty more honorable than
wealth of many talents, both gives him his daughter to dwell with and left him
in his power to pursue the life according to his heart. And for him the life
was both of the mountain and private, freed from all marketplace tumult, as he
lived privately in the care of the sheep according to the desert. =1.20= And
time having passed in such a form of life, the history says a fearful
theophany came to be for him, in the standing noon another light beyond the
solar light having flashed around the eyes; and he, being made strange by the
unwonted spectacle, both looked up toward the mountain and saw a bush from
which the brightness was kindled in a fire-like way; and the branches of the
bush flourishing together with the flame as in dew, he said to himself these
words, that: Having crossed over I will see


this great vision; and having said it, no longer to have received the wonder
of the light with the eyes alone, but, what is most paradoxical of all, even
the hearing to have been illuminated by the rays of the brightness; for the
grace of the light having been divided toward both the senses, shone around
the eyes with the gleamings of the rays, and guided the hearing by the unmixed
doctrines. The voice of that light at any rate forbids Moses to approach the
mountain weighed down with the dead sandals, but having loosed the sandal from
the feet thus to touch that earth as much as was lit by the divine light.
=1.21= Upon these things (for one must I think not delay the discourse too
much in the bare history concerning the man, so that it may also hold to the
things set before us) having been empowered by the theophany that was seen he
is commanded to ransom his own race from the slavery of the Egyptians. And so
that he might especially learn the strength coming to him from God, he makes
the trial from the things in his hands by a divine command. And the trial was
this: A rod having fallen from the hand was ensouled and became an animal (and
the animal was a dragon) and again having been taken up by the hand, it became
that which it was before the becoming-beast. And the appearance of the hand
now, having been put forth from the bosom, was changed into the whiteness of
snow, and again, having come within the same, returns to its own nature.
=1.22= And as Moses was going down to Egypt and bringing along both the
foreign wife and the children born to him from her, when also a certain angel
is said to have met him, bringing on the fear concerning death, which the
woman propitiated by the blood of the circumcision of the child, then there
comes to be a meeting with Aaron, he too having been urged from God toward the
meeting. =1.23= Then by both the people in Egypt is gathered into a common
assembly and the release from slavery is announced around to those already
worn by the hardship of the works, and there comes to be a word toward the
tyrant himself concerning this. Indignation of the tyrant upon these things
both against those overseeing the works and against the Israelites themselves
greater than before; and the tax of brick-making is increased and a more
laborious command is sent down, they suffering evil not only at the clay, but
also being distressed about the chaff and the straw. =1.24= And Pharaoh (for
this was the name of the tyrant of the Egyptians) attempting to
counter-sophisticate the signs that had come from God through them by the
trickery of the sorcerers, then again Moses having made his own rod a beast in
the eyes of the Egyptians, the sorcery was thought to work the equal
wonder-making in the rods of the magicians. And the sophism was convicted
through the operation, the dragon that had been changed from the staff of
Moses having shown from swallowing the sorcerous woods, the supposed snakes,
that the rods of the sorcerers had no defensive nor any living power, except
the shape which the sorcery having sophistically devised presented to the eyes
of those easily deceived. =1.25= Then Moses, having seen the whole subject
people thinking with the leader of vice, brings a common plague upon the whole
nation of the Egyptians, excepting no one from the experience of the evils.
And there was moved together with him toward such a rush against the Egyptians
as some subject army the very elements of the things that are, those
contemplated in the universe, earth and water and air and fire, changing their
operations together with the choices of men; for by the same power according
to the same both time and place the disorderly was punished and that free of
vice remained unaffected. =1.26= For then the whole nature of the waters
according to Egypt having been turned into blood by the command of Moses, so
that even the fishes were destroyed, the water having been changed into fleshy
thickness, for the Hebrews alone as they drew it the blood was water, whence
the trickery also had opportunity in the water found among the Hebrews to
counterfeit the blood-like form. =1.27= Likewise also the frogs having crept
upon


Egypt all at once, whose generation was not poured out to so great a multitude
by some accustomed sequence of nature, but the command itself of the
constitution of the frogs innovated the nature of the animal that then
appeared, the whole Egyptian being destroyed, straitened in the dwellings by
these beasts, while the life of the Hebrews was kept clean of this
unpleasantness. =1.28= Thus the air for the Egyptians furnished no distinction
of night and day, they remaining in a like gloom, but for the Hebrews nothing
in these things was innovated beyond the customary. And all the other things
in the same way, the hail, the fire, the blisters, the gnats, the dog-flies,
the cloud of locusts, each operated against the Egyptians according as it is
naturally, but the Hebrews knew the suffering of those dwelling with them by
reports and narrations, receiving in themselves no assault of such things.
Then the destruction of the firstborns made more accurate the distinction of
the Hebrew toward the Egyptian, the ones being confounded with lamentations at
the loss of the dearest, the others remaining in all quiet and safety, for
whom the salvation was secured by the pouring-on of the blood, they having
been marked through the blood according to every entrance on both sides of the
doorposts together with the lintel joined upon them. =1.29= Upon these things
the Egyptians having been struck by the disaster of the firstborns and each by
himself and all in common bewailing the sufferings, Moses leads the Israelites
in the exodus, having prepared them beforehand under a form of use to carry
off with themselves the wealth of the Egyptians. And they having come a way of
three days outside Egypt, again the history says the Egyptian made it a hard
thing that Israel not remain in the slavery and, having equipped the whole
subject people in warlike fashion, ran upon the people through the
horse-force, and he, having seen the preparation of both the horses and the
arms, being inexperienced in war and untrained in such spectacles, was at once
struck down by fear and gathered against Moses, when also the history says the
most paradoxical thing concerning Moses, being cut in two by the operations,
by the voice and the word both emboldening the Israelites and bidding them
have the good hopes, but from within in the understanding bringing to God the
supplication on behalf of those who had cowered, and being guided by the
counsel from above how he might escape the danger, God himself, as the history
says, hearing the unspoken cry. =1.30= And a cloud leading the people by
divine power, not according to the common nature (for neither was its
constitution from certain vapors or exhalations, the air being thickened by
the vapors through the misty constitution and being packed together toward
itself by the winds, but something better and loftier than human grasp), by
that cloud, Scripture bearing witness, such was the wonder that even, the
solar ray shining warmly, it was a wall toward the people, both shading what
lay under and moistening the fiery of the air with a thin dew, and through the
night becoming fire, from evening to dawn torch-bearing the brightness for the
Israelites by its own light. =1.31= Both looking himself toward this, Moses,
and having taught the people to follow the thing appearing, when they came
according to the Red Sea, the cloud there being guided toward the journey, the
Egyptians with the whole army having circled the people from behind, no flight
of the dreadful things remaining for them from anywhere, they having been
caught between enemies and water, then Moses, urged by divine power,
accomplished the most unbelievable of all things. For having drawn near
according to the shore, he strikes the sea with the rod; and it was split
toward the blow and, just as it is natural to come about upon glass, if the
break should take a beginning at some part of it, it goes through in a
straight line toward the other limit, so of that whole sea


having been broken under at the extremity by the rod, the break of the waves
also passed over to the opposite bank. And having gone down into the depth
Moses where the sea was cut through with the whole people he was in the deep
with an unwetted and sunlit body, and going through the abysses on foot on a
dry bottom of the sea he did not fear that improvised wall-making from waves,
the brine having been fixed beside them from the flanks on this side and that,
in the manner of a wall. =1.32= And Pharaoh with the Egyptians having fallen
in together upon the sea according to that innovated road in the waters, again
the water is joined to the water and, the sea having been washed back toward
itself according to the former shape, there comes to be one surface and
appearance of the water, the Israelites already resting on the farther shore
the much and intense of the journey through the sea, when also they sang the
victory-song to God who then raised an unbloody trophy on their behalf, all
the Egyptians with the whole army with their horses and arms and chariots
having been made to vanish by the water. =1.33= Then Moses goes forward to the
things in front and having traveled a waterless way of three days comes to be
at a loss, not having how he will soothe the thirst of the army. For there was
a certain lake around which they encamped of sea-water, or rather more bitter
than according to the sea. Since then, sitting down at the water, they were
parched by thirst of the water, counsel having come to him from God, having
found a certain wood about the place he throws it into the water; and it was
at once drinkable, the wood by its own power having mixed the nature of the
water from bitterness into pleasure. =1.34= And the cloud having migrated to
the things in front, they too migrated together with the movement of the one
leading, always doing this, both stopping from the journey in whatever place
the standing of the cloud gave the signal of rest and setting out again
wherever the cloud might lead their journey. They come upon then, following
this guide, a place flowed down with drinkable water, abundantly washed around
by twelve springs and shaded with a grove of palms. And the palms were
seventy, sufficing even in a small number to make much wonder for those
seeing, by surpassing according to both beauty and size. =1.35= Again from
there too the guide, the cloud, having raised the army leads it toward another
place; and this was a certain desert, in parched and burnt sand, no moisture
of waters moistening the region, in which again the thirst having worn down
the people a certain rock on a hillock having been struck with a rod by Moses
gives out water both sweet and drinkable and more abundant than the need of so
great an army. =1.36= And there also, the preparation of the foods having
failed which they had taken as provision from Egypt toward the journey and the
people being pressed by famine, there comes to be the most unbelievable wonder
of all, the food not growing from earth according to what is accustomed for
them, but flowing down from above from heaven in a dew-like way; for dew was
poured upon them according to the dawn, and the dew became food for those
receiving it. For what was poured on was not a watery drop, as it is customary
to come about in dew, but instead of drops certain crystal-formed clots
according to the shape of coriander seed having been made spherical flowed
down, whose taste played the pleasure of honey. =1.37= And with this wonder
another also was contemplated, that all going out to the gathering in
different, as is likely, ages and powers collected neither more nor less one
than another according to the difference of power, but what was gathered was
measured by the need of each, so that neither the more powerful had the more,
nor the weaker was lessened from the equal share. Again upon this the history
says another wonder, that what each collected toward a day they stored nothing
for the next, and for the one who by some sparing stored from the present food
toward the morrow the stored thing was useless for food, being changed into a
nature of worms. =1.38= There is present also another paradoxical thing to the
history concerning this food; of


the days of the week the one having been honored with inactivity according to
a certain mystical reason, on the day before it, the inflow coming to be equal
as toward the time in front and the eagerness of those gathering having the
equal, what was gathered was found double of the accustomed measure, so that
they might have no occasion by the necessity of food to loosen the law of
inactivity. In which indeed the divine power was shown still more, that on the
other days the surplus becoming useless, in the preparation alone before the
sabbath (for this is the name of the inactive day) what was stored remained
uncorrupted, so as to seem in nothing more stale than the fresh. =1.39= Then a
war is joined for them toward a certain foreign nation. And the account names
those who then rose against them Amalakites. And then first the Israelite
people is armed for array, not all having been moved with the whole army upon
the battle, but having been chosen according to excellence and the picked of
them having taken up the war, in which again Moses showed a new manner of
generalship. For Joshua, who after Moses led the people, then leading the army
out against the Amalakites, Moses outside the arms on a certain lookout of a
hillock looks up toward heaven, two of those close to him standing with him on
either side. =1.40= Then we hear such a wonder in the things coming about from
the history: if Moses raised the hands toward heaven, the subject people was
strengthened against the enemies; but if he let them down, the army also gave
way to the rush of the foreigners, which indeed those standing beside him
having understood, the hands of Moses having become both heavy and hard to
move from some hidden cause, having gone under on either side they propped
them; and since they were weaker than so as to hold him up in an upright
shape, having propped his seat with a stone, thus they made Moses raise the
hands toward heaven through themselves, which having come about the foreign
people is defeated with might by the Israelites. =1.41= And the cloud
remaining upon the same place which led the journey of the people, there was
every necessity that the people too not migrate, there being no one leading
toward the migration. And thus the abundance toward living being present for
them without toil, the air from above raining ready bread for them, and from
below the rock furnishing the drink, and the cloud again treating the
unpleasantness of the open air, so as both to become a wall of the heat by day
and by night to loose the gloom, shining in a certain fire-like gleam, that
sojourn according to that desert in the foothill of the mountain according to
which the camp was established was painless for them. =1.42= And in this Moses
also led them in a certain more unspeakable initiation, the divine power
itself initiating both the whole people and the leader himself through the
wonders beyond word. And the initiation was accomplished in this manner. It
had been said beforehand to the people to be outside of all the other
pollutions, as many as are observed about both body and soul, and to purify
themselves with certain sprinklings, and even of marriage itself to be clean
in a stated number of days, so that having been washed of every passionate and
bodily disposition one might approach the mountain clean of passion about to
be initiated (and Sinai was the name of the mountain) which at that time was
given over for approach to the rational alone and of these to the men alone
and of these again to those purified from every stain; and there was every
guard and forethought that none of the irrational should set foot on the
mountain, and if somewhere it should even come about, that everything that was
of the irrational nature appearing according to the mountain be stoned by the
people. =1.43= Then the aerial light from a clear sky was blackened with
gloom, so that the mountain became invisible, taken around in a circle by the
darkness. And fire appearing through from the darkness made the sight fearful
for those seeing, feeding upon the circuit of the mountain from every side, so
that also by smoke through the running-around of the fire all that appeared
was smoldered under. And Moses led the people toward the ascent, not even
himself being bold


toward what was seen, but having cowered in the soul from dread and being
shaken in the body by fear, so that the suffering of the soul was not even
hidden toward the Israelites, but he confessed toward them that he had been
struck down by the things appearing and was not still in the body. =1.44= For
it was not the thing appearing only such as to produce astonishment in the
soul through the eyes, but also the fearful things were poured in through the
hearing, a voice from above being broken upon in a striking way upon all that
lay under; whose first assault even was hard and unmeetable for every hearing,
coming about in a likeness of the sound of the other trumpets, but surpassing
by the heavy and striking of the voice every such example, and becoming more
fearful as it went forward, always intensifying its sound toward the more
striking through addition. And this voice was articulate, the air by divine
power articulating the word apart from the vocal organs. And the word was not
articulated at random, but it legislated divine ordinances. And the voice
going forward was increased through advance and the trumpet sounded beyond
itself, always surpassing the preceding voices by those next. =1.45= And the
whole people was weaker than so as to bear what was both seen and heard.
Wherefore also a common request from all is brought to Moses that the law be
mediated through him, as the people will not disbelieve, that every command is
divine which may be commanded from him according to the teaching from above.
Again then all having run down toward the foothill, Moses alone was left,
having shown in himself the contrary of what was likely; for while all the
others are more bold in the fearful things in the communion of those sharing,
this one having been made alone of those standing with him was more
courageous, so that from this it became clear that the fear by which he was
held at the beginning was not a private suffering of his, but that he suffered
these things through sympathy toward those who had cowered. =1.46= When then,
having been stripped as of some burden of the cowardice of the people, he was
upon himself, then he even dares the darkness itself and comes to be within
the invisible things no longer appearing to those seeing. For having slipped
through toward the inner shrine of the divine initiation, there he was with
the invisible not being seen, teaching, I think, through the things he did,
that it is necessary for the one about to be with God to go out from every
thing that appears and, having stretched his own understanding toward the
invisible and incomprehensible, as upon some peak of a mountain, to believe
the divine to be there in which the understanding does not arrive. =1.47= And
having come to be in that he receives the divine commands. And these were
teaching of virtue, whose heading then is piety and having the fitting
conceptions concerning the divine nature, that it lies above every cognitive
thought and example, being likened to none of the things known. For he is
commanded to look toward none of the things grasped in the conceptions about
the divine nor to liken the nature lying above the all to any of the things
known from grasp, but believing that it is, to leave uninvestigated the
of-what-sort or how-much or from-where or how it is, as unattainable. =1.48=
And the word adds also as many achievements as are of character, making the
teaching by both generic and private laws. For a generic law destructive of

them, someone would not condemn the bound, which defines arrogance to be the
lowest falling. =2.282= Looking toward these things Moses trains to be
moderate and not to be raised by the achievements, but always to dispose the
present well. For not because you have become better of the pleasures, is it
already unaccountable for you to be captured by another form of passion. For
every passion is a falling, so long as it is a passion. And there is no
difference of fallings in the variation of the sufferings. He who has slipped
in the smooth of pleasure has fallen and he who has been tripped under through
arrogance has been overturned. For no form of a falling-down is choiceworthy
for the one who has understanding. But equally every falling is to be fled, so
long as it is a falling. =2.283= As if you should also see some of those now
keeping clean in a certain part of the sickness according to the pleasures,
and again by zeal, by seeming to be above the others, pushing himself in
toward the priesthood, think you see that one falling under-earth in the
height of arrogance. =2.284= For the law teaches in the things next that the
priesthood is a certain divine thing and not human. And it teaches thus.
Having marked rods from each tribe upon the name of those who had given he
sets them before the altar, so that the rod become a witness of the
appointment from above, becoming notable beside the others by a certain divine
wonder. And this having come about the rods of the others remained what they
were, but that of the priest, itself having been rooted in itself, not through
some foreign moisture, but of the power put in it from God, sprouted branches
and fruit and the fruit went forward into perfecting. And the fruit was a nut,
=2.285= which having come about, the whole subject people was trained toward
good order. And it is fitting to understand through the fruit, which the rod
of Aaron sprouted, of what sort the life in the priesthood must be, a certain
self-controlled and astringent and hardened-around in the appearing life, but
containing from within the eatable in the hidden and unseen, which is then
uncovered, whenever the eating is ripened by time and the astringent wrapping
is broken around and that woody covering-before of the eatable is rubbed
around. =2.286= And if of some so-called priest you should learn thoroughly
the life apple-formed and well-breathing and rose-colored, such as of the many
are flowered through with linen and purple and being fattened in the rich
tables who drink the strained wine, and being anointed with the first myrrhs
and as many other things as seem sweet according to the ready taste for those
fruiting the enjoying life, you would well say upon this the evangelical,
that: seeing the fruit, I do not recognize through the fruit the priestly
tree. Other is the fruit of the priesthood and this is other. That fruit was
self-control, and this luxury. That one was not fattened from earthly
moisture, and for this one many channels of the pleasures flow from below,
through which the autumn of the life is over-reddened toward such a season.
=2.287= And when the subject people became clean even of this passion, then it
goes through the foreign life, the law leading of the royal highway, the
turning-out coming about according to neither part. For the turning-beside
upon the sides is unsafe for the one journeying. For just as if two cliffs
should straiten one path upon the extreme ridge, there is a danger for the one
walking through this to be turned beside of the middle from here or from here
(for equally from either side the pit from the cliff succeeds the
turning-out), thus the law wishes the one following it according to a track
not to leave the road which, as the Lord says, is both narrow and pressed,
neither upon the left and well-named, nor upon the thought right. =2.288= And
this word is a doctrine defining the virtues to be contemplated in a middle,
because every vice is naturally operated either according to a falling-short
or according to an over-falling of virtue, as upon manliness a certain
falling-short of virtue is cowardice, and an over-falling is rashness; and


what keeps clean of each of these is both contemplated in the middle of the
neighboring vices and is virtue. =2.289= According to the same manner also all
the other things, as many as are eager toward the better, are somehow taken in
the middle by the evil neighborings. Wisdom has the middle of both cleverness
and simplicity. Neither the prudent of the snake is praiseworthy, nor the
simple of the dove, if it should be necessary to take each of these upon
itself alone. But the mixed habit through the middle of these two becomes
virtue. The falling-short according to self-control is incontinent, the
overflowing has been cauterized in the conscience, as the Apostle defines. For
the one has been poured out unrestrainedly with the pleasures, and the other
abominates even marriage equal with adultery. And the habit contemplated
through the middle of these is self-control. =2.290= And since, as the Lord
says, this world lies in the wicked, and what is opposed to virtue is foreign
for those following the law, which is wickedness, he who journeys through this
world according to the life will safely finish this necessary journey of
virtue, if he should guard the highway truly both worn and being whitened by
virtue, nowhere being turned beside toward the neighboring roadlessnesses
through vice. =2.291= And since, as has been said, with the ascent of virtue
even the plot of the opposing one goes up together, finding out
correspondingly of each the starting-points of the turning-beside into the
evil, then the people having increased toward a greater in the life according
to God, the opposing one takes hold of another plot according to those clever
in the warlike things, who having judged the array according to face of those
prevailing according to the power hard to overcome, general down the opposing
one with certain ambushes and traps. Thus the general of vice upon those
empowered by law and virtue no longer leads out the power against according to
face, but works out the plot according to the unseen with certain traps.
=2.292= He then calls into alliance against those being plotted by him the
sorcery, which the history says to be a certain seer and bird-diviner from a
certain operation of demons having a harmful power somewhere against those
opposite and having been hired by the one prevailing of the Midianites upon
this, to harm through a curse those living to God, this one to turn the curse
into a blessing. And we understand through the sequence of the things
contemplated before that not even the sorcery is operative against those
living in virtue, but that those fortified through the divine help prevail
over every plot. =2.293= For that the remembered one practices the
bird-watching divination is witnessed by the history in the things in which it
says both to have the oracles through the hand and to be counseled from the
birds and before these that through the braying of the ass he was taught the
things about the eagerness set before him, whose note, through his being
accustomed to be counseled by the voices of the irrational by a certain
demonic operation, Scripture related as articulate, indicating that those
pre-taken by such a deceit of the demons arrive as far as these things as to
receive instead of a word the teaching from the voice of the irrational coming
to them from the of-what-sort observation, attending to which, through the
things themselves by which he was deceived he was trained out in the
unfightable of the power against whom he was hired. =2.294= For also in the
evangelical history there had been prepared =2.294= into resistance of the
authority of the Lord the composition of the demons, the legion. And the one
having the might against all having drawn near, it shouts the overlying power
and is not hidden of the truth, that this one is the divine nature, who in the
due times will bring on the punishment to those who have sinned. For the voice
of the demons says: we know you who you are, the holy one of God, and that you
have come before time to torture us. Which indeed also then came about, the
demonic power following the seer teaching Balaam the both unfightable and
unantagonizable of the people. =2.295= And we say, fitting the history to the
things examined before, against those living in virtue the


one wishing to curse not to be able to send forth any both grievous and
unfitting voice, but to turn the curse into a blessing. And what we understand
is such, that the blame of railing does not touch those living according to
virtue. =2.296= For how will the one without possessions be railed upon for
greed? and how will someone noise about prodigality upon the one withdrawn and
living privately, or anger upon the meek, or pride upon the moderate-minded,
or some other of the blameworthy upon those known from the contrary, whose aim
is to furnish the life ungraspable for blame, so that, as the Apostle says,
the one from the contrary may be turned in, having nothing to say? For this
reason the voice of the one taken along into a curse says: how shall I curse
whom the Lord does not curse? that is: how shall I rail upon the one who has
not given the material of the railing, for whom through looking toward God the
life is unwounded into vice? =2.297= Yet not, since the finder of vice missed
of this, did he altogether cease of the device against those being plotted,
but he turns the device toward his own wrestling, again baiting the nature
toward the evil through pleasure. For truly pleasure having been put forth as
some bait of every vice easily draws the more greedy souls upon the hook of
destruction. And somehow especially through the incontinent pleasure the
nature is dragged beside unguardedly toward the evil. =2.298= Which indeed
also then came about. For those who prevailed over the arms and showed every
throwing-on from iron weaker of their own power and turned the phalanx of
those opposing with might, were wounded by the womanly missiles through
pleasure. And they became both better of the men, and lesser of the women. For
at the same time the women were seen by them putting forth the forms instead
of arms, and at once they took a forgetting of the tone of manliness, having
loosed the spirit into pleasure. =2.299= And they were in these things, in
which it is likely that those who raged upon the unlawful mixing of the
foreigners are. And the kinship toward the evil was a making-foreign of the
alliance of the beautiful. For at once the Divine was made warlike out for
them. But the zealous Phinehas did not wait for the sin to be purified by the
vote from above. But he himself became both judge and executioner together.
=2.300= For having been moved into the anger according to those who had raged,
he did the work of the priest, having purified the sin with blood, blood not
of some blameless animal that had partaken nothing of the stain of
incontinence, but of those yoked together with one another in vice, upon whom
the point having gone through according to the same of the two bodies stood
the movement of the divine justice, having mixed down the pleasure with the
death of those who had sinned. =2.301= And it seems to me the history lays
down a certain soul-profitable counsel for men, through which we are taught
that of many passions which overcome the reasonings of men no other passion
has so great a strength against us, as to be equaled toward the disease of
pleasure. For those Israelites all at once, who were shown even better of the
Egyptian horse and who prevailed over the Amalakites and appeared fearful to
the nation after them and after these things prevailed over the phalanx of the
Midianites, these being enslaved to the disease together with the sight of the
foreign women shows nothing other, as has been said, than that pleasure is a
certain hard to fight and hard to antagonize warlike one of us, =2.302= which
having prevailed down of those unconquered by the arms together with appearing
raised the trophy of dishonor against them, having pilloried their shame with
the light as witness. For through itself it showed the men as cattle, whom the
beast-like and irrational rush toward incontinence persuaded to forget the
human nature, not even hiding the pollution, but parading in the dishonor of
the passion and beautifying themselves in the stain of shame, in the manner of
swine openly wallowing in the mire of uncleanness in the sights of one
another. =2.303= What then are we trained by the narration? That having
learned how great a strength toward the evil the


disease of pleasure has, as much as possible to settle our own life far from
such a neighboring, so that the disease may not take some passage against us,
as some fire working the wicked flame through the drawing near. For this
Solomon teaches saying in the Wisdom not to touch the coal with a naked foot
nor to store fire in the bosom, as being upon us to remain in impassibility so
long as we are far from the kindling. And if we should come to be according to
this so as to touch this thoroughly-burning heat, the fire of the desire will
become in-bosom and thus there will follow both the burning for the foot and
the destruction for the bosom. =2.304= And in the Gospel the Lord by his own
voice, so that we may be guarded far from such an evil, cut out as some root
of the passion the desire coming to be through seeing, teaching that he who
has received the passion by the sight gives a road against himself to the
disease. For the wicked of the sufferings in the manner of a plague whenever
it once prevails down of the vital things, is ceased by death alone. =2.305=
But it is not necessary, I think, to lengthen the word, setting forth all the
life of Moses as an example of virtue for those who happen upon it. For for
the one who has stretched himself toward the loftier life, the things said
would become not small provisions toward the true philosophy. And for the one
being softened toward the sweats on behalf of virtue, even if many-times of
the things said should be written, no gain would come from the multitude.
=2.306= Except, so that a forgetting of the definition according to the
preface may not come about, in which the word insisted for us that the perfect
life is such, whose no circumscription of perfection hinders the going
forward, but the always coming increase of the life toward the better is a
road for the soul toward perfecting, it would be well, having led the word
toward the limit of the life of Moses, to show firm the bound of perfection
given to us. =2.307= For he who through so many ascents was being raised
according to all the life was not at a loss to become again loftier of
himself, so that, I think, through all things in the manner of an eagle his
life may be contemplated above-cloud and aloft, being wound about the ether of
the intellective ascent. =2.308= He was born, when for a Hebrew to be born
seemed an injustice among the Egyptians. The tyrant then punishing the one
born by a law, he becomes better of the destruction-making law, having been
saved around first by those who begot him, and after these things by those
themselves who had set the law. And those by whom his death was made eager by
a law, these brought in every forethought not of the life only, but also of
the well-reputed nurture, having led the young man through all wisdom. =2.309=
After these things he becomes better of the human honor and superior of the
royal dignity, having judged it stronger and more kingly instead of the
spear-bearers and the royal adornment to have the guard of virtue and to be
beautified in its adornment. =2.310= After this he saves around his own race
and throws down the Egyptian by the blow, we understanding through the
examining contemplation both the enemy of the soul and the friend. Then he
makes quiet a teacher of the lofty lessons and thus is lit in the
understanding by the light that shone out from the bush. And then he makes an
eagerness to share with those of the same race the beautiful things that have
come to him from God. =2.311= In these things he made a twofold showing of the
power, having shown the one defensive against those contrary in both
many-mannered and successive plagues, and the other benefiting upon those of
the same race. He leads through the sea so great a people by foot, not having
prepared for himself a fleet of ships, but having ship-built the faith for
them toward the sailing-through, he makes the depth dry land for the Hebrews,
and the dry land a sea for the Egyptians. =2.312= He sang the victory-songs.
He was led by the pillar. He was lit by the heavenly fire. He set beside
himself a table from the food from above. He was filled of the rock. He
stretched up the hands upon the destruction of the Amalakites. He approaches
the mountain. He comes to be in darkness. He heard a trumpet. To the divine
nature


he drew near. He was encompassed by the tabernacle above. He adorned the
priesthood. He pitched the tabernacle. He corrected the life with the laws. He
achieved the last wars according to the said manner. =2.313= Upon the limit of
the achievements he punished the incontinence through the priesthood. For this
the anger that came through Phinehas according to the passion hinted at. Upon
all these things, he goes toward the mountain of rest. He does not set foot on
the land below toward which the people below looked from a promise. He who
practiced to live by that flowing from above no longer tastes earthly food,
but having come to be above according to the ridge itself of the mountain, as
some knowing statue-maker, having worked out accurately the whole statue of
his life, upon the extreme of the construction he set not an end, but a peak
upon the work. =2.314= For what does the history say concerning him? That
Moses died, a household servant of the Lord, through a saying of God, and no
one knew his burial, and his eyes were not dimmed, and his face was not
destroyed. For we are taught through these things that having come to be
through so many achievements he is then deemed worthy of this lofty name, so
as to be called a household servant of God, which is equal to say that he
became better of every. For someone would not serve God unless he became
better of all the things in the world. And this is for him also an end of the
life according to virtue being achieved through a saying of God, which indeed
the history calls an end, a living end, which a tomb does not succeed, upon
which a mound is not heaped, which does not bring on dimness for the eyes and
destruction for the face. =2.315= What then are we trained through the things
said? To look through the life toward one end, to be called household servants
of God through the things lived. For whenever you overcome all the enemies,
the Egyptian, the Amalakite, the Idumaean, the Midianite, and you pass the
water and are lit by the cloud and are sweetened through the wood and drink of
the rock and taste of the food from above and through both purity and holiness
you make a road for yourself of the ascent upon the mountain and having come
to be there you are catechized the divine mystery by the sound of the trumpets
and in the hard-to-contemplate darkness you draw near to God through faith and
there you are taught the mysteries of the tabernacle and the dignity of the
priesthood, =2.316= whenever you become a stone-cutter of your hearts, so as
to engrave in these the divine sayings from God, whenever you make the image
of the gold vanish, that is if you wipe out the greedy desire of the life,
whenever you are raised so much, so as to appear unfightable to the sorcery of
Balaam (and having heard sorcery, understand for me the varied deceit of this
life, through which men, being drugged as by a certain Circean mixing-bowl,
having stood out of their own nature are formed over into forms of irrational
ones), whenever you come to be through all these and there sprouts in you the
rod of the priesthood, which draws no certain moisture of earth toward the
sprout, but has from home the power of the fruit and of a nut-fruit, whose
first encounter is both bitter and astringent, and what lies in is sweet and
eatable, whenever everything that rises against your dignity passes over into
vanishing, becoming under-earth according to Dathan or having been consumed
out by the fire according to Korah, then you will draw near to the end.
=2.317= And I call an end that for the sake of which all things come about, as
an end of the farming the enjoyment of the fruits, an end of the construction
of the house the dwelling, an end of the trading the wealth and of the toils
in the contests the crown. Thus also an end of the lofty manner of life is to
be called a household servant of God, with which is contemplated together not
to be heaped under by the mound, and this is for the life to come to be both
naked and unsuperfluous of the wicked after-drags. =2.318= And of this slavery
the word also says another mark, neither for an eye to be dimmed, nor for a
face to be destroyed. For the eye that is through all in light how will it be
dimmed through darkness of which it has been made foreign? And he who has
achieved incorruption in all the life altogether receives no destruction upon
himself. For he who has truly come to be according to image of God and nowhere
been turned aside


of the divine character bears the marks upon himself and agrees through all
things with the likeness toward the archetype, beautifying his own soul with
the both incorruptible and unalterable and unmixed of every vice. =2.319=
These things to you, O man of God, concerning the perfection of the life
according to virtue, Caesarius, this short word of ours proposes, having
sketched for you as some prototype in a form of beauty the life of the great
Moses, upon which to transcribe in ourselves through the imitation of the
practices according to each of us the character of the beauty shown beforehand
to us. For of Moses having achieved the possible perfection who would be found
for us a more trustworthy witness than the divine voice which says toward him
that: I have known you beside all, but also that he was named a friend of God
by God himself and that having chosen rather to be destroyed together with
all, unless the Divine should also be propitiated through goodwill for those
upon the things they offended, to stand the anger against the Israelites, God
having turned aside his own judgment, so as not to grieve the friend. And all
such things are a clear witness and a proof of the life of Moses having gone
up toward the highest bound of perfection. =2.320= Since then this was the
thing sought from us, what the perfect of the virtuous manner of life is, and
the perfect has been found through the things said, it is time for you, O
noble one, to look toward the example and the things contemplated through a
loftier raising concerning the things said historically; transferring them
upon your own life both to be known by God and to become a friend of him. For
this is as truly the perfection, no longer to be separated of the life
according to vice slavishly by fear of punishment, nor to operate the good by
the hope of the wages, trading down the virtuous life with a certain
business-like and exchange-like disposition, but having looked over all things
even of the things stored in promises through hope, to think only fearful to
fall out of the friendship of God, and to judge for themselves only both
honorable and lovable to become a friend to God, which is, according at least
to my word, the perfection of the life, =2.321= and whatever may be found by
you toward the both more magnificent and more divine, your understanding
having been raised (and many, I know well, will be found), the gain will
altogether be common in Christ Jesus, amen.
