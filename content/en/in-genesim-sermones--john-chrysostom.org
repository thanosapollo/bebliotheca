be able to say. How then is it not absurd that one who is ignorant of the
clearer and easier thing should busy himself and meddle with the more
difficult and more unspeakable?

3. Do you want me to lead you also to another and easier thing, happening
every day? Yet not even of this will he tell me the account. We eat bread
every day. How then, tell me, is this nature of bread changed into blood and
phlegm, and bile, and the remaining humors in us? For the one is dense and
hard, but the blood is loose and flowing, and the one is white or wheat
colored, but the other red and black. And if one should go through the
differences of the other qualities, he will find a great interval between
bread and blood. How then do these things happen, tell me, and give the
account. But you would not be able. Then, not having the account to give of
food altered every day, do you demand of me accounts of the creation of God?
And how is this not of the last folly? For if God is as we are, demand the
account of the things that came to be; or rather not even so; for many of the
things that come to be by human art we cannot say how they come to be; for
example, how from the earth in the mines the nature of gold comes to be; how
sand is changed into the purity of glass. And there are more than these to
tell, which come to be by human art, but we do not know the account. Yet if
God is as we are, demand an account; but if


he stands infinitely apart from us and incomparably exceeds, how would it not
be of the last madness, when we confess that both his wisdom and his power are
infinite, and divine and incomprehensible, to demand accounts of him for each
of the things that come to be, as of some human art? But leaving the
reasonings, let us return to the unbreakable rock: In the beginning God made
the heaven and the earth. Stand upon this foundation; let no one drag you down
into the confusion of human reasonings; For the reasonings of mortals are
cowardly, and their devices unsafe. Do not then, leaving the firm, commit the
salvation of your soul to the rotten and unsafe; but remain in the things you
learned and were assured of, and say: In the beginning God made the heaven and
the earth. And if a Manichean comes, or Marcion, or those sick with the
disease of Valentinus, or anyone else, put forward this saying; and if you see
him laughing, do you weep for him as mad. They have a box =54.585= wood
complexion, and a lowered brow, and mildness of words; but flee the bait, and
learn the wolf hidden in the sheep's skin. For this especially hate him, that
toward you his fellow servant he seems gentle and tame, but toward the common
Master of us all he is fiercer than raging dogs, introducing an undeclared
battle into heaven and a truce less war, and setting some power opposite
against God. Flee the venom of wickedness, hate the destructive drugs; and the
inheritance you received from the fathers, the faith and teaching from the
divine Scriptures, hold this with much security. In the beginning God made the
heaven and the earth. What is this? first the heaven, then the earth? first
the roof, then the floor? For he is not subject to necessity of nature, nor
does he slave to sequence of art. For the will of God is creator and craftsman
both of nature and of art and of all things that are. And the earth was
invisible and unformed. For what reason did he bring forth the heaven
completed, but Moses says that he crafts the earth little by little? So that,
having learned his power in the better element, you may be fully assured that
he was able to bring this too, as that, completed. But for you and your
salvation he did not do so. How for me and my salvation, he says? The earth is
a common table and fatherland and nurse and mother of all, and city and common
tomb. For our bodies are from it, and food for our bodies from there, and
dwelling in it and sojourn, and after death a return to it again. So that the
necessity of need may not prepare you to marvel at it beyond its worth, and
the multitude of benefits trip you into impiety, he shows you, before it came
to be, it formless and unshaped, so that seeing the weakness you may marvel at
him who brought it forth and put all this power into it; so that you may
glorify him who prepared such things for our ease. And God is glorified not
through right doctrines only, but also through a best way of life; For let
your light shine, he says, before men, that they may see your good works, and
glorify your Father who is in the heavens.

ourselves, but =54.586= for the poor. And how, I say. If you deposit the

eye sees, no robber filches, no housebreaker digging through, no household
slave taking it runs away; for that treasury is inviolable. But if you bury it

and sycophant and household slave and every harm. Often at any rate, after a

then we are rather masters of the possessions, when we deposit them with the
poor? Not for guarding only is that place more secure, but it is also a ground
of greater gain and revenue. For if you lend to a man, you received a
hundredth; but if you lend to God through the poor man, you will receive not a
hundredth, but a hundredfold. And if you sow a fertile field, when it bears
much produce, you will bring the seeds tenfold; but if you sow into heaven,
after they become a hundredfold, you will also receive back life eternal and
unaging and immortal. And here there is much toil for those who cast the

rains, nor blight, nor hail, nor an army of locusts, nor floods of rivers, nor
anything else is there ever to fear for those sowing there; but the seeds cast

SERMON II.

Why ever upon the sun and moon and heaven and the others did he say, Let it
be; but upon man, Let us make? and what ever is the, According to image.

1. Do you then remember the questions put before you formerly? For you have
lifted us to such a pitch of madness and daring, as even now to dare
questions; or rather this would not be of daring, nor of madness. For not
trusting in our own power, but throwing the whole upon the prayers =54.587= of
the presidents and upon yours, we stripped for the stadium. And a prayer of
the Church is able to such a degree, that even if we were more voiceless than
stones, it would make our tongue lighter than any wing. For just as a west
wind falling into the midst

of the ship's sails sends the vessel along sharper than an arrow; so also a
prayer of the Church falling into the tongue of the speaker sends the word
along more vehemently than a west wind. Therefore we too every day strip with
confidence. For if in the outer contests one who has only ten or twenty lovers
in so great a people comes down to the contest with eagerness; much more we,
where not ten, nor twenty only, but the whole theater for us is composed of
brothers and fathers, will do this with confidence. And yet in the outer
contests the contestant would reap nothing great from the spectator, except
that he shout, and marvel at what has happened, and contend with those who
contradict while sitting above; but to come down into the stadium, and stretch
out a hand, and drag the foot of the opponent, or show any other such thing,
is not lawful for these. For those who arranged those contests from the
beginning, having fixed sharp stakes, and thrown ropes around in a circle,
thus restrain the madness of the spectators. And what wonder, if it is not
lawful for a spectator to come down, when even the trainer they seat outside
by the dust, and bid him bring in from afar the alliance from teaching for
those competing, but they do not let him come near? But here it is not so; but
both teacher and spectator can come down to us, and stand near in disposition,
and through the prayers join our power. Come then after the manner of those
athletes let us take hold of the wrestlings. For when those, having caught one
another in the middle, are shot out by the force of the holds toward the crowd
standing around them outside because of the narrowness of the place, loosing
the holds, they return again to the place of the contest; then having returned
they do not intertwine upright again, but having set themselves in that manner
of the bonds in which they also were when they were loosed. Since then the
narrowness of place forced us too to break off the word, come, returning to
the place of the contest, let us loose the holds from the things read to us
today. And God said, he says, Let us make man according to our image and
likeness. This one thing first is worth asking, why ever, when the heaven was
coming to be, nowhere was the, Let us make, said, but, Let heaven be, Let
light be, and

SERMON III.

What is the, According to likeness, and for what reason, though God said that
we rule the beasts, we do not rule, and that this is of much care.

1. Just as there is no profit for those who sow, when the seeds are thrown
beside the way; so neither will there be anything more for the speaker, when
the word is not borne toward the mind of those who hear, but the echo of the
voice, simply poured out into the air, leaves the hearer without profit. And I
have not said these things to you simply, but so that you may not gape only
toward the simpler of the thoughts, but may also dare the deeper. For if we do
not now go down to the depth of the Scriptures, when our limbs are light for
swimming, and the eye sharper, no longer troubled by the wicked stream of
luxury, and the breath more sufficient, so as not to be choked, when will we
go down? When there is luxury and feasting and drunkenness and a table full of
gluttony? But then it is not even easy to be moved, so does the heavy load of
luxury press the soul. Do you not see that those too who wish to find costly
stones do not, sitting above by the shore and counting the waves, thus find
what is sought, but they dive down into the depth itself; and yet there is
much toil in the search, and much danger in the finding, and after the finding
no gain? For what great thing would the finding of costly stones bring into
our life? Would that it had not brought in great evils. For what overturns our
life and makes all things above and below is nothing other than the madness
for money. But nevertheless those men throw away both soul and body for the
sake of daily food, and dare the waves; but here there is not danger, not

toil so stretched, but little and light, and this for the guarding of the
things found. For the things found with ease seem to many to be also easy to
despise. There is not a disturbance of waves in the sea of the Scriptures, but
this sea is more fair than any harbor; =54.591= there is no necessity to be
carried down toward the dark bosoms of the abyss, nor to commit the salvation
of the body to a rush of irrational waters; but here there is much light, and
brighter than the rays themselves, and much calm, and every disturbance out of
the way, and of the things found so great a gain as it is not possible even by
word to set forth. Let us not then grow weary, but let us attempt the search.
You heard that according to the image of God God made man; and what ever this
According to image and according to likeness is we have said; that it is not
unvarying of essence, but likeness of rule; and the, According to likeness, is
to be tame and gentle, and to be likened as far as power goes to God according
to the account of virtue, as Christ says; Become like my Father who is in the
heavens. For just as in this broad and spacious earth some of the living
beings are more irrational, and some more beastlike; so also in the breadth of
our soul, some of the reasonings are more irrational and brutish, and some
more beastlike and wilder. We must then rule them and prevail, and hand over
the rule of these to reasoning. And how could one prevail over a beastlike
reasoning, he says? What do you say, man? We prevail over lions, and we tame
their souls, and you doubt whether you will be able to change the beastliness
of reasoning toward tameness? And yet for the lion the wild is according to
nature, but the tame contrary to nature; but for you the contrary, the gentle
according to nature, but the beastliness contrary to nature. Will he then who
casts out what is according to nature, and puts what is contrary to nature
into the soul of the beast, not be able to keep what is according to nature in
his own soul? and of how much sloth would this not be? And upon the soul of
the lions there is also another difficulty with this; for the soul of the
beast is empty of reasonings. But nevertheless you have often seen lions led
through the market tamer than sheep, and many of those in the workshops have
often paid silver to the one holding it, some wage of the art and of the
wisdom by which he tamed the beast; but upon your soul there is also
reasoning, and fear of God, and much help from many sides. Do not then tell me
excuses and pretexts. For it is possible, if you will, for you to be tame and
gentle. Let us make man according to our image and according to likeness, and
let them rule the beasts.

2. Here Greeks spring upon us, and they say the oracle lies; for we do not
rule the beasts; they rule us, and they throw much agony upon us. This then is
not true at all; for even a human sight appearing is enough to put a beast out
of the way; so great is the fear in them toward us. But if somewhere it should
rush toward defense, either hunger compelling, or we ourselves often crowding
them, and driving them out toward necessity, this then would not be of a
completed rule. For neither, if one of us seeing robbers coming on should arm
himself, and go toward the defense, is the matter of rule, but much provision
of his own =54.592= salvation; yet I do not make the defense from here, but
from elsewhere, whence it is useful for you also to hear. We fear the beasts
and are afraid, and we have fallen out of the rule; I do not contradict; I say
it myself too; but this does not show the law of God false. For at the
beginning things were not so disposed, but it feared and trembled, and
submitted, as to a master, to man, but when we fell out of the freedom of
speech and of the honor, for this reason we fear them. Whence is this clear?
God brought the beasts to Adam, to see what he would call them; and


Adam did not leap away, as being afraid, but as to servants subjected he set
the names upon all. And this is a symbol of mastery. Therefore God also,
wishing to show him through this too the dignity of the authority, entrusted
to him the setting of the names, and the names set remained from that time
upon them. For everything that Adam called, this is a name to them, he says.
This then is one sign that the beasts were not fearful to man at the
beginning; and a second and clearer than the first, the conversation of the
serpent toward the woman. For if the beasts were fearful to men, the woman
seeing the serpent would not have remained, but would have fled; she would not
have received counsel, she would not have conversed with him with so much
fearlessness, but at once at the sight she would have been struck and leaped
away; but now she converses, and does not fear; for this fear was not yet. But
when sin entered, the things of the honor also were taken away; and just as
among household slaves, those who are in good standing are fearful to their
fellow servants, but those who have offended fear even their fellow servants;
so it happened also upon man. For as long as he had freedom of speech toward
God, he was fearful to the beasts; but when he offended, he fears even the
last of the fellow servants thereafter. And if this is not so, you show me
before the sin that the beasts were fearful to men; but you would not be able.
And if after these things the fear entered, this too is of the Master's care.
For if, when the commandment given by God to man was moved and loosed, the
honor given him by God remained unmoved, he would not have risen easily. For
when men both disobeying and not disobeying enjoy the same honor, they are
trained rather toward wickedness, and they do not easily stand off from evil.
For if now, when fears and punishments and chastisements lie upon them, they
do not endure to be temperate, what would they have been, if they suffered
nothing for the terrible things they committed? So that it is clear that
caring for us and thinking of us God cast us out of the rule. And here too
consider his unspeakable love of mankind. For Adam overturned the whole
commandment, and transgressed the law; but God did not loose the whole honor,
nor did he cast him out of the whole authority; but those living beings only
he let be outside the rule, which do not contribute much to him toward the
need of life; but the necessary and useful and those bringing much service
into our life, these he let remain in the slavery. He left herds of oxen, so
that we may draw a plow, so that we may cut the earth, so that we may cast the
=54.593= seeds; he left the kinds of beasts of burden, so that they may take a
share with us of the toils toward the carrying of loads; he left flocks of
sheep, so that we may have a sufficient supply for coverings of garments; and
other kinds of living beings he let remain bringing us much other need. For
when punishing man he said, In the sweat of your face you will eat your bread,
so that this sweat and the labor and the toil may not be unbearable, he
lightened the heavy and burdensome of the sweat by the multitude of the
irrational things that take hold with us of the toil and of this misery. And
just as a master loving mankind and careful, having whipped his own household
slave, applies some healing to the whip strokes; so also God, having laid on a
sentence, wishes in every way to make this sentence lighter, having sentenced
us to sweat and continual toil, but having prepared many kinds of the
irrational to take hold with us of the toil. For all these things then let us
give thanks to him; for both the giving of the honor to us, and the taking
away of the honor again, and the not taking it all away, and the setting the
fear of the beasts upon us, and all things, if one examines, are full of much
wisdom, much care, much love of mankind; of which may it be that we all enjoy
continually unto the glory of


him who worked these things, God; to him the glory unto the ages of the ages.
Amen.

SERMON IV.

That sin brought in three manners of slavery; and toward those who hear
slothfully, and do not honor parents.

1. You heard yesterday how God made man king and ruler of the beasts, and how
he at once cast him out of the kingship; or rather not God, but he himself
through the disobedience cast himself out of the honor. For the obtaining of
the kingship came to be of the love of mankind of God alone. For he did not
give him this as a return of successes, but even before he came to be, he
adorned him with the honor. For so that you may not say that after these
things man came to be, then having succeeded in many things, he thus drew God
to give him the rule of the beasts, being about to form him he converses
concerning his rule, saying thus; Let us make man according to our image and
according to likeness, and let them rule the beasts of the earth. Before the
life the honor, before the creation the crown, and before he came to be he is
led up to the kingly throne. For men honor those subject to them in last old
age after many toils and countless dangers, those in peace, those in wars,
then; but God not so, but as soon as he came to be he led him to this honor,
showing that what comes to be is not a return of successes, but divine grace,
and not a debt. The receiving of the rule then came to be from the love of
mankind of God alone; but the falling out of the rule, from his own sloth. For
just as kings loose from the rule those who do not obey their commands; so God
also did upon man, then loosing him from the rule. And it is necessary today
to say how much other honor also the nature of sin took away, and how many
manners of slavery it brought in =54.594= , as some tyrant in many formed
bonds, binding our nature with the authorities of every kind. There is then a
first rule and slavery, according to which the men also hold the women; for
after the sin the need of this came to be. For before the disobedience she was
of equal honor with the man; for when God formed her, with the words he used
also upon the forming of the man, with these he used also upon the creation of
the woman. As then he said upon him, Let us make man according to our image
and according to likeness, and he did not say, Let man be; so also upon her he
did not say, Let woman be, but here too, Let us make a helper for him; and not
simply a helper, but, According to him, showing again the equal honor. For
since the irrational things brought in much partnership of help toward the
need of our life, so that you may not think the woman also to be of the
servants, see how he makes the distinction clear. He brought the beasts, he
says, before Adam, and a helper like him according to him was not found. What
then? is not the horse a helper, arrayed with us in the wars? is not the ox a
helper, drawing a plow and toiling with us in the castings of the seeds? are
not ass and mule helpers, working with us toward the carrying of loads? But so
that you may not say this, for this reason he makes the division exact. For he
did not say simply that a helper was not found for him, but that A helper like
him was not found. So also here, Let us make a helper for him, not simply,
but, Let us make for him a helper according to him, he said. And these things
before the sin; but after the sin, Toward your husband your turning, and he
will lord it over you. I made you, he says, of equal honor; you did not use
the rule well; pass over toward the

subjection. You did not bear the freedom, accept the slavery. You do not know
how to rule, and

concerning this subjection, so that you may learn again the harmony of Old and
New. Let a woman, he says, learn in quietness in all subjection. Did you see

But tell also the cause. For Adam, he says, was not deceived, but the woman
having been deceived has come to be in transgression. For this reason he
brought her down from the throne of teaching. For he who does not know how to
teach, let him learn, he says; and if he should not wish to learn, but should
wish to teach, he will destroy both himself and those who learn; which then
also happened upon the woman then. But that she has been subjected to the man,
and was subjected because of the sin, is clear from here; and I want to hear
that, Toward your husband your turning, and he will lord it over you.

2. I want to learn how Paul also converses concerning this care, and mixes the
mastery with the affection. Where then does he do this? Writing to the
Corinthians he says; Husbands, love the wives; behold the, Toward your husband
your turning. The wives that they fear the husbands; behold the, He will lord
it over you. Did you see how the mastery is not grievous, when the master is a
mad lover of the one serving, when fear is with love? For thus the burdensome
of the slavery has been taken away. One rule then the disobedience brought in.
For do not look at this, that God arranged it toward what was due, but that
sin constructed the very nature of the slavery; which is also a second kind of
slavery more burdensome than the former, and this too has the beginning and
the ground from the sin. For after the flood upon Noah, and the common
shipwreck of the whole inhabited world and that total destruction, Ham sinned
against the one who had begotten him, and seeing the father stripped, he
stripped him more by the accusation toward the brothers, and from here he
became a household slave of the brothers. For the wickedness of the choice
ruined the nobility of the nature; and very reasonably. Scripture then
composes countless defenses for the just man, or rather with one word it gives
him all pardon. For Noah began, it says, to be a man a farmer. And the, He
began, has much defense upon the drunkenness. For he knew neither how much
wine he ought to drink, nor how to drink, unmixed, or mixed with water, nor
when to drink, immediately drawn from the vats, or having waited a little
time. Scripture then through these things defends Noah; but the one begotten
from him, the one saved through him (for through the honor toward the father
he was not destroyed together with the swell of the rest), not looking toward
the nature itself, not remembering the salvation, not being made temperate by
fear, seeing still the


remains of the wrath of God remaining, and the traces of the disaster
appearing, and the fear of the things that had happened in its prime, he
insolently insulted the one who had begotten him. For this reason a wise man
also exhorts saying; Do not glory in the dishonor of your father; for the
dishonor of a father is not glory to you. But he did not even know this; but
he sinned a sin greater than all pardon and defense. For this reason he gave
slavery as punishment of the sin, and became a household slave of the
brothers, and he betrayed the presidency from =54.596= nature to the evil of
the mind. Behold also a second manner of slavery. Do you want to learn a third
also? And this is more burdensome than the two former, and much more fearful.
For when he was not made temperate by those, God stretched the bonds for us.
What then is this? That of the rulers, that of the authorities; not such as
that of the woman, nor such as that of the slaves, but much more fearful. For
one can see swords sharpened everywhere, executioners, chastisements,
tortures, punishments, the authority even unto death and life. And that this
manner of rule too became necessary because of the sin, hear again Paul
himself philosophizing concerning this. And if you wish not to fear the
authority, do the good, he says, and you will have praise from it. If you do
the evil, fear; for he does not wear the sword in vain. Do you see that
because of those who do the evil there is both ruler and sword? Hear then this
again more clearly. For he is an avenger, he says, to the one who practices
the evil. And he did not say, For he is not a ruler in vain; but what? For he
does not wear the sword in vain. He set the judge armed over you. For just as
an affectionate father, when children are slothful toward him, and because of
the paternal affection despise him, because of the goodness gives them over to
fearful pedagogues and teachers; so also God, our nature despising him because
of the goodness, gave it over, as to teachers and pedagogues, to the rulers,
so that they might turn their sloth. But if you will, let us see this very
thing also from the Old, that because of our wickedness the need of this rule
also came to be. One of the prophets, being provoked at the unjust, says
somehow thus; Will you be silent when the impious swallows the just, and will
you make men as the fishes of the sea, and creeping things having no leader?
Therefore a leader for this reason, so that we may not be as creeping things;
a ruler for this reason; so that we may not as the fishes swallow one another.
For just as because of the diseases the drugs, so because of the sins the
chastisements. Since that he who lives with virtue does not need the oversight
from there, hear what Paul says; And if you wish not to fear the authority, do
the good, and you will have praise from it. The judge is your spectator, he
says. If you live rightly, not a spectator only, but also a praiser. And why
do I speak of the need from the rulers, when those who pursue philosophy are
even above other greater things? For the laws are rulers of rulers. But
nevertheless he who lives with fairness does not even need laws; and hear this
from Paul saying, A law is not laid down for a just man. And if a law is not
laid down, much more a ruler is not set over him. Behold a third manner of
rule, and this too having the ground from the sin and wickedness.

3. How then does Paul say that There is no authority except from God? Because
he himself set it down toward what is profitable for us. For the sin brought
in the need of it; but God used it toward what is expedient for us. And just
as the need of drugs comes from the wounds, but the bringing on of the drugs
from the wisdom of the physicians; so also the need of the slavery came from
the sin; but the arranging it toward what is due, from the wisdom of God. But
rise up, and put away the sloth. For what reason do I say this? We recount the
Scriptures to you, but you, having stood your


eyes away from us, transferred them toward the lamps and the one lighting the
lamps. And of how much sloth is this, leaving us, to attend to this man? I too
kindle a fire, that from the Scriptures, and upon our tongue a little lamp of
the teaching burns. This light is greater and better than that light; for we
do not kindle a wick soaked with oil, as this man does, but we kindle souls
watered in piety with the desire of the hearing. Paul too once conversed in
some upper room. But let no one think I compare myself to Paul; for I am not
so mad; but so that you may learn how much haste one must show concerning the
hearing. Paul then conversed in an upper room, and evening overtook them, as
also now, and there were lamps in the upper room; then Eutychus fell from the
window, and the fall did not dissolve the gathering, nor did the death raise
the theater; but they were so nailed to the hearing of the divine oracles, as
not even to perceive the fall; but you, having seen nothing strange, nor
paradoxical, but a man doing the usual things, transferred your eyes there.
And of what pardon would this be worthy? Let no one then, beloved, think the
rebuke some burdensome thing; for we accuse not hating, but caring. For wounds
of friends are more trustworthy than willing kisses of enemies. Rise up then,
I beg, and leaving this fire, attend to the light of the divine Scriptures.
For I want to tell you another manner of rule, not taking the ground from sin,
but from nature itself. What then is this? That of parents toward offspring.
For such honor is a return of birth pangs. For this reason a wise man also
says; Serve those who begot you as masters. Then he also brings on the cause
saying; For what will you give back to them, as they to you? And yet what ever
is there that a child cannot give back to a father? Nothing else then than
this he says; As they begot you, you will not be able to beget them. Since
then we have been lessened in this, let us have the advantage from elsewhere
by the honors toward them, not through the law of nature only, but before
nature through the fear of God. For God strongly wishes the parents to be
honored by those begotten; and those who do this he rewards with great goods
and gifts, but those who transgress the law he chastises with great and
terrible evils. He who speaks evil, he says, of father or mother, let him end
by death =54.598= . But to those who honor them he says somehow thus; Honor
your father and your mother, that it may be well with you; and you will be
long lived upon the earth. What is thought to be the greatest good, a sleek
old age and length of life, this he set as a prize for those who honor them;
and what seems to be the last evil, untimely death, this he set as a penalty
for those who insult them; drawing the ones toward goodwill by the promise of
the honor, and leading the others even unwilling from the insult by the fear
of the punishment. For he does not simply command the father beater to die,
nor that executioners taking him from the court lead him out through the
market, nor that they cut off the head outside the city; but the father
himself leads him into the midst of the city, and without any proof he is
believed; and very justly. For he who chose to spend even money and bodies and
anything whatsoever for the child would never have become his accuser, unless
the excess of the insult was great. He leads him then into the midst of the
city, then he calls the whole people, and he says the accusation; and all who
hear, each taking a stone, thus strike the father beater. For the lawgiver
wishes them to be not spectators only of the punishment, but also doers, so
that each, looking to his own right hand, through which he himself also threw
the stone against the head of the father beater, may have a sufficient
reminder toward correction.

And not only this, but also another thing


the lawgiver hints to us, that he who insults the parents has not wronged
those only, but also all men. For this reason he calls all to the partnership
of the punishment, as if all had been insulted, and he sets around both people
together and a whole city, training even those who have nothing in common with
the wronged to be angry at parents being insulted, as the common nature being
insulted, and to drive such a man as some plague and common disease, not from
the city only, but even from the light itself. For such a one is an enemy and
a common foe of all, both of God, and of nature, and of the laws, and of the
common life of us all. For this reason he commands all to take hold of the
slaughter, working as it were some purification of the city. May many goods
come to you, that with so much pleasure you received the words about the
father beater, and instead of the stones you struck him with the shouts; for
this is a proof that each of you has shown much goodwill concerning his own
father. For then especially we are accustomed to marvel at the laws that
chastise the sins, when we are not conscious of the sins to ourselves. For all
these things then let us give thanks to the God who loves mankind, who cares
for our life, and provides for parents, and thinks of children, and manages
all things for our salvation. For to him belongs glory, honor, and worship
with the beginningless Father, and the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto
the ages of the ages. Amen.

SERMON V.

That we are not chastised because of Adam, and that the good things are
greater than the grievous things brought in through him, if we attend to
ourselves; and against those who run past the poor.

1. You perhaps think that the whole word about the mastery has been completed
for us; but I still see much fruit appearing in it. But do not grow weary, I
beg, until we harvest it all. For the labor loving of the farmers, when they
see a vine flourishing with many leaves, and weighed down with much fruit, do
not cut the outer clusters only, but they go further in, bending back the
shoots, and lifting the leaves, so that not even small berries of a grape
hidden under the petals may escape. Do not then appear more slothful than
those, nor stand off, until you take the whole; and especially, that the toil
is mine, but the fruit yours. We accused the women yesterday, or rather not
the women, but Eve, that through the sin she brought in the slavery. The women
might say to us, For what reason then, when that one sinned, were we
sentenced, and the fall came to be of one person, but the charge is common of
the whole nature? This slaves too might say; Why ever, when Canaan insulted
the father, did the things of the punishment pass through to the whole race?
And those who fear the rulers might also blame this; Why ever, when others
lived in wickedness, were they themselves given over to the yoke of the rule?
What then would we say to all these? For there will be one solution of all
questions; that the former ones sinned, and through their own disobedience
brought in the slavery; but those after these things confirmed it once brought
in by the sins from themselves. For if they could show themselves clean of
sins continually, perhaps it would be well for them to seem to contradict; but
if they themselves also are liable to many penalties, superfluous are the
things of this defense

are. For I did not say that already sin does not bring in slavery, but that
every sin has been allotted with slavery, and I accused the nature of sin, not
a difference of sin only. As then all incurable diseases lead to death, but
they are not all of the same nature; so also all the sins beget slavery, but
they are not all of the same nature. Eve sinned touching the tree, and she was
condemned through it. For this reason do not you sin again perhaps another sin
more terrible than that. This it is worth saying also upon the slaves, this
also upon those ruled, that the first brought in the sin, but those after
them, succeeding, held the power of the mastery through the things they
committed. I have also another way to defend; that many, having returned
toward virtue, were released from the mastery. And first, if it seems good,
let us exercise the word upon the women, so that you may see how the blessed
Paul, who put the bonds around them, himself loosed them again. For if a
woman, he says, has an unbelieving =54.600= husband, and he is content to
dwell with her, let her not leave him. Why? For what do you know, wife, if you
will save the husband? And how, he says, can a woman save? Teaching,
catechizing, leading toward the word of piety. And yet yesterday, O blessed
Paul, you said that I do not permit a woman to teach. How then do you again
make her a teacher of the husband? I do not do this fighting with myself, but
even strongly agreeing. Hear then both for what reason he cast her out, and
for what reason he brings her again to the throne of teaching, so that you may
learn the wisdom of Paul. Let the man teach, he says. Why? For he was not
deceived. For Adam, he says, was not deceived. Let the woman learn, he says.
Why? For she was deceived. For the woman, he says, having been deceived has
come to be in transgression. But here the contrary. For when the man is
unbelieving, but the woman believing, let the woman teach, he says. Why?
Because she has not been deceived; for she is believing. Let the man then
learn; for he was deceived; for he is unbelieving. The things of the teaching
have been reversed, he says; let the things of the mastery too be reversed
thereafter. Did you see how everywhere he shows the slavery following not the
nature, but the deceit and the sin? The deceit then came to the woman at the
beginning, the subjection followed the deceit; after these things the deceit
passed over to the man, the subjection also passed over. And just as at the
beginning he entrusted the salvation of the woman to the man, because he had
not been deceived, saying thus, Toward your husband your turning, and he will
lord it over you; so also here upon the believing woman who has the
unbelieving husband, he entrusts the salvation of the husband to the woman,
saying; For what do you know, wife, if you will save the husband? What could
become clearer than this proof, that the slavery follows not the nature, but
the sin? This one can say also upon the slaves. You were called a slave? Let
it not concern you. Do you see how again he shows the slavery to be a bare
name, when virtue is present? But if you can also become free, rather use it,
that is, rather remain in the slavery. Why? For the slave called in the Lord
is a freedman of the Lord. Did you see the slavery as far as the title, but in
the thing the freedom? And for what reason did he let him remain a slave? So
that you may learn the abundance of the freedom. For just as than the
quenching of the furnace upon the three children it was much more wonderful to
keep the bodies unharmed while it remained; so than the loosing of the
slavery, to show the freedom while it remains is much greater and more
wonderful. For this reason he says; If you can also become free, rather use
it; that is, remain a slave; for you have the truest freedom.

2. Do you want to see this also upon the rulers? Nebuchadnezzar was king, and
he kindled the furnace with much vehemence, and the three children into the
midst


he brought, young, deserted, having no protection, slaves, captives, without a
city. And what does he say? If truly, Shadrach, Meshach, Abednego, you do not
serve my gods, and the golden =54.601= image which I set up you do not
worship? What then did those? See how virtue made them, being captives, more
kingly than the king, and showed their mind higher. For not as conversing with
a king, but as having the word toward a subject, they answered thus with
freedom of speech. There is no need, they say, for us to answer the king
concerning this word. Not through words, but through deeds we will make the
proof. There is a God in heaven able to deliver us. They reminded him of the
benefit upon Daniel, saying the very words which the prophet said then. For
what did that one say? The word which the king demands is not of magi,
Gazarenes, Chaldeans; but there is a God in the heavens revealing mysteries.
This voice then they remind him of, so as to make him more fair. Then he says;
And if not, let it be known to you, O king, that we do not serve your gods,
and the golden image which you set up we do not worship. See the wisdom of
those young men. For so that those then present may not condemn weakness of
God, if it should happen that they, having fallen into the furnace, die, they
confessed his power beforehand, saying that There is a God in heaven who is
able to deliver us; and so that again, having escaped the flame, they may not
be thought to serve God for a wage and a return, they added that And if not,
let it be known to you, O king, that we do not serve your gods, and the golden
image which you set up we do not worship; at once both proclaiming the power
of God, and showing the standing of their own soul, so that what the devil
said upon Job slandering, this no one may say also concerning these. And what
did the devil say concerning Job? that Job does not worship you for nothing;
for you have fenced his insides and his outsides. So that no one may have this
to say also concerning these, they beforehand blocked the shameless mouth. But
as I was saying, even if one is a captive, even if a slave, even if a
stranger, even if he spends time upon a foreign land, and has the virtue with
himself, he will be more kingly than all kings. Did you see also the slavery
of the women loosed, and that of the household slaves, and that of those
ruled? Come, I will show you thereafter also the fear of the beasts cast out.
They threw Daniel into the pit once upon the same Babylon; but the lions did
not dare to touch him. For they saw the ancient and kingly image shining in
him, they looked at those characters which they saw upon Adam before the sin.
For with the same subjection coming to Adam, they received the names then. And
not here only, but this happened also upon the blessed Paul. For he too,
having fallen out onto the island of the barbarians, sat by a fire warming
himself; then a viper leaping from the sticks seized his hand. What then
happened after these things? The beast fell off at once. For since it did not
find sin, it did not even have strength to bite; but just as we, whenever we
wish to go up some smooth crag, finding holds from nowhere, fall down at once,
whether a sea lies under, or a pit; so also that beast, even with a fire lying
under, not finding the hold of the sin, nor where it might plant the =54.602=
teeth, fell down into the fire, and was destroyed. Do you want me to tell you
also a third manner of defense? One is, that not the former only have sinned,
but also those after them; a second, that those who succeeded, and this while
spending time in the present life, experienced a lighter slavery; or rather
were released from this entire, as then also upon the women, and upon those
ruled, and upon the beasts

we showed. A third after those, that Christ coming promised us now goods
greater than the goods from which those who sinned at the beginning cast us
out. For why do you lament, tell me? that Adam having sinned cast you out of
paradise? Succeed, and do you emulate virtue; and not paradise, but I open
heaven itself to you, and I let you suffer nothing terrible from the
disobedience of the first formed. You lament that he loosed you from the rule
of the beasts? Behold I subject demons also to you, if you attend. For tread,
he says, upon serpents and scorpions, and upon all the power of the enemy. He
did not say, rule, as upon the beasts, but, Tread, bringing in a stretched
rule.


3. For this reason Paul also did not say, God will subject Satan under your
feet, but God will crush Satan under your feet. No longer, as before, He will
keep your head, and you will keep his heel; but the victory entire, the trophy
clean, a complete disappearance of the enemy, and crushing, and destruction.
Eve subjected you to the man; but I make you not only of equal honor with a
man, but even of the angels themselves, if you will. She deprived you of the
present life; but I grant you also the one to come, the unaging and immortal,
and full of countless goods. Let no one then think he has been despitefully
used because of the former ones; for if we should wish to go through all that
he is about to provide, we will find the things given much greater than what
we lost. And from the things said the rest also are clear. Adam brought in a
toilsome life; Christ promised a life, where pain and grief and sighing have
fled, and he promises to grant the kingdom of the heavens. For come, he says,
you blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the
foundation of the world. For I hungered, and you gave me to eat; I thirsted,
and you gave me drink; I was a stranger, and you gathered me; I was naked, and
you clothed me; in prison, and you came to me. Will we then also hear that
blessed voice? I would not strongly insist; for there is much contempt of the
poor among us. A time of fasting, so much exhortation and teaching of saving
lessons, continual prayers, daily gatherings, and what profit after so much
care? None. For we withdraw from here seeing strings of poor standing in a
line on either side; and as seeing pillars, but not human bodies, we thus run
past without pity. As then seeing soulless statues, but not breathing =54.603=
men, we thus hasten home. For hunger constrains, he says. And yet let the
hunger persuade to remain; for the filled bellies, according to the proverb,
do not know the hungry; but the hungry from their own necessity also recognize
the other's; or rather not even so is it possible to see it all. For you run
to a table prepared, and you do not endure to wait even a little; but the poor
man stands even until the evening itself, hastening and pressing to complete
the daily food; and seeing the day fulfilled, but the silver not yet all
fulfilled, that sufficient for daily food, he is pained and burned, and is
forced to dare things greater than his own power. For this reason in the
evening they attack us more vehemently, swearing, putting to oath, lamenting,
wailing, stretching out the hands, forced to be shameless in countless other
things. For they fear lest, all having withdrawn home, they are about to
wander in the city as in a desert. And just as those who have fallen into
shipwreck in the day, whenever they take hold of a plank, hasten to reach the
harbor before evening, lest when the night has come on remaining outside they
experience a more terrible

trial of the shipwreck; so also the poor, fearing the famine as a shipwreck,
hasten before evening to gather the silver sufficient for food for them, so
that they may not, all having gone home, remain outside the harbor. For a
harbor for them are the hands of those who take hold.


4. But we are not bent in the market toward their disasters, nor having gone
home; but even when the table lies beside us, often even full of countless
goods (if indeed one must call goods what we eat upon the condemnation of our
lack of humanity), yet even when the table lies beside, hearing those walking
below through the alleys, and shouting greatly in the streets, pained in
deepest darkness, in much solitude, not even so are we bent; but even having
been filled, and going toward sleep, and in that time again hearing them
wailing greatly below, as of a raging dog, but not hearing a human voice we
thus run past; and we are turned neither toward the time, that at an untimely
hour of the nights when all sleep that one wails alone, nor toward the
lightness of the request, that he demands of us nothing more than bread, or a
little silver, nor toward the greatness of the disaster, that he wrestles with
continual famine, nor toward the fairness of the suppliant, that held by so

from beginning until end was called Isaac; but the father of Isaac was called
Abram before this; and after these things Abraham. For God said toward him, he
says, No longer will your name be Abram, but Abraham your name will be called.
For he was called Abram formerly, but this name is not Greek, nor in our
tongue, but in that of the Hebrews it is said thus. What then is this name
being interpreted? A passer over. For the Abram in the =54.625= voice of the
Syrians means the beyond, and they know as many as of this voice are
experienced. And much to the


voice of the Syrians toward the tongue of the Hebrews is the kinship. And why
was he called a passer over, he says? The Judean country, that is, all
Palestine, that from Egypt until the Euphrates, lies opposite Babylon, whence
Abraham was; and through both the river flows in the middle, being a common
boundary of either land. Since then he was not from Palestine, but he came
from the beyond from Babylon, for this reason from the place and the thing he
received the name, and he was called a passer over, since he came from the
beyond. And why did he come from the beyond? Because God commanded. And why
did God command? So that he might show the obedience of the just man. And how
did he show the obedience of the just man? That through his command, having
left his own, he exchanged the foreign instead of that. Did you see how great
a chain is woven from the title of the just man? This name opened a whole sea
of history for us. Learn then his ancient name, so that when you see him
dwelling Palestine, being led by his title toward the ancient fatherland, and
learning the cause through which he forsook it, you may take a sufficient
exhortation toward the same zeal. For a man being before law and the lawful
way of life showed the philosophy in the grace, and what Christ said later to
the apostles; Amen I say to you, that there is not anyone who left house, or
brothers, or sisters, or father, or mother, who will not receive a
hundredfold, and will inherit eternal life; this that one did through the
works before the grace. And not from this only is it possible to see the
philosophy of the just man, but also from the promise itself of God. Come, he
says, into the land which I will show you. For even if both fatherlands are of
sense, yet the one was his own, and the other foreign; and the one clear, and
the other unclear; and the one in hands, and the other in hopes; and
nevertheless having left the clear and evident and the things in hands, he ran
toward the unapparent and unclear, and of which he was not master, so that he
may persuade and train you, when God commands to leave the evident things, and
to look toward the unclear, not to contradict, nor to dispute. For the things
in hands are not so evident, as those things in hopes; the present life is not
so evident, as that one to come. For this we see with our eyes, that we see
with the eyes of faith; this we see lying in our hands, that we see also being
guarded by the promises of God.

4. And the promises of God are much more powerful than our hands. Do you want
to learn how this present life lies in the unclear continually, but that one
to come, which seems to be unclear, is more apparent than the present and more
firm and lasting? Let us examine, if it seems good, the bright things of the
present life, the wealth, the glory, the power, the honors from men; and you
will see nothing more unclear than these. For what is more untrustworthy than
wealth, which often does not even remain with us until evening? For as an
ungrateful runaway it continually moves from this one to that one, and again
from this one toward another. Such a thing is glory also. For he who before
this was bright and notable, becomes without honor and nameless suddenly
=54.626= ; and the contrary again. And just as it is not possible to see the
wheel continually turning upon the same part of the rim; for by the density of
the spin continually the things above become below, and the contrary again; so
also the bearing of our affairs continually turning makes the things above
below; so also upon the wealth and upon the power and upon all the other
things. It has never stood upon the same seat, but it imitates the streams of
rivers, standing nowhere ever. What then would be more unclear than these, of
things so continually changing, and before appearing flying off, before
arriving


leaping away? For this reason the prophet also, conversing concerning luxury
and wealth and such things, and mocking those who gape toward them as
remaining, says; As standing they counted these, and not as fleeing. He did
not say, not passing, but what is much greater, As fleeing; for it does not
withdraw gently, but with much speed. But the patriarch not so, but having
left all these, he looked only toward the promises from God, making a way
beforehand for us toward the faith of the things to come, so that, when God
promises you the things to come and not seen, you may not say that those are
unclear and unapparent. For those unclear things are clearer than these things
seen, if we have the eyes of faith. For even if we have not seen them, yet God
promised them and undertook. When God promises, no change touches the
undertakings; so that those especially remain and have been fixed in the hand
of God. For from the hand of God no one is able to snatch, he says. To snatch
from the hand of my Father no one is able. Since then no one is able to snatch
from the hand of God, it remains in a clear treasure continually. But these
things are subject to changes of every kind and turns; for this reason often
we endure the toil of them, but we are deprived of the end of them. But upon
those hopes this is not, but the one who toiled must altogether also obtain
the crowns. For the hope does not put to shame. For it is a promise of God,
and the gifts imitate the one who undertook. Having left then the unclear,
hold the things that appear; for the things that appear are not the present,
but the things to come. And if some, fluttering toward the present, despise
the things to come, since they are weaker than the height of that hope, for
this reason they despise, not because these are unapparent and unclear.
Consider then the philosophy of the just man. God promised him things of
sense, and he himself sought things of intellect. And where did God promise
him things of sense, but he himself wanted things of intellect? Go out, he
says, from your land, and from your kinship, and from the house of your
father, and come into a land which I will show you. Of sense both the former
land, and the one about to be given later. What then he? Or rather let us not
hear him, but Paul speaking concerning him, so that we may learn that he did
not look toward this land, even if God promised this, but having left the
present, he gaped toward the things to come. What then does Paul say?
According to faith these all died, speaking concerning Abraham, and Isaac, and
Jacob, and all the just. For not Abraham only, but all were partners of the
same philosophy. According to faith these all died, not having received the
promises, but =54.627= having seen them from afar. What do you say? did
Abraham not receive the promises, nor did he come into Palestine? Are the
things said a lie then? Not so, he says. For he came into Palestine, but he
did not look toward this promise, but he desired another, that in the heavens,
and he thought himself a stranger of all the present things. Which then Paul
also witnessing to him added; And having confessed that they are strangers and
sojourners. A stranger he who received a fatherland, tell me, and so great a
country? Yes, he says; for he looked not toward this, but toward heaven. For
those who say these things make it appear that they seek another fatherland,
of which God is craftsman and creator, that heavenly Jerusalem, the one above.
Did you see how God promised him things of sense? how that one sought the
Jerusalem above? For those who say these things, he says, make it appear that
they seek a fatherland. And if they remembered that from which they went out,
they would have had a time to bend back; but now they reach after another
better, that is, a heavenly. Therefore the undertaking is of sense, but the
desire of the just man of intellect, opposite to us. For to that one he
promised Palestine, but he looked around at heaven; but to us


he promises the heaven, and we have gaped toward the earth.

5. These things we gained from the name of Abraham, having learned why he was
called a passer over, and that having left his own land, he passed over toward
the foreign, and having run past the evident, he looked toward the unclear,
having thrown the things in hands, he gaped toward the things in hopes, and
having received things of sense, he stretched up toward the things of
intellect, and these things before the grace, before the law, before the
teaching from the prophets. Whence it is clear, that he had no guide, but
nature sufficed for him, having moved the reasonings of the conscience, and
thus he found God the creator of the whole; for this reason he was called
Abram, for this reason the parents also set this name upon him. But perhaps
someone might say, that this is a lie. For were the parents of Abraham just?
for were they well pleasing to God? for did they know the things about to be?
for did they foresee the undertaking about to be said from the Master? were
they not impious and idolaters, most barbarous of barbarians? I know it too,
and for this reason I praise the just man, that having such fathers, he
himself became such. And the wonderful and paradoxical is this, that from a
foreign and wild root a tame fruit was brought. For it is not an accusation of
children living in piety, the evil of the fathers, but if one must say
something wonderful, even rather an encomium, that not having received the
piety from ancestors, nor having those who lead by the hand, but as traveling
in a desert and untrodden, they were able to find the way that bears into
heaven. It is not then an accusation to have an impious father, but to imitate
the impiety of the father; and a greatest charge, not when we have cheap
parents, but when we do not care for them, and bring in all the things from
ourselves toward releasing them from this evil. As when we show all things
concerning their soul, but they remain upon their wickedness, we have been
released from all blame and cause. And I said these things, so that you may
not be ashamed, beloved, when you hear that Abraham had an impious father. For
Timothy also had an impious father; For he was a son of a Judean woman
=54.628= , he says, of a believing mother, but of a Greek father. And that the
father remained in impiety and was not changed, is clear from there; for
praising the faith of Timothy, Paul says thus; Which dwelt in your grandmother
Lois, and in the mother Eunice, and I am persuaded that also in you. And
nowhere the name of the father. Why? Because he remained in the impiety, he
was not worthy to be numbered with the child. And the apostles also had wicked
fathers, and this Christ showed saying; If I cast out the demons in Beelzebul,
your sons in whom will they cast them out? For this reason they themselves
will be your judges. But let this not trouble you, nor scandalize you. For
from this we also learn, that not of nature, but of choice are the evil and
the virtue. For if it were of nature, the wicked would altogether have
begotten wicked, and the good such; but since it is of choice to be cheap and
eager, for this reason both children of wicked fathers became good, and of
slothful children fathers eager often, so that we may not count both of these
everywhere to the nature, but to the mind. But as I was saying, the parents of
Abraham being impious, how did they set this name upon him? This too
especially was a work of the wisdom of God, having managed through an
unbelieving tongue a name to be set, having the history of the things to come.
For he also forced Balaam once to say the things to come, showing his own
power, and making clear that not in his own only, but also in the foreign he
is able to rule. And so that you may learn, that upon the just, even if the
fathers are not pious, often not knowing they set names showing much economy
of the things to come, from another example also I will make this clear to
you. Lamech the of


Noah father, of that Noah, upon whom the flood came to be, was not just, was
not well pleasing to God, was not approved to God; for if he was just, if he
was well pleasing, if he was well approved to God, he would not have said that
Noah alone was found perfect in his generation; he would not have run past the
father of the just man, if he was just. What then did that one? He set a name
upon the child, having much history of the things to come, and the title
itself of the just man was a prophecy. For this name showed the flood about to
be. And how did the name of Noah show the flood about to be? The, Noah, this
name is said in a Hebrew tongue and is interpreted, the one who rests. For
the, Nia, in the voice of the Syrians, is rest. As then there from Abar, which
is beyond, he was called Abraham, and from Aidem, which is earth, he was
called Adam, which is earthy; so also here from Nia, which is rest, he was
called Noah, which is one who rests; and the bringing on also shows this. For
he called him Noah, saying; This one will rest us, calling the flood rest. For
since the flood was about to come to be upon him, and the flood was death, And
death is rest to a man, for this reason the man upon whom the flood came to
be, he called the one who rests.

6. And that I do not force the interpretation, let us hear this from the
Scripture itself. Lamech lived eight and eighty and a hundred years, and he
begot a son, and he called his name Noah, saying; This one will rest us from
our toils, and the griefs, and the works of our hands, and from the earth,
which God cursed. What do you say, He will rest us? And why did he not say, he
says, This one will kill us, this one will make the flood come to be; but he
said, He will rest us? The whole creation was recast, abysses burst from
below, cataracts were opened from above, all things were a sea foreign and
paradoxical and dreadful, and in a common tomb in the slime both bodies of men
and bodies of horses and of beasts were hidden; and so many evils, tell me,
and so great a disaster do you call rest? Yes, he says. For since the men
lived in evil, and the flood cut off the evil, those released from the evil
rested. For just as a body held by various diseases, and finding no healing,
if death having come on takes it, rested; so then also that generation of
those men, as a body sick incurably and occupied beforehand by many terrible
things having no comfort, =54.630= the flood having come on snatched and
rested. For if Death is rest to a man, much more to those living in evil
without correction death is rest, releasing them from the toils, and not
letting the pasture of the wickedness go further, nor the load of the sins
become heavier than what is. For nothing is so heavy and hard to bear, as a
nature of sin; nothing so provides us labor and toil, as the kind of
wickedness and the offenses. For this reason Christ said to those living in
sins; Come all who toil and are loaded, and I will rest you. For this reason
Lamech also called the flood rest, having come on and stopped the wickedness.
I wanted to stretch this word further; for many things have been left from the
title of Noah; but for the present having deposited these things in the memory
of your mind, and having said them toward our brothers who were left behind,
so that we may not again be forced to make the prefaces longer, taking up the
sequence of the things said, let us break off the word into prayers, giving
thanks to God who has given these things to say, that to him is the glory,
unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
