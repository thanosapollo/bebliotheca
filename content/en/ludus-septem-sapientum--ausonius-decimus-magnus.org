** AUSONIUS THE CONSUL TO DREPANIUS THE PROCONSUL greeting.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

Whether you think these lines deserve pardon or deserve to be known,
read them through, Drepanius, with a careful mind.
I shall take your verdict calmly, whether you judge
the poems I have given should be read, or should be hid.
The first thing is to have earned your favor, Pacatus;
next will come the care of my guarded honor.
I can bear a stern reader's censure,
and I can content myself with moderate praise.
A horse knows how to love the sound of a patted neck,
and knows, unshaken, how to endure a slow lash.
What polish the critic Aristarchus and the rule of Zenodotus
sought for Maeonian Homer!
So set the obeli, the brands of the first poets:
I shall count them my palms, not my faults;
and I shall call more corrected than condemned
whatever a learned man's file shall lay on me.
Meanwhile, about to take on so great a judgment's weight,
I shall wish to please; if not, to lie hid.

* Section 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

** Prologue
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

The Seven Sages, to whom a Ssuperior age gave that name
and a following age did not take it away,
come forth today onto the orchestra in Greek cloaks.
Why do you blush, you togaed Romulus,
because men so famous will walk onto the stage?
This is a shame for us, not for the men of Attica as well:
for them the theatre serves in place of a senate-house.
Our public business has its places allotted:
the Campus for the assemblies, as the Curia for the conscript fathers;
the forum and the rostra the law 1 of the citizens keeps apart.
At Athens, and throughout all Greece, there is one
public seat for taking counsel,
which luxury built late in our city.
Once the aedile used to furnish a boarded stage,
raised in a hurry, with no mass of stone.
So Murena and Gallius: I speak of things well known.
Afterwards men of power, not fearing the cost,
believed their name would last forever, if once
a pile built on a stone foundation
should found a place for the games for all time:

this huge theatre grew in wedges:
Pompey gave it, and Balbus, and Caesar
Octavian, competing in outlay.
But why do I talk of this? I have not come out here
to explain who founded the theatres, who the forum,
who the several parts of the walls:
but that I might go before men to be revered and praised by the gods,
and make plain what they mean to do.
They are used to pronouncing their own sayings,
which each of the wise has already put first.
You know them well enough; but if memory
fails in ancient matters, a player will come
as expounder of those I hold less surely.

** THE PLAYER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h1
:END:

Report has it that Attic Solon wrote at Delphi

γνῶθι σεαυτόν, which in Latin is: know yourself.
Many think this belongs to Laconian Chilon.
Spartan Chilon, they dispute whether it is yours or not,
that which is reported next, ὄρα τέλοσ μακροῦ βίου,
you who bid us look to the end of a long life.
Many think Solon said this to Croesus.
And report has it that Lesbian Pittacus said

γίγνωσκε καιρόν: he bids you know the time.
But that καιρὸσ is the timely season.
Bias of Priene said οἱ πλεῖστοι κακοί,
which in Latin is: most men are bad;
but know that those he called bad are the unskilled.

μελέτη τὸ πᾶν, that is Periander of Corinth's,
who holds that practice can accomplish the whole.

Cleobulus of Lindos says ἄριστον μέτρον is:
that is, the best measure for all things.
But Thales put forth ἐγγύα, πάρα δ' ἄτα,
who forbids us to stand surety, because harm is the bondsman.
This warning of ours does not please those who have lent at interest.
I have spoken: I withdraw; Solon the lawgiver comes.

** SOLON
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

After the Greek custom I, Solon, come forth onto the stage,
to whom the fame of the Seven Sages gave the palm.
But fame 1 is not the severity of judgment;
for I do not count myself first, but one,
because equality cannot endure rank.
Rightly once the Delphic god advised a fool
who asked who would be first of the sages,
that he should set a garland of names on a smooth circle,
lest anyone be first, or even last.
From the middle of their ring I, Solon, come forth,
so that what I am thought to have said to king Croesus,
every school of men may take as said to itself.
In Greek it is packed as ὄρα τέλοσ μακροῦ βίου,
which grows longer if you put it in Latin:
I bid all look to the end of life.
Therefore avoid calling men wretched or blessed,
because they are always in a doubtful state.
It is so. If I can, I shall speak in few words.

Whether king or tyrant of Lydia, Croesus was
among these blessed, rich to a mad degree,
who used to give the gods temples with golden sides.
He summoned me. I come obedient to the word,
that the Lydians may have use of a better king.
He asks me to name a blessed man, if I have known any.
I name Tellen, a citizen not obscure:
fighting for his country that man had thrown his life in the way.
He scorned him, seeks another; I found Aglaus:
he had never gone beyond the bounds of his own little field.
But he, laughing: "Where then do you put me,
who alone am called blessed in the whole world?"

I say /the end of life must first be looked to:/

/then one must judge whether happiness remains./

Croesus took the saying ill: I
leave the king; he prepares war against the Persians;
set out, beaten, bound, handed over to the king,
he stands, already catching the likeness of his own funeral,
where the flame was giving itself through the whole circuit,
rolling swollen globes of heat on high.
And almost too late Croesus with a huge cry,

/O true seer,/ he says, /O Solon, Solon/

with a great shout names Solon three times.
Cyrus, moved by that voice, orders it quenched
through the whole ring and the burning pyre pulled down:
and in good time rain poured from the clouds
checked the fire. Croesus is led at once to the king
through a chosen band of soldiers:
he is asked whom he meant by Solon
and what cause he had for calling the name.
Through the whole series he sets forth all things to the king.
That man pities him and, seeing the force of fortune,
praises Solon: thence he keeps Croesus among his friends

and orders him bound with golden fetters to spend with him
all that was left of life.
I, by the testimony of two kings,
was praised and approved by both.
And what was said to one, let each take as said to himself.
I have now finished the cause for which I came forth here.
See, Chilon comes. Fare you well, and applaud.

** CHILON
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1
:END:

My loins ache from sitting, my eyes from watching,
while I wait for Solon to take himself off.1
Hui, how few things, gods, the men of Attica say!
One saying in three hundred verses
he has at last finished, and looking back at me, he goes.
I am Spartan Chilon, who now come forth.
By the well known brevity we Laconians use,
I commend our γνῶθι σεαυτόν, know yourself,
which already stands on the Delphic column.
That troublesome labor bears the best fruit:
to tell what you can bear, and what you cannot;
by night and by day to search what you do, what you have done,
even to the likeness of a thin point.2
All duties, shame, honor, constancy
are in this, and any glory we have despised.
I have spoken: farewell, and remember; I do not delay the applause.

** CLEOBULUS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

I am Cleobulus, citizen of a small island,
but author of a great saying, by which I am famed:

they hold that I said ἄριστον μέτρον.
You there, nearest the orchestra,
sitting in the fourteen rows on the neighboring steps: interpret.

Is ἄριστον μέτρον the best measure?
Speak! You have nodded? I thank you; I shall go on
in order. Already from that place your African poet
has said /nothing too much,/ 1
and a certain one of ours μηδὲν ἄγαν.2 Both senses
belong here, Italian or Dorian.
Of speaking, of silence, of sleep, of waking that measure is,
of benefits, of thanks, of injury,
of study, of labors: whatever there is in all life
asks that measure of the best pause.
I have spoken: I withdraw; let there be measure; Thales comes.

** THALES
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1
:END:

I am Milesian Thales, who said that water is first
among things to be created 3, as the bard Pindar, 4

and to whom once, by Apollo's order, a golden tripod 5

the fishermen gave, drawn from the sea;
for these, at the Delian's command, had chosen me,
because he had sent this gift to a sage.

I refused, did not take it, and gave it back
to be carried to others whom I thought earlier.
Then through all the seven sage men
sent and sent back they bring it again to me.
I received it and consecrated it to Apollo;
for if Phoebus bids a sage be chosen,
it is fitting that not any man, but a god, be believed.
I therefore am that man. But the cause of my coming forth
onto the stage was the same as for the two before me:
that I might become the champion of my saying.
It will displease, yet not the prudent,
whom use has taught and made skilled.
Behold ἐγγύα, πάρα δ' ἄτα, so we say in Greek.
In Latin: give surety, but harm is ready at your side.
I could run through a thousand examples, to prove
sureties and bondsmen guilty of repentance.
But I do not wish to name anyone by name:
let each of you say to himself and think with himself
how many have found surety a loss and an ill.
Yet let this grateful office remain for both.
Part therefore applaud; part, offended, hiss me off.

** BIAS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h1
:END:

I am Bias of Priene. What 1 I said, oἱ πλεῖστοι κακοι,
I take in Latin to mean: most men are bad.
I would rather not have said it; truth begets hatred.2
But I called bad the unskilled and the barbarians,
who neglect right and fair dealing and sacred custom.
For that people that rings this theatre
is all good men. The enemy's land holds

those of whom you think I spoke, the many who are bad;
but let no judge be so bad
that he does not already join the party of the good,
whether he is truly good, or only eager to be called so.
Already that hated name of "bad" has fled.
I go. Farewell and applaud, you many who are good.1

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9

** PITTACUS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

I am Pittacus, a Lesbian born at Mytilene,

who taught the saying γίνωσκε καιρὸν.
But that καιρόσ warns you to know the time,
and that καιρόν is what they call the timely season.
So too there is a Roman phrase: I have come in time.2
That comic poet of yours also, Terence,
affirms that timing is first of all things,
when the slave Dromo 3 had come to Antiphila
unhindered by none, keeping the turn of time.
Reckon, all of you, how often offense befalls
the man who has not watched his opportunity.
Time warns me not to be a nuisance: applaud.

* Section 10
:PROPERTIES:

** PERIANDER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h1
:END:

Born of Ephyra, I, Periander, come forth here,

who said μελέτη τὸ πᾶν and now I prove the saying:
what you do rightly comes of practice.
For he alone is effective at getting a thing done
who first thinks through every business.

Adverse things or favorable Terence the comic poet
declares must be thought through by all. 1
To pitch a camp, to wage war or to lay it down,
great matters and moderate, even small ones too:
whoever wishes to act should always think them through.
For all are slower in new beginnings
if thought of the thing to be done has been lacking.
There is nothing that asks fuller care
than to think what is to be done. After that,
chance, not counsel, rules the thoughtless.
But I now take myself back to my part: applaud,
and by thinking take care of your public business.
