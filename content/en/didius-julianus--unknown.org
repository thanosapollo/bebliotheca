* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Didius Julianus, who gained the empire after Pertinax, had as
great-grandfather Salvius Julianus, twice consul, prefect of the city, and a
jurist, which more than anything else made him distinguished. His mother was
Clara Aemilia, his father Petronius Didius Severus, his brothers Didius
Proculus and Nummius Albinus, his uncle Salvius Julianus. His father's father
was an Insubrian of Milan; his mother's father came from the colony of
Hadrumetum.

He was brought up in the house of Domitia Lucilla, the mother of the emperor
Marcus. He was chosen among the Board of Twenty by the support of Marcus's
mother. He was designated quaestor a year before the lawful age allowed. He
obtained the aedileship by Marcus's support, and was praetor by the same
support. After the praetorship he commanded in Germany the Twenty-second
Legion, the Primigenia. From there he governed Belgica long and with
integrity. There, when the Chauci, a German people who lived along the Elbe,
broke out, he held them off with hastily raised provincial auxiliaries, and
for this he earned the consulship on the emperor's testimony. He also subdued
the Chatti. Then he received Dalmatia to govern and freed it from the enemies
on its borders. After Germany

he governed Lower Germany. After this he earned the charge of public
maintenance for the poor in Italy. Then he was accused by a certain Severus
Clarissimus, a soldier, of conspiracy with Salvius against Commodus. But
Commodus, because he had already killed many senators, and indeed noble and
powerful ones, on charges of treason, and did not wish to make himself more
hateful, set Didius free and condemned the accuser. Acquitted, he was sent
again to govern a province. Then he governed Bithynia, but not with the same
reputation as the rest.

He was consul with Pertinax, and in the proconsulship of Africa he succeeded
him, and Pertinax always called him colleague and successor, most of all on
that day when Julianus, betrothing his daughter to a kinsman of his own, came
to Pertinax and told him of it. Pertinax said:
/...and due reverence, because he is my colleague and successor./
For at once the death of Pertinax followed. When Pertinax had been killed, and
Sulpicianus wished to be named emperor in the camp, Julianus had come with his
son-in-law to the senate, which he had heard was summoned. He found the doors
shut, and there he met two tribunes, Publius Florianus and Vectius Aper. The
tribunes began to urge him to seize the throne. When he told them that another
man had already been named emperor, they held him and led him to the
praetorian camp. But when they came to the camp, while Sulpicianus, prefect of
the city and father-in-law of Pertinax, was addressing the troops and claiming
the empire for himself, no one would let Julianus in, though from the wall he
made huge promises. Julianus first warned the praetorians not to make emperor
a man who would avenge Pertinax. Then he wrote on tablets that he would
restore the memory of Commodus. And so he was admitted and named emperor, the
praetorians asking that he do no harm to Sulpicianus because he had wished to
be emperor.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

Then Julianus, by the support of the praetorians, made Flavius Genialis and
Tullius Crispinus prefects of the guard, and he was attended by the imperial
escort through Maurentius, who had earlier joined Sulpicianus. Though he had
promised twenty-five thousand to the soldiers, he gave thirty thousand.

Then, after holding a military assembly, he came in the evening to the senate
and entrusted himself wholly to it, and by a decree of the senate he was named
emperor, and he obtained the tribunician power and the proconsular right,
being enrolled among the patrician families. His wife Manlia Scantilla and his
daughter Didia Clara were also named Augustae. From there he withdrew to the
Palace, his wife and daughter being summoned thither, coming in fear and
against their will, as if they already foresaw impending ruin. He made
Cornelius Repentinus, his son-in-law, prefect of the city in place of
Sulpicianus.

Meanwhile Didius Julianus was hated by the people, because it had been
believed that the ills of Commodus's age were to be set right by the
authority of Pertinax, and it was held as if Pertinax had been killed by
Julianus's counsel. And already those who had first begun to hate Julianus
spread it about that on the very first day, in scorn of Pertinax's dinner, he
had prepared a luxurious banquet dressed with oysters, fatted birds, and fish.
This is agreed to have been false. For Julianus is reported to have been of
such thrift that he would divide a sucking-pig over three days, and a hare
over three days, if anyone happened to send him one; and often, even when no
religious observance required it, he was content with greens and pulse and
dined without meat. Then he did not dine before Pertinax was buried, and he
took food in the deepest gloom because of that death, and he spent the first
night in unbroken watches, anxious at so great a crisis.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

But when first it grew light, he admitted the senate and the equestrian order
as they came to the Palace, and he addressed each most kindly, as brother or
son or father, according to his age. But the people at the Rostra and before
the senate-house assailed him with huge insults, hoping that the empire which
the soldiers had given could be laid down by him, and they also stoned him. As
he came down with the soldiers and the senate into the senate-house they
called down curses, and when he was performing the rite they wished that he
might not obtain a favorable omen. They even threw stones at him, while
Julianus always sought with his hand to calm them. When he entered the
senate-house he spoke calmly and with prudence, gave thanks that he had been
received, and that he himself and his wife and his daughter had taken the name
of Augusti. He also received the name of Father of the Fatherland; he refused
a silver statue. As he went from the senate to the Capitol the people blocked
him, but they were driven back and put to flight by the sword, by wounds, and
by promises of gold pieces, which Julianus himself showed on his fingers to
make them believe him. From there they went to the circus spectacle. But when
the benches of all had been seized without distinction, the people doubled
their insults against Julianus; they called Pescennius Niger, who was already
said to be ruling, to the protection of the city.

Julianus bore all this calmly, and through the whole time of his empire he was
most mild. The people, however, inveighed most fiercely against the soldiers,
who had killed Pertinax for money. Therefore, to win the people's favor, he
restored many things which Commodus had established and Pertinax had taken
away. Concerning Pertinax himself he did nothing either bad or good, which
seemed to very many a most serious matter. It is agreed, however, that from
fear of the soldiers he was silent about the honor of Pertinax.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

And Julianus indeed feared neither the British armies nor the Illyrian, but he
had ordered Niger to be killed, sending a chief centurion, fearing above all
the Syrian armies. Therefore Pescennius Niger in Syria and Septimius Severus
in Illyricum, with the armies they commanded, revolted from Julianus. But when
it was announced to him that Severus had revolted, a man he had not suspected,
he was thrown into confusion, came to the senate, and obtained that Severus
should be declared a public enemy. For the soldiers also who had followed
Severus a day was appointed, beyond which, if they were still with Severus,
they should be counted among the enemies.

Besides this, consular envoys were sent by the senate to the soldiers, to
persuade them that Severus should be rejected, and that he should be emperor
whom the senate had chosen. Among the others sent was Vespronius Candidus, an
old man of consular rank, long hated by the soldiers for a harsh and mean
command. Valerius Catullinus was sent as successor to Severus, as if a
successor could be given to a man who already held the soldiery for himself.
There was sent besides the centurion Aquilius, known for the slaughter of
senators, to kill Severus. Julianus himself ordered the praetorians to be led
out into the field and the towers to be fortified, but he brought out idle
soldiers, unstrung by city luxury and most unwilling for military exercise, so
that they hired substitutes at a wage for the work prescribed to each.

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

And Severus indeed was coming toward the city with a hostile column, but
Didius Julianus accomplished nothing with the praetorian army, which the
people hated more each day and also mocked. And although through Laetus he had
escaped the hand of Commodus, Julianus, ungrateful for so great a benefit,
ordered Laetus to be killed, expecting that he would side with Severus. He
ordered Marcia also to be slain with him.

But while Julianus did these things, Severus seized the fleet at Ravenna. The
envoys of the senate who had promised Julianus their service went over to
Severus. Tullius Crispinus, prefect of the guard, sent against Severus to
bring out the fleet, was driven back and returned to Rome. When Julianus saw
this, he asked the senate that the Vestal Virgins and the rest of the priests
should go out with the senate to meet Severus's army and entreat them with
fillets held forward, preparing a vain gesture against barbarian soldiers.

While Julianus was doing this, Plautius Quintilius, a consular augur, opposed
him, declaring that a man who could not resist an adversary with arms ought
not to rule. Many senators agreed with him. Therefore Didius, in anger, sought
soldiers from the camp to force the senate to obedience or to cut them down.
But that plan was disliked, for it was not fitting that, when the senate had
judged Severus an enemy for Julianus's sake, it should suffer that same
Julianus as a foe. Therefore with better counsel he came to the senate and
asked that a decree be made for a sharing of the empire, which was done at
once.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Then an omen which Julianus had made for himself when he received the empire
came to everyone's mind. For when the consul designate, giving his opinion on
him, had pronounced:
/I hold that Didius Julianus should be named emperor,/
Julianus suggested
/Add also Severus,/
the surname of his grandfather and great-grandfather, which Julianus had taken
as his own. There are some, however, who say that Julianus had no plan of
cutting down the senate, since the senate had resolved so much in his favor.

After the decree of the senate Didius Julianus at once sent one of the
prefects, Tullius Crispinus; he himself made Veturius Macrinus a third
prefect, to whom Severus had sent letters that he should be prefect. But both
the people said and Severus suspected that the peace was feigned and that the
killing of Severus had been entrusted to Tullius Crispinus, prefect of the
guard. At last Severus preferred, by the soldiers' consent, to be Julianus's
enemy rather than his partner. Severus at once both wrote to very many at Rome
and secretly sent edicts, which were posted. There was besides in Julianus
this madness, that he did many things through magicians, by which he thought
the people's hatred might be soothed or the soldiers' arms held back. For they
sacrificed certain victims not fitting for Roman rites and chanted unholy
songs, and Julianus performed that which they say is done at a mirror, in
which boys, with their eyes bound and a charm spoken over their heads, are
said to look. Then the boy is said to have seen both the coming of Severus and
the departure of Julianus.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

And Crispinus indeed, when he met Severus's forerunners, was killed by Severus
at the instance of Julius Laetus. The decrees of the senate were also thrown
down. Julianus, having called the senate and asked their opinions as to what
needed to be done, learned nothing certain from the senate; but afterward, of
his own accord, he ordered the gladiators at Capua to be armed through
Lollianus Titianus, and he summoned Claudius Pompeianus from his Tarracinan
estate to a share of the empire, because he had been an emperor's son-in-law
and had long commanded soldiers. But that man refused, answering that he was
old and of failing eyesight. Soldiers from Umbria had also gone over to
Severus, and Severus had sent letters ahead in which he ordered that the
killers of Pertinax be kept under guard.

In a short time Julianus was deserted by all and remained in the Palace with
one of his prefects, Genialis, and with his son-in-law Repentinus. At last it
was moved that the empire be taken from Julianus by the senate's authority,
and it was taken, and Severus was at once named emperor, while it was
pretended that Julianus had made away with himself by poison. Nevertheless men
were sent by the senate, and by their care the same Julianus was killed in the
Palace by a common soldier, calling on the protection of Caesar, that is, of
Severus. When he obtained the empire he had emancipated his daughter and given
her her patrimony. This was at once taken from her, together with the name of
Augusta. His body was given back by Severus to his wife Manlia Scantilla and
to his daughter for burial, and it was carried to the monument of his
great-grandfather at the fifth milestone on the Labican Way.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:

These things, to be sure, were charged against Julianus: that he had been a
glutton, that he had been a gambler, that he had trained with gladiatorial
arms, and that he had done all this as an old man, though as a youth he had
never before been branded with these vices. Pride was also charged, though
even in the empire he had been most humble. He was, on the contrary, most
gracious at banquets, most kind in answering petitions, and most moderate in
granting freedom.

He lived fifty-six years and four months. He ruled two months and five days.
This especially was blamed in him: that those whom he ought to have ruled by
his own authority he himself made his chiefs for governing the republic.
