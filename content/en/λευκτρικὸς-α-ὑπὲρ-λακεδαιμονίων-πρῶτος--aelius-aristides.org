With this mind, men of Athens, I have risen, that if it were not too simple to
say so, I should be near to saying that I am still seeking which of the two I
should join; and if someone rising a little later should refute the
calculations that are slipping from me and himself show something better, I
would be the first to be persuaded by him: so little goodwill toward either
side exists in me from of old. But the things that have made me both rise with
confidence and already [persuade] myself to incline toward the one side, these
I will first indicate, and I will at the same time show which of the two,
after first setting in the midst, for both parties, the main point of the
opinion I hold about these matters. I want the Thebans to be at your disposal
against the Lacedaemonians, and the Lacedaemonians in turn against the
Thebans, and neither to fare more fortunately than is expedient for the city.
If, then, they had stood on equal terms and the argument for both had been
about the same things, I should have thought there was no difficulty. But such
a turn having taken place toward the Thebans, I fear the weakness of the one
party and the power of the other. If, then, we had to restore the former by
one action and dissolve the latter by another, first we should have had a
double task, and then each would have had to do his own work in its season.
But since both have fallen together into the same moment, it seems to me we
should use the conjunction of the occasion and contribute to those men as much
as it is expedient to take from these. For I think that if they are equalized
by us they will be occupied with their affairs toward one another, but if one
side is destroyed, the victors will already have their account with us. From
these calculations I declare that what might seem paradoxical will come about:
if we stand out of the way

of both, we shall be in danger against the remaining one; but if we help the
weaker, we shall thereafter stand in the middle of both. These, then, are the
calculations by which I have ceased to be in the middle, and the reasons for
which I have chosen the Lacedaemonians. But your readiness for humanity, and
your being first to pity the unfortunate rather than wait for them to ask,
might perhaps not allow these things to be examined exactly. For I know that
when first the herald came announcing the battle, whom the Thebans sent to you
crowned, you heard him with displeasure and grief, and were so far from
sharing their joy that you neither invited him to the prytaneum nor showed any
other mark of humanity. So the old saying is true, that for those who suffer
as individuals there underlies for all a common good fortune: the character of
this city, by which they are saved. But as for the man who would remind you of
what the Lacedaemonians did in the Decelean war, I will ask who shared those
deeds with them. I think not the Samians, but those to whom he will think it
right to give help as to the guiltless.

If, then, these things must be granted to some occasion and to humanity, it is
just that oblivion be common to both. We ought not to remember the wrongs
against those who were persuaded by them from the start and were eager to make
a settlement in the middle and then were prevented, while we let the blame go
for those who were ungrateful throughout. But if we must accuse both, as I by
the gods would no longer think right unless someone could persuade me, why
accuse of these things those from whom it is not possible to separate the
others? For of all the worst things that the closest examiner could name,
these men shared with them.

And indeed if one must pick something out to mention, leaving aside the common
things of the war, let us look at what each side did and resolved afterward.
We shall find that what the Thebans resolved was the destruction of our city
and enslavement and that no one should remain anywhere. I shrink

from adding the name to the blasphemy. Yet not even the Persian formerly
decided this about us, but thought it enough if he had us willing to do what
was commanded. What the Lacedaemonians resolved, on the other hand, was this
very thing which they now think it right to obtain from you: to inhabit their
own land and suffer nothing irreparable. And indeed I consider with myself: if
at that time the Lacedaemonians had agreed with the Thebans and had undertaken
this deed, and if some god had not held his hand over such a thing, what would
now be left except for the Thebans to seek whom they will call against these
men? And yet, let me be allowed to say this too without contentiousness: if
one examines with full justice, he will find that the Lacedaemonians perhaps
have a share of pardon both for what was done in the war and if they were
ungrateful in any way afterward. Why? Because for those who contend over
empire and hegemony the very form of the war gives an excuse, if they are
carried too far; but for those who contend for no such honor, but press in
otherwise and go beyond measure, as the Thebans then did, anyone at all would
justly be indignant, because they have an undisguised ingratitude and use a
naturally wicked character. And indeed if anyone thinks the examination of
these things has passed, and seeks exactly the pretext of the present war, I
know that he judges the Lacedaemonians to be in the wrong and thinks we ought
to stand with the Thebans.

That Phoebidas was the one who did these things, and that none of this was
done from the common resolve, but that Phoebidas came in as he was passing and
seized the Cadmea when those of the Thebans themselves who were dissatisfied
with the present state of things called him: this I neither quite believe nor
would I say. For those at home, I suppose, after hearing these things, could
have called him back, fined him, withdrawn him, been seen to be angry, which
they are far from having done. Nevertheless, if we were standing in the way of
the Thebans when they wished to take

satisfaction, we should truly be doing wrong and not acting in our own
character. But since they have taken this, and are grasping at what lies
beyond, I do not think we ought, on the pretext of justice, to concede to them
a most unjust thing: the enslavement of the Peloponnese and the rest of the
Greeks. For to destroy the Lacedaemonians means this. If, then, both had sent
before the battle, asking for help just as they do now, I should have spoken
for the Thebans: I say this without having deliberated. Why? Because I should
have thought the Lacedaemonians' appeal was to join in enslaving the Thebans,
and the Thebans' to take justice from the Lacedaemonians. And I should have
thought this had a far better claim than that. But now that the embassy has
arrived at the time when they already have the advantage in what they seek, I
do not see the manner of the help. For my part, and let no one take offense at
the paradox: if even at the start, when both sent, I had spoken for the
Thebans, and then after the battle both sent again as they do now, I should
have confidently urged a change of sides. For when the war was being set on
foot I should have urged us to take the part of the injured; but when that for
the sake of which we should have joined them had been fulfilled, I should have
thought one of two things: either they ought to have stopped, or, if they went
beyond measure, they ought to be checked by us again. And the more eagerly we
should have helped them before, the more I say we must now stand against them.
For since, having obtained the justice they put forward, they do not cease to
press, they themselves have come, by a just road, into the Lacedaemonians'
accusation, and the pretext of the war, for which we then had to go against
those men, has changed sides. For they are not defending themselves for what
they have suffered, but they seek to make greater what they have succeeded in.
Therefore the just claim they had has expired, so far as they would have
obtained help, and the present is recognized as having the rank of injustice.
Therefore they would justly be checked by us. Then too it is not even
reasonable that, when we should then have refused to join the Thebans in

enslaving them to the Lacedaemonians, we should now endure joining these men
in destroying the Lacedaemonians. For it is far more fitting, by Zeus and the
gods, that the Thebans should stand aside because of us than that we should
take part because of them in such a deed.

For it is possible, it is possible even for those who are defending themselves
to do wrong without measure. And if anyone, in filling up his punishments
against the wrongs, goes beyond with these, he in turn begins a second time.
What I mean is this: the Lacedaemonians came against the Thebans. Well. Then
the Lacedaemonians have also paid the penalty to the Thebans. There the
complaint has its end. Whatever is done after this by the Thebans is, so to
speak, the beginning and cycle of a second injustice. And this is not so only
in the case of cities, but all the affairs of private men also come under this
argument. That someone, or the man who did the deed, was punished: this has
become, so to speak, the undoing of the wickedness. But if, having once taken
vengeance, he thereafter drives and plunders the man who has come to
misfortune, the laws already command that he himself be punished: and justly.
For this is in the interest of the nature of our whole race, that there be
something for those who err after which the anger of the sufferer must stop.
For now let someone tell me: if we join the Thebans and the Lacedaemonians are
again utterly defeated, what is there to prevent the Thebans from seeking yet
another penalty they will take, and this going on without end forever because
of the first charge? But why do I split these hairs? For even if the
Lacedaemonians have done things most terrible and greater than any punishment,
the occasion, men of Athens, pleads for them, and the fact that it is not
honorable for you to join in attacking them in their misfortunes. For when
more than a thousand of them have died, their allies have revolted, the
Thebans' temples have been adorned with their arms, and their hopes hang on us
alone, how is it right to become the killers of these men with our own hands
and not to help preserve the remaining resources? For even if you yourselves,
waging a private war, had done these things to them, you would justly, it
seems to me, stop here. But to join the Thebans in working out the complete

destruction of your opponents: how is that not a thing to be fled even in
speech? Against this let anyone bring forward the peace in the time of
Antalcidas and whatever else he wishes. This very matter of the peace the
Lacedaemonians would buy at the highest price of all to have spoken against
them, compared with their present need. For the more you blame that peace, the
more eagerly you must now save them. For it is written in it, I suppose, that
the King may do whatever he wishes to the Greeks who inhabit Asia itself; and
those who are displeased at this cannot neglect the present embassy of the
unfortunate. For surely no one would say that the Lacedaemonians and the
Athenians ought to have an equal care of the Greeks at all, looking if to
nothing else then to these very treaties with the King, of which theirs have
given over the Greeks on the sea, while ours hold the King back from the sea
inland. Nor is it the same thing to concede these things when shut into
necessity and desiring peace, and, when there is peace, out of desire for war,
to overlook justice of one's own will. And when even to write that the King
may do what he wishes concerning those in the King's land is heavy and
terrible, how is it not far more shocking to join in working out, with the
Thebans, the Greeks in Europe? For if it has been judged an accusation and a
reproach that the Lacedaemonians, when they were pressed by war, wrote that
the King may do what he wishes to those in the King's land, how can it be
tolerable for you, being Athenians and masters of the counsel, to hand over
with your own hands those in Europe to the Thebans, and these the
Lacedaemonians? From whom, if nothing else, at least the dignity they once had
is being taken away.

Now perhaps the greatest argument for those who speak for the Thebans would be
that you formerly made them allies, and they will remind you of the expedition
to Haliartus and the battle at Coronea. It would be strange, then, they would
say, if you make friends of those who were then enemies and enemies of those
who were then allies, at the same time. But I am so far from thinking this is
against the Lacedaemonians that if

I thought no one would speak for the Thebans, I should still have used them as
examples on behalf of these men. For who does not know that then too we made
the Thebans friends and allies, though they had been enemies and at war for
all the time before, and nothing of the existing oppositions prevented us from
coming together and taking their part, when they seemed to us to say the same
just things? That the former alliance, then, is no obstacle to receiving the
Lacedaemonians now is clear from this. But that, men of Athens, even according
to that very alliance it is necessary to defend the Lacedaemonians, and that
those who most remember the former zeal for the Thebans are the very ones who
cannot do otherwise than change sides: this I will show still more clearly
than the former point. For if someone asked you, men of Athens, how much money
the Thebans paid you and so obtained the alliance, you would say, "Hush! The
Thebans, to us? From where? Or what? And who else?" How then did you receive
them? For they were not friends of old. You could name no other pretext than
the danger and the need, and that if they were overlooked by us they would at
once be in the Lacedaemonians' power. If, then, some private intimacy had
existed between you and them, not even so, I suppose, would anyone justly have
blamed us for not being willing to share even their errors to the end. But
perhaps you would have seemed to change, yet reasonably, putting more account
of our own reputation than of their gratitude. But since the cause for which
you then stood with them is the one I have named, I would say that in fighting
with them now too there is the appearance of changing, but in choosing the
other side instead there is that of remaining the same, since both have taken
each other's post. And if anyone says he fears for the city lest it incur a
reputation for impulsiveness, first he fears too late: for why did he not
reckon these things in the case of the Thebans before? Then

let him be dismissed free of this charge, but let him at least be persuaded of

always with what carries, keeping the same art and the same calculation in
season, whether I am carried from here to there or back again." This is the
pilot's argument, I think, and this same one I must now speak for the city.

And that you may know that I look simply at all things and mix in no
contentiousness, I will say something even greater than these. If we remember
the former alliance strongly, as we ought, and hesitate to change sides now,
let us not make war on the Thebans, yet let us not overlook the
Lacedaemonians: I speak no riddle, but let us send men to tell the Thebans in
advance to keep their hands off the Lacedaemonians. And if they hear this and
are persuaded, nothing contrary at all will have been done by us toward these
men, nor will these be destroyed because of it, but the one party will remain
friends as before, and the other will be added to them, and both will know
gratitude, the one for having had no trouble, the other for having been saved
through you. I hear that long ago too, before the Median affairs, these same
Thebans were reconciled to us when the battle was already about to take place,
the Corinthians happening to be present and reconciling both. And now, I
suppose, the Thebans would more justly be persuaded by us, when they see us,
though a Lacedaemonian embassy is calling us against them, so careful of them
that we invite and persuade them by speech that we may not be forced to do
them any harm, rather than compel them with arms, though we can. If, then, as
I said, they are persuaded, this is best. But if they pay no attention, they
will blame themselves, no longer us. For we shall be seen both remembering the
alliance and not wishing to dissolve it, and they will be the ones who have
overlooked these calculations and who care nothing if they now set their

arms against those with whom they were formerly arrayed. Do not, then, allow
the speakers to say to you what ought to be said to the Thebans, nor if anyone
attempts to persuade you of what is not yours, endure it. For how is it not
strange, when we are willing, if it is possible, to persuade even the Thebans,
and not only ourselves, that we must not be divided; if the Thebans drag us
out even against our will, that they should advise us to keep quiet and judge
as allies those who are running away?

Come now, by Zeus: if the Thebans had said this to us when they first came,
that if they beat off the war and the danger that surrounded them, they would
make you enemies and hate you, unless you allowed them once and for all to
destroy the Lacedaemonians and bring Greece under themselves, would anyone
have thought there were more wicked men, and more justly deserving to perish?
Let us not, then, now grant on that pretext what they would not have obtained
at the start on these terms, but let us prevent the very opposite, unless even
that former alliance must seem to have been made for these ends. And I think
the Thebans are not so poor and so insensible, even though they are Thebans,
as to make more complaint against you now if they are prevented from
destroying the Lacedaemonians, than to remember gratitude for their own
salvation, which formerly came to them through you. I should gladly ask those
who speak for the Thebans whether they say they are allies, or have been. For
if they are, why do they persuade us to make them so? But if they have been,
the Lacedaemonians became so much earlier than these. Then too it is not even
reasonable to say they have been, and again to talk as if the alliance still
exists, bidding us not to dissolve it. And indeed that you have rejected it,
the messenger of the victory knows, whom after the battle you received with no
more honor than those sent of old by the barbarian. As for how many even
lamented the Lacedaemonians with tears when they heard, I will not speak,
though I remember.

What then is the cause? Are you so envious and wicked in your ways, and have
you surpassed all men in wickedness and vice, that you are vexed and jealous
if your friends fare well in anything? It is not so. It would be a pretty
thing, by Zeus, to say even this. It is not so. But what? There is something
long native to this city, well reputed among all, which neither passed over to
these Megarians here, though they are neighbors, nor to the second, the
greatest of the Greeks, these now wretched Lacedaemonians, but here it both
grew and flourished from the very beginning. What then is this? A turn toward
the weak, and pity for the unfortunate that is not empty, but sufficient for
salvation. This does not allow the city to remember wrongs, nor to examine who
the unfortunate man is, but whenever it perceives anyone faring badly, it at
once forgets what it has itself suffered. Therefore for all men the very
figure of misfortune is enough toward it, like some other token. For this
reason it received both the Thebans and the Lacedaemonians, when it first knew
them unfortunate in the time of the Heraclidae, and pitied them. Now as for
the good services which someone might say the Thebans rendered us, this that
they did not join the Lacedaemonians in the expedition against the Piraeus,
and that they received Thrasybulus: I will speak no blasphemy, for I should be
mad. And yet I have heard someone already saying that we ought not so much to
thank them for not joining the expedition as to blame them, and by Zeus, he
said, even to hate them for the reason for which they did this. For he said
they were angry at having failed to destroy our city. These things I leave to
those who wish to say them. But what do I myself say? If the Thebans had been
in some danger, it would have been just for someone to remind us of these
things, thinking it wrong to join an expedition against their city, just as
they formerly did not join these same Lacedaemonians against ours when it was
in a poor state. But when

the argument is for the salvation of the Lacedaemonians, why must these
outside things be used to lead you aside? For so much the more must they not
be overlooked, by as much as the shame is greater if the Thebans did not
shrink from appearing to share our pain when we fared badly, though no appeal
came to them from us, while we, being Athenians, shall attack the
Lacedaemonians in their disasters, who have confessed by their embassy that
their own affairs are nothing apart from us.

And indeed I will not shrink from saying this either, that if the Thebans have
not received from us thanks many times greater, or rather in every way
greater, than these good services, we owe them everything. But is it possible,
by Zeus, that anyone should think it equal or nearly so not to follow the
Lacedaemonians when they were invading, and to have been ready to stand
against the Lacedaemonians for their sake; or to receive the exiles, which is
common to all men, and to arrange that those men's people shall not be exiled
nor made homeless? These things, then, have been done by us for the Thebans in
return for those, and we killed Lysander and set up a trophy and showed
Boeotia clear of enemies. Do they have the good service back by the very
measure? Or even if we had put it in their hands, could they have asked for
more? I wonder, then, if we shall remember the Thebans' humane acts, and
nowhere set the Lacedaemonians' resolve; which, if those men had not then had
a moderate one concerning us, these later good services of the Thebans would
have had no place. For all the Thebans' deeds are so far inferior to this one
thing, as even for the Thebans themselves and for all who wished it, the power
to do us good did not exist once we had been destroyed; and as we could not
have been more unfortunate if we had suffered the opposite of what the
Lacedaemonians resolved, while that we could be treated well more greatly than
later by the Thebans, many ways still available show. For for these things
perhaps some other resource might be found even without them, but for not
suffering those things the Lacedaemonians were altogether needed, because of
the fortune they then had: which I wish they had never had. For that fortune
both destroyed our city

when it was flourishing and has brought those men themselves into their

And let no one think I am saying this, that you are safe because of the
Lacedaemonians. May they never fare so well. But what saves and guards our
city, men of Athens, is the just providence of the gods and the good fortune
that was allotted to it from the beginning. As for what came to rest on men,
the Lacedaemonians once took a share, and the Phocians, and someone else I
think, yet not least the Lacedaemonians, having themselves the authority of
the vote. Now, then, we must think that that occasion has come, in which we
ourselves then stood, and the mind we should then have thought it right for
the Lacedaemonians to have on our behalf, when we were hanging upon such a
danger and such a matter: this, since we have anticipated them, let us give
back in kind. The Lacedaemonians have become our suppliants, who formerly
deliberated about you and gathered the Greeks and held land and sea, and they
say in their supplication moderate things: that they too ought to have some
enjoyment of your nobility, and that you will have saved not only the Thebans,
nor the rest of the Greeks, but them as well. And if those who then helped
with votes now think it right to be saved by you with arms in return, receive
this most of all. For the rank of justice has the same account, but the manner
of the good service, prevailing in dignity, will show the surplus of your
greatness of soul. For other favors have force according to those that exist,
but the one you will now give back to the Lacedaemonians will all shift onto
yourselves. For to save in return with war and arms those who were saved by a
vote: this is what makes the ambition, and that by which it is clear that it
is Athenians, not some of the other men. And if you too now had to vote about
them in the same way, I should not have rejoiced with you for saving them, but
I should have judged the deed theirs. And indeed when you were saving the
Thebans, you were not giving them like for like, I suppose, and yet at that
time you neither had plenty of triremes nor did the walls yet have

anyone to raise them, but the city was almost unguarded and bare. But now you
see, I suppose, so many ships gathered in the Piraeus, and you see these walls
here and all the rest of the equipment with which war wishes to be furnished.
And you have such a surplus that both sides agree that the turn will come upon
whichever you join.

I think, then, we should not consider this either idly or without reason: that
if the Thebans, when the occasion has fallen their way, do not destroy the
Lacedaemonians, but handle them with some reasonable and moderate mind, it is
the Thebans who will be well reputed instead of us. What could be more
shameful than this? But if they utterly destroy them, it will be no slight or
chance suffering for Greece that there should be no Lacedaemonians; and this
being such, the appearance that we are the cause will be added, which is much
greater than the former, if it is possible to say so. Nor, then, can I expect
that they would spare them in any way at all, being Thebans, if they get an
occasion. For when I think of what they resolved about us and that fine vote
and from where they first stood apart from them, I think the Lacedaemonians
ought long since to have been lamented in advance, if they should come into
such a danger. For it is not even likely that they would have cast this vote
about a war of which others were the leaders, but that a war of which they
themselves have become masters and champions they would, if it came into their
hands, settle moderately; or that those who hate the Lacedaemonians both
because of themselves, nearly, and because of us, because they then spared us,
would do them some small thing. For they did not hate us more because of them
than they now hate them both for their own sake and because of us. Nor, then,
do you seem to me likely to fear with reason that the Lacedaemonians will give
us trouble again, if they are saved. For if we granted them to hope the
greatest things, they would not, I suppose, expect or pray for anything
greater than defeating us. This, then, they know exactly never profited them
before, but when they took over the hegemony by land and by sea, then the
evils of mankind surrounded them, to Corinth, to

Boeotia, again to Argos, running Greece up and down and being destroyed, until
they shamefully needed even the King of the Persians, and at last, struck in a
single battle, they were brought down. Those, then, for whom it is clear that
even to fare as they would pray is not profitable, and for whom, if they have
that for which one must make war, there is harm and danger: by what, once
elated, will these men give themselves to such unreason in affairs, or will
they wish to make war, when it does not profit even about victory? And hoping
what would they seek again to take over the cities which, when they had given
themselves, they could not hold? Neither would anyone, I think, dare to mount
a chariot when he sees the cliff, knowing that he cannot stay on as rider, nor
to mount upon so many affairs, from which, once thrown, he will lie at once.
For he who takes up a bulk greater than himself, even if he bears it easily
for the moment, in a little while is destroyed, crushed. And the
Lacedaemonians remember this too, with how great a preparation and with how
great an organization they entered the former war, and what allies they had,
and what dignity they had in those times.

And this too has not escaped them, that if the city itself had not stumbled
concerning itself, both by throwing itself into the war against the Sicilians,
and by losing the ships at Aegospotami through the generals' carelessness, not
even if they had taken all mankind would anything more at all have come to
them, but either they would have given up and begged us to end the war, or at
last, defeated, they would have been subject. But now, looking to what of all
things will they stir up so great a matter? What empire? What strength? What
revenues? What noble resource of war? There is nothing. Let us not, then, men
