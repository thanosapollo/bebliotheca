looking toward the world are dim-sighted toward the one shown through these,

having named unmanly we have made known the coward, and it is possible to say

useful thought through the denial of the wicked, or conversely we are turned
in our suspicions toward the worse, having indicated the wicked by the
subtraction of the beautiful. Thus then even upon the present word someone
having thought would not miss the due thought about the thing sought. And it
is sought what one must think the mind according to the essence itself. The
one then not doubting that this about which the word is, is, through the
activity shown to us by it, and wanting to know what it is, would find it
sufficiently, not to learn that it is itself what sense comprehends, not


color, not figure, not resistance, not weight, not magnitude, not the
dimension into three, not the position upon place, nor if he should see at all
nothing of the things comprehended about matter, what else besides these it
is. And I, while she was going through, {G.} I do not know, I said, how it is
possible, all these being subtracted from the word, that the thing sought not
be wiped out together with them. For to what the apprehensive curiosity is
naturally fitted apart from these, according to my suspicion at least, is not
yet seen. For everywhere in the search of beings through the examining mind,
as far as the thing sought is, just as some blind being led by the hand
through walls toward =46.41= the door, we altogether touch one of the things
said, either finding color, or figure, or magnitude, or some other of the

removed from the existent, neither would the one saying this altogether

but having been taught the bodiless and formless about the divine nature, he
reckons from such a consequence altogether that it is not at all. And if there
the not-being of these things does not become a circumscription of being, how
is the human mind squeezed out of the existent, being spent together by the
subtraction of the bodily properties? {G.} Therefore, I said, from absurd
things we take over another absurd through this consequence. For the word is

indeed each is thought by the taking-away of the things found according to
sense. {M.} Do not say the same, says the teacher (for this word also is
impious), but as you were taught from the divine Scripture, say this like to

those unspeakable properties of the godhead shine out, so that the word being

elementally in the world not coinciding with it according to the word of the

hypostasis is from the mixture of the elements, is there some communion
according to the word of the essence for the simple and formless of the soul
toward


the bodily coarseness; but nevertheless that the vital activity of the soul is
in these is not doubted, having been mixed by some word better than human
comprehension. Therefore neither, the elements in the bodies having been
resolved toward themselves, has that which binds them through the vital
activity perished. But just as, the compound of the elements still standing
together, it also ensouls the things according to each, the soul putting
itself equally and likewise into all the parts that fill the body, and one
would not say that it is either solid and resistant, being mixed with the

these, and putting the vital power into each: so, even the compound having
been loosed, and having run back again into its own, to think that that simple
and uncomposed nature is present to each of the parts even after the
dissolution is nothing of the likely; but that what has once grown together by
some unspeakable word with the compound of the elements also remains forever
with the things with which it was mixed, being pulled away in no way from the
growing-together that has once come to be for it. For =46.45= it is not
because the composed is loosed that what is not composed is in danger of being
dissolved together with the composite. {G.} And I said, But that the elements
both fall together toward one another, and are distinguished from one another,
and that this is the constitution and the dissolution of the body, no one
would contradict. And since much the middle is thought for each of these
things that have toward one another in another kind, both according to the
local position, and the difference and property of the products, the elements
having run together with one another about the underlying, it is consequent
that this intellectual and unextended nature which we call soul has grown
together toward the united; and if these should be distinguished from one
another, and come to be there, wherever nature should lead each, what will the
soul suffer, its vehicle having been scattered in many places; just as some
sailor, the cargo-ship having been dissolved in a shipwreck, being unable to
swim at once upon all the parts of the boat scattered some elsewhere some
elsewhere of the sea (for having taken hold of whatever he happens upon he
will leave the rest to be carried by the waves), in the same manner the soul,
not having the nature to be split together with the distinction of the
elements, if indeed it has itself hard to be released from the body, having
grown toward some one of the elements altogether, will be split off from the
others, and the consequence of the word gives to think it no more immortal
through living in one, than mortal, through not being in the more. {M.} But
the intellectual and unextended, she says, is neither contracted nor diffused
(for contraction and diffusion are proper of bodies), and equally according to
its own formless and bodiless nature it is present both to the compounding of
the elements about the body and to the distinguishing, being cramped neither
when the elements are squeezed together in the compound, nor being left behind
when they have gone away toward the kindred and according to nature for them,
even if much the middle seems to be that is contemplated of the otherness of
the elements. For much is the difference of the upward-borne and light toward
the heavy and earthy, of the hot toward the cold, and of the wet toward the
contrary; but nevertheless there is no toil for the intellectual nature
=46.48= to be present to each, to which it once grew in, not being split
together through mixture with the contrariety of the elements. For it is not
because according to the local interval, and the qualitative property, these
are thought far from one another, that because of this the unextended nature
labors being joined to the things locally standing apart, since even now it is
possible for the mind both to contemplate the heaven together and to be
stretched by its curious dealings upon the limits of the world; and the
contemplative of our soul being stretched toward so many lengths is not torn.
Therefore there is no hindrance for the soul to be present equally to the
elements of the body, both mixed through the running-together, and released
through the re-mixture. For just as of gold and silver having been melted
together some technical power is contemplated that melted the materials
together; and


if again the one should be melted off from the other, no less does the word of
the art remain in each; and the matter has been divided, but the art has not
been cut together with the matter; for how would the uncuttable be divided?
according to the same word even the intellectual nature of the soul is both
contemplated in the running-together of the elements, and having been
dissolved is not separated, but both remains in them and being co-extended in
their separation is not cut through, nor is it chopped into partial cuts
toward the number of the elements. For this is proper of the bodily and
dimensional nature; but the intellectual and unextended nature does not take
up the passions from interval. Therefore the soul is in them, in which it once
came to be, no necessity pulling it away from the growing-together toward
those. What then is the gloomy in these things, if the formless is exchanged
for the seen? and for what has your mind been so set against death? {G.} And
I, having taken up in the mind the definition which she made in the words
before these about soul, said that that word had not sufficiently shown me the
powers contemplated in the soul; which says that it is an intellectual
essence, and puts into the organic body a vital power toward the activity of
the senses; for not only about the scientific and contemplative mind is our
soul active, working such a thing in the intellectual of the essence, nor does
it manage the sense-organs only toward the activity according to nature; but
much is the motion according to desire, and much also the motion according to
spiritedness is contemplated in the nature; and each of these existing in us
generically =46.49=, we see the motion of both going forward in the activities
into many and various differences. For it is possible to see many things of
which the desirous leads; many again which grow out of the spirited cause, and
