* On Vainglory and on Educating Children
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1

Of our father among the saints John Chrysostom, On Vainglory and How Parents
Ought to Bring Up Their Children.

Has anyone done what I asked? Has anyone begged God both for us and for the
whole body of the Church, that the fire born of vainglory might be quenched,
the fire that ruins the whole body, that divides one body into many members
and splits love apart? For just as a wild beast that has fallen upon a noble
and tender body unable to defend itself, so it has fixed its filthy teeth and
injected its venom and filled it with much stench, and some parts it has cut
off and thrown away, some it has torn, and some it has even chewed through.
And if it were possible to see with the eyes both vainglory and the Church,
one would have seen a pitiable spectacle and far more grievous than those that
happen in the stadia: the body thrown down, and that thing standing over it
from above and looking round everywhere and holding those who have fallen in
and never standing off nor sparing. Who then among us will scare this beast
away? It is the work of him who set the contest, when he is called upon by us,
to send his angels and, as with some ropes, to stop her shameless and brazen
mouth, and so to lead her away. But then the one who set the contest will do
this, when we do not seek her after she has been led away; but if he sends,
commanding the terrible beast to stand off from us, and we, after being saved
and after it has been led away to its own den, rise up with ten thousand
wounds and seek it again and knock and overturn, so as to bring it out, he
will no longer pity us nor spare: "For who," it says, "will pity a
snake-bitten enchanter, and all who come near to beasts?" What then? How might
we be delivered from the evil and wicked demon? For it is truly a demon having
a lovely appearance. For just as if some demon, having shaped himself into a
courtesan and putting on much gold and dressing in soft garments and smelling
of many spices, should come under a very splendid image of a woman and hide
the excess of all beauty; then also should appear at such an age as most of
all lifts the souls of the young, bearing the very flower of youth, girt with
a golden girdle and showing locks from the head variously plaited, resembling
a Persian topknot; then should set a diadem about the head, putting much
adornment on the bare hair and showing gold flashing about the neck and
precious stones, and should stand, having put on a harlot's very young age, in
a lonely place before the house, and then also should display much modesty,
whom of those passing by would he not take? And after this, having led him
into the house, he should put off all that beauty, and having shown himself
some black and fiery and wild thing, as a demon is likely to be, he would
astound the wretched one who has fallen in, and leaping and seizing the soul
he would madden that one's mind: such a thing is the wicked demon of
vainglory. For what seems more fair than she? And what more lovely? But if we
see that the thing is a fantasy and that it is hypocrisy, we shall not be
caught by the nets nor fall upon the stage; for what has been said about the
harlot, this one might reasonably say also about this one: "For honey drips
from the lips of a harlot woman"; the same one would not err in saying also
about vainglory. For as the fruit of Sodom, such is vainglory; for that too
has a splendid appearance and puts into those who see it the suspicion of
healthy fruits from

the look. But if you take into your hand either a pomegranate or an apple, it
yields at once to the fingers and, the outer rind being crushed, it lets them
fall into the dust and the ash. Such a thing is vainglory also: seen, it seems
some great and wonderful thing, but when it is held by our hands it has at
once thrown our soul into the dust. And that vainglory is such a thing is
clear from many sides. What then? Do you wish that we begin first from those
outside? The theatre is filled and the whole people sits above, presenting a
very splendid sight and composed of so many faces that often even the roof
itself and the overlying ceiling are covered with the bodies of the men, and
it is possible to see neither tiles nor stones, but all faces of men and
bodies. And before all, when the ambitious man who has gathered them has
entered, they stand up at once and as from one mouth they send forth one
voice, all together calling him guardian and protector of the common city and
stretching out their hands. Then in the midst of them all they compare him to
a greater river, likening the stout and poured-out quality of his ambition to
the abundance of the Nile waters, and they say that he is a Nile of gifts. But
those who flatter him more, thinking this example, that of the Nile, to be a
small thing, let rivers and seas go, and bringing the Ocean into the midst
they say that he is this: what that one is in waters, this one is in ambitious
shows; and they leave out no form of applause at all. Splendid is the look of
vainglory; but you, remember for me the type of the girl into which we
fashioned the demon, putting gold about him and bringing him to the age of
some courtesan, and you will see that there is not much interval between the
image. What then after this? Having bowed to them and having honored them in
this way himself also, he sits being counted blessed by all those, each of
them praying to become what that man then was, and at once to die. But after
he has spent much gold and silver, horses, garments, slaves, and all those
things, and has emptied many estates, they escort him again with much
applause, yet not so many any longer; for when the theatre has been dissolved
each hastens to his own. Then at the house there are costly dinners and much
feasting and great is the splendor of the day. About late afternoon the same
things again, and so for two and three days. When then he has emptied
everything and ten thousand talents of gold, then at last is shown the ash of
this voice and the cinder and the dust. For when at the house he seeks the
words and thinks of the excess of the expense, then he wails. For as long as
he enjoys the desire, taken as by some drunkenness of vainglory, he would even
consume himself, and he cannot take even a small perception of the loss. But
when he has come home, inside into the house of this demon, and sees the
beauty of the gathering gone, the assembly having been dissolved, and looking
toward the theatre finds it empty of men and no one uttering anything, and the
loss no longer in fantasy but already come to pass in money, then he perceives
the ash. And if, having spent even beyond his substance, he is in need and,
lifted from his feet, he begs in the middle of the marketplace, and then of
those who were then shouting "protector" no one comes nor stretches out a
hand, but they even take pleasure in what is happening (for even then, when
they were applauding, they were also bitten by envy and counted it a
consolation of their own evils that that man, who had become so splendid, was
going to be more dishonored than all), when then no one comes nor stretches
out a hand, what is more pitiable than these things? Is it not more worthy of
tears? And what is more grievous than these things? Perhaps you know someone
who has suffered this? Not at all? And would that then


they were not stretching out a hand, but even the contrary: he is struck with
accusations by those very ones who praised him. "For why," he says, "was he
mad? Why a lover of glory? For whose sake was he granting favors to harlots
and mimes?" Ungrateful man, were you not admiring? Were you not praising? Did
you not lead him on to this by the clapping and the applause? Did you not call
him Nile? Did you not call him Ocean? Did you not spend the whole day on the
applause of that man? From where then have you suddenly changed? And when it
is necessary to pity, then most of all you accuse him for the things for which
you also used to clap of old? For if in the things for which we accuse, when
we see a man being punished, we are not so stony as not to be bent, when we
see him suffering some evil for the things for which we also praised him,
ought we not much more to be bent? But now you accuse; when he was delighting
you with the spectacle, when you were spending the day having left all your
own affairs, why were you not accusing? Do you see of what sort the things of
the devil are? Of what sort the fruits of vainglory? I said ash and dust; but
I see not ash and dust only, but also fire and smoke; for the thing does not
stop at getting no profit, but even goes as far as surrounding with evils. But
on those it would be ash and dust, on those who spend much and harvest
nothing; not however also on those who suffer the things I just now went
through. "What then?" he says, "when through those public services they are
honored and admired by many, is this fruit a small one?" Very much so; for
this honor is not great, which I just now went through, being struck also with
jeers and being accused and being slandered. "But what of those who are
honored?" For those men are not honored through the public services, but
through the expectation that he will spend again on the crowd. And if it is
because of what has gone before, why do they accuse those who do not have? And
why do they not even approach, but even mock, calling them prodigals and
filthy men? Did you see that vainglory is some madness? But let this form be
left, being of one man and a second somewhere; and let us come to another. And
if someone should say: "What then of those who spend in measure in the
amusements of the cities?" Tell me, I beg, what is the gain? For even among
those the glory and the shout is of a day. And that this would be so, if
someone gave them a choice to take back that money, or even the third, or even
the smallest part, and to have heard no such voice, do you think they would
not have chosen it ten thousand times? For those who for one obol act
shamelessly and brazenly in ten thousand ways, what would they not have done
for so much money emptied in vain? Here my word hastens toward the faithful
among us, who to Christ in poverty and lacking necessary food are not willing
to stretch out even the least; and as much as those men give to harlots and
mimes and the dancers for one voice, these men do not give for a kingdom
without end. But let us come to another form of vainglory; what then is this?
That which belongs to the many and no longer to one and a second; we rejoice
when we are praised even in matters in which we are conscious of nothing in
ourselves, not even a small thing. And the poor man does everything so as to
put on fine garments, for no other reason but so as to be glorified by the
many; and often, though able to serve himself, he has bought a household slave
not for need, but so as not to seem dishonored in serving himself. Since for
whose sake, tell me, having been served all the time by your own hands, do you
now wish to be served by another's? Then, if more gold is added, he acquires
also silver vessels and a splendid house. None of these things is for need;
for if these things were coming about for need, the greater part of the race
of men would have perished and been destroyed. What I mean is this: there are
necessary things and things without which it is not possible to live, as the
produce of the earth is a necessary thing, and if this does not bear fruit it
is not possible to live; the coverings of garments, a roof and walls and
shoes, these belong to the necessary things, but the


rest are all superfluous. Since if those too were necessary and it was not
possible for a man to live without a servant, just as it is not possible to
live without those, the greater part of men would have perished, since the
greater part do not have those who serve them. If it were necessary to use
silver vessels and it was not possible to live without these, the greater part
of men would again have been destroyed, since there is not even silver among
the many. If then someone should say to those who possess the silver
themselves: "What in the world does this vessel mean for you? Tell the cause
and what use it furnishes," he <would> have no other to say, but the honor
from the many. "And I possess it so as to be admired and not to be despised,
but I hide it again, so as not to be envied and despitefully used." What could
become worse than this unreason? If you have it for the honor from the many,
display it to all; but if you fear the envy, it is not even good to possess it
at all. Shall I say another unreason also? Often some, having deprived
themselves of the necessary things and being destroyed by hunger, do not
neglect these vessels. And if you ask them, "I ought," he says, "to have my
figure." What figure, O man? This is not the figure of a man. Since then the
righteous Elijah and Elisha and John were behaving with a great unseemliness;
since for the one there was nothing more than the sheepskin and he had need of
a widow woman, and her a poor one, and he lived a beggar's life, coming to the
doorway of that poor woman and uttering the words of beggars. And Elisha
himself then was behaving unseemly, being feasted from the poor woman. And
John too was behaving unseemly, having not even a garment, not even one loaf.
There is one unseemliness only, to possess many things, and it is truly a
great unseemliness. For one takes a reputation of cruelty, of softness, of
sloth and conceit, of vainglory, of beastliness. It is not figure to wear fine
garments, but figure is to wear fine actions. And I hear many being admired
for this. "So-and-so," he says, "has his figure; the bed is spread and he has
many bronze vessels; he is a householder man." "And why do you accuse us," he
says, "who possess these things, when you ought to accuse those who have
more?" Through you I accuse those far more; for if I do not let those who have
the few things go without blame, much more those who have possessed the more.
Figure is not splendor of a house, nor costliness of coverlets, nor a spread
pallet, nor an adorned bed, nor a multitude of household slaves. For all these
things are outside us and nothing toward us. But the things toward us are
gentleness, contempt of money, contempt of glory, laughing at the honor from
the many, counting human things as nothing, embracing poverty, overpassing
nature by the virtue of life; this is seemliness, this glory, this honor. But
the cause of all the evils from the beginning becomes this, and how, I say.
The little child has been born at once; the father devises everything not how
he might order his life, but how he might adorn him and wrap him in gold and
garments. Why in the world do you do this, O man? Be it so, you yourself wear
these things; why also the little child, who has not yet taken experience of
this madness, do you train in these things? For whose sake do you put
adornment about the neck? There is need of an exact pedagogue, so as to order
the boy, but of gold no longer. And you also leave hair behind into the figure
of a girl, at once making the boy womanish and softening the firmness of
nature, putting into him from the beginning a superfluous love of money and
persuading him to be fluttered about the unprofitable things. Why do you
prepare for him a greater plot? Why do you make him fluttered about the bodily
things? "A man," it says, "if he wears long hair, it is a dishonor to him."
Nature does not wish it; God has not permitted this; the thing has been
forbidden; it is a work of Hellenic superstition. And many even hang gold from
the ears; would that not even girls enjoyed these things, but you even upon
the


males bring the ruin. Perhaps many laugh at what is being said, as if these
things were small. They are not small, but even exceedingly great. A girl
trained in her mother's chamber to be fluttered toward womanish adornment,
when she has gone out from her father's house, will be hard and grievous to
the bridegroom and more burdensome than those who demand the taxes. I have
said already also to you that from there the vice becomes hard to pull out,
that no one takes thought for the children, that no one speaks with them about
virginity, no one about temperance, no one about contempt of money and glory,
no one of these things commanded in the Scriptures. When then from the first
age the children lack teachers, what will they be? For if some who are
nourished from the womb and trained until old age still do not succeed, those
who from the prefaces of their life are accustomed to these hearings, what
terrible thing would they not work? But now, how he might train his own
children in arts and letters and speeches, each one makes all haste; but how
the soul might be exercised, of this no one any longer has any account. I do
not cease exhorting you and begging and entreating, so that before all other
things you order your children first. For if you spare the child, show it from
this; and otherwise you also have a reward. For hear Paul saying: "... if they
continue in faith and in love and in sanctification with temperance." And if
you are conscious of ten thousand evils in yourself, yet nonetheless invent
some consolation for your evils. Rear an athlete for Christ. I do not say
this, withdraw him from marriage and send him into the deserts and prepare him
to choose the life of the monks; I do not say this. I wish this and I would
pray that all accept it, but since it seems to be burdensome, I do not compel.
Rear an athlete for Christ and, while he is in the world, teach him to be
devout from the first age. If the good teachings are stamped while the soul is
still tender, no one will be able to take them out, when it has become hard
like a stamp, just as also wax. You have him still trembling and fearing and
dreading both a look and a word and anything whatever; use the beginning as
needed. You first enjoy the good things, if you have a fair son, and then God;
you labor for yourself. Pearls are said, when they are taken at once, to be
water. If then the one receiving is skilled, having set that drop on the hand
and having moved the hand, receiving with the hand upturned on the palm and
turning it round, he rounds it off to exactness and makes it exceedingly
circular. But when it happens to have been shaped, he is no longer master to
reshape it. For the tender is fit for everything, not yet having its own habit
solidified; wherefore it is easily drawn toward all things; but the hard, as
having taken on some condition, hardness, does not easily stand off from it,
nor is it moved over to another condition. Let each then of you, of the
fathers and of the mothers, just as we see the painters exercising the images
and the statues with much exactness, so take care of these wonderful statues.
For the painters, having set forth the tablet, each day smear it toward what
is needed. And the stone-cutters of the stones themselves also do the same,
taking away the superfluous and adding what is lacking. So then you also; as
some makers of statues, have all your leisure toward this, constructing the
wonderful statues for God; and take out the superfluous, and add what is
lacking; and each day look round them, what advantage it has from nature, so
as to increase it, what defect from nature, so as to take it away. And with
much exactness first banish from them the account of incontinence; for this
love most of all troubles the souls of the young. Rather, before he comes into
the experience of this


teach him to be sober, to be watchful, to keep vigil unto prayers, in
everything both speaking and doing to put on the seal. Think that you are a
king having as a subject city the soul of the child; for the soul is truly a
city. And just as in the city some steal, some do just deeds, some work, some
simply do all things as it happens, so then also in the soul there is thought
and reasonings; some campaign against those who do injustice, as in a city are
the soldiers; some take thought for the whole, both body and house, as are
those who take part in public affairs in the cities; some command, as are the
rulers; and some recount wanton things, as are the incontinent; some solemn
things, as are the temperate; and some have been made womanish, as are the
women among us; some converse more mindlessly, as the children; and some
command as slaves, which the household slaves are; some are well-born, which
the free are. We need then laws, so as to banish the wicked and to enroll the
good and not to let the wicked rise up against the good. For just as in a city
if someone sets laws that give much license to the thieves, he has overturned
the whole; and if the soldiers do not use anger as needed, they have ruined
the whole; and if each, having left his own rank, pursues that of another, he
has destroyed good order by greed; so then also here. The soul of the child
then is a city, a city just now founded and constructed, a city having foreign
citizens, not yet experienced in anything. And such as these it is especially
easy to order. For those who have been reared with a bad constitution, of
which sort old men are, would change with difficulty, yet not impossibly; for
it is possible for those too to be turned, if they wish; but those
inexperienced of everything would easily receive the laws from you. Set then
laws for this city and for those who take part in public affairs in the city,
fearful and vehement, and become a protector of those that are transgressed;
for it is no use to set laws, if the vengeance does not also follow. Set then
laws and attend exactly; for the legislation is for us on behalf of the
inhabited world and we are founding a city today. Let then the walls and gates
be <the> four senses; let all the rest of the body be as a wall, and let the
gates for it be the eyes, the tongue, the hearing, the smell, if you wish also
the touch; for through these gates both enter and go out the citizens of this
city; that is, the reasonings through these gates both are corrupted and
succeed. Come then, first let us come to the gate from the tongue, since this
is the one that most of all transacts business, and before all other things
let us construct for it first doors and bars not from wood nor from iron, but
from gold. For truly golden is the city that is thus constructed. For not some
man, but the king of all things himself is going to dwell in this city, if it
is constructed. And as the word proceeds you will see where we also assign the
palaces to him. Let us construct then for it doors and bars from gold, the
oracles of God, as the prophet says: "The oracles of God are above honey and
honeycomb to my mouth; above gold and much precious stone." Let us teach these
things to be turned about on the lips through everything, and on the walks,
not simply nor as a side-work nor rarely, but continually. Not scales of gold
only must lie upon the doors, but they must have been constructed wholly
throughout from gold, thick and solid, and have precious stones instead of
stones fastened on from outside. And let the bolt of these doors be the cross
of the Lord, constructed wholly throughout from precious stones and thrown
aslant through the midst of the doors. And when we have constructed the doors
thus thick and golden and have set on the


bolt, let us construct the citizens also as worthy; of what sort then these?
Training the little child to utter solemn and pious words. And let us make a
great expulsion of foreigners, so that some mixed and corrupting men may not
enter upon these citizens; the insolent and railing words, the mindless, the
shameful, the worldly, the secular, let us drive all out. And let no one walk
through these gates, but the king only. And let this gate be opened for him
and for all his, that it may be said also about it: "This is the gate of the
Lord, the righteous shall enter in it." And according to the blessed Paul: "If
any good word for edification, that it may give grace to those who hear." Let
the words be thanksgiving, solemn hymns; let them converse always about God,
about the philosophy above. How then will this be? And from where shall we
train them? If we are vehement judges of what happens; for for the child the
ease is great. How? He does not fight over money, not over glory; he is still
a little child; not over a woman, not over children, not over a house. So what
cause of insolence and blasphemy would he have? All his contest is toward
those of the same age. Set a law at once, to insult no one, to blaspheme no
one, not to swear, to be without fighting. And if you see the law being
transgressed, punish, sometimes with a vehement look, sometimes with words
able to bite, sometimes with reproaches; and sometimes flatter and promise.
But blows not continually, nor accustom him to be trained thus; for if he
learns to be trained continually, he will learn also to despise; and having
learned to despise he has overturned all things. But let him always fear
blows, yet not receive them; and let the strap be shaken, yet not be brought
down. And let threats go forward not into deed; and let this not be clear,
that the words are as far as threats; for a threat is then a good, when it is
believed that it will come into deed; since if the one who has sinned learns
the management, he will despise. But let him expect to be trained, yet not be
trained, that the fear may not be quenched, but that it may remain as a fire
in full force and drawing up all the thorns from everywhere, or as a sharp and
deep mattock digging into the very depth. When however you see him having
gained from the fear, relax; for our nature needs also some relaxation. Teach
him to be gentle and humane. And if you see an attendant being insulted, do
not overlook it, but punish the free one. For he who knows that it will not
even be permitted to insult his own household slave will much more not
blaspheme nor rail at the free and equal in honor. Stitch up the mouth from
vice. If you see him slandering someone, stop his mouth and transfer the
tongue against his own sins. These things advise also the mother to speak with
the child and the pedagogue and the attendant, so that all together may be
guards and watch that none of those wicked reasonings leap forth from the
little child and from that mouth and from the golden doors. And do not think
that the thing needs much time. If at the beginning you apply yourself
vehemently and threaten and set so many guards, two months suffice, and the
whole has succeeded and the thing is established into a firmness of nature.
This gate then would thus become worthy of the Lord, when it utters neither
shamefulness, nor jesting, nor anything mindless, nor anything else, but all
the things that befit the Master. For if those who train their own children
for the bodily campaign at once teach those who campaign both to shoot and to
put on a cloak and to mount a horse, and the age is no hindrance, much more
those who campaign above must put on all this royal adornment.


Let him learn then to sing to God, that he may not have leisure for shameful
songs and untimely stories. And let this gate thus be made secure and let
those citizens be enrolled; but the others let us put to death within, as the
bees the drones, not letting them go forward outside nor buzz. Let us go then
also upon another gate; what then is this? The one lying near it and having
much kinship toward it, I mean the hearing. For that one has the citizens
going out from within, and no one enters through that, but this has them
entering from without, and no one goes out through it. This then has much
kinship toward that; for if it does not allow any of the corrupters and the
corrupted to set foot upon its thresholds, it does not furnish much difficulty
to the mouth; for he who does not hear shameful things nor wicked things does
not utter shameful things either; so that if this is spread open to all, it
will ruin that one and will make a tumult for all those within. And perhaps it
was necessary to say all things first about this one and to block the entrance
first. Let the children then hear nothing out of place, neither from household
slaves nor from a pedagogue nor from nurses. But just as plants then most of
all have need of much care, when they are tender, so also the children; so let
us take thought for good nurses, that a fair foundation may be laid for them
from the base and that they may receive nothing wicked at all from the
beginning. Let them not then hear even silly and old-womanish myths.
"So-and-so," he says, "kissed so-and-so." "The king's son and the younger
daughter did this." Let them hear none of these things; but let them hear
other things without any circuit with much simplicity. For it is possible also
from children and from attendants; not from all; for let it not even be
permitted for all the household slaves to mix with them, but let them be
manifest, as those approaching a statue are manifest, who help us with the
art. For how is it not out of place, if we were builders and were founding a
house for a ruler, not to let all the household slaves simply approach the
building, but now, founding a city for the heavenly king and citizens, to
entrust the work to all? But as many of the household slaves as are useful to
help; and if there is no one, seek a free man for hire, a virtuous man, and to
him especially entrust the whole, so that he may help with the work. Let them
not then hear such myths. But when he is released from the toils from the
lessons (for the soul loves to spend time in the old stories), speak to him,
leading him away from all play; for you are rearing a philosopher and an
athlete and a citizen of the heavens; speak then to him and narrate that
"There were at the beginning some two children of one father, two brothers."
Then having paused bring on: "And they came out from the same womb. And the
one was older, and the other younger. And the one was a farmer, the older, and
the other a shepherd, the younger. And that one used to lead the flocks out
upon glens and pools." And sweeten the stories, so that there may be some
delight also for the child and his soul may not grow weary. "And the other
used to sow and plant. It seemed then at some time good to these to honor God.
And the shepherd, taking the first things of the flocks, offered them to God."
Is it not much better to narrate these things instead of the gold-fleeced
sheep and that marvel-talk? Then stir him also (for the narration too has
something) bringing on nothing false, but the things from the Scripture. "For
when he offered the first things to God, at once fire was brought down from
heaven and snatched all things up to the altar above. The older however did

gracious; for nothing so makes mindless as these passions. The fear of God
suffices toward wisdom and to have a judgment about human affairs, of the sort
one also ought to have. For the crown of wisdom is this, not to be fluttered
toward the


childish things. Let him be trained to count money as nothing, human glory as
nothing, power as nothing, death as nothing, the present life as nothing; thus
he will be prudent. If with such exercise we lead him upon the bridal
chambers, think how great the gift will be for the bride. And let us make the
marriages not with pipes, not with a lyre nor with dances; for such a
bridegroom to be shamed with such things is of much absurdity. But let us call
Christ there; for the bridegroom is thereafter worthy of him; let us call his
disciples. For that one all the fairest things will be. And he himself
thereafter will learn thus to order his own children, and those theirs, and
thus there will be a golden chain. And let us make him also take hold of
political affairs according to power, of those that do not have sins. For
whether he campaigns, let him learn not to gain shamefully; or whether he
advocates for those who are wronged, or anything of such a sort. And let the
mother also learn to train her own girl in these things and to lead her away
from costliness and adornment and all the other things, which belong to harlot
women. According to this law then let her do all things and lead away from
luxury and drunkenness, both the young man and the girl. For this too is great
toward temperance; for desire troubles the young men, and the women the love
of adornment and the lifted-up. And let us then settle all these things, and
thus we shall be able to please God rearing such athletes, that both we and
the children may be able to obtain the good things promised to those who love
him, by the grace and love of man of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom to the
Father together with the Holy Spirit be glory, power, honor, now and always
and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
