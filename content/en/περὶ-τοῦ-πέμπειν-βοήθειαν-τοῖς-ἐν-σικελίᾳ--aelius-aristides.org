To accuse Nicias, men of Athens, and to say that he neither from the beginning
wished to sail from here, and now does one of two things, either he makes the
difficulties more and greater by his speech, or if these things are especially
so, he himself is altogether responsible: this I leave to another. For it is
neither in the nature of a temperate man readily to wish to accuse even any of
the citizens whatsoever, and to Nicias a greater share toward goodwill from
all of us is owed from what exists, if we do what is just. And so that you may
know, men of Athens, how far I stand from rivalry or from slandering the man,
I shall make the beginning from this. For there being two things about which
you deliberate, one and first whether one must send for the army, or send aid,
and the other of relieving Nicias of the command or not, if sending prevails,
I say that Nicias must command in addition to whatever you now choose, and
that nothing must be done without him. And that it is not possible to call
those from there, and that it is necessary to send those who will aid, hear
these things. First then, men of Athens, it belongs to the counsel to let go
the present despondency, reckoning that for none of our affairs is the
situation there of this kind. For neither have we been worsted by the enemy in
preparations, nor would we say, I suppose, that we are inferior in natures,
but for these reasons the affairs would long ago have had an end, and it would
have been no worse than anyone would have prayed. But that neither the attempt
became swift, nor did the generals sail at once against

Syracuse itself, and that newer things happened in the camp, Alcibiades having
departed, Lamachus having died, and Gylippus having come on, these are what
have produced the waste of time. For I fear nothing more, be it said with the
gods. For where would this counterwall that now troubles us have been built,
if the aid from Lacedaemon had not come on? Or when would the Syracusans have
had, not to say this aid, but even any leisure to send for help, if someone
had either pressed from the beginning then, or if those assigned had prevented
it when it was arriving, if they had perceived beforehand and had not yielded
something to our men, on equal terms with those in Sicily? But as it is, the
slowness of one man and of two, and the fortune of a moment, have made us
deliberate about things ready at hand; since in the whole Nicias himself also
testifies by how much we have become superior.

Why then do I say these things now? Not for accusation of Nicias, nor because
the affairs are worse than they are, whether they have come here in this way
or another, but because it differs very greatly at least toward the future.
For if the enemy altogether surpassed us, I myself would have hesitated; but
since in all things and everywhere we have beaten them, doing well, these
things in part at least can surely for the rest both be guarded against and
set right. And let us consider that there are many of the affairs there that
do not allow us to withdraw. First, men of Athens, and most common for all to
see, that one must not undo what has been decided, nor like children easily
desiring and readily abandoning. For perhaps even among these Megarians and
Boeotians and all who inhabit small or greater cities it is strong that one
must abide by what has been resolved, and that the end of all disputed things
is the vote; yet to none will this appear more fitting than to you. For by as
much as you yourselves are the wisest of the Greeks, both best on your own to
calculate what is needed and to judge when another speaks, by so much more of
the shame is it necessary to inherit, if you should appear dissolving any of
what you have resolved. For one of two things must be: either when first
voting you seem to be outside of sense, or you err in the second. And indeed
not even

that at least can be said, that few speeches about these things arose among us
from the beginning, or that we entrusted the whole to a small part of one day.
But that even at the beginning still of the war you voted both that the
Syracusans be enemies and to aid those of the Greeks there who asked, and the
expeditions which you sent for a trial of the whole, I will let pass; but when
we were intending to send this great armament, what speech was not spoken
among us? Or what time was not spent? Or to whom of all did we not grant to
speak? You who even at the last, making an assembly about the preparation
itself, when Nicias came forward and again argued almost from the beginning
the same things which even now are in the letters, that Sicily is large and
neither easy to take nor to hold, if we should acquire it, * * it is possible
to say both, men of Athens; for we neither made any anger whatsoever, so as to
prevent saying what seemed good, and having heard through all we still better
resolved that it was necessary to sail. Reasonably, men of Athens. For far
more are all such speeches of those who urge and stir than of those who
hinder, as far as possible. For both the size of the island and the
circumnavigation are a prize worthy of the venture, and that those who hold it
are mixed and do not think the same is on our side. For if they were all one
race they would have been hard both to persuade and to force through to the
end, but being a mixed crowd and gathered from everywhere they have the rank
of allies for us no less than of enemies. For they would not become one, so
that it is possible to have the ones against the others. For just as I think a
city in faction is easier to bring over, so also an island which from the
beginning is divided. Still, men of Athens, before our fleet put in to Sicily,
the Syracusans had some resource from their allies, but with a refuge present
those who formerly of necessity listened to them become unsafe, if they should
remain, as partners of their affairs, and hard enemies if they should change
sides;

and the war has stirred these things well. Now then toward their sudden
prosperity and the seeming to have recovered beyond hope they compose
themselves, drawn in. But if again another armament should appear, in a little
you will find, men of Athens, the large and populous Sicily yours.

To persuade you from the beginning to sail perhaps needed speech and more
examination; but now looking to the affairs themselves one would find you
everywhere both to have deliberated things advantageous and not greater than
you are able to accomplish and not impossible to be done. For of the sea we
have gained as much mastery as was most possible, and by land no one would be
able to say which battle we have not won by much, of so many of all that have
happened up to this. And yet how is it not strange if because we have won so
many in succession we shall think we must not take heart, but because the one
battle now did not go according to mind, we shall set this down as
inescapable? And the Syracusans know how to take heart having so many and
continuous misfortunes, but we, for whom so many trophies from them stand,
shall not do this same thing? Nor from the very things in which we seem to
have stumbled shall we know that it is not good to yield easily? Come, by
Zeus, if someone speaking among the Syracusans, when they were failing in all
things in succession, that they must end the war and surrender the city to us,
then persuaded them, is there any way Gylippus would first have come to them,
or by Zeus having come would have had anything to do? And how would he? In no
way. But as it is I think, having stretched their hopes and always becoming
superior in their opinions to the difficulties at their feet, they have at
least gained this much, that the argument already seems to them to be about
equal things. One must not then fear that something of all things has gone
according to their mind more than, having made this itself a pattern, one must
aid the affairs vigorously, so that we should neither appear worse than they
in hoping, and that though all men have supposed us not only strongest to
accomplish what we resolve, but also to differ most in dangers, nor should we
destroy at random those things which have become gains for us from the time
past. And indeed when with Nicias

leading, being most opposed to the expedition, and times having been spent, we
have nevertheless used so many advantages in the affairs, it is quite clear
that from the beginning at least we erred in nothing of what we voted. But if
again, with nothing so great having been advanced for us, the trial had an

and the country outside being under us, another armament putting in will
easily finish all the rest. For do not think that the Syracusans took heart
again when Gylippus came with two triremes, but that our soldiers, when you
send as great a force as is likely, will not have far greater spirits, for
whom together with the sight of their own people the consciousness of the
multitude of what they accomplished on their own is already a great weight
toward good cheer.

And indeed, men of Athens, to vote down the voyage from the beginning belonged
to those using judgment, but not only having voted, but also having sent so
great a force and having become conspicuous to all for what we intended, then
to call them back, will be a sign of two things to all, of ill counsel and of
cowardice: of ill counsel, if we knew none of these things from the beginning,
of cowardice if we shall seem to flee the Syracusans. And yet no gain of all
things is surely so precious that for its sake one should undergo so great a
shame, and that being Athenians, whose business is not to consider if we shall
suffer something into more account, but by doing what we would be in a fair
figure. Nor indeed would anyone say this at least, that we shall gain
anything, now undergoing these and such reproaches. For it is necessary, I
suppose, that those plotted against have been stirred no less hostilely,
whether we finish the attempt or not, and to the enmity we add the seeming to
be worse. Therefore more shame, not gain, comes of fleeing; not if at least
you are persuaded by me. But since the enmity is necessary, we shall honor it
with some worthy deed, and we shall know that by going through with what we
have resolved we rid ourselves of enemies, but by running away from the
affairs we leave enemies for ourselves. Thus choosing what is fitting we also
acquire the benefit with it, but with the

shameful we draw the harm after us. And let none of you consider that, the
affairs not being similar, we first sent then and now we shall bring them
back, if these things prevail. For not even then were we sending trusting in
the truce with the Lacedaemonians. And a great sign of this, men of Athens:
for still with the war standing and much before the truce we put our hands to
the affairs. But what then was it that made us eager for the expedition? That
which, men of Athens, is both most just and which everyone well-disposed to
you joined in praying, the taking heart in what we had and the thinking that
this addition belongs to the rule of the sea, which was going both to increase
our things by no small amount and to make many difficulties for the
Peloponnese. This then must not be considered, whether we were then at peace,
but whether it is advantageous to the affairs of the war that things there be
through us, and that if the very first things must be sought, these seemed
good to you at the height of the war.

And indeed it is not worth setting either the fortification or the desertions
in the way. For if we were going, taking those of us who are in Sicily, to
engage the Peloponnesians by land, this would be something; but if it is
necessary to remain inside the wall, even if we send for twice as many, I do
not see the gain that belongs to the summons, unless this too is of all things
most contrary to the siege which some say we are undergoing, that all gathered
in the same place be idle. And I for my part think that not even the household
slaves now run away because they look down on our numbers, but in part
trusting from the beginning that they will escape notice, and in part thinking
that if they should once be among the enemy they will suffer nothing, and
altogether using the nature of slaves, which just as by the name is contrary
to the masters, so also in their wishes. For instead of the good they receive
they hate and wish to take justice for things for which it was reasonable to
pay thanks. And this is not to be altogether well-disposed to the masters. I
then find the expedition more suitable even against the desertions themselves.
For seeing

us ourselves running away, they will attack having despised us, but if they
perceive us taking heart, perhaps, if it should happen, they would become
better than their nature. And further those of them whom we convey in the
ships will not have there to do what they now do here, but just as they now go
off to the enemy who control the country, so there at least, we again
controlling the country and the enemy having suffered this same thing which we
have suffered here, they will not have how to change sides, but from
likelihood those from them will rather come to us. And apart from these things
one must not consider if we shall endure something of all things greater or
less, but if we should hold out in all these things for great ones, we shall
also receive the wage from the affairs. For it is far better, I think, to
dissolve the present difficulties by later good things than both not yet to be
able to do these and voluntarily to withdraw from those. And altogether the
carrying of the affairs out toward Sicily agrees with the whole figure of the
war. For if it had been resolved for us to wage war against the Lacedaemonians
in this manner which they challenged, there would be another argument; but as
it is what seemed good to us from the beginning? To let them invade, and
ourselves to use the ships, and not to care more though the land is being
ravaged, having much other, but to press upon the Peloponnese, as not fighting
those before our feet is what will save the affairs, but harming their forces
outside.

Do not then think that what is accomplished in Sicily is any less advantageous
to the whole than sailing about the Peloponnese, nor that you would otherwise
better lead those from Decelea away than if we should take from them their
resources from outside. For if they will have neither to send into Sicily nor
to receive anything from those there, and all these things will again be set
in your hand, there is no way they would be better brought down nor how they
would more be at a loss what to do. And indeed if we make the Hellespont of
more account than Attica for the sake of the grain-convoy, by how much is
Sicily reasonably of more account than the Hellespont? For the one has for us
the

escort of the ships from the Pontus, but the other becomes instead of both
these places; for it has the safe conveyance of those from all Sicily as far
as the Piraeus. And to those who are heavy at the affairs here and at Decelea
having been fortified I would first say this, that it is of all things most
strange to yield the whole of Attica, but to be indignant over the wall of
Decelea, as if those from the wall were not having more trouble from us. Next
let us consider, and may the blasphemy turn upon others, whether now the
affairs of the city are worse and worthy of more fears, or at that time when
all the Peloponnesians themselves and the allies came and were ravaging the
country, and the plague was pressing, making peace toward the enemy seem to
be, and all things were full of smoke, burning, destruction both outside and
inside throughout the city. For I find not even in the whole war so many of us
dead, and may it not happen another time, Zeus savior, as this disease
destroyed. If then at that time having lost heart we had made an end, as some
were persuading us, is there any way any of the many and fair deeds after
these would have been done? It is not possible. But I think having borne the
present things by calculation, and having entrusted the future to the better
of hopes, we beat them in many sea-fights, and at last at Pylos having taken
them alive we brought them here with their arms. Such things war is willing to
grant to those who are willing to toil and endure hardship. And these things
not only we who are now have so resolved, but there is also the example of the
fathers, very great, who gave up both children and wives and the city itself
and the country, thinking nothing more fearful than yielding contrary to
worth. But if either hearing of Athos being altered or the Hellespont they had
at once been struck as in a tragedy, or even after these things when they won
the victories at Artemisium, being forced to flee into the inside of Greece
through the multitude of the ships coming on, they had considered that they
toil without end and the war will surround them, and much more when they
already saw all

Attica full of enemies and fire, if they had not borne all these things both
with their eyes and with their mind nobly and beyond human nature, if it is
possible to say, where would so many trophies be, where the hegemony of the
Greeks, where even the Greeks themselves being? Which of these things would
there have been, without which, to speak as of things done, it is not fair to
live? One must then using the examples that exist not be worse toward the
future, knowing that ease and what is according to pleasure come from the
toils and the greatest of deeds await the best. And some also speak about the
Egestaeans, that they lied in what they said about the money. But I, if we
made this expedition for the sake of the Egestaeans, agree that one must
accept the speeches against them; but if the Egestaeans and Leontines
furnished the figure of the expedition, but the mixing-bowls were of those
wishing something else and the contest as far as Aegina, why do we talk this
nonsense? For not if the Egestaeans have become paltry toward us is it fitting
that we too follow, nor because they lied of necessity should we too on
purpose vote contrary to ourselves; nor since we missed in the things at
Egesta, should we also of the many other resources, nothing like what was
hoped from them, be willingly deprived. And this much I still wish to say: if
by the money from the Egestaeans, men of Athens, and the hopes which they set
before us, if trusting in these that there would be a sufficient resource for
the war we were raised to campaign, let us withdraw from Sicily, let us call
those from there, let every speech be useless which will not agree with these
things. But if they, being barbarians, promised besides in order to seem to be
somebody, and our strength was from elsewhere, [the remaining argument is
this].

And let us recall some deed of our own, bearing on the present things. For
there was once a time, men of Athens, when some were treating us badly with
the King's three hundred triremes and still more, and others were sitting
against Aegina, and the Corinthians having been raised came to

Megara, as either they would move those at Aegina, or they would take the
Megarians empty of helpers. But we found a third expedition, of which
Myronides was leader; and these were the oldest and the youngest in the city,
and engaging the Corinthians we prevailed before Megara; and again when they

those from Sicily here having been frightened; for not those at the prytaneum

Gylippus collecting an army from Sicily, but who having gone as counter-envoys

and one from wherever it happens, these things to consider, and to those
speeches to say farewell, that not even if we should take Sicily would we
easily guard it. For I, men of Athens, would wish Sicily already so to be, as
I suppose it most suitable of all to possess securely at least. And let us
look to the example of the King, who holds Cyprus, being so far not only from
Cyprus but even from the sea altogether, and that through the Phoenicians'
navy; and besides even the Phoenicians themselves who have the ships, himself
having no domestic one, so to speak. And I let pass speaking of Cilicians and
this whole circle. What then is the cause, men of Athens, that even if someone
is not strong with like things, but prevails by the whole power, he enslaves
all and holds them easily, using fear as a resource? Upon these speeches at
least, men of Athens, no great empire would ever have been constituted, if
some frightened those attempting, that not even if it should go forward would
they hold it. But as it is here indeed one might most fairly see

how divine a thing empire is; for it saves itself. For just as in private
houses it is hard to use one and two household slaves, but the many are there
against one another, so also in dominions the multitude of those enslaved
confirms the strength for those who have taken it first; for all in a circle
fear one another. So then also Sicily added to us who are here will both
itself be saved through these and will make things here exact and will have
been set, if one must speak the truth, as a bond of the empire. For many men
of every race having been subdued will not have a refuge, but every evil not
further than the present they will think as a windfall, having given up on the
whole. But here already we would be of the fears; yet I see others nearer than
these, if we should move the fleet, men of Athens, now from Sicily. And I need
not learn from the oracles of Bacis nor of Amphilytus, but myself knowing well
I foretell to you that the Syracusans and Selinuntines and all the enemies
from there will come here, if we call them. For we call these no less than our
own, if we vote what some bid. For it will not suffice the Syracusans, if at
least they have suffered nothing, but this they will reckon to fortune and to
the Lacedaemonians who took it away, and they will judge us enemies likewise
as if we had brought them over, but that it is possible now only to defend
themselves.

And yet it is wonderful if someone, men of Athens, judges our triremes being
in the harbor of the Syracusans fearful for the city, but the Syracusans' and
their allies' sailing down into the Piraeus, this he expects as if a salvation
for the affairs. For if there is some surety for us that those men will keep
quiet, and will not wish to return the same favor to the Lacedaemonians which
these now have begun toward them, let us think it is in our power whichever of
these we wish. But if we shall not so flee them as not wherever they may
attack us, there of necessity to have to defend ourselves, why do we fill
Attica with confusion having neglected Sicily? And why do we transfer the war
here, when it is possible to hold those men, so that neither willingly nor
unwillingly they go forward from their own? Or is it hard that we deliberate
about them, but that

either they will not move at all, or they will have nothing more.
