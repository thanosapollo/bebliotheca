OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA TO HIERIUS CONCERNING THE INFANTS SNATCHED AWAY
BEFORE THEIR TIME

To you, O best of men, all sophists and speech-writers will altogether display
the power of speaking, running as in some stadium through the breadth of your
wonders; for somehow a noble and ample subject set before those who are able
knows how to make the discourse more grandiloquent, about which the discourse
is lifted, raised together with the greatness of the facts. But we, like the
aged among horses, remaining outside the stadium of the subject, will stir
only the ear at the contests of discourses about you, if perhaps some sound
should reach even as far as us, driving the discourse through your wonders
with a vehement and strained leap. Since it happens that, even if the horse
remains outside the contests by reason of old age, often, being awakened to
eagerness by the noise of those who stamp, he both holds up his head and looks
as if in contest and breathes spiritedly and jointly moves his feet,
frequently striking the hooves against the ground, in whom there is eagerness
alone toward the contests, but the =68= power of running has already been
spent by time: in the same way our discourse also, remaining out of the
contest because of old age and yielding the stadium to those in their prime in
education, shows you only the eagerness of wishing that it might compete even
about you, if it were young like those now flourishing in discourses. And as
much as the display of my eagerness is, it is not in narrating something of
yours. For of this even the vigorous and strained discourse would scarcely
succeed, so as not to be left far behind the worth, the one interpreting this
unmanageable harmony of character somehow mixed together from opposites. For
just as by the projections of the eyelids nature, shading the unmixed
[quality] of the rays, brings mixed light to the eyes, so that the sun may
become gentle, being blended in due measure with the need toward the shade
from the eyelashes: so the august and high-natured quality of the character,
being mixed with measured humility, does not turn away the gazes of those who
look, but prepares them to look with pleasure, so that neither the gleam of
dignity is dimmed nor the inward disposition is despised through lowliness,
but in each the other is contemplated equally, both the commonness in the
lofty and conversely the dignity in the humble. Let another go through these
things and hymn the many-eyed quality of the soul, how the eyes of the soul
are perhaps equal in number to the hairs of the head, looking from all sides
equally sharp and unerring, so as both to foresee from afar and not to be
ignorant from what is =69= near, nor to wait for experience as teacher of what
is profitable, but to foresee one thing with the eyes of hopes, to contemplate
another through memory, and another according to the present to look around in
a circle, and to work all at once without confusion, distributing the mind
correspondingly to all such activities. And let that one again admire the
august wealth of poverty, if there is anyone in the life among us who knows
how to make such a thing a matter of praise and wonder. And perhaps even if it
was not so before, yet now through you both a desire of poverty will bloom and
your simplicity will be counted blessed before the many-talented bricks of
Croesus. For whom have earth and sea shown so blessed, each greeting with its
own revenues, as your life has been shown by the disposition away from
material abundance? For as those who scrape the rust from iron work it bright
and silver-like, so for you the ray of life has become more shining, always
being cleansed through care of the rust of money. And let these things be left
to those able to speak, and that


you know well in what things taking is more glorious than remaining clean of a
taking; for grant me to speak with frankness, that you do not overlook all
takings, but those which none of those who came before has yet been able to
touch, you alone have grasped with both hands; for instead of some clothing or
money or slaves you have taken the very souls of men and hold them, having
stored them in the treasury of love. =70=

Let speech-writers and sophists go through these things, for whom it is a fine
ornament to write such things, but let our aged discourse stir itself only so
much as to go through at a walk the problem set before us by your wisdom, what
one must know concerning those snatched away before their time, in whom
generation is all but joined to death; which even the wise among those
outside, Plato, having philosophized many things from the person of the one
who had come back to life about the courts there, left unspoken, as being
clearly greater than to come under the reasoning of men. If then there is
something of this sort in what has been examined, so as to dissolve the
ambiguities of the problem, you will clearly accept the account found; but if
not, you will altogether pardon old age, having accepted only our eagerness to
furnish you something of what is pleasing. For even Xerxes, that one who made
almost all under the sun into one camp and who moved the whole inhabited world
together with himself, when he campaigned against the Greeks, the account says
received with pleasure a gift of some poor man. And the guest-gift was water,
and this not carried in a jar, but held in the hollow of the palm of the
hands. So then you too, according to the high nature that is yours, will
altogether imitate that one, for whom the choice became the gift, if our gift
should be found both short and watery. =71=

Just as in the case of the heavenly wonders the appearing beauties are seen
equally whether the one looking up to heaven happens to be educated or a
private person, yet they do not think about them in the same way, both the one
starting from philosophy and the one committing what appears to the senses
alone (for this one either is pleased with the rays of the sun or judges the
beauty of the stars worthy of wonder or has observed the number of the lunar
course upon the month, but the one clear-sighted in soul and purified through
education toward the understanding of the heavenly things, leaving those
things through which the sense of the more irrational is gladdened, looks
toward the harmony of the whole and from the circular motion inspects the
well-fittedness from opposites; how with the unwandering revolution the inner
circles unwind toward the reverse, how the stars contemplated in them are
figured in many forms, in approaches and distances and under-runnings and
eclipses and the side-runnings, always working out that uninterrupted harmony
according to the same things and in the same way; for whom not even the
position of the smallest of the stars is overlooked unobserved, but all things
furnish equal care to those who have moved the mind through wisdom to the
things above): in the same way you too, O head precious to me, looking at the
economy of God in the things that are, having left those things about which
the thought of the many is busy (I mean wealth and conceit and desire of empty
glory, which flashing round like rays are accustomed to strike the more
irrational), you do not leave even the things that seem =72= smaller among
those contemplated in the things that are unexamined, searching out and
considering the irregularity of human life, not only that contemplated
according to wealth and poverty or the differences according to ranks and
families (for you know that these are instead of nothing for those whose being
is not according to their own subsistence, but in the vain supposition of
those who gape at things that are not as if they were existing; if at any rate
someone should take away from the one made splendid by glory the opinion of
those looking toward him, nothing will be left for the one thinking big upon
the empty inflation, even if all the matter of money should happen to be
buried beside him), but it is a care to you


to know both the other things of the divine economy, toward what each of the
things that come to be looks, and for what reason for one life is stretched
out into long old age, while another partakes of living only so much as to
draw the air through breathing and immediately to cease from life. For if
nothing of the things that come to be in the world is without God, and all
things hang from the divine will, and the divine is wise and providential,
there is altogether some account upon these too, bearing the marks of the
wisdom of God together with the providential care; for what comes to be at
random and without reason would not be a work of God; for it is proper to God,
as the scripture says, to Do all things in wisdom. What then is the wise thing
in that? The human being has passed through =73= generation into life, has
drawn the air, has begun living from a wail, has paid the tear as a due to
nature, has made a first-offering to life of laments, before partaking of any
of the pleasant things according to life; before the sense is strengthened,
still loosened in the joints of the limbs, soft and diffused and inarticulate,
and to speak the whole, before becoming a human being (if indeed the rational
grace is proper to a human being, and this one has not yet received the reason
in himself), this one who has nothing more than the one still held in the
maternal womb except having come to be in air, being in this [stage] of age,
is dissolved through death, either exposed or smothered or of himself having
ceased from living through illness: what must one think about him? How must
one be concerning those who have died thus? Will that soul too see the judge?
Will it stand with the others at the tribunal? Will it undergo the judgment of
things lived? Will it receive the requital according to desert, either being
purified by fire according to the voices of the gospel or being refreshed in
the dew of the blessing? =74= But I do not know how one must think these
things concerning such a soul; for the name of requital signifies that
something must altogether have been furnished beforehand, but of the one who
has not lived at all the matter of furnishing something has been taken away
beforehand; and in cases where there is no giving, neither will requital be
named in the proper sense. And if there is no requital, neither is what is set
forward according to hope good nor evil; for this name promises the exchange
of the things thought according to either; and what is found neither in good
nor in evil is altogether in nothing; for the opposition of such a contrast is
immediate, I mean that of the good and the evil, of which neither will belong
to the one who has not begun the other. Therefore what is in nothing one would
not even say exists at all. But if someone should say that such a one both
exists and is among goods, God giving, not paying back, the goods to such,
what cause of this allotment does he name? Where will he show the just
together with the account? And how will he show the thought in agreement with
the gospel voices? There it says that a certain transactional commerce of the
kingdom comes to those counted worthy; for since you have done this and that,
it says, you are just to receive the kingdom in return. But here, when neither
any deed nor choice is present, what occasion is there to say that even for
these there comes to be from God what is hoped from exchange? But if someone
will accept such an account without testing, that the one who has passed thus
into life will altogether be among =75= goods, from this not partaking of life
will appear more blessed than life, if indeed for that one the participation
of the goods is undoubted, even if he happens to be of barbarian parents and
is conceived not in a recognized marriage, while for the one who has lived the
time both possible and lawful for nature the stain of vice is altogether mixed
with life either more or less, or, if he is going to be completely outside of
communion with evil, he needs many sweats toward this very thing and toils;
for virtue is not accomplished without effort for those who pursue it, nor is
alienation from the things according to pleasure without toil for human
beings; so that the one who has partaken of the more lasting life must
altogether be brought together with one of the painful things from both,
either now competing in the toilsome [way] of virtue or then being pained by
the requital of pains because of the life in vice; but in the case of the
untimely there is nothing of this sort, but immediately the good lot awaits
those who have departed in untimeliness, if indeed


the account of those who have supposed thus is true. Therefore from what
follows unreason will be shown more honorable than reason, and virtue will
appear through these things worthy of nothing; for if no loss has come about
toward the participation of the goods for the one who has not partaken of
virtue, it would be vain to toil about this and unprofitable, if the
irrational condition takes precedence in the judgment of God.

You, considering all these things and such, commanded that the account about
this be examined, so that through the consequent =76= inquiry the thought
about it might be settled for us upon some firm notion. But I, looking toward
the hard-to-see quality of the inquiry set before us, think that the voice of
the apostle also fits the present discourse, which he has made upon the
unattainable things, saying O depth of wealth and wisdom and knowledge of God;
how unsearchable are his judgments and untraceable his ways. For who has known
the mind of the Lord? But since again the apostle says it is proper to the
spiritual to examine all things and accepts those enriched by the divine grace
In every word and all knowledge, I say it is well not to neglect the possible
examination nor to overlook the thing sought in these as unsearched and
unobserved, so that the discourse about it may not, in likeness to the subject
of the problem, disappear unfinished and untimely, being destroyed like some
infant of those just born before coming forth into light and being
strengthened, as by a death, by the slackness of those who are weak toward the
search of the truth.

I say then that it is well not to be entangled at once face to face with the
objections rhetorically and agonistically, but having imposed a certain order
on the discourse to lead forward through sequence the contemplation about the
problem. What then is the order? To know first from where human nature [is]
and for what reason it came into generation; for if we do not miss these, we
will not have missed the contemplation set before us either. That then
everything from God, whatever after him is =77= thought and seen in the things
that are, has being, it would be superfluous to establish by discourse, since
no one, I think, of those who have in any way examined the truth of the things
that are stands against such a supposition; for it is agreed by all that the
whole hangs from one cause and that none of the things that are has being from
itself nor is beginning and cause of itself, but that there is one nature
uncreated and eternal, always having itself according to the same things and
in the same way, lying above every interval-thought, some unincreased and
undiminished [nature] and contemplated beyond every limit, whose work is both
time and place and all things in these and if anything before these the
thought grasps, both intelligible and supercosmic. And we say that human
nature also is one of the things that have come to be, using as a guide toward
this a certain word of the God-inspired teaching, which says that when all
things had been brought forth into generation by God the human being also was
shown forth upon the earth, mixed in nature from different kinds, the divine
and noetic substance having been mixed toward the portion collected for him
from each of the elements, and that this one came to be from the one who made
him, in order to be a certain living likeness of the divine and overlying
power. And it would be better also to set beside [us] the very wording having
thus: And God made, it says, the human being; according to the image of God he
made him. And the cause of the construction of this living being =78= some of
those before us have given as this, that the whole creation having been
divided in two, as the apostle says, into the visible and the invisible (and
through the invisible is signified the intelligible and bodiless, and through
the visible the sensible and bodily); all the things that are having been
divided then into these two (into the sensible, I mean, and into what is
contemplated according to thought) and the angelic and bodiless nature, which
is of the invisibles, dwelling in the supercosmic and superheavenly because
the dwelling is corresponding to the nature (for the noetic nature is some
fine and pure and weightless and easily moved [nature] and the heavenly


body is fine and light and ever-moved), while the earth, which is the last of
the sensibles, does not have [it] properly and correspondingly toward the
sojourn of the intelligibles in it (for what communion would there be of the
upward-borne and light toward the heavy and weighty?), so that the earth might
not be completely without share and without allotment of the noetic and
bodiless sojourn, for this reason by a better foresight the human generation
was constituted, the earthly portion having been molded around the noetic and
divine substance of the soul, so that by the connaturality toward the weighty
and bodily the soul might dwell in the element of earth correspondingly,
having something kindred and of the same stock toward the substance of the
flesh. And the aim of the things that come to be is that in all creation
through the noetic nature the power overlying the whole be glorified, both the
heavenly and the earthly being joined to one another toward the same aim
through the same activity (I mean through looking toward =79= God). And the
activity of looking toward God is nothing other than a life like and
corresponding to the noetic nature. For just as the bodies, being earthly, are
held together by earthly nourishments and we grasp some bodily form of life in
these, working similarly in both irrational and rational [beings]: so one must
also suppose there is some intelligible life, through which the nature is
preserved in that which is. And if the nourishment of the flesh, being some
inflowing and outflowing [thing], through the very passage deposits some vital
power in those in whom it comes to be, how much more does the participation of
that which truly is, of that which always remains and at all times has itself
in the same way, keep the one who has partaken in being. If then this is the
proper and corresponding life for the noetic nature, to partake of God, the
participation would not come about through opposites, unless the thing
partaken of were in some way kindred to the one desiring. For as the enjoyment
of the gleam comes about for the eye by having a natural gleam in itself
toward the reception of the kindred, and neither a finger nor any other of the
members of the body works vision because no gleam has been constructed from
nature in any other of the members, so there is every necessity also in the
case of the participation of God that there be something kindred toward what
is partaken of in the nature of the one enjoying. For this reason the
scripture says that the human being has come to be According to the image of
God, so that, I think, by the like he might look at the like. And =80= to look
at God is the life of the soul, as has been said in what has gone before. But
since somehow the ignorance of the truly good, like some mist, has
overshadowed the clear-seeing [faculty] of the soul, and the mist having been
thickened became a cloud, so that through the depth of the ignorance the ray
of the truth does not pass, necessarily with the separation from the light its
life also failed together; for it has been said that the true life of the soul
is worked in the participation of the good, and since ignorance hinders toward
the divine understanding the soul that does not partake of God fell out of
life.

And let no one compel us to give a genealogy of ignorance, saying from where
this is and from what, but let him understand from the very meaning of the
name that knowledge and ignorance indicate the soul's being somehow toward
something. And none of the things thought and said as relatives presents a
substance; for other is the account of the relative and other that of
substance. If then knowledge is not a substance, but an activity of thought
about something, much more is ignorance agreed to be far from being according
to substance. And what is not according to substance does not even exist at
all. It would then be vain to be curious about that which is not, from where
it is.

Since then the account says that the life of the soul is the participation of
God, and the participation is knowledge according to what is possible, and
ignorance is not an existence of something, but an abolition of the activity
according to knowledge, and by the participation of God not being worked the
alienation from life necessarily followed (and this would be the last of
evils), =81= consequently the maker of every good works in us the healing of
evil; for healing is a good. And the manner of the


healing one who does not look toward the gospel mystery altogether is ignorant
of. Then, alienation from God, who is the life, having been shown to be an
evil, the healing of such an illness was to be made familiar to God again and
to come to be in the life. This life then being set forward according to hope
for human nature, it is not possible to say in the proper sense that the
participation of life comes to be a requital of those who have lived well and
punishment the reverse; but what is said is like the example according to the
eyes; for we do not say that for the one whose sight has been purified the
understanding of things seen is some prize and privilege, or for the one who
is sick the reverse, that not partaking of the seeing activity is some
condemnation and punitive sentence. But as seeing necessarily follows the one
disposed according to nature, and not having vision in activity [follows] the
one carried aside from nature by passion, in the same way the blessed life
also is connatural and proper to those who have been purified in the
sense-organs of the soul, but in those for whom the passion according to
ignorance, like some rheum, becomes a hindrance toward the participation of
the true light, there necessarily follows not partaking of that, the
participation of which we say is the life of the one who partakes.

These things then having been distinguished thus for us, it would be time to
examine thoroughly by the discourse the problem set before us. And what was
said was something of this sort: if the requital of the goods comes about
according to the just, in what will the one who has ended life in infancy be,
having deposited in this life neither =82= something good nor evil, so that
through these the requital according to desert may come about for him? To
which, looking toward the sequence of the things examined, we will answer that
the expected good is proper according to nature to the human race, and the
same is also called requital according to a certain thought. And this notion
will again be made clear by the same example; for let two persons be supposed
for the discourse who have been brought together with some illness according
to the eyes. And of these let the one give himself over more earnestly to the
healing, enduring all things brought by the medical art, even if they are
toilsome; and let the other be more unrestrained toward baths and
wine-drinking, having accepted no counsel of the healer toward the health of
the eyes. We say then, looking toward the end of each, that each worthily
received the fruits of his choice, the one the deprivation of the light, the
other the enjoyment. For we name from catachresis the thing that necessarily
follows a requital. These things it is possible to say also upon those
inquired about according to the infant, that the enjoyment of that life is
proper to human nature, and since the disease according to ignorance prevails
over almost all those living in the flesh, the one who has purified himself by
the due healings and has washed away ignorance like some rheum from the
clear-seeing [faculty] of the soul has the gain worthy of the earnestness,
having come to be in the life according to nature, while the one fleeing the
purifications through virtue and making the disease of ignorance hard to heal
for himself through the deceitful pleasures, having been disposed contrary to
nature, has been alienated from what is according to nature and becomes
without share of the life proper and corresponding to us; but the infant
inexperienced in evil, no disease =83= of the eyes of the soul blocking toward
the participation of the light, comes to be in what is according to nature,
not needing the health from being purified, because he did not even at the
beginning receive the disease into the soul. And it seems to me that the
present manner of life has itself properly through some analogy toward the
expected life. For just as the first age of infants, being nursed with nipple
and milk, is reared, then another nourishment corresponding to the subject
succeeds this, having itself properly and suitably toward the one being
nourished, until it arrives at the complete: so I think the soul also through
the things always corresponding to it in a certain order and sequence partakes
of the life according to nature, partaking as it has room and is able of the
things set forward in blessedness, as we have also learned such a thing from
Paul, nourishing otherwise the one already grown through virtue and otherwise


the infant and more imperfect; for toward such he says that I gave you milk to
drink, not meat; for you were not yet able; but toward those who have filled
the measure of the intelligible age

But solid food is, he says, of the complete, of those who through habit have
the sense-organs exercised. Just as then it is not possible to say that the
man and the infant are among likes, even if no disease attaches to either of
these =84= (for how would they come to be in the equal of luxury who do not
partake of the same things?), but not being harmed by some disease is said
similarly both upon this one and upon that, as long as each is outside of
passion, yet the enjoyment of the things according to luxury is no longer
worked similarly from the same [people] (for to the one it belongs both to be
gladdened through words and to superintend affairs and to pursue offices with
good repute and to be made splendid by benefactions of those in need and to
dwell with a wife, if she happens to be according to his heart, and to rule a
house and whatever pleasant things with these there are to find through life;
both hearings and spectacles, hunts and baths and gymnasia and drinking-party
delights and if there is any other such thing; but for the infant the luxury
is the milk and the nurse's arm and a gentle motion drawing on and sweetening
sleep; for the incompleteness of the age does not have nature to contain the
gladness beyond this): in the same way those who have nourished the souls
through virtue in the life here and, as the apostle says, have exercised their
own intelligible sense-organs, if they should be moved to that bodiless life,
will partake of the divine luxury in proportion to the habit and power
existing in them, partaking of the things set forward either more or less
according to the present power of each; but the soul without taste of virtue
remains =85= without share of the evils from wickedness, inasmuch as it was
not even at the beginning brought together with the disease of vice, and of
that life, which the account before these defined as knowledge and
participation of God, it partakes at first only so much as the one being
nourished has room, until having been strengthened as by some corresponding
nourishment by the contemplation of that which is it becomes capable of the
more, partaking in abundance of that which is according to authority.

Looking toward these things we say that the soul of both the one who has come
through all virtue and of the one who has not partaken of life at all is
similarly outside of the evils from wickedness; yet we do not think the
sojourn of each of these in the like. For the one heard, as the prophet says,
the heavenly narrations, through which the glory of God is proclaimed, and
through the creation was led toward the understanding of the master of the
creation and used as teacher of the true wisdom the wisdom contemplated in the
things that are, and having understood the beauty of this light analogically
he thought the beauty of the true light, and in the firmness of the earth he
was taught the unchangeableness of the one who made it, and having understood
the unmeasured [quality] of the heavenly greatness he was led toward the
infinite and unbounded [quality] of the power encompassing the whole, and
having seen the rays of the sun reaching from such heights as far as us he
believed through the things that appear that the providential activities of
God do not fail to come down toward each of us from the height of the godhead.
For if the luminary, being one, both jointly takes hold of all that is subject
with the lighting power and, distributing himself to all who partake, is
present whole to each and undivided, how much more does the craftsman of the
luminary both Become all in all, as the =86= apostle says, and is present to
each, giving himself so much as the subject receives. But also someone having
seen an ear of grain upon the earth and the sprout from a plant and a ripe
cluster and the beauty of fruit either in fruits or in flowers and the
spontaneous grass and a mountain stretching up to the ethereal height from the
peak and the springs on the foothills, flowing like breasts from the flanks of
the mountain, and rivers flowing through the hollows and a sea receiving the
streams from everywhere and remaining in the measure, and waves bounded by the
shores


and the sea not going out beyond the appointed limits against the mainland:
the one looking at these and such things, how would he not take in all things
by the account, through which the teaching of that which is comes about for
those who speak of God, having prepared for himself no small power toward the
participation of that luxury by the thoughts being with him, through which the
mind is led toward virtue, both geometry and astronomy and the understanding
of the truth through number and every method demonstrative of things unknown
and confirmatory of things grasped and before these the philosophy of the
God-inspired scripture, bringing a complete purification to those educated
through it in the divine mysteries? But the one who has come to be in
knowledge of none of these nor been led by the hand through the world toward
the understanding of the supercosmic goods, having passed through life some
soft and unexercised and unused =87= in thought, that one would not be among
the same [things] in which the account showed the one who went before, so that
for this reason the one who has not partaken of life is no longer shown more
blessed, according to the objection brought forward to us, than the one who
has partaken well. For not only would the one inexperienced in evil be more
blessed than the one who has lived together with vice, but perhaps also the
one who has not even at the beginning passed into life; for such a thing we
have also been taught about Judas through the gospel voice, that upon such it
is better than the one existing according to vice to be completely without
subsistence; for for the one, because of the depth of the vice that has grown
in, the punishment through purification is stretched out into the infinite,
but of the one who is not how would pain take hold? But if someone should
judge the infantile and untimely toward the life according to virtue, truly
untimely is such a one using such a judgment about the things that are.

You ask then for what reason someone being in this [stage] of age is taken out
of life, what the divine wisdom is providing through this. But if you should
speak about those things which become a proof of unlawful conception and for
this reason are made to vanish by those who generated them, you would not
reasonably demand of God the account of the works of vice, him who brings the
things not well done upon this to judgment; but if someone, even when the
parents are rearing and tending through care and being earnest through prayer,
nevertheless does not partake of life, the illness prevailing as far as death
(which alone is of the cause without doubt), these things we conjecture about
=88= such, that it belongs to complete providence not only to heal the
passions that have come to be, but to provide that one may not even at the
beginning come to be among the things forbidden. For the one who has known the
future equally with the past it is likely to hinder the advance of the infant
to the completion of life, so that the evil understood by the prognostic power
upon the one who was going to live thus in the future may not be completed and
life become matter of vice for the one going to live together with such a
choice. And it would be easy to present the thought by some example; for let
there be by hypothesis some feast of every sort set before the drinking-party,
and let some such one preside over the diners as knows accurately the
peculiarities of the nature of each and what is corresponding to such a
temperament, and what is harmful and unfitting; and let there be attached to
this one also some authoritative power of allowing according to his own will
one to partake of the things set forward, and of turning another away, and of
doing all things, so that each according to his own temperament may touch the
corresponding things, in order that neither the sickly be worn down, having
added matter through the foods to the disease, nor the more robust grow weary,
falling into a plethoric unpleasantness by the uncorresponding food. In these
[circumstances] if, while the drinking-party is in the middle, someone of
those given to wine-drinking is led out, either being at the beginning of the
drunkenness or remaining at the dinner as far as the end, the president =89=
managing these things so that the table may be well-ordered throughout,
remaining clean of the evils from plethora and drunkenness and derangement. As
then the one being pulled away from the steam of the seasonings does not make
the deprivation of the desired things a pleasure, but condemns some lack of
judgment of the one presiding, as


keeping [him] from the things set forward by some envy and not by providence,
but if he should look toward those still behaving unseemly by the extension of
the drunkenness in vomitings and headaches and in uttering something of what
is not due, he acknowledges thanks to the one who stood him away from the
unmeasured fullness before such a passion; if then the example has been
understood by us, it would be easy to bring the theorem grasped in this to the
thought set before [us]. For what was the thing set before? For what reason
does God, when there is much earnestness in the fathers that the successor of
life be kept for themselves, often allow the one born to be snatched away
untimely in the incomplete [stage] of age? Toward whom we will say that
drinking-party example, that the table of life having much and every sort of
preparation of foods (and think for me according to the cook's experience that
not all the things of life are sweetened with the honey of pleasure, but there
is where life is also seasoned with the more austere of the accidents, such as
those skilled in the pleasures about belly and feast devise, stirring up the
appetites of the diners with the more pungent or salty or astringent); since
then life is not honey-sweet through all the affairs, but there is in which
the relish is brine or astringency [or] vinegar-like, or some biting and =90=
pungent quality, having been rubbed into the affairs, makes the cookery of
life hard to take, and the mixing-bowls of deceit are full of every sort of
mixture, of which some have made the inflated passion through the conceit of
deceit for those who drank, and others have called out those who drank into
derangement, and for others they have stirred the vomit of things wickedly
acquired through the shameful paying back; so that the one who has not used
the drinking-party as he ought may not linger upon such a preparation of the
table, he is more quickly taken out from the fullness of the diners, gaining
not coming to be in those things of which the unmeasuredness of enjoyment
becomes a procurer for the greedy. This is what I say is an achievement of
complete providence, not only to heal the passions that have been constituted,
but also to hinder them before the constitution. Which indeed we have also
conjectured about the death of the newly born, that the one who makes all
things by reason, from love of humanity, takes away the matter of vice, not
giving occasion to the choice that has been recognized through the prognostic
power to be shown through the works in an eminence of vice of what sort it is,
whenever it should have the impulse toward the evil.

And often the one who constitutes the drinking-party of life convicts through
such things the sophistical necessity of love of money, so that, I think, the
illness of love of money may appear naked of the fair-faced coverings, not
being shaded by any deceived curtain. For the many say that for this reason
=91= they are broadened in the desires of the more, so that they may make
those born from them richer; whose disease, being their own, not coming about
from necessity, the unexcused greed of the childless convicts; for many,
neither having a succession upon the many things for which they toiled nor
being in hopes of having [one], instead of countless children nurse many
desires in themselves, not having where to refer the necessity of this
illness.

But if some, dwelling badly in life, both tyrannical and bitter in choice,
enslaved to all licentiousnesses, struck beside themselves with anger, sparing
none of the incurable evils, robbers and man-slayers and betrayers of
fatherlands or if there is anything more accursed than these, such as
father-slayers and mother-destroyers and child-killers and raging upon
unlawful mixings, if being such they grow old in this vice, how will someone
say that these things agree with what has been examined before? For if the one
snatched away before time, so that he may not according to the example of the
drinking-party be greedy to the end in the passions of this life, is taken out
of the living drinking-party providentially, for what reason does such a one
riot in the drinking-party as far as old age, scattering upon himself and the
fellow-drinkers the stale mixture of evils? Toward whom we will say that no
less even upon these the providential of God


economy is shown through; for that it is possible even for the one who has
driven to this measure of wickedness, through certain long circuits of times
through the eternal =92= purification, to give [him] back again to the
fullness of those being saved, is altogether quite clear to everyone who looks
toward the divine power. For who is so unobservant of the divine nature as,
seeing the other things of God, as many as he has accomplished by the
overlying of the power in the impulse of the will alone, to think that in this
alone he is weak? For if someone should wish to examine in a human way, it
will be found more difficult to bring heaven and earth and all the world in
these, not being, into being, than to lead back again a soul that has wandered
through vice [itself] toward the life according to nature, so that the humane
will would not be idle even upon these. And the lastingness of the life in
vice for this one does not have the starting-points from God; for the power of
living he received from the one who made the life, but of living badly his own
choice leads, and if this did not have the inclination toward this, the human
being would not even be in vice at all. For there is no evil according to its
own subsistence, not being crafted in the choice of the one living badly. If
then God is without cause of the vice being constituted in someone, no longer
would the cause of the life of these in vice reasonably be referred to that
one.

But why, you will altogether say, does he take one out of life providentially
before the choice is completed through the evil, and =93= leave the other to
become such that it is better for him not to have come to be at all? And
toward this we will give some such account to the more fair-minded, that often
the life of those who have lived well becomes a cause of the better even for
those from them. And countless are the testimonies of these from the
God-inspired scripture, through which we are clearly taught that those from
them also share in the care that has come from God to the worthy. And it would
be a good heading also to become a hindrance to someone toward vice for the
one who was altogether going to live according to it. But since the account
here aims at the unclear things, no one will altogether blame [us] when the
thought leads the aiming in many ways. For one would say not only that God,
granting a favor to the leaders of the race, takes out of the evil life the
one who was going to live according to it, but even if there should be nothing
of this sort upon those snatched away before time, it is nothing unlikely to
think that those would receive something harsher of the life according to vice
than each of these who have been recognized upon wickedness through life. For
that nothing comes to be without God we have come to know through many things,
and again that the things managed from God are not chance and irrational
everyone would agree, knowing that God is word and wisdom and all virtue and
truth, and nothing of the word is irrational nor of the wise anything unwise,
nor would virtue and truth accept what is not virtuous and outside of the
true.

Whether then according to the causes spoken some are snatched away untimely,
or there is also something other besides what has been said, it is fitting to
be agreed that these things altogether come to be upon =94= the better aim.
And I know also some other account having learned from the wisdom of the
apostle, through which some of those exceeding according to vice were allowed
to live according to their own choice; for having exercised such a thought
more in the discourse toward the Romans and having brought against himself the
thing opposed from the sequence, that it would not be reasonable for the evil
one still to have come to be in accusation, if indeed he has from God being
evil, not having come to be at all, if indeed it was unwilled by the one who
prevails over the things that are, thus he meets the account, dissolving the
counter-objection through some deeper contemplation; for he says that God,
distributing to each according to desert, there is where he also gives room to
vice upon the better aim; for for this reason he allowed even such a one to
come to be and the Egyptian tyrant to become such, so that by that one's blow
Israel might be educated, that numerous people and going beyond every compass
from number. For perhaps, the divine power being recognized through all things
and having itself sufficiently toward benefiting the worthy and not being weak
toward the punishment of vice, since it was altogether necessary for that
people to be emigrated from


Egypt, so that there might not come about for them a communion of the Egyptian
evils according to the wandering of life, for this reason that fighter against
God, Pharaoh named =95= upon every evil, both rose together with and
flourished together with the life of those being benefited, so that, the
double activity of God being distributed upon each of the two, Israel might
worthily receive the knowledge, being taught the better upon himself, and
seeing the more gloomy upon those being scourged through vice, as God knowing
by the exceeding of the wisdom to handle even the evil upon the cooperation of
the good. For there is need for the craftsman (if it is necessary that the
apostolic account also be confirmed through our words), there is need then for
the one shaping the iron through the art toward some of the tools useful for
life not only of that which yields easily to the art from some natural
softness, but even if the iron is very resistant, even if it is not easily
softened through fire and is not able by the unyielding and stiff to be shaped
toward the vessel of the necessities of life, the art seeks the cooperation of
this too; for it will use such as an anvil, so that by the blows upon this the
well-worked and soft may be stamped toward something of the useful.

But someone will say that not all through this life have enjoyed their own
wretchedness; for neither have those living according to virtue in this life
enjoyed the sweats of virtue; what then, he says, is the useful of these upon
those who have lived in vice without punishment? I will set beside you also
toward this an account higher than human reasonings; for the great David says
somewhere of his own prophecy that some such thing also is a part of gladness
for the virtuous, whenever they contemplate in comparison with their own goods
the destruction of the condemned =96=; For the just, he says, will be
gladdened, whenever he sees vengeance of the impious; he will wash his hands
in the blood of the sinner; not as rejoicing at the pains of those being
distressed, but as then especially recognizing what comes about from virtue
for the worthy. For he signifies through what has been said that an addition
of the gladness and an intensification comes about for the virtuous, the
juxtaposition of the opposites; for to say that He washes the hands in the
blood of the sinner presents such a thought, that the being-purified of his
activity according to life is shown through in the destruction of the sinners;
for the word of washing interprets an emphasis of purity, but one is not
washed in blood but is stained, so that through this it becomes clear that the
juxtaposition of the more gloomy things shows the blessedness of virtue.

One must then join the account upon a heading, so that the thoughts examined
may become easy to remember. For the untimely end of infants neither suggests
thinking that the one who has thus ceased from living is among painful things,
nor does it come about equally with those who have been purified through all
virtue according to the life here, God by a better foresight hindering the
unmeasuredness of evils upon those who were going to live thus. And that some
of the evil live on does not overturn the thought given; for by the favor
toward those who generated them the evil was hindered upon those; but for
those for whom there was no frankness from parents toward God neither was such
a form of the benefaction still sent along with those from them; or the one
hindered through death from becoming evil would have appeared much harsher
than those known upon vice, if indeed he had the wickedness unhindered, or if
some have also come forward to the topmost =97= measure of wickedness, the
apostolic contemplation consoles the inquiry, as the one who does all things
in wisdom knowing even through the evil to work some of the goods. And if
someone both has the peak in wickedness and has been forged by the art of God
upon nothing useful according to the example spoken by us, this becomes an
addition of the gladness of those who have lived well, as the prophecy
suggests to think, which one would reckon not small among goods nor unworthy
of the foresight of God.
