FIRST THEOLOGICAL ORATION, A PRELIMINARY DISCOURSE AGAINST THE EUNOMIANS

1. The discourse is against those who are clever in speech. And that I may
begin from Scripture: Behold, I am against you, O insolent one. For there are,
there are some who itch in the ear and the tongue, and already, as I see, even
the hand, for our words, and who rejoice in profane empty voices, and in
oppositions of knowledge falsely so called, and in strifes about words that
lead to no usefulness. For so Paul names everything superfluous and
over-curious in speech, the herald and confirmer of the Word cut short, the
disciple and teacher of the fishermen. But as for these of whom the discourse
is, would that they, just as they have a tongue nimble and clever at attacking
words more noble and more approved, would occupy themselves a little at least
with deeds as well, and perhaps they would be less sophists, and less absurd
and paradoxical tumblers of words, that I may say something even ridiculously
about a ridiculous matter.

2. But since, having overthrown every path of piety, they look only to this
one thing, what they will bind or loose of the things proposed, just as those
in the theatres who exhibit wrestling-bouts, and of wrestling-bouts not those
that bring victory according to the laws of contest, but those that steal the
sight of the unlearned in such things and snatch the applauder; and every
marketplace must be buzzed about with their words, and every banquet worn out
with chatter and unpleasantness, and every feast and every mourning, the one
to be unfestive and full of dejection, the other to be consoled by a greater
calamity, their questions, and every women's quarter, companion of simplicity,
to be harassed, and the flower of modesty to be stripped by swiftness about
speech. Since these things are so, and the evil is unrestrainable and
unbearable, and our great mystery is in danger of being a paltry craft: come
then, let the spies at least bear with us so far, as we are moved with
fatherly bowels and, as the divine Jeremiah says, torn in our senses, as not
to receive the discourse about these things roughly, and holding back the
tongue a little, if indeed they can, let them lend us the ear. At any rate you
will suffer no loss. For either we have spoken into ears of those who hear,
and the word has had some fruit, your benefit, since the sower sows the word
upon every mind, but the good and fertile bears fruit, or you have gone away
having spat even this of ours, and having taken more matter for contradiction
and railing against us, that you may feast yourselves even more. Do not wonder
if I speak a paradoxical word, and contrary to your law, you who promise to
know and to teach everything, very youthfully and nobly, that I may not pain
you by saying ignorantly and rashly.

3. It is not for everyone, O you, to philosophize about God, not for everyone;
the matter is not so cheap and of those who go upon the ground. And I will
add, not always, nor for all, nor all things, but there is a when, and for
whom, and to what extent. Not of all, because it belongs especially to those
who have been examined and have advanced in contemplation, and who before
these things have been purified in soul and body, or are being purified. For
it is perhaps not even safe for what is not clean to touch the clean, just as
neither for a weak sight to touch a sun-ray. And when? When we have leisure
from the slime and disturbance from without, and our ruling part is not
confused with the wretched and wandering impressions, as mixing the beauties
of letters with wicked letters, or the fragrance of myrrhs with mire. For one
must truly be at leisure, and know God; and when we take a season, to judge
the straightness of theology. And for whom? Those for whom the matter is a
serious pursuit, and not as one of the other things this too is pleasantly
chattered, after the horse-races, and the theatres, and the songs, and the
belly, and the things under the belly; for whom this too is a part of luxury,
the idle talk about these things and the cleverness of oppositions. And what
must be philosophized, and to what extent? As much as is attainable for us,
and to the extent that the habit and power of the hearer reaches; lest just as
excessive sounds, or foods, harm the hearing or the bodies, or if you will,
burdens beyond strength those who go under them, or the more violent of rains
the earth, so these too, pressed down and weighted by the hard, so to speak,
of the arguments, should be damaged even in their former power.

4. And I do not say this, that one must not always remember God. Let not those
who are easy and quick at everything fasten on us again. For one must remember
God more than one must breathe; and, if it is possible to say this, one must
do nothing else but this. And I too am of those who praise the word which
commands to meditate day and night, and to tell at evening and morning and
midday, and to bless the Lord at every time; if one must also say the word of
Moses, lying down, rising, walking, doing anything else whatever, and being
formed by memory toward purity. So I do not hinder the remembering
continually, but the theologizing; nor theology, as if it were impious, but
untimeliness; nor teaching, but lack of measure. Or does fullness and satiety
of honey produce vomiting, even though it is honey, and is there a time for
every matter, as it seems to Solomon and to me, and is the beautiful not
beautiful when it does not happen beautifully, like a flower in winter
altogether untimely, and manly adornment for women, or womanly for men, and
geometry in mourning, and a tear at a drinking-bout, and here alone shall we
dishonor the time, where most of all the timely is to be honored?

5. By no means, O friends and brothers; for I still call you brothers, though
you do not hold yourselves brotherly. Let us not think so, nor like hot and
hard-to-hold horses, having thrown off the rider reason, and having spat out
the reverence that well reins us, run far from the turning-post; but let us
philosophize within our own bounds, and not be carried out into Egypt, nor be
swept down to the Assyrians, nor sing the song of the Lord upon a strange
land, I mean every hearing, both foreign and our own, hostile and friendly,
well-disposed and ill-disposed, which very carefully watches our things, and
would wish the spark of the evils in us to become a flame, and kindles and
fans and raises to heaven with its own breezes secretly, and makes it higher
than the Babylonian flame that burns up the things around. For since they do
not have the strength in their own dogmas, they hunt it in our weak points,
and for this reason, as flies to wounds, so they attack ours, whether one must
say misfortunes or sins. But let us at least no longer be ignorant of
ourselves, nor dishonor the due order about these things; but if it is not
possible to dissolve the enmity, let us at least agree with one another on
this, to utter the mystical things mystically, and the holy things holily, and
not to throw into profane ears the things not to be published, nor let us show
those who worship the demons and are attendants of shameful myths and deeds
more solemn than ourselves, who would sooner share blood than certain words
with the uninitiated. But let us know that, just as there is a certain decorum
of clothing and diet and laughter and gait, so also of speech and silence,
because we also honor the Word among the other titles and powers of God. Let
even our contentiousness be lawful.

6. Why does the bitter hearer of the things said hear generation of God and
creation, and God from things that are not, and cutting and division and
analysis? Why do we seat the accusers as judges? Why do we put the swords into
the hands of the enemies? How, do you think, will he receive the discourse
about these things, or with what mind, who praises adulteries and the
corruption of children, and worships the passions, and is able to think
nothing above the body, who yesterday and the day before set up gods for
himself, and these known by the most shameful things? Not materially? Not
shamefully? Not ignorantly? Not ignorantly? Not as he is accustomed? Will he
not make your theology an advocate of his own gods and passions? For if we
ourselves abuse these words, we would hardly persuade those to philosophize in
our things; and if they are inventors of evils from themselves, when would
they abstain from the things given? These things our war against one another
brings us. These things those who fight for the Word more than is pleasing to
the Word, and suffering the same as the mad, who set fire to their own houses,
or tear their children, or push aside their parents, thinking them strangers.

7. But since we have put away from the discourse what is alien, and have sent
off the great legion into the herd of swine, having gone down into the depths,
which is the second thing, let us look to ourselves, and let us polish to
beauty, as a statue, the theologian. And let us first consider this, what is
this so great rivalry about speech and garrulity? What is this new disease
and insatiability? Why having bound the hands have we armed the tongues? Do we
not praise hospitality? Do we not admire brotherly love, conjugal affection,
virginity, feeding of the poor? Not psalmody, not all-night standing, not a
tear? Do we not press down the body with fasts? Do we not go abroad to God
through prayer? Do we not yoke the worse to the better, I mean the dust to the
spirit, as those who judge the mixture justly would? Do we not make life a
practice of death? Do we not establish ourselves masters of the passions,
remembering the nobility from above? Do we not tame anger swelling and growing
wild? Not pride that casts down, not irrational grief, not uneducated
pleasure, not whorish laughter, not disordered sight, not insatiable hearing,
not measureless speech, not outlandish thought, not as many things as the evil
one takes from us against us, bringing in the death through the windows, as
Scripture says, that is the senses? Nay, quite the contrary, and we have given
freedom to the passions of others, as kings give the victory-releases, only if
they nod toward us, and they are borne against God more boldly, and we give in
return an evil reward of a thing that is not good, the boldness of speech of
impiety.

8. And yet, O dialectician and talker, I will ask you something small: But
answer thou, says to Job the one who speaks through whirlwind and clouds. Are
there many mansions with God, as you hear, or one? Many, you will grant of
course, and not one. And must all be filled, or some, and some not, so as to
be empty and prepared in vain? Yes, all; for nothing of the things that have
come to be from God is in vain. And this mansion, whatever you will set it as,
could you say? Is it the rest there and the glory laid up for the
blessed, or something else? Not other than this. Since we have agreed this,
let us also examine that. Is there something that procures these mansions, as
my word has it, or nothing? There is, certainly. What is this? That there are
different ways of life and choices, and one leads one way, another another,
according to the proportion of faith, which we also name ways. Must one then
travel all, or some of these ways? If it is possible for the same person, all;
if not, as many as possible; if not, some; and if not even this, it is a great
thing even if one, excellently, as it seems to me at least. You take this
rightly. What then? When you hear that there is one way, and this a narrow
one, what does the word seem to you to show? One because of virtue; for it is
one, even if it splits into many; and narrow because of the sweats and because
it is not passable for many, as compared with the multitude of the contrary
and as many as travel through vice. So it seems to me also. Why then, O best
one, if this is so, as if having condemned our doctrine of a certain poverty,
having left all the other ways, do you rush and push toward this one, the one
through speech and contemplation, as you yourselves think, but as I say, of
idle talk and wonder-mongering? Let Paul rebuke you, bitterly reproaching this
after the enumeration of the gifts, in which he says: Are all apostles? Are
all prophets? and the rest.

9. Be it so; you are lofty, and beyond the lofty, and above the clouds, if you
will, the spectator of the unseen, the hearer of the unspeakable, the one
lifted up after Elijah, and the one deemed worthy of theophany after Moses,
and heavenly after Paul. Why do you also mould the others into saints on the
same day, and ordain theologians, and as it were breathe in the instruction,
and have made many councils of ignorant oracles? Why do you bind the weaker in
spider-webs, as if it were something wise and great? Why do you raise
wasp-nests against the faith? Why do you improvise for us an uprising of
dialecticians, as the myths of old did the giants? Why, having gathered of men
as much as is light and unmanly, like some refuse, into one torrent, and
having made them more womanish by flattery, have you created a new workshop of
impiety, not unwisely harvesting their mindlessness? Do you contradict even
these? And are the other things nowhere for you? And must the tongue rule at
all costs, and do you not hold back the birth-pang of speech? You have also
other subjects, many and ambitious. There turn the disease with what is
useful.

10. Strike for me the silence of Pythagoras, and the Orphic beans, and the
newer boast about He himself said. Strike for me Plato's ideas, and the
transmigrations and circuits of our souls, and the recollections, and the
loves that are not beautiful through beautiful bodies upon the soul; Epicurus'
atheism, and the atoms, and the unphilosophic pleasure; Aristotle's petty
providence, and the artificial, and the mortal discourses about soul, and the
human character of the doctrines; the eyebrow of the Stoa, of the Cynics the
greedy and the marketplace. Strike for me the void, the fullness of the
nonsense, as much as they wonder-monger about gods or sacrifices, about idols,
about demons good and evil-doing, as much as about divination, god-evoking,
soul-evoking, power of stars. But if you think these unworthy of discourse, as
small and often refuted, and you turn about your own, and seek the ambitious
in these; I will provide you here too with broad ways. Philosophize for me
about world or worlds, about matter, about soul, about rational natures better
and worse, about resurrection, judgment, recompense, sufferings of Christ. For
in these both to hit the mark is not useless, and to miss is not dangerous.
But we shall converse with God, now a little, and a little later perhaps more
perfectly, in Christ Jesus himself our Lord, to whom be the glory unto the
ages; amen.
