OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA CONCERNING NOT THINKING TO SAY THREE GODS TO
ABLABIUS

It is right that you, who are flourishing in every power according to the
inner man, contend against the enemies of the truth and not shrink from the
labors, so that we the fathers may be gladdened by the noble sweats of our
children; for this the law of nature proposes. But since, reversing the order,
you turn upon us the assaults of the darts with which those who oppose the
truth shoot, and you command that the desert coals be quenched by the shield
of faith from us old men and that the sharpened arrows of falsely named
knowledge be sent away, we accept the command, becoming for you a pattern of
obedience, so that you yourself also may give us the equal return upon like
commands, if ever we should stir you up to such contests, O noble soldier of
Christ, Ablabius. 3,=1.38= And this account which you have put forward to us
is not a small one, nor such as to bring little harm if it should not meet
with the fitting examination. For by the force of the question it is
necessary, according to the ready sense, to be brought together with one of
the unseemly things, and either to say three Gods, which is unlawful, or not
to bear witness of the Godhead to the Son and the Spirit, which is both
impious and absurd. What is said by you is of this sort: Peter and James and
John, being in one humanity, are called three men; and it is no absurdity that
those joined according to nature, if they should be more, be numbered in the
plural from the name of the nature. If then there custom grants this, and
there is no one forbidding us to say two of the two and three of those beyond
two, how, when in the mystical doctrines we confess the three hypostases and
conceive no difference of nature upon them, do we in a way fight against the
confession, saying that the Godhead of the Father and of the Son and of the
Holy Spirit is one, yet forbidding to say three Gods? The account, as I said
before, has much that is hard to handle. But we, if we should find something
of such a sort, by which the uncertainty of our mind will be stayed, no longer
wavering and shaken toward the dilemma of absurdity, 3,=1.39= it would be
well. But if our account should also be shown weaker than the problem, we
shall keep the tradition which we received from the fathers forever firm and
unmoved, and we shall seek from the Lord the account that is advocate of the
faith; which if it should be found by someone of those who have the grace, we
shall give thanks to him who gave the grace; but if not, we shall none the
less have the faith unchangeable upon the things that have been known. Why
then, in our custom, having numbered one by one those exhibited in the same
nature, do we name them in the plural, saying so many men and not one of them
all, while in the case of the divine nature the account of the doctrine casts
out the multitude of gods, both numbering the hypostases and not admitting the
plural signification? Now one might seem, speaking this offhand to the
simpler, to be saying something: that the account, fleeing the likeness of
Hellenic polytheism, declined to number gods in a multitude, so that there
might not be thought some community even of doctrines, if among us too the
divine were numbered not monadically but plurally, after the likeness of the
custom among them. And this, said to the more unmixed, might perhaps seem to
be saying something; but for those who seek that one or the other of the
proposition stand, either not to confess the Godhead upon the three, or
altogether to name as three those of the same


sharing 3,=1.40= Godhead, what has been said is not yet such as to produce
some solution of the question. Therefore it is necessary to make the answer
through more, tracing out the truth as far as it is possible; for the account
is not about chance matters. We say then first that it is a certain abuse of
custom to name in the plural, according to the very name of the nature, those
who are not divided in nature, and to say that there are many men, which is
like saying that there are many human natures. And that these things are so
would become clear to us from this: for when we call someone, we do not name
him from the nature, so that the community of the name may not produce some
error, each of those who hear thinking himself to be the one summoned, because
the call comes not by the peculiar address but from the common name of the
nature; but having said the voice peculiarly laid upon him (I mean the one
significant of the subject), we thus separate him from the many, so that there
are many who have shared in the nature, say disciples or apostles or martyrs,
but one man in them all, if indeed, as has been said, "man" is not of each
one, but of the common of the nature; for Luke or Stephen is a man, yet if
someone is a man, he is not also altogether Luke or Stephen. But the account
of the hypostases, through the peculiarities 3,=1.41= contemplated in each,
admits the partition and is contemplated in number by way of composition; but
the nature is one, itself united to itself and an exactly undivided monad, not
increased by addition, not diminished by subtraction, but being what it is,
one, and remaining one even if it appears in a multitude, unsplit and
continuous and complete and not co-divided with those who participate in it,
the individuals. And just as a people and a populace and an army and an
assembly are all said in the singular, yet each of these is conceived in a
multitude, so according to the more accurate account man also would properly
be said to be one, even if those exhibited in the same nature are a multitude,
so that it is much better that the mistaken custom among us be corrected
toward no longer stretching the name of the nature toward multitude than,
being slaves to this, to transfer the error here also upon the divine
doctrine. But since the correction of the custom is impossible (for how would
anyone be persuaded not to say many men of those exhibited in the same nature?
for in every case custom is hard to change), in the case of the lower nature,
not going against the prevailing custom, we would not err so much, there being
no harm from 3,=1.42= the mistaken use of the names; but in the case of the
divine doctrine the indifferent use of the names is no longer similarly
without danger; for here what is beside a little is not little. Therefore one
God is to be confessed by us according to the scriptural witness, "Hear,
Israel: the Lord your God is one Lord," even if the voice of the Godhead
extends through the Holy Trinity. And I say these things according to the
account given to us upon human nature, in which we have learned that one must
not broaden the appellation of the nature by a plural character. But more
accurately the very name of the Godhead is to be examined by us, so that
through the signification lying in the voice some cooperation may come toward
the clarification of what lies before us. Now it seems to the many that the
voice of the Godhead is laid peculiarly upon the nature, and just as the
heaven or the sun or some other of the elements of the world is signified by
peculiar voices significant of the subjects, so they say that also upon the
highest and divine nature the voice of the Godhead has been fitted to what is
shown, as some proper name, fittingly. But we, following the suggestions of
Scripture, have learned that it is both unnameable and unspeakable; and every
name, whether it has been found by human custom or has been handed down by the
Scriptures, of the things conceived about the divine 3,=1.43= nature, to be
interpretive


we say, not to contain the signification of the nature itself. And one would
not need much labor toward the proof that these things are so. For the
remaining names, as many as are laid upon the creation, one would find even
without some etymology fitted to the subjects by chance, since we are content
to mark the things in any way through the voice upon them so that the
knowledge of the things marked may become unconfused for us. But as many names
as are toward guidance of the divine comprehension, each has its own meaning
comprehended in it, and you would not find, apart from some thought, any voice
among the more God-befitting of the names, so that from this it is shown that
the divine nature itself has not been marked by some one of the names, but
some one of the things about it is made known through what is said. For we say
that the divine is incorruptible, if it should so happen, or powerful, or as
many other things as it is customary to say. But we find of each of the names
a peculiar emphasis, fitting to be conceived and said about the divine nature,
yet not signifying that which the nature is according to essence; for the very
thing, whatever it is, is incorruptible; but the thought of the incorruptible
is this, that what is is not dissolved into corruption. Therefore having said
"incorruptible," we said what the nature does not suffer; but what that is
which does not suffer the corruption, we did not present. So even if we should
say "life-giving," having signified by the 3,=1.44= appellation what it does,
we did not make known by the word the thing that does it. And all the other
things according to the same account we find from the signification lying in
the more God-befitting voices, either forbidding us to know what ought not to
be upon the divine nature, or teaching what ought, yet not containing an
interpretation of the nature itself. Since then, contemplating the varied
operations of the power that lies above, we fit the appellations from each of
the operations known to us, and we <say of God> that this too is one, the
operation, the superintending and seeing and, as one might say, beholding,
according to which he looks upon all things and oversees all things, seeing
the thoughts and entering by the contemplative power even upon the unseen, we
have supposed that the Godhead (theotes) has been named from the beholding
(thea), and that the beholder (theoros) of us is addressed as God (theos) both
by custom and by the teaching of the Scriptures. And if someone grants that to
behold is the same as to see, and that the one who looks upon all things both
is and is called God, overseer of the all, let him reckon this operation,
whether it belongs to one of the persons believed in the Holy Trinity, or
whether the power extends through the three. For if the interpretation of the
Godhead is true, and the things seen are beheld, and that which beholds is
called God, 3,=1.45= no longer would any of the persons in the Trinity
reasonably be separated from such an appellation through the signification
lying in the voice. For Scripture bears witness of seeing equally to Father
and Son and Holy Spirit. "Our defender, see, O God," says David; and from this
we learn that seeing is a peculiar operation of God, insofar as God is
conceived, from his saying "See, O God." But Jesus also sees the thoughts of
those who condemn, because he remits the sins from his own authority [of men].
For "Jesus," it says, "seeing their thoughts." And concerning the Spirit Peter
says to Ananias, "Why has Satan filled your heart, that you should lie to the
Holy Spirit?" showing that of the things dared in secret by Ananias the Holy
Spirit was a true witness and knower, through whom also the manifestation of
the hidden things was to Peter. For he became a thief of himself, escaping
notice, as he thought, of all, and hiding the sin; but the Holy Spirit was at
once in Peter and detected that man's mind dragged down toward love of money,
and through himself gives Peter to see through the 3,=1.46= hidden things;
which he would clearly not be doing, if he were without sight of the secret
things. But someone will not yet say that the construction of the account
looks toward what is sought. For not even if it should be granted that the
appellation common of the nature


of the Godhead is, has it already been established through this that one must
not say gods. On the contrary rather we are compelled from these things all
the more to say gods. For we find in human custom not only those who share the
same nature but, even if some are of the same pursuit, the many are not
remembered in the singular, as we say many rhetors and geometers and farmers
and shoemakers and all the other things likewise. And if the appellation of
the Godhead were of nature, it would rather have had occasion, according to
the account already given, to include the three hypostases in the singular and
to say one God because of the uncut and undivided of the nature; but since it
has been established through what has been said that the name of the Godhead
signifies operation and not nature, the account is somehow turned toward the
contrary from the things being established, so that one must rather in this
way say three Gods of those contemplated in the same operation; as they say
three philosophers or rhetors are said, or whatever other name is from a
pursuit, when those who share in the same are more. These things I have worked
out with more labor, bringing 3,=1.47= forward the opposing account of the
adversaries, so that the doctrine may be more firmly fixed for us, being
strengthened by the more vigorous of the antitheses. Therefore the account
must again be taken up. Since it has been moderately shown to us from the
construction that the voice of the Godhead is not of nature but of operation,
perhaps one might reasonably say this as the cause of those who share with one
another in the same pursuits being numbered and named in the plural upon men,
yet the divine being said monadically as one God and one Godhead, even if the
three hypostases should not be separated from the signification shown in the
Godhead: that men, even if the more are of one operation, each by himself
operates what lies before him in an assigned way, having no communication in
the operation according to himself with those practicing the equal; for even
if the rhetors should be more, the pursuit, being one, has the same name in
the more, but those who follow it each by himself operate, this one
rhetorizing peculiarly and the other peculiarly; therefore among men, since
the operation of each in the same pursuits is distinguished, they are properly
named many, each of them being cut off from the others into his own
circumscription according to the peculiar manner of the operation; but in the
case of the divine nature we have not learned thus, that the Father does
something by himself, of which the Son does not take hold together, or again
that the Son operates something peculiarly apart from the Spirit, but every
operation which extends from God upon the creation and is named according to
the varied 3,=1.48= conceptions starts from the Father and proceeds through
the Son and is perfected in the Holy Spirit. For this reason the name of the
operation is not split into the multitude of those who operate, because the
zeal concerning something is not assigned of each and peculiar; but whatever
comes to be of the things that either reach our providence or are toward the
economy and constitution of the all, comes to be through the three, yet the
things that come to be are not three. And let us understand what is said from
one certain thing, I mean from the very chief of the gifts. Life has become
the lot of as many as have partaken of this grace. Examining then whence such
a good has come to be for us, we find through the guidance of the Scriptures
that it is from Father and Son and Holy Spirit. But not, since we set down
three persons and names, do we reckon that three lives also (peculiarly one
from each of them) have been given to us, but the same life is both operated
from the Holy Spirit and prepared from the Son and depends upon the will of
the Father. Since then, after the likeness of what has been said, the Holy
Trinity does not operate every operation dividedly according to the number of
the hypostases, but there comes to be some one motion and transmission of the
good will, being carried through from the Father through the 3,=1.49= Son
toward the Spirit, as we do not say three life-givers of those who operate the
one


life, nor three good ones of those contemplated in the same goodness, nor do
we announce all the other things in the plural, so neither can we name as
three those who operate this divine, that is superintending, power and
operation conjointly and indiscriminately through one another upon us and all
the creation. For just as, having learned concerning the God of the all,
Scripture saying that he judges all the earth, we say for this reason that he
is judge of the all, and again having heard that "The Father judges no one" we
do not think Scripture fights against itself; for he who judges all the earth
does this through the Son, to whom he has given all the judgment; and
everything that comes to be from the Only-begotten has its reference to the
Father, so that he both is judge of the all and judges no one, because, as has
been said, he has given all the judgment to the Son, and all the judgment of
the Son has not been made alien from the paternal will; and no one would
reasonably say either two judges or that the one has been made alien from the
authority and power according to the judgment; so also in the case of the
account of the Godhead, 3,=1.50= Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of
God, and the superintending and beholding power, which indeed we call Godhead,
the Father operates through the Only-begotten, <God who makes all things in
wisdom>, while the Son perfects every power in the Holy Spirit, both judging,
as Isaiah says, in the spirit of judgment and the spirit of burning, and doing
good according to the voice of the Gospel, which he has made toward the Jews,
in the Spirit of God; for he says, "But if I by the Spirit of God cast out the
demons," including from a part every form of doing good through the unity
according to the operation; for where the one is operated through one another,
the name of the operation cannot have been partitioned into many. For since
the account of the superintending and beholding power is one in Father and Son
and Holy Spirit, as has been said in what was before, starting from the Father
as from some spring, being operated by the Son, and in the power of the Spirit
perfecting the grace, and no operation is distinguished toward the hypostases,
being fulfilled peculiarly by each and in an assigned way apart from that
which is contemplated together; but every providence and care and 3,=1.51=
superintendence of the all, both of the things according to the sensible
creation and of those according to the supermundane nature, both that which
preserves the things that are and that which corrects the things that have
been missed and that which teaches the things that have been set right, is one
and not three, being accomplished by the Holy Trinity, yet not cut in three
according to the number of the persons contemplated in the faith, so that each
of the operations, contemplated upon itself, is either of the Father alone or
of the Only-begotten peculiarly or of the Holy Spirit separately; but the one
and the same Spirit divides the goods to each peculiarly, as the Apostle says;
yet the motion of the good from the Spirit is not without beginning; but we
find that the power preconceived of this, which is the Only-begotten God,
makes all things, without whom none of the things that are comes into
generation; but this source of the goods again starts from the paternal will.
If then every good thing and name, depending from the beginningless power and
will, is brought to perfection in the power of the Spirit through the
Only-begotten God, timelessly and without interval, no extension coming to be
or being conceived in the motion of the divine will from the Father through
the Son upon the Spirit, and Godhead also is one of the good names and
thoughts, the 3,=1.52= name would not reasonably be poured out into multitude,
the unity according to the operation preventing the plural numbering. And just
as the Father has been named by the Apostle one Savior of all men, especially
of believers, and no one from this voice says either that the Son does not
save those who believe, or that the salvation comes to be for those who share
in it apart from the Spirit, but the God over all becomes Savior of all, the
Son operating the salvation in the grace of the Spirit and


none the more for this are three saviors named by Scripture, even if the
salvation is confessed from the Holy Trinity, so neither three Gods according
to the assigned signification of the Godhead, even if such an appellation fits
the Holy Trinity. And to fight against those who contradict, that one must not
conceive the Godhead as operation, does not seem to me altogether of the
necessary things, as toward the present construction of the account. For we,
believing the divine nature to be indefinite and incomprehensible, conceive no
comprehension of it, but we determine that the nature is to be conceived in
every way in unboundedness. And that which is unbounded altogether is not
bounded in some respect and not in another; but unboundedness in every account
escapes the bound. Therefore that which is outside bound is not altogether
bounded even by a name. So that the thought of the indefinite may remain upon
the divine nature, we say that the divine is above every name, 3,=1.53= and
the Godhead is one of the names. Therefore the same cannot both be a name and
be thought to be above every name. Yet if this should please the opponents,
that the signification is not of operation but of nature, we shall run back to
the account from the beginning, that custom, mistakenly, raises the name of
the nature into a signification of multitude, neither diminution nor increase
according to the true account coming to the nature, when it is contemplated in
more or in fewer. For only those things are numbered by way of composition
which are contemplated in their own circumscription; and the circumscription
is grasped in surface of body and size and place and the difference according
to shape and color; but that which is contemplated outside these escapes the
circumscription through such things. And that which is not circumscribed is
not numbered, and that which is not numbered cannot be contemplated in a
multitude. Since we say also of gold, even if it is coined into many types,
that it both is and is said to be one; but we name many coins and many
staters, finding no increase of the nature of the gold in the multitude of the
staters. Therefore gold is also said to be much, when it is contemplated in a
greater bulk or in vessels or in coins, but many golds are not named because
of the multitude of the matter; unless someone should so say, many golds, as
the darics or the staters, upon which not the matter but the coins received
the signification of the multitude. For properly it is possible to say not
golds but golden 3,=1.54= these. As then the golden staters are many, but gold
is one, so also those exhibited individually in the nature of man are many, as
Peter and James and John, but the man in these is one. And even if Scripture
broadens the name according to plural signification in saying "Men swear by
the greater" and "sons of men," and as many such things, it is to be known
that, using the custom of the prevailing speech, it does not legislate that
one must use the words thus or otherwise, nor does it go through these things
making some technical teaching about words, but it uses the account according
to the prevailing custom, looking only to this, that the account may become
beneficial to those who receive it, making no exactness according to the
diction, in things where no harm according to the thought is constituted from
the words. And it would be long to catalogue from Scripture the
solecism-appearing constructions of the account toward the proof of what has
been said; but in things where there is a danger that some of the truth be
harmed, the unexamined and indifferent is no longer found in the scriptural
words. For this reason it grants to name men in the plural, because no one by
such a figure of the voice falls away in his suspicions into a multitude of
humanities, nor thinks that many human natures are signified because the name
of the nature has been announced in the plural; but the voice "God" it
announces observantly according to the singular 3,=1.55= form, taking this
forethought, that different natures upon the divine essence not be introduced
in the plural signification of gods. Therefore it says, "The Lord God is one
Lord"; but also the Only-begotten God


it proclaims by the voice of the Godhead, and it does not dissolve the one
into a dual signification, so as to name the Father and the Son two Gods, even
if each is proclaimed God by the holy ones; but the Father is God, and the Son
is God, yet in the same proclamation God is one, because no difference either
of nature or of operation is contemplated in the Godhead. For if, according to
the suspicion of those who have been deceived, the nature had been altered
upon the Holy Trinity, the number would consequently have been broadened into
a multitude of gods, being co-divided with the otherness of essence of the
subjects. But since the divine and simple and unchangeable nature thrusts away
all otherness according to essence, for as long as it is one, it does not
admit upon itself a signification of multitude. But just as one nature is
said, so also all the other things are named in the singular, God, good, holy,
savior, just, judge, and if some other of the God-befitting names is
conceived, which whether someone says looks toward nature or toward operation,
we shall not differ. And if someone should calumniate the account as, from not
admitting the difference according to nature, constructing some mixture of the
hypostases and an interchange, we shall make this defense concerning such a
blame, that confessing the invariability of the nature 3,=1.56= we do not deny
the difference according to the cause and the caused, in which alone we grasp
that the one is distinguished from the other, by believing the one to be cause
and the other from the cause; and of that which is from a cause we again
conceive another difference; for the one is immediately from the first, and
the other through that which is immediately from the first, so that both the
Only-begotten remains undoubted upon the Son, and that the Spirit is from the
Father is not doubted, the mediation of the Son both guarding the
Only-begotten for him and not shutting out the Spirit from the natural
relation toward the Father. And saying cause and from cause we do not by these
names signify nature (for no one would give the same account of cause and of
nature), but we indicate the difference according to the how of being. For
having said that the one is in the manner of being caused and the other is
without cause, we did not divide the nature by the account according to the
cause, but we indicated only that neither is the Son unbegottenly nor is the
Father through generation. And it is necessary that we first believe that
something is, and then examine closely how that which has been believed is;
therefore the account of what it is is other, and the account of how it is is
other. So to say that something is unbegottenly sets down how it is, but what
it is is not co-indicated by this voice. For if you asked a farmer about some
tree, whether it is planted or of itself, and he answered either that the tree
is unplanted or that it came to be from planting, did he indicate the nature
through the answer, 3,=1.57= he who said only how it is, or did he leave the
account of the nature unclear and uninterpreted? So also here, having learned
"unbegotten," we have been taught how it is fitting to conceive him to be, but
what he is we did not hear through the voice. Saying then such a difference
upon the Holy Trinity, as to believe the one to be cause and the other from
cause, we would no longer be accused of melting together the account of the
hypostases in the common of the nature. Since then the account of the cause
distinguishes the hypostases upon the Holy Trinity, advocating that the one is
without cause and the other from the cause, and the divine nature is grasped
as invariable and undivided through every thought, for this reason properly
one Godhead and one God and all the other of the God-befitting names are
announced monadically.
