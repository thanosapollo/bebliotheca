Epithalamium for the sons of the most fortunate Caesar
Even the ancient pitching of the tabernacle needed goat hairs, wise Caesar.
For let others make their own Caesars known by other names, some by the Julii,
some by the Augusti, and let them make others clear by still other titles; but
I, though I have countless other names with which to honor my divine Caesar,
would not estrange him from this finest and most fitting one, that of the
wise. You would need, I think, even my contribution of speech for the nuptial
chamber-raising of your holy pair of sons; but there the hairs also
contributed something and were no ignoble ornament to the work, whereas here
the contribution is neither for the sake of completeness, nor for ornament of
those things to which it is contributed. For what ornament could there be for
those who are ornament in themselves and beautiful in themselves? And what
completeness for those who lack nothing? But as a showing of the goodwill of
those who contribute. And if we have not arrived at the proper day of the
rite, but our contribution is overdue, one must not wonder; for we did not
keep silent about the rite out of ingratitude (or toward whom else would we
show gratitude, if not toward those who are masters and benefactors both of
all in common 343 and of ourselves in particular?), nor because we thought
that one time had been allotted to marriages among you and another to
writings: you who, a wise man persuaded by the wise Solomon, hold that there
is a most fitting time for every other thing, but for speech alone the whole
of time, so that not even when you stand face to face with enemies and breathe
the air do you neglect the graces of Hermes. But first I was shrinking from
the Gospel parable, and from it a not unreasonable fear crept over me, lest it
happen that my speech (for I hesitate to say also the father of the speech),
because it had not been dressed richly for a wedding, with hands and feet
bound, whatever one is to take the hands and feet of a speech to be, be thrown
out of the festival.
Then, for I will not hide the passion, by the greatness of the pleasure I was
also secretly robbed of the power of speech; for just as deep grief, so too
pleasure, when it has poured over a human soul, is wholly wont to assault the
rational powers in it. These are the causes of my lateness; by these I have
kept silent until now, and yet even so I do not come wholly out of season to
the festival. For just as with the visible sun some of the rays, when it is
about to rise, leap forth first and appear above the mountains and as it were
announce the one who will rise, and when it is setting again they do not all
sink together, but there are some that remain above the earth: so also with
these imperial festivals there are some pre-festivals and preliminary rites
and, so to speak, first leaps of the feasts, and yet there are also remnants
of them, not far from which our speech too has arrived; and having arrived, it
is wholly at a loss and in doubt what it should do with itself, whether,
having gone into the antiquities of marriage itself, it should hymn that, or
far rather first address the grooms before the marriage. And it judges the
second better; for the first would be both a greater undertaking than the
present one, and otherwise it is not necessary to try to establish marriage,
which has already been ratified by nature and by law and by time; and the
second, though this too is higher than our speech, is nevertheless necessary
and, so to speak, the head of the whole feast. I address you then, holy and
honored pair of grooms who are also brothers, a pair yoked first under one
yoke, that of brotherhood 345, and now newly yoked under two nuptial ones,
fair young shoots of a fair cultivated olive, brothers no more by nature than
by character, nor more by character than by the rest of the graces, naturally
gifted sharers of the scepters from which you sprang, each confirming his
proper lot no less by his manners than by his names: and you, the active mind,
Alexios, the fair youth, the hoary understanding, the bearing truly worthy of
your ancestors' fortune, who present in yourself the whole grandfather and
emperor living more in your manners than in the names Komnenos and Alexios,
and who confirm from what is in you the mystery of the succession of the
lines. For if one will grant even these things to the myth, that men once came
to be, the other limbs of the body κα....., but one of the shoulders of ivory,
and that it was somehow passed from an ancestor to those from him by
succession, then again our affairs are by far more marvelous than the mythical
ones, inasmuch as we do not confirm the lineage from one part, and that a
bodily one, but from the whole body and from all the dispositions of the soul.
And you too, the shoot of the Doukas root, you of the gracious name, the
flower of the season, the statue of youth, whom I have never seen without
putting that verse in mind: Which? you will say. The first form worthy of
sovereignty, I shall answer. Rejoice, both in common and each in particular,
you who have grown rich in the whole of that much-fabled happiness, and have
confirmed in your own persons what until now lay in the incredible: that all
goods should come together into one.
And if anyone should wish, I shall show at least from some points, in bulk and
in passing, the happiness of the grooms; for if the matter were not far from
the nuptial festival, I would not have spared any of them all, as far as I was
able. There were then two scepters for the Romans, finely reflecting one
another, the Komnenian a little earlier, and immediately at its heels the
Doukas one, both fortunate and illustrious and not unworthy of worldly
excellence; then as if by agreement they came together into one much 347 more
manifest and more brilliant, this Komneno-Doukas and ancestral one of the two
grooms. Remember, you who are present, the Alexian power; yet lest you weep
over the wedding, call to mind that good order in peace, the valor in battles;
without a sigh, O holy empresses, without a sigh, for the sake of the fair
festival, remember that gentleness, that steadfastness, the rest of the grace.
But what is this? I see the senate gloomy at these things. Better then to sing
another ornament, and already we sing it. Look, I pray, at this holy empress,
Irene of the fitting name, the Doukas shoot, who mastered no less all the
passions than all the Romans; well done, for the audience has again been
melted in soul. Look at this hand, of which you always have experience and we
have experience as a giver of wealth, the love of God, the pity, all the rest
of virtue. From such grandfathers these comely grooms have been put forward
for us; from such roots these fair fruits have been raised up for us. And what
of their august begetters? Whom, when I have spoken of them, I shall certainly
not fall short of the greatness of the facts: I mean this purple-born empress,
whose circular eye, moving from itself toward itself and at once revolving,
hints at the helical ascent toward Mind and God, of those things whose motion
we have known to be from themselves and toward themselves; whom, if the
Hellenes speak any truth at all, we would add as a fourth Grace to the Graces
and a tenth Muse to the Muses; who initiated herself, through the activity
according to virtue, into the whole philosophy of character, whose end is not
knowledge but action; then, having thought more nobly and more royally at
once, and having judged that as the purple is the ornament of a royal body, so
the science of beings is the ornament of a royal soul, she was initiated into
the knowledge of them and hunted the truth in them, and showed that virtue is
a possession of choice, not of birth; and having surpassed all in all
beauties, she outstripped even herself by the happiness of her marriage,
having shared the bed with such a man, my master and Caesar, as neither sight
has presented another 349 to us, nor the histories of the ancients have
taught.
For others among Greeks and Romans had perhaps succeeded in other things, and
some led the people, some commanded armies, some were distinguished in
letters, and of these one had practiced philosophy, another poetry; but that
the same man should at once command armies and preside over poems and
philosophize and cast his vote and judge rhetors, and not fall short of the
summit in all, but should, true to his name, defeat those distinguished in
each field, would be nothing of all things except of the Caesar alone. With
these fair grooms who are also brothers ranked under such parents, then, and
looking toward such examples, how, tell me, ought their souls to have been
trained? Not otherwise, I think, than as they were trained; and they were
trained just as Plato legislates that the one who is to preside over his
republic should be trained. As many goods, then, as nature has lavished on
them, I mean beauty and speed and strength, and as many as their easily
trained upbringing: to ride well, to play at ball, to hunt with ready skill,
to command not ignobly when it was needed to lead a phalanx; and further the
things about the body, imperial garments and silver and gold and whatever
other matter man has honored, and splendid houses and Arabian horses and no
ignoble throng of subjects, for these too complete the whole of happiness: let
each man's eyes confirm each of these. But I marvel exceedingly at their
humility and gentleness amid such things, and I am astonished at their
obedience in every part toward those who begot them; the speech is about to
consider also the αλ... What then still is lacking to such men and from such
stock toward the full co-inheritance of happiness? Nothing else at all except
to marry fortunately and to be named fathers of children. The one, then, the
marriage, has already been celebrated, and this we hymn today: the most lawful
351 union of two imperial and fair sons and two maidens, of the first lineage,
of incomparable form and character; one might have said a newly appearing
conjunction of two suns and two moons. The other is good offspring together
with many offspring; it still remains, but is well omened by the sequence from
the ancestors up to now.
What do you say to this, divine soul and tongue of the Caesar? Has not the
speech shown, as far as it was able, that your two sons are happy? I think so,
and perhaps not far from the mark, I think. What else, then, is it fitting to
do at this than to rejoice, all others indeed, but most of all those nearest?
Do you then rejoice for me before the others, holy and divine empress; for she
who is foundress of the race must also be foundress of the joy; and you too, O
I know not what I might even call you to address you worthily, most divine
Porphyrogenita, rejoice and rejoice with my master, the wise Caesar. For look,
the prayer has been fulfilled for you and the desire has been accomplished.
You have seen your sons happily led as bridegrooms, crowned, proclaimed to the
senate, to the people, to the tribunal, escorted to the nuptials by the uncle
and emperor who yesterday was escorted in his victory rites. Let all envy be
gone; you have wedded the fair to the fair; one might have said the thing is
worthy of the Pythian. And you too, uncles of the children, rejoice, emperors
and divine empresses; and above all and before all, you the grooms; for you
who have become a cause of gladness to others would much more become so to
yourselves; for the women joined to you have been fortunate in you, and you in
return in those joined to you: those from princes have gained those from
emperors, those from emperors those from princes, the maidens the youths and
the youths in turn the maidens. And you too, thrice-fortunate brides, be glad,
and share the gladness with the august Doukaina 353, the fair sister of the
fair grooms, whose all else you envy, apart from her untimely widowhood.
At these things let the whole subject people exult, or more truly let us
exult; for it is of ungrateful slaves not to rejoice with their masters when
they rejoice, nor to make their joy their own, just as also the contrary; but
turn away from us the ill-omened words, Christ the King. If there is any
sensation, do you too share in the joy, chamber and bridal bower. Let no one
here be gloomy, no one downcast; let all things smile, let all things rejoice,
let all things exult together with what is being celebrated. I defeat your
marriages, O myth, even if we grant that the mythical things are true; for you
join to Peleus Thetis, daughter of the old man of the sea, but I join to sons
of emperors daughters of most illustrious princes; and you in a rocky cave,
but I array the bride in the very palaces; and you have Aphrodite at the feast
and Hera and Athena, but I all the ladies, more imperial than Hera, more
chaste than Athena, and fairer than Aphrodite; and you bring in Strife as if
uninvited and throw the apple into the midst, that apple the origin of the
whole Iliad, and you disturb the drinking and clash the chairs like cymbals,
as the saying goes, and arm the guests against one another, but I too take
Strife into the feast and do not insult the face of the festival: this Strife
not the blameworthy one nor the one who delights in evil, but the one whom a
man would praise when he understood her; so much more august than the myths
are our affairs, even if it were not a myth, and more illustrious. Who then is
so envious and so unloving of the beautiful as not to rejoice at the present
things, and to pray better things for the leaders of the rite, as I do? And I
almost leap at the thing and skip, and in this part I imitate the prophet of
Jesse 355, from whom I also borrow this psalm verse opportunely for you,
divine Porphyrogenita, having the one already, and praying the other: for I
see you a mother rejoicing over children, and I would add also a father
rejoicing with her, my master and Caesar; and I pray to see sons of sons and
again sons of those, unto the third and fourth generation. And for you, O fair
grooms, what better could one pray than that you live fortunately, and be
named fathers of such children as those who begot you were named, and having
been fortunate in the paternal name, not also lose the title of sons, but
always be called fathers and named sons, and leave behind in life works worthy
of ancestral virtue, and drive on to a deep and sleek old age? Rejoice.
