UNFINISHED CHRONICLE COMPOSITION OF GEORGE AKROPOLITES THE GREAT LOGOTHETE.
1 The usefulness of history was defined even before us by those who wrote, and
whatever it occurred to them to say, these things we too would be able to say.
For what newer thought could we find, when so many have written history and
have declared every excellence of history in their own writings? Perhaps,
however, that which is more worth saying than they said in the matters now set
before us, this we would put forward as belonging to our composition. And it
is this: that newer affairs being set before us, which no one has yet
committed to writing, the benefit too happens to be newer, those things having
come into the knowledge of men which the speech that flows at random does not
declare truly. Those then who have written historically of our times have each
made a different beginning: for some of them began from the generation of the
world, others from some noteworthy beginning, whether of Persians or Greeks or
Romans or of any other of the nations whatsoever, each fitting his own writing
to his own purpose. And for us too the work of the composition will be
completed not least in this way. For the things that have happened from the
beginning of the generation of the world have been narrated by many and often,
and one need not even say whether the greater number have spoken against one
another in writing of the affairs of emperors, and changes of lands and
cities, seditions and collisions of wars and enslavements and victories and
defeats and all such things as happen among us. For these things being
many-branched, and perhaps not even known by those who enacted them, scarcely
would the attainment of the truth altogether come to those who narrate.
Rather, even as many things as are known by common report, these the narrator
ought to take up, if he should not wish to wrong the truth as those who damage
obols with small coin or who wish even to counterfeit. Therefore the writer
ought to write neither for favor nor for envy, nor yet for hatred or even for
goodwill, but for history alone and that the things done by some, whether they
happen to be good or base, may not be given over to the depth of forgetfulness
which time knows how to beget. Let our beginning be the capture of
Constantinople, being so conspicuous and known to all that there is not one of
the nations that has not come to know it.
2 When Alexios Angelos was then steering the sceptres of the Romans, those
from Italy marched out against the city of Constantine. And the cause was of
this kind. He had cast down from the rule his brother Isaac and had blinded
his eyes. And Isaac had a son, who, bearing ill the outrage against his father
and contriving flight, goes to Rome and casts himself upon the high priest of
that city, seeking paternal vengeance. And since it happened at that time that
many crowds were gathered into one, some from Italy itself, others from the
realm of the Franks, others from the Venetians, and others from other places,
and the pretext was to go for the liberation of Jerusalem, where the Lord's
tomb is; and the one who had gathered these was the pope, who, being put to
shame by the entreaties of the boy, or rather by the promises (for they were
substantial), hands the boy over to the leaders of the armies, that turning
aside from the purpose set before them they might set him on the paternal
throne and receive from him the expenses, as many as they might spend on the
way and as many as they might spend lingering about the city of Constantine.
These then were put to sea in triremes and hollow ships. And when they had


put in at the city of Constantine fortunately in fair winds, they make known
to those there the boy, the injustice, the commands of the bishop of Rome on
account of these things. For a time then there were words from both sides and
battles. But when the emperor Alexios shrank from such affairs, and especially
when he despaired of those within who looked toward confusion and were sick
with instability, leaving all else farewell he took to flight, saying this:
"David fled and was saved."
3 The inhabitants send an embassy to the Italians, that Alexios the son of
Isaac might be brought within and proclaimed emperor. He is brought in then
upon the covenants which he had promised to the Italians who had raised him
up, and he is proclaimed emperor by the whole people. From this time then the
citizens and the Italians were at peace in appearance, the Italians demanding
the promises and the expenses, and the inhabitants of the city considering the
sum beyond number and insisting that they would not be able to give so much to
the Italians. And it happened at that time that on this account also a
murmuring arose in the city. For Alexios's father Isaac Angelos, the city not
yet having been taken, had given counsel that first a gathering of the sacred
treasures should be made and that from there the debt toward the Italians
should begin, and that the remainder should be given from the imperial
treasuries and from the inhabitants of the city. And in the meantime, while
the dispute was going on and envoys were arriving from both sides, Alexios the
son of Isaac is murdered by Alexios Doukas, whom he had honored as
protovestiarios, and those of the city, finding fault with something of his,
called him Mourtzouphlos. The said Alexios Mourtzouphlos was then proclaimed
emperor by the citizens. For this reason then the Italians, raging still more,
made the enmity against the Constantinopolitans without truce. And it happened
that another counsel too, worthy of praise, came to the citizens. For the
notables and those in office counselled that the Latins whom the city of
Constantine had as inhabitants should be sent away from the city, that they
might not have them within as plotters. And they go over as deserters to the
enemy in many thousands, first assuring the citizens with unbreakable oaths
that they would never meditate treachery against them, but would die with them
if it should befall them, as natives and autochthones. And yet, though they
even offered to take wives and children away to safer places, they did not
persuade. And going out they contributed much to the adversaries, being a
great multitude and knowing the affairs.
4 Forty days then passed and the city of Constantine was taken by these, on
the twelfth of April. For in the tenth year, it being May, they had put in at
the city, and in eleven months the sack happened to take place. And the
greatest city was taken, was taken, one man as they say or even two having
leapt upon the wall from a ladder lying against the mast of a very great
hollow ship. As many things then as happened to the city would be of longer
discourses and not toward the present subject; but it is possible for all to
understand how many sufferings have fallen upon cities taken captive, murders
of men, enslavements of women, plunderings, demolitions of houses, and all the
rest as many as have been established as the work of the sword. When then the
Italians had become masters of the city, setting out as from a commanding
height they overran all the west, and indeed also a not lesser part of the
east. First then they subdue the parts of the west, all fleeing from them,
driven by some God-sent blow.
5 The emperor Alexios Angelos then, whom the account had said fled from the
city of Constantine, coming to Philippopolis and not being received by the
inhabitants, arrives at Mosynopolis and there made his sojourn. And Alexios
Doukas, who had killed the son of Isaac, wishing to attach


alliances by marriage to himself, at the same time murdered the son of Isaac
and at the same time took to wife Eudokia the daughter of the emperor Alexios,
being the last of that man's daughters. For he had three, and the first was
named Irene, whom he also joined to a husband, Alexios Palaiologos, whom he
also honored as despot, but this man had departed before the capture of the
city of Constantine; and the second was called Anna, to whom he made Theodore
Laskaris a son-in-law; and the third Eudokia, whom her father had previously
joined to the kral of Serbia, but upon some passion, as they said, this man
having detected her, he sends her back again to her father; and so it was.
This woman the said Alexios Doukas Mourtzouphlos had taken, leaving his own
wife. When then the city of Constantine was taken by the Italians, he too fled
from there, carrying with him also his wife Eudokia. And learning that his
father-in-law the emperor Alexios was making his sojourn in Mosynopolis, he
went to him confidently. But the other loathed him for many other reasons and
not least for the daughter. Putting on then the mask of kinship by marriage he
receives Alexios, and having prepared a bath he commanded him to bathe
together with the daughter. When then Alexios had come within the bathing-tub,
the servants of the emperor Alexios fall upon him all at once and there they
dig out his eyes. And those who were present said that the daughter, standing
by the door of the bath, washed her father with insults, and he reviled her
for shamelessness and for wanton love. Having become blind then, Doukas
Alexios wandered about the parts of Mosynopolis, going through those places as
a wanderer. And the emperor Alexios, setting out from there, proceeded toward
the parts of Thessalonike. And when the Italians had set out from the city of
Constantine and had gone to Mosynopolis, finding Mourtzouphlos there they led
him away to the city of Constantine; and avenging the pollution which he had
done to the son of Isaac, they cast him down from the column of the Tauros.
6 The Italians then, having become masters of Mosynopolis, gave leave to its
inhabitants, those who wished to remain and be under their hand, and the
others to go away unhindered wherever they might wish. There went out then
some openly and some also secretly, as many as were of the more conspicuous.
And Laskaris Theodore together with his wife Anna had already gone out
beforehand from the city of Constantine, having been honored as despot by that
man. Going then with also his little daughters Irene, Maria and Eudokia to
Nicaea he begged the Nicaeans to receive him within the city. And they did not
receive him. And he, even begging them at least to receive his wife, scarcely
persuaded them. Leaving then his wife he went through the parts around, Prusa
and the parts about it, that he might put these under his hand and might rule
them as emperor in place of his father-in-law Alexios. Which indeed he also
obtained. For in the meantime he also came to the Persian ruler, being
familiar to him, and took on an alliance, and accomplished the things toward
his aim.
7 Two years then having passed and Laskaris being rumored by all as despot,
when a gathering had come about at Nicaea both from conspicuous men and from
the notables of the church, a deliberation came to them, how the despot
Theodore might be rumored emperor. And a patriarch not being present there
(for John Kamateros, who adorned the patriarchal throne when the Italians
became masters of the city of Constantine, had gone off to Didymoteichon and
there made his sojourn, and being sent word by Laskaris and the rest he
refused the coming to them, making the resignation in writing). Michael
Autoreianos is then voted patriarch, being learned and experienced in every
writing, both of ours and of that outside, who also binds the despot Theodore
with the diadem of empire. From this the Laskaris, having been proclaimed
emperor, laid hold of affairs more intensely, and with battles


he met not a few and hard ones. For the Italians, having made the crossing
toward the east, put the greater part of it under themselves. For the whole
theme of Opsikion and of the Aegean and Adramyttion itself had become the
Italians', and Baris and Aulonia, Poimanenon and Lentiana as far as Lopadion
itself knew Italians as masters, and indeed also all the parts of Thynia as
far as even Nikomedeia itself. From this the emperor Theodore was much
straitened. And no less was he pressed also from Romans. For in the confusion
of the capture of the city of Constantine, from one place and another men
happening to be leaders, or even those who were foremost of the others, made
the land under themselves as their own rule, either having set out toward this
of themselves or even having been summoned by the inhabitants for the defence
of the land. At once Theodore, whom they also called Morotheodoros, was ruler
of the city of Philadelphia. And another, Sabbas by surname, was master of the
town of Sampson together with those lying near him. And of all Paphlagonia
David was master, being brother of Alexios who had become master of Trebizond,
who was also named Great Komnenos, who were grandsons of the emperor
Andronikos, born to Manuel his son. But Morotheodoros and Sabbas he pursued
easily, and from this he was master without fear of all Kelbianon, Maiandros,
Philadelphia and Neokastra.
8 But here let my account wait; for it wishes to narrate the things that
happened to the emperor Alexios. He arrived then, as I had said, at
Thessalonike, and was received by his daughter-in-law, who was sprung from
Hungarians, and had been made a wife to the emperor Isaac after the death of
his own wife, whom those who saw her said was very fair in form. The Italians
then having allotted the parts of Romania in many portions, and Baldwin sprung
from Flanders having been proclaimed emperor, and the duke of Venice, who was
also present in person, having had a not small share and having been honored
with the dignity of despot, and having altogether toward the whole, which the
Frankish part acquired, a fourth and the half of a fourth, since the marquis
too had brought a noteworthy portion of the alliance, he is honored as king of
Thessalonike by the man of Flanders, taking also as wife the Maria who has
been shown. By this woman Alexios was received. And after a little, being
detected as intending revolution among those there, he was driven out together
with his wife and his daughter Eudokia, whom also, having come to Corinth, he
joined to Sgouros. And having lingered a little among those there, learning
from some that he was about to be taken, he fled; and meeting with some from
Longibardia he is taken, making his movements toward his first cousin Michael;
for that man too at that time had become master of some part of old Epeiros
and was giving much trouble to the Italians who had arrived toward those
parts. And this man was ruling such a land, for he ruled Ioannina and Arta and
as far as Naupaktos. When then the emperor Alexios was taken by the
Longibardians, he is also sold by them together with his wife. And having
waited a little with Michael he plans to go to the sultan of Ikonion, whom
they named Iathatines; for he was familiar to him. For the said Iathatines,
having fled the hands of his brother Azatines, who then ruled the Muslims, had
come to this Alexios and is adopted by him as a son, and he also fled together
with this emperor. But not many days passed, and a man arrives to him
announcing to him secretly the death of his brother; and he, dressed in poor
rags, arrives with him and, having become known to his own, is proclaimed
Persian ruler. This man also appeared useful to the emperor Theodore, giving
an alliance; for he named the empress Anna as a sister.


9 Setting out with provisions from the land of Michael and using a fair wind,
he put in at that of Attalos. Most gladly then he has been received by the
sultan. And the emperor Theodore was spending time in Nicaea, and an embassy
from the sultan arrives to him, making known the arrival of the emperor and
father-in-law, and that he was unjust, having laid hold of a foreign rule. The
emperor then was troubled at the words. For the sultan had Alexios as a
pretext, but his aim was to overrun the land of the Romans. And his affairs
were, as they say, on a razor. Gathering then his own he tried them, whether
they were attached to him or to the father-in-law. And they said with all
speed that they would either live with him or perish with him. Boldly then he
went out from Nicaea, having also the envoys of the sultan. And using a
swifter course he arrived at Philadelphia. And the sultan, setting out with
himself also the emperor Alexios whom he carried as a bait, made the attack at
Antioch. And this city sits in front of the land of the Maiandros, and his aim
was to become master of it. For this reason he also set up siege-engines and
was sacking the city. And it was indeed in a hair's breadth of being taken.
Which indeed the emperor Theodore also feared (for it was also true, that if
the sultan became master of this, there would be nothing in his way of not
subduing all the land of the Romans). And as if setting the war upon a die, or
rather to speak truly having trusted in the Lord Christ, whose name we the
pious carry as a kind of signal or seal, he hastens the course, commanding
that no one carry either a tent or a burden or any other of the things not
profitable in the war, except however the necessaries, a little food and
clothing. And his whole army was numbered at two thousand, of whom eight
hundred were Italians, men noble and strong of hand, as the time showed, and
the rest were Romans.
10 When then the emperor had come somewhere near Antioch he lets the Persian
envoy go to arrive at his own lord; and he went off and announces to the
sultan the attack of the emperor, being altogether hard for that man to
believe. With oaths then the envoy confirmed that the emperor is present near.
The sultan then, having heard these things, with what speed he had gathers the
forces and arrays himself for the war. The Italians then fall first upon the
forces of the sultan, but the multitude of the Muslims was great; and having
shown works of a strong hand and of a more noble soul, they almost all fall,
having made many times their number the work of the sword. The Muslims then,
having prevailed over the Italians, more easily were masters also of the Roman
forces; and some fled headlong, but a few held out looking to the end of the
battle. The sultan then, having become master of the battle, sought the
emperor, and someone shows this man to him in a state of helplessness. With
what speed he had then he rushes toward the emperor, trusting in the strength
of his body against him. And the sultan strikes the emperor on the head with a
mace, and he falls from the horse; for he had been made dizzy by the blow. For
the horse too, as they say, was entangled by the blow, I do not know if this
one also received a second blow from the sultan. The emperor then, having come
off the horse, and as if strengthened by a more divine power, and standing on
his feet draws the sword from the sheath, and when the sultan had turned from
there and had said insultingly, "Take this man," the emperor strikes the hind
feet of the sultan's horse; for he was riding a female and one of excessive
size. As from a tower then the sultan is thrown down and his head is suddenly
cut off, neither the emperor knowing nor another of those standing with the
emperor by whom he has been beheaded. The emperor then conquers from this,
having been for the most part defeated. For he was not able to go forward,
left with moderate numbers. This became a cause of relief for the Romans; for
from this unbreakable truces


the Muslims made with the Romans. From this the emperor had a cessation of the
battle there, and he occupied himself with the wars of the Italians. And he
also took on his father-in-law, finding him in the battle, and having honored
him as was fitting he both led him away to Nicaea and, having stripped him of
the imperial insignia, commanded him to spend his time in the monastery of
Hyakinthos. Where he also dies. For his wife Euphrosyne died in the land of
Arta, where also her corpse was laid.
11 And the emperor Theodore also prevailed over David who was master of
Paphlagonia, and he brought over Herakleia and Amastris and all the land
around and the little towns. Again my history wishes to write the things that
happened in the west. And that my history may be easily known to all, it is
needful to say beforehand a few things. When the emperor Isaac was ruling the
city of Constantine and his wife had died, he took to wife the woman whom I
had said before was sprung from Hungary, being a daughter of the ruler of the
Hungarians. And since he was about to make the marriage, and this was
imperial, the expenses of it had to be noteworthy. From this from all the
province of the Romans sheep and swine and oxen were gathered; and since the
land of the Bulgarians feeds these more than the others, they were demanded
also more from this. And the tribe of the Bulgarians, being at first out of
covenant for the Romans and having become a cause of many wars for them and of
enslavements and captures of cities and of countless other terrible things,
and these in many years, are last subdued by the emperor Basil, whom rumor on
this account called Bulgar-slayer. Until then the emperor Isaac the race
remained tributary to the Romans, and on account of such a pretext it had
meditated revolt. And a certain man named Asan rises and becomes emperor of
the land, having put under himself all the parts within the Haimos as far as
even the Istros. From this not a few troubles happened to the Romans; for the
Bulgarians, having the Scythian as ally, did many terrible things to the land
of the Romans. Then also the emperor Isaac, being angry, gathering all the
army of the Romans, proceeded against them. Going then along the seacoast and
making the passage through the city of Mesembria, he comes within the Haimos.
And Asan with the army under him enters within the little town whose name is
Strinabos, where also the emperor Isaac, having made his tents, was besieging
the Bulgarians. And he is outgeneralled by them; for one of the Bulgarians,
putting on the mask of a deserter, arrives at the emperor and announces to him
an attack of Scythians, and the emperor, being alarmed by the word, and this
when he was about on the next day to subdue the little town, rises from the
place. He did not then go out by the road by which he had entered, but being
deceived by the Bulgarian he planned to go through the quicker one, as he
supposed. The Bulgarians then fall upon him as he was going through, when he
had come upon a certain narrow way, and they destroy all the army under him,
and they plunder all the baggage of the army, and indeed also that of the
emperor himself. And many of the Romans fall, and those who were saved with
the emperor were both naked and very few. From this the tribe of the
Bulgarians was puffed up, having taken much booty from the Romans, and indeed
also the more honorable of the imperial insignia. For they took up the
emperor's pyramids and bowls of the notable ones and money in quantity and the
imperial cross itself. For one of the priests having thrown it they found it a
little later in the river, made of gold, and carrying in the middle from the
holy wood formed into the shape of a cross, and having very many small cases,
in which were relics of holy martyrs and the milk of the Mother of God and
some part of her girdle. The emperor Isaac then went off to the city of
Constantine.


12 From this not a few troubles for the Romans from Bulgarians, and when
Alexios had become emperor many wars for him from these about Berroia, where
also the protostrator Kamytzes was taken by Bulgarians while fighting, John
the brother of Asan then ruling. For Asan had two brothers, Peter and John;
and John he had with himself, and having cut off a portion he allowed Peter to
rule this. Having then completed nine years Asan is murdered by his first
cousin Ivanos, and his brother John becomes emperor, the son of Asan, John,
being under age.
13 Who became a cause of many evils to the Romans and to the Italians
themselves. For this man was emperor of the Bulgarians when the city of
Constantine was taken. When then the Italians had put all the land of
Macedonia under themselves, Baldwin who first ruled directing their affairs in
imperial fashion, they also sent word to the city of Hadrian, how it might
come under them. For the emperor of the Bulgarians John had anticipated in
subduing Philippopolis and making a great enslavement of the Romans from
there. And the men of Hadrianople not wishing to come under the hand of the
Italians, the Italians march out against them. Coming then into no small
strait the men of Hadrian's city send to the emperor of the Bulgarians John,
that he might join with them. And he receives this gladly, and taking
Scythians with himself he is eager to prevail over the devices of the Latins.
And he himself lay somewhere far from Hadrianople, and he sends out the
Scythians against the Italians. For Italians it is custom to ride on
high-necked horses, in full armor, but Scythians more lightly armed leap more
freely upon the adversaries. Which the Italians not understanding are
outgeneralled by the Scythians, so that even the emperor Baldwin himself is
taken by them and is led away bound to the emperor of the Bulgarians John;
whose head, as they say, served as a cup for the barbarian, being covered
around with ornament. The Hadrianopolitans then did not at once recognize what
had happened; for if they had known, they would have made booty of the Italian
tents. And the Italians who were left, having lit bright lights in the tents
and having set out in the middle of the night, proceed toward the city of
Constantine. In the morning then the men of Hadrian's city, having learned
this, took up the things left in the tents. The emperor of the Bulgarians John
then, according to the things promised to the Hadrianopolitans, set out to
become master of their city. And they refused this. Being then ill at ease he
wished to besiege them, but the Bulgarians are altogether unskilled at siege;
for they neither know how to set up siege-engines nor to think of any other
thing. Setting out then from there, as the Italians had been overthrown, he
overruns all Macedonia and drives much booty, taking the cities with their men
and finally demolishing them, that the Romans might never have a recovery. For
he demolished Philippopolis, then Herakleia, Panion, Rhaidestos, Charioupolis,
Traianoupolis, Makre, Klaudioupolis, Mosynopolis, Peritheorion and others,
which it is not needful to number. And taking the people from there he settles
them about the streams of the Istros, putting upon the settlements the names
from the enslaved villages and cities. He was making then, as they said, a
return for the evils which the emperor Basil had done to the Bulgarians. And
they said that that man was called Bulgar-slayer, and he named himself
Roman-slayer. Having then come as far as even Thessalonike itself he dies
there, being seized by a disease of the side, and as some said, that the death
came to him from a God-sent wrath. For it seemed to him in sleep that an armed
man stood over him and struck his side with a spear; so that a name was also
put upon him from a dog; for he was surnamed Skyloioannes by all. For having
made the race of the Scythians his own and having shared with them a
communion,


and having partaken of a more beastlike manner, he delighted in murders of
Romans. When then he had thus died, the son of his sister, Borilas by name,
having taken the Scythian aunt as wife, became master of the rule of the
Bulgarians, and the child of Asan, John, still being under age, someone taking
him secretly went toward the Scythians.
14 And Michael, whom we have said ruled Epeiros, possessed three brothers,
Constantine, Theodore and Manuel; of whom Theodore was with the emperor of the
Romans Theodore Laskaris. And Michael begs the emperor Theodore to send this
man to him, because he did not yet have a child of age nor even a legitimate
one (for Michael born to him had been born to him from a concubine) and he
feared the untimely of death. And he sends this man to the brother, having
first confirmed with oaths a faith of service to keep toward this man and
toward those after him. And he, having arrived, was with his brother Michael.
And after not long Michael is murdered by one of the servants; and Romanos was
the name of the murderer. The brother Theodore then lays hold of that man's
authority, having also the brothers Constantine and Manuel. And he increased
his rule. For he acquired in addition not a little land both from Italians and
from Bulgarians, and he put Thessaly under himself, and Achrida and Prilapos,
Albanon and Dyrrachion itself; where also Peter, setting out with a very great
army from Italy and travelling toward the city of Constantine, having been
proclaimed emperor by the pope, and having taken Dyrrachion [from the Latin
part of Baldwin, and of Erres after him], he routed most nobly. And Peter was
son-in-law of Baldwin upon a sister Yolanda, from whom there were children to
him, Philip, Robert and Baldwin. Of whom Robert and Baldwin have been emperors
of the city of Constantine, their first brother Philip having yielded the
empire to the brother Robert. There were also sisters to them, of whom one
Maria, to whom the emperor Theodore was joined. And Theodore of Epeiros,
taking his own army, arrays himself against Peter, who had a little overpassed
Dyrrachion and had come to be in the difficulties of Albanon. The men of
Komnenos Theodore then defeat the army of the Latins, so as to make all
utterly prisoners with all gear, and the emperor Peter himself to become the
work of the sword. This then at that time became a great help to the Romans.
15 And the emperor Theodore Laskaris from his wife Anna made three daughters,
Irene, Maria and Eudokia. Maria then, the second, he gives to the king of
Hungary as he was going through his land from Jerusalem, for kinship upon the
son, and the first he joins to Palaiologos Andronikos, whom he also honored as
despot. And after not long the despot Palaiologos dies, as some said from
erotic dispositions, and the emperor takes on as son-in-law John Doukas, whose
surname is Batatzes, being protovestiarios from Didymoteichon. The empress
Anna then had died beforehand. And the emperor was taking a wife from
Armenians; and being displeased toward her he sends her out to her own
fatherland, and he brings to himself as wife the sister of the emperor of the
Italians Robert. For Robert also succeeded his uncle Erres, who had defeated
the Romans much, especially from when the emperor Theodore had murdered the
sultan. For then he had lost the army present to him from Franks, wherefore
Erres, having heard the victory of the emperor, said "Laskaris has been
