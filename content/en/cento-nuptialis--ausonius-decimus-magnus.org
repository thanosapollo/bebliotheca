* Preface
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Read this too, if it is worth the trouble: a frivolous little piece of
no value, which labor never forged and care never filed, without any
sharpness of wit or the ripeness that delay brings.

Those who first played at this kind of patchwork call it a cento. It is
a task for memory alone: to gather what is scattered and make whole what
was torn, a thing you would laugh at more than praise. If it came up at
auction among the little figures at the Sigillaria, Afranius would not
give a scrap for it, nor would Plautus offer his husk. I am ashamed to
have dishonored the dignity of Virgil's verse with so playful a subject.
But what was I to do? It was commanded. And the most powerful kind of
command is this: he asked who had the power to order, the sacred emperor
Valentinian, a man learned in my judgment. He had once described a
wedding in a game of this sort, with verses that were indeed well chosen
and a festive arrangement. Then, wishing to try how far he would outdo
us in a contest, he bade us patch together something like it on the same
theme. Understand how delicate a position this put me in: I did not want
to be set first, nor to be put behind, since others too would judge, and
clumsy flattery would be laid bare if I yielded, arrogance if I stood
out as a rival. So I took it up like one who refuses, and luckily I both
kept his favor as a man under obligation and, though the winner, gave no
offense.

This piece, hurried through in one day with a night of extra work added,
I have just now found among my blotters. So great is my trust in your
frankness and love that I would not even hide from your strictness
things that deserve a laugh. Receive, then, a little work made
continuous from unconnected pieces, one from many, playful from serious,
ours from another's: so that you need not wonder, in rites and stories,
at either Thyonianus or Virbius, the one remade from Dionysus, the other
from Hippolytus.

And if you will let me teach, though I myself need teaching, I will set
out what a cento is. From various places and different senses a
structure of verse is made solid, so that into one line there meet
either two cut pieces, or one line and the next, a middle joined to a
middle. For to set two complete lines together is clumsy, and three in a
row are mere trifles. They are split through all the caesuras that
heroic verse admits, so that there can join either a penthemimeres with
the remaining anapaestic part, or a trochaic cut with the later segment,
or seven half-feet with a choric anapaest, or, after a dactyl and a
half-foot, whatever is left of the hexameter: so that you may say it is
like the game the Greeks called ostomachion. They are little bones: in
all they have fourteen geometric figures, for they are equally
triangular, either with extended lines or of the same face, either with
right angles or with slanting. They themselves call them isosceles or
equilateral, also right-angled and scalene. By various joinings of these
little joints a thousand kinds of shapes are imitated: an elephant or a
wild boar, a flying goose and a murmillo in arms, a crouching hunter and
a barking dog, even a tower and a wine-cup and countless other figures
of this kind, which one man and another vary more skillfully. But the
fitting of the skilled is a marvel, the joining of the unskilled a joke.
With that said you will know that I have imitated the latter. This
little cento, then, is handled like that game, in the same way so that
different senses agree, so that what is adopted seems related, so that
what is foreign does not show through: so that forced pieces do not
betray the strain, dense ones do not bulge beyond measure, gaping ones
do not lie open. If all this will seem to you as the rule requires, you
will say I have composed a cento, and because I then served under my
emperor, you will order my pay to go forward among those in regular
service. But if otherwise, you will have me docked of pay, so that when
the heap of verse has been gathered back into its own treasury the lines
may return from where they came. Farewell.

Receive this in your hearts and turn glad minds this way,

both of one spirit, both renowned for outstanding arms;

both in their prime, a race that war cannot break.

And you first, for it is plain that you go over the deep
under greater auspices: none was more just
in piety, nor greater in war and arms;

and you and your son, the other hope of great Rome,

flower and valor of the men of old, my greatest care,

recalling your grandfather in name, your father in mind and hands.

I do not sing unbidden. Each man's beginnings will bring him
toil and fortune: for me it is right to take up what is ordered.

** THE WEDDING FEAST
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

The long-awaited day was here, and with hymns fit for marriage

mothers and men, young men before their parents' eyes,

come together and recline on purple coverlets.
Servants pour water for the hands and heap the baskets with
gifts of Ceres's labor, bread, and they bring roasted
flesh of fat game. A very long array of dishes:

birds and cattle, and the following goats

are not missing there, nor sheep and wanton kids

and the race of the sea, fallow-deer and fleeing stags:

ripe fruit is before the eyes and in the hands.

When hunger was taken away and the love of eating checked,

they set great mixing-bowls and serve Bacchus.

They sing the rites, they beat out dances and speak songs.

And the Thracian priest in a long robe
answers in measures the seven distinct notes.

But from another quarter the double pipe sounds its strain.

For all there is one rest from work, and all rise
from the abandoned tables: crowding through the glad thresholds,

they run about and take turns, people and fathers,
married women, boys, and voices roll through the wide
halls: lamps hang from the golden paneled ceilings.

** DESCRIPTION OF THE BRIDE COMING FORTH
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1
:END:

At last she comes forth, Venus's most rightful care,

already ripe for a husband, already of full marriageable years,

wearing a maiden's face and carriage, on whom a deep blush
cast fire and ran over her heated face,

rolling intent eyes, and she burns them by the sight.

All the youth poured from roofs and fields,
and the crowd of mothers, wonder at her. The print of her
foremost foot was white, and she had given her hair to the winds.

She wears garments pictured with a weft of gold,

the adornment of Argive Helen: and such as golden Venus
is wont to appear to the heaven-dwellers, and as great,

such was her look, so she bore herself gladly

to her parents-in-law, and sat high, propped on a throne.

** DESCRIPTION OF THE BRIDEGROOM COMING FORTH
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

But from another quarter he came in at the high doors,

a youth whose unshorn face still marked first youth,

his gilded cloak embroidered with the needle, around which much
Meliboean purple ran in a double meander,

and a tunic which his mother had spun with soft gold:

in face and shoulders like a god, and the light of youth.

As when Lucifer, bathed in Ocean's wave,

has lifted his sacred face to the sky: so he bore his face,

so his eyes, and in a mad course he stretches toward the threshold.

Love throws him into turmoil and he fixes his look on the maiden;

he tasted kisses and, clasping her right hand, clung.

** THE OFFERING OF GIFTS
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1
:END:

Boys come forward together before their parents' eyes

and bring gifts, a mantle stiff with figures and gold,

carrying presents and talents of gold and ivory
and a chair and a veil painted with saffron acanthus,

huge silver for the tables and a pearl necklace
and a double crown of gems and gold.

To her a slave-woman is given and twin children at the breast:

to him four youths and as many unwed girls:

all with hair cut in the custom; at the top of the breast
a pliant hoop of twisted gold about the neck.

** EPITHALAMIUM FOR BOTH
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h1
:END:

Then the mothers, pouring forth in eagerness, lead them to the
threshold;

but a chorus of equals, boys and unwed girls,

play in rough verses and speak songs:

"O you joined to a worthy man, most welcome wife,

be happy, having known Lucina's first labors

and, a mother, take the cups of Maeonian Bacchus.

Scatter nuts, husband; bind these altars with a fillet,

flower and valor of the men of old: a wife is led to you,

that she may spend all her years with you for such deserts
and make you a father of fair offspring.

Fortunate both, if pious powers can do anything,

live happy." The Fates, of one mind under the stable will of fate,
said "run" to the spindles.

* Section 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

** ENTRANCE INTO THE BEDCHAMBER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

After they had come into the chamber whose roofs hang with pumice,
they enjoy at last permitted speech.

Meeting they join right hands and lay themselves on the covers.

But Cytherea, and Juno the brideswoman, stir new arts

and urge him to provoke unknown wars.

He, when he cherishes her in a soft embrace and suddenly
has received the accustomed flame and the marriage bed:

"O maiden, a new face to me, most welcome wife,

you have come at last, my only and late delight.

O sweet wife, these things do not come about without the gods' will:

will you fight even against a pleasing love?"

She, long since turned away, watches him saying such things

and delays in fear and trembles that the weapon is at hand

and between hope and fear pours these words from her mouth:

"By you, and by the parents who made you what you are,

o beautiful boy, I beg you, for this one night no more,

comfort me in my helplessness and pity the one who prays.

We sink: the tongue has no strength, the familiar powers
of the body do not suffice, nor do voice or words follow."

But he: "In vain you weave empty reasons,"

and he hurries all delays and undoes her shame.

Thus far, with roundabout speech and circumlocution, I have veiled the
nuptial mystery that chaste ears may hear. But since the wedding feast
also loves Fescennine verses, and a game known by old custom admits
wantonness of words, the rest too, the secrets of chamber and bed, will
be brought out, gathered from the same author, so that we may blush
twice, we who even make Virgil shameless. You, if you please, set a
limit to reading here already: leave the rest to the curious.

** The Deflowering
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h1
:END:

After they have come together under the lonely night, through the
shadow,

and Venus herself has given them the mind, they try new battles.

He raises himself erect: as she tries many things in vain

he seizes mouth and face, burning he presses foot with foot,

faithless, seeking the depths: the branch that lay hidden in the
clothes,

red with blood-dark elderberries and with vermilion,

with head bared and feet woven through each other,

a monster dreadful, shapeless, huge, robbed of light,

he snatches from the thigh and, burning, presses on her as she
trembles.

There is in a recess, where a thin path leads,

a fiery cleft flashing: the dark place breathes out a foul vapor.

It is not lawful for any chaste person to stand on that wicked
threshold.

Here a dreadful cave: such a breath pouring from black
jaws touches the nostrils with its smell.

Here the young man is carried along a known path

and, lying over her, with knots and raw bark
he hurls the spear, straining with his utmost strength.

It stuck and, driven deep, drank the maiden blood.

The hollow caves sounded and gave a groan.

She, dying, draws the weapon with her hand, but among the bones
the point sank deeper to the quick with the wound.

Three times she raised herself, leaning on her elbow she lifted,
three times she was rolled back on the bed. He remains unterrified.

No delay, no rest: fixed and clinging, the tiller
he nowhere let go, and he held his eyes toward the stars.

Again and again he goes and returns the way, and with the womb shaken

he drives through the ribs and beats with an ivory comb.

And now almost at the last stretch, weary, they were coming
under the very end: then frequent panting shakes the limbs

and the dry mouth, sweat flows everywhere in streams,

he slips bloodless, seed drips from the groin.

Be content, my Paulus,
with a wanton page, Paulus:
I ask to laugh, nothing beyond.

But when you have read, stand by me against those who, as Juvenal
says, "They sham the Curii and live the Bacchanalia," lest they
judge my character from the poem.

"Our page is wanton, our life is upright,"
as Martial says. Let them remember, being learned men, that in the
most upright man Pliny there was wantonness in little poems, in his
character a standing censure; that Sulpicia's little work itches, her
brow knits; that Apuleius was a philosopher in life, a lover in
epigrams; that in Cicero's precepts severity stands out, in the
letters to Caerellia wantonness lies underneath; that Plato's
Symposium contains little poems composed on young men. For why should
I speak of Annianus's Fescennines, of the Erotopaegnia books of the
most ancient poet Laevius? Why of Evenus, whom Menander called wise?
Why of Menander himself? Why of all the comic poets, whose life is
strict and whose matter is gay? Why even of Maro, called Parthenias
for the sake of modesty, who in the eighth of the Aeneid, when he
described the coupling of Venus and Vulcan, mixed in αἰσχροσεμνία
decently? What? In the third of the Georgics, on the husbands sent
down into the flock, did he not veil an obscene meaning with an honest
transfer of words? And if the draped severity of some men condemns
anything in our joke, let it know it was fetched from Virgil.
Therefore whoever does not like this game of ours, let him not read
it, or when he has read, let him forget, or if he has not forgotten,
let him pardon: for the story is of a wedding, and, whether he will or
not, these rites do not stand otherwise.
