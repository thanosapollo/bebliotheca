OF CHRYSOSTOM ON VIRGINITY That the virginity of the heretics has no reward.
The Jews turn away from the beauty of virginity, and it is no wonder, since
they even dishonored Christ himself who was from a virgin. The Greeks marvel
and are struck with amazement, but the Church of God alone emulates it. For I
would never call those of the heretics virgins: first because they are not
pure. For they are not betrothed to one husband as the blessed bridesman of
Christ wishes, saying: "I betrothed you to one husband, to present a pure
virgin to Christ." For even if this was said of the whole fullness of the
Church, nevertheless what was said includes those women also. Those then who
do not love the one husband but introduce beside him another God who does not
exist, how could they be pure? First then according to this argument they
would not be virgins. Second, because they came to abstaining from marriage
after they had dishonored marriage. For by legislating beforehand that the
thing is evil they stripped themselves of the prizes of virginity. For those
who abstain from what is base it would be just not to be crowned but only not
to be punished. And one may see these things so arranged not in our laws only
but also in those of the outsiders. Let the murderer be put to death, it says;
but it is not added, and let the one who does not murder be honored. Let the
thief be punished; but they did not also command that the one who does not
ravage what belongs to others receive a gift. And while they put the adulterer
to death they deemed the one who does not break into the marriages of others
worthy of no honor. And very reasonably. For being praised and admired belongs
to those who accomplish good things, not to those who flee evils; for for
those it is honor enough to suffer nothing terrible. Therefore our Lord also,
if someone is angry with his own brother idly and in vain and calls him fool,
threatened Gehenna. But he did not also promise the kingdom of the heavens to
those who are not angry in vain and who turn aside from railing, but he sought
something more than this and greater, saying: "Love your enemies." And wishing
to show how exceedingly small and cheap and worthy of no honor it is not to be
at enmity with the brothers, having set down what is far more than this, to
love them and be fond of them, he said that not even this is enough for us to
be deemed worthy of any honor. For how, when we have nothing more than the
gentiles in this? So that we need another addition far greater than this if we
are going to demand a reward. Do not, he says, because I do not condemn you to
Gehenna when you abstain from railing and anger toward the brother, already on
that account deem yourself worthy of crowns also. For I do not demand so small
a measure of kindness only, but even if besides not railing you say you also
love him, you are still turning about somewhere below and ranking yourself
with the publicans. But if you wish to be perfect and worthy of the heavens,
do not stop at this only but go up further and take a reasoning greater than
nature itself. And this is to love the enemies. Since then this has been
agreed to us from every side, let those from the heresies stop wearing
themselves out in vain; for they will receive no reward. Not because the Lord
is unjust (far be it), but because they themselves are thankless and wicked.
How? It has been shown then that no gift is laid down for the mere flight from
what is base. But they, having thought marriage base, fled it thus. How then
will they be able to demand a reward for withdrawal from what is base? For
just as we will not claim to be crowned for not committing adultery, so
neither will they for not marrying. For he will say


to them who judges on that day: I have not set the honors only for those who
have abstained from the wicked (for this part is small with me), but those who
have gone through all virtue, these I bring into the unaging inheritance of
the heavens. How then do you, having thought marriage to be an unclean and
accursed thing, demand for deliverance from the accursed the prizes laid down
for the workers of good things? For this is why Christ also sets the sheep on
the right and blesses them and brings them into the kingdom, not because they
did not seize what belonged to others but because they also distributed their
own to others. And he accepts the one entrusted with the five talents, not
because he did not diminish them but because he multiplied what was given, and
returned the deposit double. Until when then will you not stop running in vain
and toiling to no purpose and boxing idly and beating the air? And would that
it were only idly. And yet not even this is small as a ground of punishment,
that after toiling much and expecting prizes greater than the labors they are
ranked at the time of honor among the dishonored. 2 That the heretics are also
punished for practicing virginity. And now this is not the only terrible
thing, nor does it stop for them at not gaining the things of the loss, but
others far harder than these will await them: the unquenchable fire, the
undying worm, the outer darkness, the affliction, the anguish. So that we need
ten thousand mouths and the power of angels that we may be able to render to
God the thanks worthy of his care for us. Or rather not even so is it
possible. For how? For the toil over virginity is equal for us and for the
heretics, and perhaps even much greater for them. But the fruit of the toils
is not equal, but for them bonds and tears and wailings and being punished
undyingly, and for us the lots of the angels and the bright lamps and the sum
of all good things, the dwelling with the bridegroom. Why then are the wages
of the same toils contrary? Because they chose virginity in order to legislate
against God, but we do this in order to accomplish his will. For that God
wishes all men to abstain from marriage, the witness is the one who has Christ
speaking in himself: "For I wish," he says, "all men to be as also myself," in
continence, clearly. But the Savior, sparing us and knowing that "The spirit
is willing, but the flesh is weak," did not shut the matter up into the
necessity of a commandment but entrusted the choice of it to our souls. For if
it were an injunction and a law, those who accomplished it would not have
enjoyed honor, but would have heard: "You have done what you were bound to
do," nor would those who failed have obtained pardon but would have undergone
the penalty of the lawbreakers. But now having said: "He who is able to
receive it, let him receive it," he did not condemn those who are not able,
but showed the contest of those who are able to be great and excessive.
Therefore Paul also, walking in the tracks of the teacher, says, "I have no
commandment of the Lord, but I give a judgment." 3 That to abhor marriage is
of satanic inhumanity. But neither Marcion nor Valentinus nor Manes endured
this measure. For they did not have in themselves Christ speaking, who spares
his own sheep and lays down his soul for them, but the man-slayer, the father
of the lie. For this very reason they also destroyed all who obey them, here
weighing them down with unprofitable and unbearable toils, and there dragging
them down with themselves into the fire prepared for them. 4 That the heretics
are more wretched than the Greeks also in respect of virginity. O you more
wretched even than the Greeks. For the Greeks, even if the terrors of Gehenna
await them, nevertheless at least harvest the pleasure here, associating with
marriages and enjoying money and the rest of the ease of life. But for you
there is being tortured and being miserable from both sides, here willingly,
and there against your will. And


to the Greeks for fasting and virginity neither will anyone give a reward nor
will punishment be laid up; but you, for the things for which you expected ten
thousand praises, for these you will pay the last penalty, and with the others
you will hear: "Depart from me into the fire prepared for the devil and his
angels" because you fasted, because you practiced virginity. For fasting and
virginity are neither good in themselves nor evil, but each becomes either
from the choice of those who pursue them. And for the Greeks such virtue is
fruitless; for they have their reward, because they did not pursue it through
fear of God. But you, because you fight against God and slander his creatures,
not only <do not> have your reward but will also be punished. And for
doctrines you will be ranked with those men, because likewise you set aside
the God who is and introduced polytheism. But for manner of life they will
fare better than you. For for them the loss is as far as receiving no good,
but for you even as far as taking on evils besides. And it was possible for
them to enjoy all things in the present life, but you will be deprived of
both. Is there then any punishment harder than this, to receive as the returns
of toils and sweats the penalties? The adulterer and the greedy man and the
one who revels in what belongs to others and seizes the things of the neighbor
have some consolation, small indeed, but they have it nonetheless, that they
are punished for the things they enjoyed here. But the one who underwent
voluntary poverty that he might be rich there, and the toils of virginity that
he might dance with angels there, and then suddenly and contrary to all
expectation is punished for these things for which he hoped to enjoy ten
thousand goods, it is not even possible to say how great a pain he undergoes
in suffering these things contrary to hope. For I think he is scourged equally
with the fire by his conscience, when he considers that those who toiled the
same things as he are with Christ, but he, for the things for which those men
enjoy the unspeakable goods, for these pays the last penalty, and the one who
lived in dignity suffers harder things than the unbridled and the lustful. 5
That the virginity of the heretics is more unclean even than adultery. For the
temperance of the heretics is worse than all wantonness. For that stops the
injustice at men, but this fights against God and insults the infinite wisdom.
Such are the snares the devil sets for those who serve him. For that the
virginity of the heretics is precisely an invention of his wickedness, the
word is not mine but of the one who is not ignorant of his thoughts. What then
does this man say? "The Spirit expressly says that in later times some will
depart from the faith, giving heed to deceitful spirits and teachings of
demons, in the hypocrisy of liars seared in their own conscience, forbidding
to marry, to abstain from foods which God created for partaking." How then is
she a virgin who has departed from the faith, who gives heed to the deceitful
and obeys the demons and honors the lie? How is she a virgin who is seared in
conscience? For the virgin must be pure not in the body only but also in the
soul, if she is going to receive the holy bridegroom. But this woman, with so
many brands, how could she be pure? For if even worldly cares must be driven
from this bridal chamber, as it is not possible to be seemly with them, how,
when an impious mind is turning about within, will the beauty of virginity be
able to be preserved? 6 That the heretics when they practice virginity defile
not the souls only but also the bodies. For even if her body should remain
intact, nevertheless the better part has been corrupted, the thoughts of the
soul. What then is the use, when the temple has been destroyed, that the
enclosing walls stand? or what is the gain, when the throne has been polluted,
that the place of the throne is clean? Or rather not even so has it been freed
from the pollution. For the blasphemy and the base words are born within, but
they do not remain within in the soul, but they defile


the tongue as they are brought out through the mouth, and they defile the
hearing that receives them, and like deadly drugs falling into the soul itself
they eat through the root more terribly than any moth and with it destroy also
the whole remaining body. If then the definition of virginity is to be holy
both in body and in spirit, and this woman is from both parts profane and
accursed, how could she be a virgin? But she shows me a pale face and wasted
limbs and a cheap garment and a gentle look. And what is the use when the
inner eye is bold? For what could be bolder than that look which even
persuades the outer eyes to give heed to the creatures of God as to things
evil? "All the glory of the daughter of the king is within." But this woman
has reversed the sequence of the saying, wearing the glory around her from
without, but having all the dishonor within. For this is the terrible thing,
that toward men she displays much mildness, but toward the God who created her
she uses much madness, and she who does not even endure to look a man in the
face (if indeed there are any such among them) looks toward the master of men
with shameless eyes and speaks injustice unto the height. Their face is of
boxwood and like a corpse. For this reason they are worthy of tears and of
many lamentations, because not idly only but also unto destruction and against
their own head they have taken on so great misery. 7 That one must test
virginity not from garments but from the soul. Cheap is the dress. But
virginity is not in the garments nor in the colors, but in the soul and the
body. For how is it not absurd if we will not test the philosopher from the
hair nor from the staff nor from the sleeveless tunic but from the manners and
the soul, and the soldier not from the cloak nor from the belt but from the
strength and the courage, but the virgin, a thing so wondrous and surpassing
all that is among men, we will, simply thus and as a side-matter, rank among
the virtue of the thing through the squalor of the hair and the dejection of
the face and the gray garment, without having stripped her soul and learned
her habit from there exactly? But the one who arranged the laws of this
contest does not allow it. For he does not command to test those who have let
themselves down into this contest from the garments but from the doctrines and
the soul. "For he who contends," he says, "is continent in all things," all
things that disturb the health of the soul, and "No one is crowned unless he
contend lawfully." What then are the laws of this contest? Hear him saying
again, or rather Christ himself who set the contest: "But the virgin, that she
may be holy in body and in spirit;" and again: "Marriage is honorable and the
bed undefiled." 8 That it is a harm to the virgin to be puffed up against
those who marry. What then is this to me, she says, who have bidden marriage a
long farewell? This, O wretched one, this has destroyed you, that you think
you have nothing in common with this doctrine. Therefore, having used a
measureless contempt against the thing, you have insulted the wisdom of God
and slandered the whole creation. For if marriage is unclean, unclean are all
the living things born through it, and you too are unclean, for I would not
say the nature of men. How then is the unclean woman a virgin? For this is a
second, or rather even a third, manner of pollutions and uncleanness invented
for you; and you who flee marriage as accursed have by this very fleeing
become more accursed than all, having found a virginity more defiled than
fornication. Where then shall we rank you? With the Jews? But those men do not
endure it; for they honor marriage and marvel at the creation of God. But with
us? But you do not wish to hear Christ saying through Paul: "Marriage is
honorable in all and the bed undefiled." It remains then for you to stand with
the Greeks. But those men too will thrust you aside again


as more impious. For Plato says "that he who composed this universe was good,"
and "that in a good man envy arises about nothing toward anyone." But you call
him wicked and a craftsman of wicked works. But do not fear; you have as
partners of the doctrine the devil, his angels (or rather not even those; for
do not, because they persuaded you to rave such things, think that they too
are so disposed). For that they know God is good, hear them crying, now "We
know who you are, the holy one of God," and now "These men are servants of the
God most high, who announce to us a way of salvation." Will you then still
make mention of virginity and be ambitious about the thing, and not rather go
away and weep for yourselves and lament the folly through which the devil,
having bound you like captives, dragged you into the fire of Gehenna? You did
not associate with marriages? But not yet by this are you a virgin. For I
would call a virgin the woman who has become mistress of being married, and
then has not chosen it. But when you say the thing is among those forbidden,
the accomplishment is no longer of your choice but of the necessity of the
law. Therefore we marvel at Persians who do not marry their mothers, but no
longer at Romans. For here the thing seems abominable to all in succession,
but there the license of those who dare these things has made those who
abstain from such unions to be praised. According to the same argument then
one must also make the examination in the case of marriage. For we, since the
thing is permitted to all among us, reasonably marvel at those who do not
marry. But you, having shoved it into the rank of the worse things, would no
longer be able to lay claim to the praises from the thing. For abstaining from
what is forbidden is not yet of a noble and youthful soul. For it is not of
perfect virtue not to do those things which, if we did them, we would seem to
all to be evil, but to be splendid in those things which neither let those who
have not chosen them be blamed for this, and which, those who have chosen them
and accomplished them, not only free from the reputation of the base but also
enroll into the rank of the good. For just as no one would praise eunuchs on
the score of virginity because they do not marry, so neither you. For what the
necessity of nature has been to those men, this the prejudice of a wicked
conscience has become to you. And just as the maiming of the body cast the
eunuchs out of ambition over the thing, so the devil, while your nature
remained intact, having cut off the right reasonings and thus having set you
into the necessity of not marrying, stretches you with the toils, but does not
allow you to be honored. Do you forbid marrying? Therefore a reward will not
be laid down for you for not marrying, but even vengeance and punishment. 9
That to exhort to practice virginity is not of one who forbids marrying. But
you, he says, do you not forbid? Far be it; may I never rave the same things
as you. How then, he says, do you exhort not to marry? Because I am persuaded
that virginity is far more honorable than marriage; yet not on this account do
I set marriage among the wicked things, but I even praise it exceedingly. For
it is a harbor of temperance for those who wish to use it well, not allowing
nature to grow wild. For having set lawful union in front like breakwaters and
receiving by this the waves of desire, it settles us in much calm and keeps us
so. But there are some who do not need this safety but instead of it tame the
madness of nature by fasts, vigils, sleeping on the ground, and the rest of
the hard training. These I exhort not to marry, not forbidding marrying. And
there is a great interval between this and that, and as much as the difference
of necessity and of choice. For the one who advises leaves the hearer master
of the choice of the things about which he makes the advice; but the one who
forbids takes this authority from him. Besides this, I when I exhort do not
blame the thing nor accuse the one who was not persuaded. But you, slandering
it and saying it is base and seizing the rank of the one who legislates, not
of the one who advises, reasonably hate those who are not persuaded. But I do
not


, but I marvel at those who have enrolled themselves into this contest, and I
do not accuse those who remain outside the contest. For accusation would
justly arise then, when someone has inclined toward the acknowledged evil. But
the one who has the lesser good, and does not reach the greater, is deprived
of being praised with that man and being marvelled at, but he would not be
justly accused. How then do I forbid marrying, when I do not accuse those who
marry? I forbid fornicating and committing adultery, but marrying never. And
those who dare those things I punish and drive from the fullness of the
church, but those who do this, if they are temperate, I even continue
praising. For thus two gains come about: one, not to slander the creation of
God, and second, not to pull down the dignity of virginity but to show it far
more august. 10 That the one who blames marriage wrongs virginity. For the one
who blames marriage also undercuts the glory of virginity; but the one who
praises this more lifts the wonder of that and makes it more splendid. For
what appears good by comparison with the worse would not be exceedingly good;
but what is better than acknowledged goods, this is what is good with excess,
which we show virginity to be. As then those who blame marriage damage the
encomia of this, so the one who frees this from slander has praised not this
more than virginity. For also in the case of bodies it is not those that are
better than the maimed, but those that are better than the sound and having no
disfigurement, that we say are fair. Is marriage good? Therefore on this
account virginity is wondrous, because it is better than a good thing, and so
much better as the helmsman than the sailors and the general than the
soldiers. But just as on the ship if you take away those who row you have sunk
the vessel, and in the war if you remove the soldiers you have handed over the
general bound to the enemies, so also here if you cast marriage out of the
best rank, you have betrayed the glory of virginity and brought it to the last
evil. Is virginity good? I too agree. But better than marriage? This too I
confess; if you wish, I also set forth by how much it is better: as much as
heaven than earth, as much as the angels than men, and if one must even force
something and say it, even more. For angels, even if they neither marry nor
are given in marriage, yet are not entwined with flesh and blood nor have
their sojourns on the earth nor endure a crowd of desires, nor need food and
drink nor can a sweet strain soften them nor a splendid sight bend them nor
any other of such things; but just as one may see the heaven at a still noon
clean when no cloud disturbs it, so also their natures, when no desire
disturbs, must remain translucent and bright. 11 That virginity makes those
who truly pursue it angels from men. But the race of men, falling short of
those blessed ones in nature, forces its own strength and contends through
zeal to be equalled to them according to power. How then? Angels neither marry
nor are given in marriage, but neither does the virgin. They stand by always
and minister to God; this the virgin also does. Wherefore Paul also withdrew
them from all the cares through the being attentive and undistracted. And if
they cannot yet go up into heaven as those do, the flesh dragging them down,
yet even in this they have much consolation, receiving the master of the
heavens himself if they are holy in body and in spirit. Did you see the
dignity of virginity? How it makes those who sojourn on the earth to live like
those who dwell in the heavens? It does not allow those who wear a body to
fall short of the bodiless powers, and being men it brings them to the same
zeal of the angels. But none of these things is toward you who so great a
thing


are damaging and slandering the master and calling him wicked. For the
punishment of the wicked servant will await you; but to the virgins of the
church will come the many and great goods, surpassing both eye and hearing and
the human mind. Wherefore leaving those men (for there is enough of what has
been said toward them) let us converse henceforth with the children of the
church. 12 That Paul, having said To the rest I say, not the Lord, did not
show the advice to be human. From where then is it good to begin the
discourse? From the very words of the Lord, which he utters through the
blessed Paul. For one must believe that this man's exhortation is an
exhortation of the Lord. For even when he says: "To the married I charge, not
I but the Lord;" and again: "To the rest I say, not the Lord," he does not say
these things as if his own were other and those of the Lord other. For the one
who has Christ speaking in himself, who is not even eager to live so that
Christ may live in him, who sets kingdom and life and angels and powers and
another creation and simply all things second to his love, how would he have
endured either to utter or even to think anything of the things not pleasing
to Christ, and this when legislating? What then is it that he says, "I" and
"Not I"? Of the laws and the doctrines some Christ gave us through himself,
and some through the apostles. For that he did not himself set them all
through himself, hear what he says: "I have many things to say to you, but you
cannot bear them now." Concerning then "a wife not to be separated from a
husband," he himself legislated beforehand when he was in flesh on the earth.
And therefore Paul says: "To the married I charge, not I but the Lord." But
concerning the unbelievers he himself said nothing to us through himself, but
having moved Paul's soul toward this he was legislating, saying: "If anyone
has an unbelieving wife and she consents to dwell with him, let him not send
her away, and a woman who has an unbelieving husband and he consents to dwell
with her, let her not send him away." Therefore then he was saying: "Not the
Lord, but I," not wishing to show that what was said was human (for how?), but
that he did not give this commandment when he was present with the disciples,
but now through him. As then "The Lord, not I" is not of one opposing the
command of Christ, so "I, not the Lord" is not of one saying something of his
own beside what is pleasing to God, but of one showing this only, that the
commandment is now being given through him. For also when discoursing about
life in widowhood, "She is more blessed," he says, "if she remain so,
according to my judgment." Then lest, having heard "my judgment," you think
the reasoning to be human, he cut out the suspicion by the addition, saying:
"And I think that I too have the Spirit of God." As then, uttering the things
of the Spirit, he says they are his own judgment, and we will not on this
account say the decision is human, so also now when he says: "I say, not the
Lord," do not on this account think the word is Paul's. For he had Christ
speaking in himself and would not have dared in a decision to set so great a
doctrine, unless he were bringing the law to us from there. For someone would
have said to him: I do not endure to be with the unbelieving woman, being
myself a believer, the pure with the accursed. You yourself said beforehand
that you say these things, not the Lord. From where then is my safety and the
firmness? But Paul would have said to him: do not fear; for I said this, that
I have Christ speaking in myself and that "I think I have the Spirit of God,"
so that you may suspect nothing human of the things said. For if this were not
so, I would not have given so great authority to my reasonings. For the
reasonings of mortals are cowardly and their devices unsafe. And the church of
the inhabited world everywhere also shows the strength of the law, keeping it
with exactness, which it would not have kept if it had not been exactly
persuaded that what was said is a command of Christ. What then does Paul,
being sounded in by the Lord, say? "Now concerning the things of which you
wrote to me, it is good for a man not to touch a woman."


One might here accept the Corinthians, because having received no advice ever
from the teacher concerning virginity, they themselves, having gone before,
ask him, already showing from this the increase that came to them from the
grace. For from the old covenant marriage was not in doubt; for not only all
the rest but also Levites and high priests and the great high priest himself
made much haste about marriage. 13 Why the Corinthians wrote to him concerning
virginity, but he did not exhort them before this. From where then did they
come to the question? Those men saw this sharply and well, that they needed a
greater virtue, since they had also been deemed worthy of a greater gift. And
it is worth examining that too, why he never introduced this advice to them.
For they would not, if they had heard anything such, have written again asking
about such things once more. For here too one may perceive the depth of the
wisdom of Paul. For he did not simply nor idly omit the exhortation of so
great a thing, but he was waiting for them first to come into desire and to
take some thought about this thing, so that, having already taken their souls
as made familiar with virginity, he might usefully sow the words about these
things, the relation of the hearers toward the thing furnishing much ease
toward the reception of the exhortation; and otherwise too he shows the
greatness of the thing and the bulk as great. For he would not, if this were
not so, have waited for their eagerness, but would himself have gone before
and introduced it, even if not as an injunction, nor as a commandment, but as
an exhortation and advice. But he who did not even endure to do this first
made it clear to us that virginity needs many sweats and a great contest. And
here too he did thus, imitating our common master. For that man too discoursed
then about virginity when the disciples asked him. For when they said "If the
case of the man with the woman is so, it is expedient not to marry," then he
said: "There are eunuchs who made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of the
heavens." For when the accomplishment is great and on this account is not even
shut up into the necessity of a commandment, then one must wait for the
eagerness of those who are going to accomplish it, preparing in them by some
other manner and without suspicion the wishing and the willing; as then Christ
also did. For he did not cast them into the desire of virginity from the words
about virginity, but having discoursed about marriage only and shown the
weight of the thing and stopped the word at this, he so wisely managed that
those who had heard nothing about not marrying themselves said from themselves
that "It is expedient not to marry." Therefore Paul the imitator of Christ was
saying: "Now concerning the things of which you wrote to me," all but
apologizing to them through these things and saying that I for my part did not
dare to lead you up onto this high peak because of the thing being hard to
accomplish; but since you yourselves, having gone before, wrote to me, I make
the advice with confidence that it is good for a man not to touch a woman. For
why, when they wrote about many things, did he nowhere add this? For no other
reason than this which I said now: so that no one may be displeased at the
exhortation, he reminds them of the letters which they sent. And not even so

dragging all these things after itself, drove them in with itself. Do not then
prefer that which was conceded because of your weakness to virginity, or
rather do not even set it as equal, since going forward according to this

thus prefer wealth to voluntary poverty and luxury to a temperate diet and
avenging oneself to bearing the one who wrongs one nobly. 16 That marriage is

even following exceedingly. For even if these commands are greater than those,
yet the aim of the lawgiver is the same. What then is this? To cut off the
wickedness of our soul and to lead it to perfect virtue. If then he had been
eager for this, not to command greater things than the former but to leave us

would have been of one opposing himself exceedingly. For just as if at the
beginning, when the race of men was more childishly disposed, he had

allowed us to remain on the earth, we would have harvested nothing great from
the condescension, that perfection for whose sake the condescension came about
not having come to us.


17 Concerning the condescension of God. And now something similar has come

the weakness added to the body from it, then henceforth, having freed them

passions destroy its health. And there is need especially in season for the
command to be correspondingly to the passions that lie under, since without

many myriads have come to be? But I again

advises and commands and adds a cause: "For because of fornications," he says.

continence, not uncovering it openly by the word but leaving it to the
conscience of the hearers. For the one who has learned that he is exhorted to
marry, not because marriage is the strongest of virtue but because Paul

marriage are hortatory toward virginity. What then does he say after these

also the wife to the husband." Then interpreting it and making it clearer he

and likewise also the husband does not have authority over his own body, but
the wife." And these things seem to be said on behalf of marriage. But the
truth is that, as wrapping a hook in the customary food, so he lets it into
the hearings of the disciples, wishing by the very words about marriage to
lead them out of marriage. For the one who has heard that after marriage he
will not be master of himself but will lie in the judgment of the wife will be
eager quickly to be freed from the

the Lord shutting them into this necessity into which Paul also then shut the
Corinthians. For "Whoever puts away his wife except for a word of fornication
makes her commit adultery," and "The husband does not have authority over his
own body," have been said in other words, but in the same judgment. And if
someone should learn more exactly that of Paul, he stretches the tyranny more
and works the slavery more burdensome. For the Lord does not allow the husband
to cast the wife out of his own house. But Paul also takes away the authority
over his own body, handing over all his rule to the wife and subjecting him
more than a bought household-slave. For this man it is often possible even to
obtain complete freedom, if he should ever be able, having come into plenty of
silver, to lay down the price to the master. But the husband, even if he has
the most troublesome wife of all, is forced to love the slavery and is able to
find no loosing nor way out of this mastery. 29 That "do not deprive one
another" is of one leading on to virginity. Having said then: "The wife does
not have authority over her own body," he adds: "Do not deprive one another,
except perhaps by agreement for a time, that you may have leisure for prayer
and fasting, and come together again to the same." Here I think many of those
who have taken up virginity both blush and are ashamed at the much
condescension of Paul. But do not fear nor suffer anything foolish. For these
things too seem to be of one granting favor to those who have married, but if
someone should examine them exactly he will find them to be of the same
judgment as the former. For if someone should wish to go over them simply,
cutting off the cause that lies before, the words will seem to be of a
brideswoman rather than of an apostle. But if he should unfold the whole aim

then does he go through this word at more length? For was it not enough,
having shown this more augustly through the things above, to stop the
exhortation at that? For what more does "Do not deprive one another, except
perhaps by agreement for a time" have than "Let the husband render to the wife
the goodwill owed" and "The husband does not have authority over his own

here more clearly through more things. And he does this imitating the holy
Samuel of God. For just as that man with all exactness narrates the laws about
the kingship to the private men, not so that they may receive them but so that
they may not receive them, and the thing seems to be of teaching, but the
truth was a turning away of their untimely desire, so also Paul turns the
tyranny of marriage more continually and more clearly, wishing by these very
words to drag them out from there. For having said: "The wife does not have
authority over her own body," he adds: "Do not deprive one another except
perhaps by agreement, that you may have leisure for fasting and prayer." Do
you see how without suspicion and without being burdensome he led those who
remain in marriage into the training of continence? For at the beginning he
simply praised the thing, saying: "It is good for a man not to touch a woman."
But here he also added an exhortation, saying: "Do not deprive one another
except perhaps by agreement." And why did he introduce even in an exhortation
what he wished to set down, and not in the form of a command? For he did not
say: deprive one another, but by agreement, that you may have leisure for
prayer, but what? "Do not deprive one another except perhaps by agreement."
Because this word became more gentle, showing the judgment of the teacher not
with vehemence this

reasonableness. And not by this only does he console the hearer but also by
compressing the rough into short words, and before the hearer feels pain,
leaping over to the sweeter things and spending time in these more. 30 Why, if
marriage is honorable, the apostle exhorts those who fast to abstain from

care and dissipation, and having gathered himself well from every side thus

of the soul and, checking the neglect that flows around the mind, turns the
whole understanding toward itself. This Paul also, hinting, leads away from
the union and uses the word exceedingly opportunely. For he did not say: that
you may not be polluted, but "That you may have leisure for fasting and
prayer," as if association with a woman leads not into uncleanness but into
lack of leisure. 31 That he necessarily made those who are going to have
leisure for prayer stand off from intercourse. For where now, after so great a
safety, the devil tries to hinder us at the time of prayer, if he takes a soul
dissipated and softened by sympathy toward the woman, what then will he work,
fanning here and there the eyes of the mind? Which, so that we may not suffer

rather to last rulers, and household-slaves meeting masters), whether they do

be burned? To gather the flame in the storehouses of the soul, but to keep the
mind untouched? For no one concedes to her to empty these coals outside, but

the Lord, but the other for the things of the world. Having gathered then

Satan may not tempt you." And wishing to show that not all of it is of that

inclined downward. If you are so a slave of the pleasures, he says, if so
loose as to have been poured out through all toward the union and to gape,
come together with the wife. The concession then is not of one accepting nor
praising but of one mocking and condemning. For he would not, if he had not
wished exceedingly to lay hold of the soul of the lovers of pleasure, have set
the word of incontinence, which is more emphatic and has much blame. For why
did he not say because of your weakness? Because that was more of one
pardoning, but to say that of incontinence is of one showing the


excess of remissness. Therefore it is of incontinence not to be able to
abstain from fornication, unless one hold to the wife through all and enjoy
the union. What then would those say here who set virginity as superfluous?

he commands neither, not for the same cause, but here so that someone wishing
to go up from incontinence may not be hindered as bound by the necessity of a
command; and there so that someone unable to go up toward virginity may not be
condemned as disobeying a command. I do not command to practice virginity, he
says; for I have feared the thing being hard to accomplish. I do not command
to come together continually with the wife; for I do not wish to be a lawgiver

blocking the eagerness further. His leading will then is not to use a woman

Therefore also at the beginning I said: "It is good for a man not to touch a
woman." 35 That he necessarily set himself as an example of continence. And

works according to God, so that if it should not have this, it becomes again
much less than marriage, carrying around thorns in the soul and choking the
clean and heavenly seed.
