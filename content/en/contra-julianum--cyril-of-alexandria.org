An address of the blessed Cyril, archbishop of Alexandria, to the most
pious and Christ-loving emperor Theodosius.
prol.
1 Worthy of hearing, excellent, and beyond all speech are the
achievements of your holy empire, and the incomparable piety which, as a
portion descending upon you from above, you have kept unconquered by the
shafts of envy, through the aptitude for all that is best, ancestral in
you and even of your grandfathers, as is plainly seen in these things. And
it is before me to fit even upon you yourselves the words of our Saviour;
for he said: A city cannot be hid that is set upon a hill; for what is
lifted on high is somehow always followed by being in full view. And what
could be of equal weight with your Serenity? Nothing at all; for the glory
of your sceptres has run through to the highest lot, flashing round all
things with the brightness of good order, and gladdening heaven, that is
the powers above and rational in heaven, with gentleness and piety toward
Christ. For you are so admired in both that, having virtue in them of
equal measure, you have in a manner outstripped every praise. Dedications
then from others toward you, O Christ-loving emperor Theodosius, are
victories, and crowns, and voices of thanksgiving, and the things by which
the powers of emperors would not unfittingly be honoured.
προλ.2 But it was needful that from us also, who have been allotted the
divine ministry, books should be brought, well composed for the glory of
God; for it is always dear among you, and customary, and truly much
prayed for, to support it, and to count those who in any way at all
outrage it as most abominable and to set them in the portion of the most
hostile, but to deem worthy of every honour as many as would choose to
think and to speak the things that make for the glory of God. And this
advantage too I would say is holy, and most becoming to your renowned
heads. For the divine prophet David said somewhere, singing to Christ the
Saviour of all: Have I not hated them that hate thee, Lord, and wasted
away over thine enemies? With perfect hatred I hated them, they became
enemies to me. And the saying has it very much as is likely; for as one
makes a clear showing of goodwill toward you by choosing to be set against
those who, I know not how, from very great thunderstruck folly have chosen
not to love, so the genuine quality of love toward Christ would show
itself in standing up stoutly against those who have slandered his things,
all but crying out and saying what is in the God-inspired Scripture: I
have been jealous with jealousy for the Lord. And of what sort the
dedication of my words toward you is, it is necessary to say.
προλ.3 And grant me pardon, who have chosen to speak not against an
emperor only, but for the glory of Christ the great King, who co-rules all
things with his own Father, to whom alone it would also be fitting that
one must say: By me kings reign. For he himself is the Lord of glory both
in heaven and upon the earth. It is then consistent and necessary that we,
the champions of the sacred doctrines, appointed to this by him, should
raise up against those who wish to wrong his glory the words that are able
to speak with them, and such as would come to

good for those who will meet with them, both a most needful help to those
who have a heart easily carried and most ready to be snatched toward what
is not fitting, and to those well established concerning the faith a kind
of staff able to hold them up toward the need of being more firmly planted
and of having the tradition of the right faith unshaken. Who then is the
one who has fought against the glory of Christ? Very many indeed, as many
as have at times been brought down to this through the perversity of the
devil, and most of all Julian, who once shone with the boasts of empire,
but did not know Christ, the giver of empire and of being able to rule.
For before the times of empire he was numbered with those who had
believed, and he was deemed worthy even of holy baptism itself, and he had
been trained also in the sacred books.
προλ.4 But I know not how men, being foul and superstitious, who had
become known to him, let fall the words for apostasy, then taking Satan as
a fellow-warrior for this they brought him over to the customs of the
Greeks and showed as a servant of unclean demons the one who had been
nurtured in holy churches and monasteries; for evil communications corrupt
good manners, as the all-wise Paul says. And I say that those who wish to
have a settled mind and who keep the tradition of the right faith as a
pearl ought to give no entrance in thought, or place of boldness, to those
who wish to be superstitious; for it is written: With the holy thou shalt
be holy, and with the innocent man thou shalt be innocent, and with the
elect thou shalt be elect, and with the crooked thou shalt pervert. Having
then a gifted tongue, the most powerful Julian sharpened it against Christ
the Saviour of us all; and indeed he has written three books against the
holy Gospels and against the holy religion of the Christians, and through
them he shakes many, and he has done no small wrong. The light and easily
led fall readily into his things and become a sweet prey to the demons;
and those established in faith are somehow sometimes disturbed; for they
think that he knows the sacred and divine Scriptures, since from them he
heaps very many testimonies upon his own words, though he does not know
what he says.
προλ.5 And since very many of the superstitious, falling in with those
who think the things of Christ, revile them up and down, putting forward
his writings against us, saying that they have an unassailable force, and
that none of the teachers among us has ever been able to answer or to
overturn his things, many having urged me to this, and having taken
courage even now as I say to God: Go, and I will open thy mouth, I have
set myself to the need of pulling down a Greek brow that is lifted against
the glory of Christ, and of helping as I can those who have been deceived,
and of showing that he who has accused Christ the Saviour of us all is
also gone astray and does not know the divine Scriptures. And I have
dedicated the words for this to your Christ-loving and most holy head,
which may God always keep in unshaken cheer, prevailing over enemies, and
having all under foot, and sending on the holy empire to children's
children, Christ granting it, through whom and with whom to God and Father
be the glory with the Holy Spirit unto the ages, amen.

1.t Of our father among the saints Cyril, archbishop of Alexandria,
for the holy religion of the Christians, against the things of Julian
among the godless


1.1 book one
The wise and shrewd and skilled in the sacred doctrines marvel at the
beauty of the truth and in every word have made it their work to be able
to understand a parable and a dark saying, the words of the wise and
riddles. For so, so, fixing upon the God-inspired Scriptures a mind
accurate and articulated, they fill their own souls with the divine light,
and having taken up a praiseworthy boast of a right and most lawful way of
life they would be also to others procurers of the highest benefit. For it
is written that: Son, if thou become wise for thyself, thou shalt be wise
also for thy neighbour. But those crooked in heart and having a maiming in
mind, and wholly without share in the divine light, rise up against the
doctrines of piety, and they are very bold of mouth and lift themselves
against the unspeakable glory, and sending out reviling voices they speak
unrighteousness unto the height, according to what is sung in the psalms.
And I think they have fallen sick of this from very great apoplexy and the
ignorance that is in them, or rather, if one must speak the truth, from
the plotting of the evil and first-evil dragon, I mean Satan.
1.2 And the divine Paul, having written, confirms us toward this. But if
our Gospel is also veiled, it is veiled in them that are perishing, in
whom the god of this age has blinded the thoughts of the unbelieving, that
the illumination of the Gospel of the glory of Christ should not dawn. That
the one supposed to be god of this age and thief of the highest glory has
darkened their heart is not unclear; for they have gone astray, as is
agreed, and they set over life gods more than number, demons and souls of
heroes, as they themselves say and have chosen to think. But the tear of
those who think well would have been upon them, if they had chosen not
very much to keep silent the things over which one would be ashamed; but
now their attempt proceeds to this pitch of impiety, that they seek to
inject into others also the disease of so shameful a superstition. For
like some snakes sitting in the crossroads they leap bitter upon those
passing through them, pouring the poison of destruction into the easily
carried; and of them it would be said, and very reasonably: Serpents,
offspring of vipers, how can you speak good things, being evil? And the
Lord would not miss the truth when he says: The good man out of the good
treasure of his heart brings forth good things, and the evil man out of
the evil treasure of his heart brings forth evil things, and: Out of the
abundance of the heart the mouth speaks.
1.3 And I say these things having met with the books of Julian, who made
an unbearable denunciation of our holy religion, saying that we have gone
astray and have departed without understanding from the way that is
straight and blameless, and go as it were against rocks, and make a
worship wholly unwilled by the God over all, agreeing neither with the
laws through the all-wise Moses, nor yet with the superstitions of the
Greeks, or their customs and manners, but as it were a middle life missing
both we have innovated. But I, that we have been freed from the
thunderstruck folly of the Greeks, and that much argument walls off the
things of Christians from their quibbling, would say: For there is no
communion of light with darkness, nor yet a portion for a believer with an
unbeliever. But that we are not at variance with the books of Moses, nor
yet have we practised a way of life that marches against his oracles, I
shall try to give full assurance as I am able, when the fitting time
exercises the argument for us in these things.
1.4 Yet I shall think I must say that for the present; for it is true
that, according to what is parodied among some, one wise man from another,
and it would surely be quite clear that the things of

the first those after them would know, and not rather the things of the
last those before them; therefore, since the children of the Greeks think
greatly of their own teachers, and suppose they frighten some by naming to
us Anaximanders and Empedocleses, Protagorases and Platos, and adding to
these also the others who have been inventors of their unholy doctrines
and, so to speak, springs of ignorance, come, let us say that one would
see them rising against one another with differing opinions, and bringing
in an inconsistent account even upon each of the things that are. Then
besides this let us show that Moses obtained the privileges in time, and
brought in a right and most unerring opinion concerning the unspeakable
and highest of all essence, and made most excellent mention of the making
of the world, and was no unadmirable umpire of laws unto piety and
justice, but that those named wise among them have been last and newest,
and have stolen his things and twisted them into their own words, even if
they have not been able wholly soundly so also to snatch a solemn opinion
and to seem to say something of the true things.
1.5 Some then have come to be after Moses, and others also flourished in
times with the holy prophets after him; of whom those who have chosen to
follow the opinions have been deemed worthy of the better vote beyond the
others, though they have not made the account of the opinion concerning
God wholly free of the false. That then the divine Moses was most ancient,
I mean as to the time of coming to be, and those after him last, we shall
make clear by searching through the books of those who have accurately
written the times. And I say that those who will meet with this ought not
to be quickly sated about the reading and the catalogue of names, or the
enumeration of times, but rather to thirst intensely for the full
assurance upon each thing, which is good and profitable.
1.6 Therefore Noah came to be, a man of those most dear to God, tenth from
the first, that is Adam; to him the God of all commanded, as the flood was
about to be and those upon the earth were to be destroyed with utter
destruction, to join together an ark, and together with dearest ones and
wives and all kinds of cattle, birds and creeping things, to drive in and
to sail through in it; which having been brought to an end, the whole
human race was destroyed and the other of the animals, but he was saved
with his whole house. And when the waters had settled, the ark came to
rest upon the mountains of Ararat, that is of Armenia, and he, setting
foot again upon the earth, performed thank-offerings. The God-inspired
Scripture then is sufficient unto faith; but since some of the
superstitious say that the things among us are idle myths and having from
nowhere either the persuasive or the true, I shall of necessity mention
also the historians among them, Alexander I mean the Polyhistor and
Abydenus. For they themselves have made the narration concerning these
things in their own writings, even if not wholly without blame, but rather
strangely, inasmuch as they have also lacked the doctrines unto piety.
1.7 And Alexander says thus: And when Otiartes died, his son Xisuthrus
reigned eighteen saroi; in his time they say the great flood came to be;
then he says that Xisuthrus was saved, Kronos having announced beforehand
to him what would be, and that it was fitting to build a chest and together
with birds and creeping things and cattle to sail in it. Abydenus at any
rate makes the narration clearer, saying thus: After whom others also
ruled and Xisuthrus; and to him Kronos signifies that there will be a
multitude of rains on the fifteenth of Daisios, and commands him to hide
whatever of writings

there was in Heliopolis in Sippar. And Xisuthrus, having made these things
complete, at once sailed up toward Armenia, and immediately the things
from God overtook him. And on the third day, when it had left off raining,
he sent out of the birds, making a trial whether they might somewhere
see land come up out of the water; but they, the unseen sea receiving them,
being at a loss where they should put in, are brought back again beside
Xisuthrus, and upon them others; and when with the third he succeeded, for
they seemed with their feet full of mud, gods make him vanish from men. And
the ship was in Armenia and furnished to the people of the country
amulets of wood as charms.
1.8 They name Xisuthrus then Noah, perhaps according to the speech of the
Assyrians, and in this again they have missed the true, saying that Kronos
gave an oracle to him instead of the God over all, and it is no wonder; for
they have not come to be in participation of the divine light, nor yet have
they seen with the eyes of the understanding the venerable beauty of the
truth. Noah then sacrificed when he had come forth from the ark, and he
dwelt there together with his dearest ones. And three sons had been born
to him, and very many others from them, and they became a second beginning
of those upon the earth, and they first dwelt together, and in the land
toward the sunrise only, but after this they were scattered everywhere,
the God over all having divided them into many tongues; for he was angry
at the building of the tower. And that those just now named by us have
also remembered this history we shall know no less from what they have
written.
1.9 For Alexander says thus: And the Sibyl says that, all men being of one
speech, some of them built a tower exceeding great, that they might go
up into heaven, and that God, having blown winds upon the tower, overturned
it and gave to each his own speech; wherefore the city was called Babylon.
And Abydenus again: In this they say that the first who rose up, being
puffed up with strength and size and thinking themselves much better than
gods, raised a steep tower, where now Babylon is; and already it was near
heaven, and the winds, helping the gods, overturned the device upon them;
of which the ruins are said to be Babylon, and those who until then were
of one tongue received from the gods a many-voiced speech.
=1.10= The descendants of the blood of Noah then having been scattered,
then time having flown by, first of the Assyrians Ninus the son of Arbelus
is said to have reigned conspicuously, of whom also the city Nineveh is
named, whose walls Semiramis, having dwelt with Ninus, prepared
magnificently. While Ninus then was administering the empire of the
Assyrians, and Europs that of the Sicyonians, they say the blessed Abraham
came to be, a man all-wise and of much learning, and most eager of those
who have been much in earnest to make it his work to need to know the
true, and who is the maker and lord of all. For he was putting off and
sending far away the opinions of the Assyrians, I mean those upon the
polytheist error. And having supposed that the knowledge of the truth
would suffice him toward all well-being, he was received by God; for he
heard: Come out from thy kindred and from the house of thy father, and
come into a land which I will show thee. What manner of life he lived,
and as many as came to be from him, I shall pass by saying for the present,
the time necessarily transferring the argument for us upon other things.
And having passed by those between because of the multitude of the
histories and of the names, I shall come at a run upon the hierophant
Moses himself.
=1.11= For when the four hundred and twenty-fifth year after the divine
Abraham had passed, Moses is born in Egypt, those of the blood of Abraham
having sojourned there. And being still young and having come to a trial
of the wisdom of the Egyptians, he made the human things as it were a

preliminary exercise of the more divine. Bringing down then the times from
Abraham upon Moses, let us begin again here another beginning of years,
setting the birth of Moses before the numbers. In the seventh year of
Moses they say Prometheus and Epimetheus came to be, and Atlas the brother
of Prometheus, and still also Argus the all-seeing. In the thirty-fifth
year of Moses, Cecrops, surnamed double-natured, first reigned at Athens;
this man they say first of men sacrificed an ox, and named Zeus the
highest of the gods among the Greeks, as they themselves say. In the
sixty-seventh year of Moses they say the flood in the time of Deucalion
came to be in Thessaly, and also in Ethiopia Phaethon, the son of Helios
as they themselves say, was burned up. In the seventy-fourth year of
Moses, Hellen so called, the child of Deucalion and Pyrrha, became a
beginning for the Greeks of such an appellation, though of old they were
called Graeci.
=1.12= In the hundred and twentieth year of Moses, Dardanus founds a city
Dardania, Amyntas ruling the Assyrians, Sthenelus the Argives, Rameses the
Egyptians; and Egypt was called both himself and the brother of Danaus. In
the hundred and sixtieth year after Moses Cadmus reigned over Thebes,
whose daughter was Semele, from whom Dionysus, as they themselves say,
from Zeus; and at that time there were also Linus the Theban and Amphion
the musicians. And at that time Phinees son of Eleazar son of Aaron
succeeded to the priesthood among the Hebrews, Aaron having died, as is
clear. In the hundred and ninety-fifth year after Moses they say there
came to be the rape of Kore sung among the Greeks by Aidoneus ruling the
Molossians; this man is said to have reared a huge dog, Cerberus, which he
set upon Peirithous and Theseus who had come for the rape of his own wife;
then they say that when Peirithous had been destroyed Heracles happened by
and rescued Theseus; wherefore they also mythologize that he fled out of
Hades. In the two hundred and ninetieth year, Perseus slays Dionysus,
whose burial they say is at Delphi beside the golden Apollo. In the three
hundred and fifty-fifth year after Moses, Priam reigns after Laomedon. In
the four hundred and tenth year after Moses, Ilium has been sacked,
Essebon directing the things of the Hebrews, Agamemnon those of the
Argives, Uaphris those of the Egyptians, Tautanes those of the Assyrians.
=1.13= There are gathered then from the birth of Moses until the taking of
Ilium four hundred and ten years. And consider with me again those in
order; for I shall speak in a running way, not lingering among those who
came to be in the middle, but rather mooring the argument upon the
necessary of the persons. Therefore in the fifth year of the taking of
Ilium Aeneas reigned over the Latins, and Demophon the son of Theseus over
the Athenians, and Samson was judge among the Hebrews. In the sixty-fifth
year of the taking of Ilium the priest Eli died, according to what is
written in the first of Kingdoms; and the aliens carried the ark away into
their own, the blessed Samuel prophesying, and Saul already anointed unto
king. In the hundred and sixty-fifth year of the taking of Ilium, they say
Homer and Hesiod came to be, Labotas ruling the Lacedaemonians, Laosthenes
the Assyrians, Alba Silvius the Latins, Agelaus the Corinthians. In the
two hundred and sixty-eighth year of the taking of Ilium, Elijah and
Elisha were prophesying, Joram administering the kingdom of the Hebrews,
and Archelaus that of the Lacedaemonians. In the three hundred and
sixty-fifth year of the taking of Ilium, Lycurgus was legislating for the
Lacedaemonians, Agemon ruling the Corinthians, and Procas Silvius the
Latins. In the three hundred and eightieth year of the taking of Ilium,
Hosea, Amos, Isaiah, Jonah were prophesying. And some say Hesiod is not
contemporary with Homer, but came to be in these times,

Azariah, who is also Ozias, administering the things of the Hebrews,
Arbaces those of the Medes, and Procas Silvius those of the Latins.
=1.14= There are gathered then also the years from the taking of Ilium
until the first olympiad four hundred and five, and from the birth of
Moses until the same first olympiad eight hundred and fifteen years. In
the first olympiad, Arctinus the Milesian epic poet is said to have come
to be, and Romus and Romulus were born, Joatham ruling Judaea, and Pekah
Israel. In the ninth olympiad they say Eumelus the epic poet came to be,
and the Erythraean sibyl. In the seventeenth olympiad they say a sibyl
whom they also named Herophila came to be. In the twenty-third olympiad
they say Archilochus came to be, Manasseh administering the powers of the
Jews. In the twenty-ninth olympiad they say Hipponax and Simonides became
known, and Aristoxenus the musician. In the thirty-fifth olympiad Thales
son of Examyas the Milesian is said to have become the first physical
philosopher, and they say his life extended until the fifty-eighth
olympiad. In the thirty-sixth olympiad the divine Jeremiah was prophesying
in Judaea, and also Zephaniah. In the forty-second olympiad Alcmaeon and
Pittacus of Mitylene of the seven wise men, and besides these still
Stesichorus the poet, became known. And in these same times those about
the blessed Daniel came to be.
=1.15= In the forty-sixth olympiad Solon legislated, taking away the laws
of Draco except the ones about bloodshed. In the forty-ninth olympiad, the
Jews being in Babylon, or in the mountains of Persians and Medes (for they
had become captives), Daniel and Ezekiel were prophesying among them. In
the fiftieth olympiad the seven wise men became known and Anaximander the
Milesian, a physical philosopher. In the fifty-sixth olympiad, Cyrus
administering the powers of the Persians, Haggai and Zechariah were
prophesying, and Simonides and Chilon were already known, being of the
seven wise men. In the fifty-eighth olympiad Theognis was named a poet. In
the fifty-ninth olympiad Ibycus the lyric poet and Pherecydes the
historian and Phocylides and Xenophanes, poets of tragedies, came to be.
In the sixty-second olympiad Pythagoras is said to have come to be. In the
seventieth olympiad they say Democritus and Anaxagoras came to be,
physical philosophers, together also with Heraclitus surnamed the dark. In
the seventy-fourth Phrynichus and Choerilus and Diagoras, physical
philosophers. In the eighty-sixth olympiad they say Democritus of Abdera
came to be, and Empedocles and Hippocrates, and Prodicus, Zeno and
Parmenides. In the eighty-eighth olympiad they say the comic poet
Aristophanes, and Eupolis and Plato, came to be. In the hundred and third
olympiad they say Aristotle heard Plato, being of quite a short age. In
the hundred and twelfth olympiad they say Alexandria toward Egypt was
founded, in the seventh year of the empire of Alexander; and at that point
of time Anaximenes and Epicurus the philosophers were born.
=1.16= In the hundred and twenty-fourth olympiad, Ptolemy surnamed
Philadelphus ruling Egypt, they say Sarapis came to Alexandria from
Sinope, the same as Pluto; whence also a temple was founded for the
statue, which the Egyptians also call in the native speech Rhakotis,
signifying through this nothing other than Pluto; in this way they also
made the shrine neighbour to the tombs. And the account about him is
disputed among the Greeks; for some do not think it right that he is
Pluto, but rather Osiris, and others Apis. There being then much fighting
about this, they set up, they say, the idol as from one signal

having come together into one voice, Osirapis, that in the same Osiris and
Apis might be understood. And of both these death is reported and burial;
for they were born men. And long custom, having thrown off the OSI, made
the image be named Sarapis. And this Ptolemy, having been of those most
given to letters, sent one of those close to him into Judaea, writing to
Eleazar, who was then priest according to the laws, that he should send
him all the books of Moses and of the holy prophets, he being very eager
to learn, and should send out together also those able to transfer them
into Greek speech; which indeed also came to be. In the hundred and
ninety-fourth olympiad, Augustus Caesar of the Romans ruling, our Lord
Jesus Christ has been born according to the flesh.
=1.17= From the accurate writing then of the times and also of the
genealogies, how is it not clear to all that the divine Moses was most
ancient of all the wise among the Greeks, and they young and as it were
just appeared? For they came to be long times later than the beginning of
the writing of the olympiads. From this, I think, one may both see more
easily and say truly that, not having been wholly without share of the
doctrines of Moses, nor yet having been unhearing of the God-given and
unadulterated wisdom that was in him, they nibble at the true sometimes,
plaiting the false upon it and as it were mixing mire with most
fragrant myrrh. For what wise and worthy learning is not in the Mosaic
words? or how would one not admire his things? For those who have been
accurate in the curiosities of the Greeks say that philosophy has been
divided into the contemplative science and also the practical, and if
someone should become apt in both, this man they say has run through so as
to seem to have succeeded well in philosophy. See then, see Moses being
such; for he speaks of God as no other the things concerning the highest
of all essence and incomparable glory and the superiority over all, and
shows the one and only God as maker and lord of all. And to those for whom
the good is in word and the matter of well-living has been an object of
zeal, he is seen legislating the best and most excellent of all things and
the things through which they would be as venerable as possible.
=1.18= But perhaps someone will say: Yes, the things of Moses are older
than those of the wise among the Greeks; yet it is not still also true to
say that they would be caught having stolen from the wisdom that was in
him or having been wholly recast. Therefore if the argument from us does
not have much likelihood, let those fond of hearing put it to the test.
The historians among them, going round as one may say the whole earth,
always wished to learn something that they might seem to know many things;
for they made it an ornament of their own writing to keep silent nothing
of the things that had come to be. Then how would such men, and accustomed
to be well-learned, have neglected the need to learn thoroughly histories
so solemn, and an accurate explanation of most ancient doctrines and laws?
And yet Pythagoras of Samos and Thales the Milesian, having spent no small
time in Egypt, and having collected the things from there and having
gathered the heap of learnings which they are said to have had, were
brought back to the land that bore them. And also Plato himself, son of
Ariston, says in the Timaeus that Solon from Athens came to the land of
the Egyptians, and learned from one of the false prophets there, or
priests, saying: O Solon, Solon, you Greeks are always children, and there
is no old Greek, and you are all young in souls. For you have in
yourselves no ancient opinion, nor any learning grey with time; but it has
escaped you, because for many generations you end in voiceless letters.
=1.19= But I think one would also see through these things that the things
of Christians are of ancient dignity; for not yet was there knowledge of
letters among the Greeks, Cadmus barely

having brought them over to them from Phoenicia, except that the things of
Moses had been written; and Solon the inventor of the laws at Athens, and
also Plato himself, having come to be in Egypt in order to seem to know
something more than all, have surely also themselves marvelled at the
things of Moses. And that Moses was most well known to the historians of
the Greeks it is possible to see from the very things they have written.
For Polemon in the first of the Hellenic histories remembered him, and
Ptolemy of Mendes and also Hellanicus and Philochorus and Castor and
others besides these. And Diodorus, having been curious about the things
of the Egyptians, says he heard from the wise there concerning him, and he
has written word for word thus: For after the ancient constitution of life
in Egypt, which is mythologized to have come to be in the time of gods and
heroes, they say a man great in soul and newest in life of those remembered
among the Jews, Moses, the one called god, persuaded the multitudes first
to use written laws. For since they beheld Moses become full of every
virtue, they also named him god, honouring him, I think, some of those in
Egypt, or perhaps having learned what was said to him by the God over all:
Behold, I have given thee as a god to Pharaoh.
=1.20= Concerning antiquity then, I think, and also that the things of
Moses rise before the wisdom honoured among the Greeks and have the older
glory, a sufficient argument has been worked out by us, having an
irreproachable and clear demonstration; and I say that after this we must
be curious about the opinion of the Hebrews which they have had concerning
God and the generation or construction of the world, then thus also make
clear to those who will meet with it what seemed good to the wise of the
Greeks. For where they agree with the words of the God-inspired Scripture,
one would see them being better than themselves and agreeing with one
another; but where each makes the account of his own inventions, it is
easy to show them differing and drunk with strange opinions. For it was
not possible at all to be able to behold the things above mind and the
words in us, unless the God who rules all both put light into mind and
engender wisdom and widen the tongue and grant them, so far as is allowed,
both to think and to speak something of the unspeakable things concerning
him. And such grace would not come indiscriminately to all, but rather to
those who would be both beyond the passions of the flesh and freed from
earthly uncleanness, sound in mind and skilled in the deeds of manhood
unto piety. And the God of all whets us toward this, saying through the
voice of David: Be still and know that I am God, and also our Lord Jesus
Christ: Blessed are the pure in heart, he says, for they shall see God.
And the nature above all would be seen not with the eyes of the body, but
with the eyes of the understanding hidden within, the curiosity putting
into it a thin and accurate thing and drawing the rays of the vision of
God with the imaginations above sense.
=1.21= The men then who came to be from Adam and until Noah have honoured
one God, the one by nature and truly maker and Lord of all; for no one has
been accused as having inclined toward other gods or as having dedicated
worship to unclean demons. But after the flood and the building of the
tower and the differences of the tongues, those scattered over the earth
have had even the opinion concerning God itself inconsistent. For having
removed the mind from the truth and having moored it in the temporary and
earthly affairs and in the pleasures of the body, they went down into
every most outlandish thing, and as it were they divided among themselves
the charges of the polytheist error. Some then supposed heaven to be God,
and others sun and moon, and besides

the stars and the elements of the world, fire and water, air and earth,
from which the several things are said to have been put together; and
others again, having slipped toward the last ignorance of all, went down
into this vulgarity of reasonings, so as even to raise temples and
precincts wrought round with the costliness of materials, and to set in
them sacred seats and stelae of men, and having wept over the dead to
think these also gods and to dare to honour them with drink-offerings and
sacrifices. Very many then in life are those who fasten upon the elements
of the world the glory that befits God alone; and the Chaldaeans most of
all others have been held by such a writing, and they measure most
curiously the motion of the stars, and they make the flights of birds a
pretext of divination.
=1.22= But, as I already said, the divine Abraham was different from their
senselessnesses; for he did not think it right to choose to number the
highest nature of God, beyond every thing, among things in sense and
visible, nor yet, shutting it up in the creatures, did he miss the truth,
but rather, carrying it up on high and setting it above every thing that
has been called toward generation, he was reasonably marvelled at. In this
way he was also taken up by God; for the one sought made himself manifest
to him thereafter. Let us see then of what sort the opinion of the
forefather Abraham is seen to be, then who of those after him will be
sufficient toward an explanation of the faith and opinion that was in him;
but I think Moses would suffice us toward this, who was his descendant and
who thought it right both himself to think and to hand down in writing
what he knew him to have thought.
=1.23= Therefore, composing the book of Genesis, he said that some had
sacked the things of the Sodomites, then having Lot as a spear-taken
together with the others they rejoiced as having conquered, but they
failed of the hope, Abraham marching against them unexpectedly, and having
taken them by force, and releasing from captivity those once caught. And
when he was returning from the slaughter of the five kings (for it is
written thus), the king of Sodom, Chodologomor, was asking him saying:
Give me the men, and take the horse for thyself, but he, not enduring to
have as a wage of deeds of manhood the losses of others, made the matter
an oath saying: I will stretch out my hand toward God the most high who
created heaven and earth, if from a thread unto a shoe-latchet I shall
take of all thy things, that thou mayest not say that I have enriched
Abraham, and he crowned him as having conquered. And the king of Salem,
that is Melchisedek, blessed saying: Blessed Abraham to God the most high
who created heaven and earth, and blessed is God the most high who
delivered thine enemies under thy hand. And sending one of those close to
him, or of the most well-disposed household servants, into Mesopotamia and
commanding him to become a bringer of a bride for Isaac, I will make thee
swear, he says, by the God of heaven and the God of the earth. One then
and only God the divine Abraham knew and clearly confessed to be, and he
named him most high and maker of all things both of those in heaven and of
those upon earth.
=1.24= The height then is not perceptible, nor yet understood in place,
nor such as one would take according to body, but in standing over all in
a manner befitting God, and that both the things in the heavens and the
things upon the earth have been made by him, and the firmament itself and
also the earth, I mean the element itself. And if all things have come to
be by him, surely he is also other than all things, and other according to
nature, since he is neither generated, nor yet from things that are not,
but rather ungenerated and eternal and most ancient of all time and
beginning of all. One God then, as I said just now, the divine Abraham
clearly knew to be, and to him alone he made the pure and unprofaned
worship. And when

time whetted his understanding unto increase of the things rightly known,
then indeed, then he was taught more thinly the words concerning the
Godhead; for he was not only learning thoroughly that there is one God,
and none other besides him, but that also in a holy and consubstantial
Trinity the fullness of the one and unstained nature is understood, as
still in types, and all but also perceptibly, he was taught. For what
cause? Because to those just called into knowledge of truth, and not
having the understanding practised in the contemplations upon it, the
light of the vision of God somehow seems unapproachable and truly is. For
such knowledge needs a mind both established and exercised and knowing how
to see sharply, and besides these having first housed faith within; for
this is a foundation and unshaken under-base of the words concerning the
Godhead. And the prophet Isaiah confirms us toward this saying: If you do
not believe, you shall not understand.
=1.25= And that those just now approaching such thin and as it were filed
thoughts, those concerning God clearly, wholly need the training through
riddles, how would one doubt, when not even the wise among the Greeks have
been ignorant of the matter? Porphyry at any rate, having among them no
ignoble glory upon worldly education, in the first discourse of the
Philosophic History says some such thing concerning those named wise, or
who have had a supposition of seeming wisdom: For not being able, he says,
to hand down clearly in speech the first forms and the first principles,
because of their being hard to think through and hard to bring out, they
came upon numbers for the sake of a well-signed teaching, having imitated
the geometers and the grammarians; for as these, wishing to hand down the
powers of the elements and these themselves, came upon the characters,
saying these to be elements toward the first teaching, later however they
teach that these characters are not elements, but through these there
comes to be a thought of the elements toward truth, and the geometers, not
being able to present the bodiless things in speech, come upon the
drawings of the figures, saying this is a triangle, not wishing this that
falls under sight to be a triangle but such a thing, and through this they
present the thought of the triangle; and upon the first words and forms
also the Pythagoreans did the same; not being able to present in speech
the bodiless things and the first principles, they came upon the showing
through numbers, and thus they named one the word of unity, and that of
sameness and of equality, and the cause of the breathing-together and of
the sympathy of all and of the salvation of that which is according to the
same and in like manner.
=1.26= Since then the proper and excellent things of the Godhead are hard
to bring out and hard to express and not ready toward beholding, and
whatever words would come to be concerning it would be caught quite small
and going behind the truth, through figures and types more clear, as in a
mirror and in a riddle, we learn the things concerning it. Thus we say the
divine Abraham was trained; for it is written thus concerning him. And God
appeared to him toward the oak of Mambre as he sat upon the door of his
tent at noon; and looking up with his eyes he saw, and behold three men
stood over him, and seeing he ran to meet them from the door of his tent
and worshipped upon the earth and said: Lord, if I have found grace before
thee, pass not by thy servant. And after other things: And he said, it
says, to him: Where is Sarra thy wife? And he answering said: Behold in
the tent. And he said: Returning I will come to thee, according to this
time into seasons, and Sarra thy wife shall have a son. Behold then very
clearly he says that God appeared to him, yet that those seen were three
men,

and that the divine Abraham ran up not as to say to three: Lords, if I
have found grace before you, pass not by your servant, but naming the
three Lord in the singular, as thinking it right that one should put in
with him, since also as being one the three were saying as again from the
person of one: Where is Sarra thy wife? and the returning I will come
according to this time into seasons. See then, see those who appeared to
him being three and each existing in his own subsistence, yet by the word
of consubstantiality gathered into one and having been eager to make the
discourses thus.
=1.27= But the images of such things are somehow dim, and much worsted by
the truth, yet they have what is needful unto a leading by the hand upon,
I mean, the things above mind and speech. Yet into the most pure
understandings the light of the vision of God enters, and as from the
perceptible we somehow fly up toward the things lying above sense and
above the power of the words in us. For there is confessedly one nature of
Godhead, that over all and through all and in all, and it is widened
noetically into a holy Trinity, venerable and consubstantial, into Father
I mean and also the Son and into the Holy Spirit. Yet even if each of
those named exists particularly, being in truth this which it also is said
to be, still the word of consubstantiality gathers them into an
unvarying nature; for the Son has been begotten from the Father and is in
him and from him naturally, and the Spirit also proceeds, being proper to
God and Father and likewise of the Son; for the Father sanctifies through
him that which is by nature to be sanctified. The forefather Abraham then
was not ignorant of the one worshipped in holy Trinity, the maker of earth
and heaven and of all and having the power over all. And those who came to
be from him, Isaac I mean and Jacob, have not thought otherwise, but going
in the track of the ancestral virtue they have become zealots of his
faith. And at any rate the blessed Moses, explaining the things concerning
them in the book of the Cosmogony, says that the God of all appeared to
Jacob, and confirming him toward good hopes said: I am the God of Abraham
thy father and the God of Isaac. And Jacob himself also confessed saying:
If the Lord give me bread to eat and a garment to put on, the Lord shall
also be to me unto God.
=1.28= And these things suffice concerning them; and I say that we must
hereafter also let the argument go upon the divine Moses himself. For he
too proclaims one God by nature and truly, not having been ignorant of him
through whom all things have been brought toward generation, I mean his
living and hypostatic Word and the life-giving Spirit in God and from him,
that is sent through the Son to the creation; For he said that: In the
beginning God made heaven and earth, and having shown him more summarily
and as in this short word maker of all, he works out the narrative, and he
has shown that through the living Word of the God who rules all the things
that once were not were brought toward existence, and they are also
given life in the Spirit. For God said, it says: Let there be light, and
there was light; Let there be a firmament in the midst of the water, and
it was so; and upon each of the things that came to be one would see the
Father saying Let this perhaps or that come to be, and bringing through
the Word toward generation whatever he wishes without any delay; for the
Word of God is living and active, and if he only nod, what seems good is.
And he said that: And the earth was invisible and unfurnished and darkness
over the abyss, and a spirit of God was borne upon the water; therefore he
remembers also very clearly Father and Son and Holy Spirit, and in other
ways also he proclaims the one Godhead by nature and truly in a holy and
consubstantial Trinity. For when

he was sending the fire upon the Sodomites who had sinned unbearably, the
Lord, it says, rained upon Sodom fire and brimstone from the Lord.
=1.29= But I think I must also bring that into the midst, I would say, of
the things most profitable to those who will meet with it. For it is
written thus concerning the construction of man: And God said: Let us make
man according to our image and according to likeness, then after a little:
And God made man, according to the image of God he made him. For the Son
is image of God and Father, toward whom we also have been formed
noetically, and this the nature of man has been enriched with as
excellent; for the beauty of him who made it flashes in it. Then what
would those say who rise against the words from us, and who feign piety
through agreeing that there is one and only God, yet not that he has also
begotten from himself the Son? To whom did God say: Let us make man
according to our image and according to likeness? Would one not reckon
that the holy and consubstantial Trinity itself makes the words concerning
these things toward itself, as the all-wise Moses having wished to show
that the construction of man did not come to be unconsidered with God, and
all but also honoured with pre-counsels? For the divine and unstained mind
would not need consideration or doubt and search upon anything; for as
soon as it has willed something, this also is, rightly and irreproachably.
But the nature of man has been honoured, as I said, even as with some
pre-counsels.
=1.30= But let us not keep silent the words of the godless. For they would
perhaps say toward these things at once: For it is not, as you yourself
think and think it right to hold, that the Father has spoken to his own
Word and to the Spirit, but to the second gods with himself and who are in
lesser things. And yet how is it not clear to all that even to those
accustomed to philosophize the things of the Greeks it seemed good to
agree that there is one God, the maker of all and beyond all according to
nature, and that other gods, as they themselves say, both noetic and
perceptible, have been made by him and brought toward generation? And at
any rate Plato says most clearly: It is then according to my opinion first
to be divided what that is which always is, having no generation, and what
that which becomes, but never is. That then which is graspable by
knowledge with reason, always being according to the same, and that again
which is opinable by opinion with irrational sense, becoming and
perishing, but never being. For that which always is, having no
generation, he names the highest nature and better than having been made,
I mean the God of all who truly is; for thus he himself also somehow says
to the hierophant Moses: I am he who is. And that which becomes, but never
is, that which has been brought from not being into existence by some
unspeakable and unthinkable power of God who wrought this whole. It is
fixed then that it has also been shown to me very clearly, not from the
sacred Letters among us only but also through what they themselves have
chosen to think and to say, that all things were brought by God toward
being, and he himself has been allotted a nature not of the same stock
with the things that have come to be. For what has been made sits under
and is surely somewhere naturally in lesser things than him who makes.
=1.31= Our argument concerning these things being thus, what then do those
say who have supposed that the God of all spoke to other gods: Let us make
man according to our image and according to likeness? For if he wished to
mould the rational animal upon earth as toward the image of the creatures,
why does he bring in their own person, saying let us make and according to
our image? But if he judged that it must be formed toward the most
God-befitting beauty of himself and of him alone, why does he receive in
with it the imitation of others, or the form, or however one would speak
well concerning these things? For not in identity

of nature and superiority and worth would maker and creation be reckoned,
generated and ungenerated nature, incorruptible and under corruption, if
it is not falsely said that that which is understood in generation would
wholly and altogether lie under also the need of being destroyed.
=1.32= For the divine Moses, looking toward the things that would be with
the mystic teachings of the Holy Spirit, took thought beforehand of the
things according to us, and in what manner I shall say; for since in
Father and Son and Holy Spirit, that is in a consubstantial Trinity, the
one and unspeakable and unthinkable nature of God is understood, lest
someone, carried aside from very great folly upon what is not fitting,
should say that man has been made according to image and likeness of God,
yet not still also of the Son, and it was surely likely also to suppose
the reverse, and to say that man came to be according to likeness of the
Son, yet not still also of the Father, having seen beforehand, as I said,
the inventions that would sometimes come to be from some, he said that the
holy Trinity itself said toward itself: Let us make man according to our
image and according to likeness, that he might be understood formed toward
the whole, noetically clearly, the unspeakable nature of the Godhead. But
they, though seeming very wise, think these things to be some idle talk,
and having through very great thunderstruck folly the light of the truth
unassailable, they perhaps contend that he spoke the voice to some
falsely-named and bastard gods. And yet how is it not worth considering
that the maker by nature would not have granted the surpassing dignity of
his own glory and superiority to the nature of generated things? And we do
not say, I suppose, that he grudged from somewhere, but that the nature of
the things made would never arrive at the superiority of the divine
dignities, nor would it, I think, be enriched with, and this essentially,
any of the things that have grown most of all only and particularly upon
the unspeakable nature.
=1.33= And it is outlandish also otherwise to think that the king and lord
of all said to other gods: Let us make man according to our image and
according to likeness. For what had he suffered that he made as it were
fellow-workers and helpers upon the construction of man alone, though he
had improvised all the other creation, I mean Angels and Lordships,
Principalities, Authorities, noetic Powers, heaven and earth, sun and
moon, stars and light, and in a word all things both in the heavens and
upon the earth? Would one then say that, having suffered lack of strength,
or not having sufficient energy, he receives in those who will help unto
this? And yet how would it not become a charge of the last folly for some
to choose to think thus? For the divine is all-powerful, and complete in
itself toward every thing, and nothing is unaccomplishable for it. Leaving
then the nonsense in these things, let us go upon that, that one must
agree that in a holy and consubstantial Trinity the fullness of the
unspeakable Godhead is understood, and we have been formed toward the true
and most accurate image of the Father, that is toward the Son, and his
divine beauty is sealed in our souls through participation of the Holy
Spirit; for it is in us, as the Son himself: For the Spirit is the truth,
according to what is written.
=1.34= Thus the all-wise Moses has truly initiated us, and the holy
prophets and apostles and evangelists who came to be after him have not
thought other things besides him. One and the same manner of theology is
seen in all, and one would not find them having fought against one another
in any way at all. For having truly spoken of God they speak the things
from one, the Holy Spirit, and our Lord Jesus Christ does not allow us to
doubt toward this, saying most clearly in them: It is not you who speak,
but the Spirit of our Father that speaks in you. Having then unfalsified the
opinion concerning God that comes down to us from above and from the holy
fathers, we boast upon it and we are not accustomed to yield it to those
from the blood of Israel as belonging rather

to them, far from it; and we also reckon ourselves among children of
Abraham. For we are the sons according to promise, and Paul writing will
persuade: For not all who are from Israel, these are Israel, nor because
they are seed of Abraham are they altogether children, but the children of
the promise are reckoned unto seed. Very many things then it is no
hardship to bring upon what has been said concerning our holy religion and
blameless opinion which we have made upon the God who rules all. But since
the argument of good craft persuades us also to assign each of the
contemplations to the fitting bounds, leaving those things for now I shall
rather turn upon the need to say the things of the Greeks, and to be
curious about the things among them and to make an accurate test of the
opinion of each.
=1.35= Orpheus then, son of Oeagrus, they say became most superstitious of
the others and anticipated the poetry of Homer, inasmuch as he was also
older in times, and wove songs and hymns to the falsely-named gods and
took no unadmirable glory upon this, then having condemned his own
doctrines, and having understood that, all but having left the highway, he
had come to be in a casting-out of the straight road, he changed over
toward the better things and chose the truth instead of the false, and
spoke thus concerning God: I will speak to whom it is lawful, and shut the
doors, uninitiated, all alike; but do thou hear, offspring of light-bearing
Mene, Musaeus; for I will declare true things; and let not the things that
appeared before in thy breast deprive thee of dear life. And looking to
the divine Word, sit by this, directing the noetic hollow of the heart,
and well set foot upon the path, and look only upon the lord of the world;
One is, self-generated, all things have been made offspring of one, and in
them he himself prevails; nor does any of mortals see him, but he himself
is seen of all. And after other things again: For he himself has been
fixed into the brazen heaven upon a golden throne, and with his feet he
has stepped upon the earth; and he has stretched his right hand upon the
limit of Ocean from every side; for the long mountains tremble about him,
and rivers, and the depth of the grey bright sea. One then, and this
self-begotten, through all and over all, he names God, and he says heaven
is a throne for him, yet that the earth lies under his feet, wishing, I
think, to signify by this the unboundedness of the unstained nature, and
that it comes through all and fills all; and that it grasps the whole he
would hint by the hand being stretched round even the limits of Ocean
themselves, mountains and sea and simply the whole world trembling. For as
the God-inspired Scripture says: All the earth calls the truth, and heaven
blesses it, and all the works are shaken and tremble. The things of
Orpheus then are in these.
=1.36= And Homer, best of the poets, someone would say, I think, differs
little from those who dance on stages; for the lovers of spectacle say
that those, being accustomed to give form to the natures of things by
figures and nods, seem to the beholders all but to show the things
themselves as present; but he has practised making virtues and vices into
gods, and the parts of the world, and the nature of the elements itself.
And at any rate he says the gods were once brought against one another in
Ilium, and he declares clearly who against whom: For in truth against lord
Poseidon stood Phoebus Apollo, having winged arrows; and against Enyalios
the goddess grey-eyed Athena; and against Hera stood the noisy Artemis of
the golden distaff, arrow-pourer, sister of the Far-shooter; and against
Leto stood the strong helper Hermes; and against Hephaestus the great
deep-eddying river. See then in these, and very clearly, that he is eager
to show in a manner the virtues rising against the vices. For it is
customary for the poets of the Greeks to call Ares both mindless and
raging, and besides this also turning to another, and Athena both most
gainful and

of many devices, and Leto likewise to suppose forgetfulness, and Hermes
memory and speech. Then he takes hold of physiology and explains the
difference of the elements toward one another saying: For in truth against
lord Poseidon stood Phoebus Apollo having winged arrows; and against Hera
stood the noisy one of the golden distaff ... and against Hephaestus the
great deep-eddying river. For here too he seems to me to have said Poseidon
the wet essence, and Apollo the sun, and Hera the air, and the noisy one
of the golden distaff the moon, and Hephaestus fire, and the river as in
the rank of the cold.
=1.37= But even if the love of myth is very much with him, still we shall
find him not wholly ignorant of the truth; for he said somewhere again:
... not even if a god himself should undertake for me, having scraped off
old age, to set me young in youth ... For he does not say, I suppose, even
if one of gods should promise me the selling-off of old age, and youth
called back; but he has kept the matter for the God over all alone,
assigning to his powers alone the being able to succeed in all things
without toil, even the things beyond hope and speech according to us. Not
even if, he says, a god himself should undertake for me; for the God
himself would not signify upon one of those fashioned in myths, but him
alone who truly is God. But it seemed good to Homer to think and to speak
thus, and let us go already also upon those themselves who are pompous and
with brows lifted, and who have obtained no ignoble glory of seeming
wisdom among the children of the Greeks.
=1.38= Porphyry then, who poured bitter words upon us, and all but danced
upon the religion of Christians, says that those named wise, being seven
in number, snatched such a calling from a cause of this sort; and he
writes thus in the first book of the Philosophic History: And being nine,
seven were called wise from such a cause. A fisherman having sold a catch
of fishes to youths, it happened that a golden tripod was found in the
catch; and the fisherman saying he had sold fishes, but not a tripod, and
the youths referring the finding to their own fortune, it seemed good to
entrust the judgment to the god; and when he had given an oracle to give
the tripod to the wise man, the tripod was first brought to Thales, and he
sent it away to Bias, saying that man was the wise one; and he sends to
another, and that one to another, until, the tripod having been carried
round to the seven and having returned again upon the first, it seemed
good to dedicate it to the god; for this one was the wisest of all. Of
what sort then their opinions have been, it will be said by us in season;
Thales then the Milesian says the god is mind of the world, and Democritus
of Abdera agrees in some respect, but he also adds something else; for he
himself also contends that the god is mind, yet in spherical fire, and
that he is the soul of the world. And Anaximander, going as it were a
wholly other path, defines god to be the infinite worlds, I know not what
he is saying.
=1.39= And he who has most and not outside of wonder the force unto tests
of thoughts, I mean Aristotle the Stagirite, the disciple of Plato, names
the god a separate form, and contends that he has stepped upon the sphere
of the all. And also those called Stoics say god is a craftsmanlike fire
going a way upon generation of a world. And Plutarch has written
concerning these things and others of the leading men among them, and
Porphyry bold against us. Are they not then different from one another,
and guessers rather than discerners of the truth, those drunk with opinions
so many-formed? And yet those who have known truly and unerringly ought in
no way to be set against one another's thoughts, as indeed it is possible
to see also upon us ourselves. But someone will say toward this, I think:
What then, my good man? Are there not many heresies even among

yourselves, and do not some split into thinking and saying other and other
things? But those, my excellent man, I would say, who have been first and
in the beginnings teachers of the sacred doctrines for us, all agree with
one another, and those through the middle and last agree with the first.
For Moses has not thought other things, and those after him taught things
fighting his, but one, as I said, word concerning God from all until the
holy apostles and evangelists.
=1.40= For all confess one God over all and through all and in all,
without beginning and eternal, unbegotten, incorruptible, life and
life-giving, maker of heaven and earth and in a word of all things in
them. And if some of those after them, not having understood their things,
have missed the true, the writing of having gone astray will follow not
those rather, but these, according at least to the reasoning that is
right. If then those who have been inventors of the folly that is in them
and leaders of the unholy doctrines have been brought together with one
another's opinions, let them show it and I shall cease. But if the first
themselves are different toward one another and of other mind, how is it
not clear to all that they go outside the path that is straight, and have
gone astray? For if someone should wish to learn thoroughly very well the
word concerning the God beyond all that is right and unadulterated, whose
opinion would he receive and not miss the mark? For Thales and
Anaximander, and others of whom we just now made mention, would be caught
having babbled at random. But Pythagoras and Plato, having spent time in
Egypt and falling in with many there, inasmuch as they were both most
fond of learning and fond of inquiry, were not ignorant of the virtue of
Moses; for the things according to him were in no moderate wonder among
the Egyptians. From this I think, having learned not inelegantly the word
concerning God, they thought more reasonably than the others the things
concerning him and also chose to hold them. And we shall find also some of
those at Athens brought together with what has been supposed to be well
from them.
=1.41= And I think one must deem worthy of speech and memory the Egyptian
Hermes, whom they also say has been named Trismegistus, those of that time
having honoured him and, as it seems good to some, likening him to the one
mythologized to have come to be from Zeus and Maia; this Hermes then in
Egypt, though being an initiator and always sitting by the precincts of
the idols, is found to have thought the things of Moses, even if not
wholly rightly and irreproachably, yet in part; for he too has been
benefited. And he who composed at Athens the fifteen books surnamed
Hermaica has also made mention of this man in his own writings; and he
writes thus in the first concerning him, and he has brought in one of the
ministers saying: That we may then come into the like, do you not also
hear that our Hermes both cut all Egypt into lots and portions, measuring
the fields with a cord, and had canals cut for the irrigations, and set
nomes, and named the lands from them, and established the exchanges of
contracts, and newly produced a catalogue of the rising of the stars, and
cut herbs, and besides found and handed down numbers and calculations and
geometry, astronomy and astrology, and music and all grammar. I shall then
make the narration of the opinions of each, and I shall mention also
others who have obtained among them no unadmirable name upon education;
and I say that those who will meet with it must approach what has been
written most fond of learning rather than quickly sated.
=1.42= Pythagoras at any rate says: God is one, and he himself is not, as
some suppose, outside the ordering, but in it, whole in the whole circle,
overseeing all generations

he is, being a blending of all ages, and light of his own powers and
works, beginning of all, a luminary in heaven, and father of all, mind and
ensouling of all, motion of all circles. Behold then clearly he says the
God of all is one, and beginning of all, worker of his own powers, luminary
and ensouling or life-giving of all, and motion of all circles; for
nothing is self-moved, but all things have been brought by him, and they
appear having obtained the motion from not being into being. And Plato
speaks somehow thus: For to find the father and maker of this all is a
work, and having found him to speak him out to all is impossible. Very
rightly indeed; For the glory of the Lord hides a word, according to what
is written, and every word concerning him is weak and comes behind the
worth; for he is beyond every thought, and we see the things concerning
him through a mirror and in a riddle, as the all-wise Paul says.
=1.43= And Porphyry says in the fourth book of the Philosophic History that
Plato both thought and also spoke again concerning one god, that no name
fits him nor does human knowledge grasp him, and that the so-called
appellations predicate him catachrestically from the later things. And if
one must at all dare to say something concerning him from the names among
us, rather the appellation of the one and that of the good is to be set
upon him. For the one shows the simplicity concerning him and through this
self-sufficiency; for he needs nothing, not parts, not essence, not
powers, not energies, but he is cause of all these, and the good presents
that from him is every thing that is good, the others imitating as they
can his, if one must say it, property and being saved through it. And
Hermes Trismegistus says somehow thus: To think God is hard, and to speak
him is impossible for him for whom even to think is possible; for to
signify the bodiless with body is impossible, and for the perfect to be
grasped by the imperfect is not possible, and for the eternal to keep
company with the short-timed is difficult; for the one always is, and the
other passes by, and the one is true, and the other is shadowed by
imagination. By as much then as the weaker stands apart from the stronger
and the less from the better, by so much the mortal from the divine and
immortal. If then there is a bodiless eye, let it go out of the body upon
the vision of the beautiful, and let it fly up, and let it be lifted, not
seeking to behold shape, not body, not ideas, but rather that which is
productive of these, the quiet, the calm, the established, the unturned,
that which is itself all things and only, the one, that itself from
itself, that itself in itself, that like itself, which is neither like
another, nor unlike itself.
=1.44= And again the same: Thinking then nothing concerning that one and
only good, never say impossible; for he himself is all power, nor think
him to be in something, nor again contained by something; for he himself,
being unbounded, is limit of all, and contained by none he contains all.
Since what difference is there of bodies toward the bodiless, and of
generated things toward the ungenerated, and of those subject to necessity
toward that which is of its own authority, or of earthly things toward the
heavenly, and of corruptible things toward the eternal? is it not that
the one is of its own authority, and the other subject to necessity, and
the things below, being imperfect, are corruptible? But also Sophocles
says thus concerning god: One in truths, one is god, who wrought heaven
and the long earth, and the bright swell of the sea, and the forces of
winds; but we mortals, wandering in heart, have set up consolations of
woes, images of gods from stones and woods, or types of gold-wrought or
ivory, and making sacrifices to these and empty festivals, thus we think
to be pious. And also the most wise Xenophon: He then who shakes all and
makes still, as some great and powerful one, is manifest; but of what sort
in form, unmanifest; nor

yet does the sun, seeming to be all-shining, even this one seem to allow
to see himself, but if someone shamelessly behold him, he takes away the
sight.
=1.45= That then there is one God by nature and truly, above every mind
and speech, incomprehensible, formless, life-giving, and beginning of
all, unbegotten, incorruptible, maker of all, has been clearly witnessed
both by the God-inspired Scripture and through the voice of the poets and
prose-writers among them; and that they themselves have also known the Son
begotten from him according to nature, his maker Word, we shall show
through what they have written, setting beside them the uses concerning
these things. For Porphyry says in the fourth book of the Philosophic
History that Plato spoke concerning the good thus: and from this in a
manner unthinkable to men mind came to be, whole and existing according to
itself, in which are the things that truly are and all the essence of the
things that are; which also first beautiful and self-beautiful, having
from itself the form of beauty, came forth pre-eternal, having started
from God as cause, being self-begotten and self-father. For the procession
has not come to be from that one being moved toward the generation of
this, but from this having come forth self-generated from God, and having
come forth not from a temporal beginning, for time was not yet, nor even
when time has come to be is time anything toward him; for mind is always
timeless and only eternal. And as the first God is one and only always,
even if all things come to be from him, by not being numbered with these
nor being able to have their worth ranked with his existence, so also mind
is only eternal and having existed timelessly, and of the things in time
he himself is time, remaining in the sameness of his own eternal
subsistence.
=1.46= And also Orpheus again says somewhere thus: I adjure thee by heaven,
wise work of the great God; I adjure thee by the voice of the Father,
which he spoke first, when he fixed the whole world by his own counsels.
And the voice of the Father which he spoke first he says is his only-begotten
Word, always coexisting with the Father; for there was not a time when God
and Father would be understood existing without his own Word; and in the
same he also showed God being maker of all. And Hermes Trismegistus speaks
thus concerning God. For his Word having come forth, being all-perfect and
fertile, and a maker, having fallen in a fertile nature upon fertile water
made the water pregnant. And the same again: The pyramid then, he says,
lying under nature and the noetic world; for it has lying upon it as ruler
the maker Word of the master of all, who after that one is first power,
unbegotten, unbounded, having come forth from that one and lying upon and
ruling the things made through him, and he is progenitor of the
all-perfect and a perfect and fertile genuine Son. And again the same, as
one of those who keep precincts in Egypt asking and saying: And why, O
greatest good daemon, was he called by this name from the Lord of all? he
says: I also said in the things before, but you did not understand. The
nature of his noetic Word is a nature generative and maker-like; this, as
his either generation, or nature, or custom, or whatever you wish call it,
only thinking this, that he is perfect in the perfect, and from the
perfect he works perfect goods and makes and gives life. Since then he
holds to such a nature, he has been well so named. And the same in the
first discourse of those To Tat in full says thus concerning God: The Word
of the maker, O child, is eternal, self-moved, without increase, without
decrease, unchangeable, incorruptible, only, always like himself, and
equal and even, stable, well-ordered, being one after the God known
beforehand; and he signifies, I think, through this the Father.
=1.47= These things then suffice toward a most complete demonstration of
this, that they themselves have also thought the only-begotten Word of
God. And I think I must bring upon what I said also the things

said by them concerning the Holy Spirit. For Porphyry says, setting out
the opinion of Plato, that the essence of the divine proceeded as far as
three hypostases, and that the highest god is the good, and after him and
second the maker, and third also the soul of the world; for the divinity
proceeded as far as soul. Behold then clearly in these he contends that
the essence of the divine proceeded as far as three hypostases; for there
is one God of all, and the knowledge concerning him is as it were widened
into a holy and consubstantial Trinity, into Father I mean and Son and
Holy Spirit, which Plato also calls soul of the world; and the Spirit
gives life, and proceeds from the living Father through the Son, and in
him we live and move and are. For our Lord Jesus Christ speaks truly: The
Spirit is that which gives life. And again the same Porphyry concerning
Plato: Wherefore in unspeakable things hinting concerning these he says:
About the king all things are, and for the sake of that one all things,
and that is cause of all beautiful things, and second about the second
things, and third about the third. For as all things are about the three
gods, but already first about the king of all, and secondarily about the
god from that one, and thirdly about the one from this.
=1.48= And he has made it clear, showing also the subsistence from one
another, beginning from the king, and the stepping-down and lessening of
those after the first, through saying first and secondarily and thirdly,
and that from one all things are and through him are saved. He has then
not contemplated wholly soundly, but equally with those who have thought
the things of Arius he divides and sets under, and he brings in the
hypostases sitting under one another, and he thinks there are three gods,
having divided the holy and consubstantial Trinity. Yet he has not been
wholly ignorant of the true, and I think that even if he had spoken and
thought soundly, he would also have brought out to all the others the
soundness of the opinion concerning God, if perhaps he did not fear the
writing of Anytus and Meletus and the hemlock of Socrates. And Hermes also
says in the third discourse of those To Asclepius: For it is not reachable
to furnish such mysteries to the uninitiated; but hear with the mind. One
only was noetic light before noetic light and is always, mind of mind
luminous; and there was nothing other than the unity of this; always being
in himself, always with his own mind and light and spirit he contains all.
And after other things he says: Outside this, no god, no angel, no daemon,
no other essence; for he is Lord of all and Father and God and spring and
life and power and light and mind and spirit, and all things are in him
and under him.
=1.49= For mind from mind, as I think, he says the Son, and as light from
light; and he remembers also the Spirit, as containing all; and he says
neither angel, nor daemon, nor yet some other nature or essence lies
outside the divine superiority or authority, but he defines all things to
be under it and through it. And again the same in the same third discourse
of those To Asclepius, as someone asking concerning the divine Spirit, he
says thus: If there were not some providence of the Lord of all so as for
me to uncover this word, not even would such a desire now have held you
that you should inquire concerning this; but now hear the rest of the
word: Of this Spirit of which I often said before all things have need;
bearing all, it gives life and nourishes all according to worth, and it
has been hung from the holy spring, being always a helper to spirits and
fertile of life to all, being one. He knows it then both existing in its
own subsistence, and giving life and nourishing all, and as hung from the
holy spring of God and Father. For it proceeds from him according to
nature, and through the Son it is supplied to the creation. =1.50= Having
then been curious as we could about the books among them, we have made the
opinion of each most transparent, that those who will meet with it may
know

that not a few of the wise among the Greeks have gone astray, having
thought of their own mind, and have had opinions rising against one
another, and there are those who have not been without share of the Mosaic
words, because they came as far as Egypt for the sake of love of learning;
these have thought somehow better than the rest, having come near the
truth, yet not having the eye of the understanding wholly unmuddied; whom,
I think, someone calling also squinting would not miss the likely
reckoning. That then the things of Christians are both older and full of
truth, and supposed to be well by the chosen of the leading men, and
incomparably in better things than the quibbling of the Greeks, a
sufficient argument, I think, has shown. [End of the first tome of the
refutations of the god-wise Cyril]

2.1 Second tome of the same
Having thought nothing unfitting, or rather having judged it needful and
necessary that one must first say who came to be before whom according to
time and also of what sort of opinions concerning God each have had, we
have made a most accurate narration of them. And perhaps someone would
blame saying: Why ever, having chosen to speak with the doctrines of
Christians, and being eager to bring against the blasphemies of Julian the
prevailing word, did you not think to walk this road, but, diverting as it
were the impulse of the argument that goes straight of what is fitting,
you let yourself go upon genealogies, and prepared to be curious about the
opinions of Hebrews and of Greeks? Let us then put off the charges upon
these things, contending that we have been able to bring the argument into
this with good occasion. For since he who has emulated the Babylonian
Rabsaces does not fear, with unbridled tongue mocking the glory of God,
and having unholy cried down our holy religion he remembers often the wise
among the Greeks, and with every praise he crowns their evil opinions, and
is bold against the sacred doctrines of the Church, and smiles down the
histories of Moses, and denounces also simply all the saints, in this way
(and very reasonably), we heaped up beforehand as it were some matter for
the contradictions, the clear and most evident demonstration both that the
writing of the all-excellent Moses came to be older than the wise among
the Greeks, and also that one would see the tradition of the faith of
Christians in incomparable superiorities in better things than what has
been glorified among them. And it was thus, and not otherwise, also to
free the words that follow from a longer casting-out, and from seeming
sometimes to be carried far from the thought of the things set forth. And
enough of these. 2.2 And one must go hereafter upon his writing itself,
and setting out the lines upon the word itself we shall set against them
the things from ourselves in the due order to his assaults, having chosen
not ignobly that one must be brought up against them. And since, as I
said, opening an ungated mouth he has made very much slander against
Christ the Saviour of us all and throws reviling voices against him, I
shall not remember such things, but having passed by, and very prudently,
the things through which one would be defiled even if one only approach
his voices, I shall rise against the necessary things, showing him
everywhere fond of mockery and of idle myth, and wholly having missed
being able to say something of the true things. Yet that also is to be
known; for in the first discourse he goes through very many thoughts, and
does not cease turning the same things up and down and cycling them. And
what he would in the beginnings

be found saying, this also through the middle and in the last setting out,
he would show the words of the refutation as perhaps not having come to be
in order; for there is somehow every necessity that those who have chosen
to contend with what is said by him be seen speaking always not once but
very often concerning the same things. Dividing then in the more becoming
order the word from him, and bringing together the thoughts in it
according to kind, not of each very often, but sufficiently once and
artfully let us meet them. He begins then the word against us and says:
{JULIAN} It seems to me to be well to set out to all men the causes by
which I was persuaded that the fabrication of the Galileans is a fiction
of men put together by knavery, having nothing divine, but having used the
myth-loving and childish and mindless portion of the soul, it led
wonder-telling into a belief of truth. {CYRIL} 2.3 Galileans then, as I
think, he says the holy apostles, and wonder-telling the writings of Moses
and the predictions and God-speakings of the holy prophets. And he has
perhaps been ignorant, or rather not without God he has made the
denunciation of his own superstition. For there are two Galilees, of which
the one is according to Judaea, and the other both bordering and neighbour
to the cities of the Phoenicians. And at any rate in the Gospels it is
written concerning Christ the Saviour of us all that walking beside the
sea of Galilee, that is the lake of Tiberias, he made the disciples chosen.
And God said somewhere through one of the holy prophets: And what also
have you to do with me, Tyre and Sidon and all Galilee of aliens? and also
the divine Isaiah: The country of Zabulon, he says, and land of
Nephthalim, and the rest who dwell the seacoast, Galilee of the nations,
the people that sat in darkness saw a great light. Galileans then would
not surely be understood only those according to Judaea, but also all the
nations; for Galilee, he says, of the nations. Unclear then and not yet
plain against whom his word would go more becomingly and more truly;
whether against us or against himself and those who have honoured the
apoplectic and dear-to-him superstition? For they too are Galileans, or
rather one would in no way doubt that the force of what has been said
would fit the thunderstruck follies of the Greeks.
2.4 For where is the mythical much and senseless and childish, both cold
and of idle counsel, and full of all lack of understanding, except among
them alone, who having wonder-told in many ways also try to lead the false
into a belief of truth? And it is so unbeautiful among them and much that
even the chosen themselves, accustomed to philosophize most excellently
the things among themselves, have made a very great outcry against the not
inspired but rather outlandish madness of the poets, and it is fitting
that they have said openly to depart from the quibbling among them. For
Plato does not receive the things of Homer who has brought in gods and
goddesses, this in being condemned upon wantonness and wronged by the
greeds from men, and this also weeping, and being vexed when those of
their race have died, and saying woe is me in a womanish way, because of
wishing to snatch someone out of death, yet not still being able to do
this, but being worsted by Fates and yielding to Destiny, as more strong
clearly even than the one who excels the other gods, whom they also call
highest Zeus. But leaving the being able still to say very many things in
this, that I may not seem to be carried from what is likely, I shall
return upon the aim of the things set forth. 2.5 The mythical is of the
Greeks, and they themselves have been eager to bring wonder-telling into a
belief of truth not from simple thoughts, but rather unholy and knavishly
invented, and they have put together the ill-natured fiction against the
unspeakable glory of the God who rules all, and as it were some snare

they have set the fabrication for the souls of the simpler. For they have
led astray those over all the earth, saying that heaven is god and the
other of the elements. And, as the all-wise Paul writes, saying they were
wise they became foolish, and they changed the glory of the incorruptible
God in a likeness of an image of corruptible man and of birds and of
four-footed things and of creeping things. And that we may also yield to
his thoughts, not letting what has been said go upon others rather but
even allowing it to leap upon the holy apostles, or once for all upon the
all-wise Moses himself and the holy prophets, let him come, let him show
clearly what is the fiction knavishly put together, and of what sort he
says the
wonder-telling, and what the myth-loving and childish of the religion of
Christians. Did Moses write myths for us, saying that there is one God by
nature and truly, unbegotten, eternal, incorruptible, without quantity,
invisible, unchangeable, untouchable, life and life-giving, wisdom, power,
maker, king, Lord of all? Has the word of the holy prophets missed the
true, having been eager to go in the track of the Mosaic doctrines? Shall
we find the teaching of the holy apostles having it otherwise? Not at all.
2.6 Then how does he contend that the things among the Galileans have
nothing divine, and say they are otherwise idle myths and wonder-tellings?
And yet who would not agree that nothing better would come to be for men
than to know well and unerringly the one who by nature and truly is Maker
and Lord of all? And they themselves would say, I know, that the
contemplative is and is called the most beautiful and excellent portion of
philosophy, through which for those who are able to behold more
mindfully the things concerning God would become barely graspable, and
according at least to what is reachable for men. And since he himself says
he has been persuaded, let him teach from where or from whom. For let him
not boast that he alone knows this; but if he himself was persuaded by
himself and this suffices toward a most complete demonstration, as at
least he thinks and says, of the things of Christians not being well, that
the matter is nonsense for him I shall pass by saying; yet let him alone
revel in the things against us, for we shall not use so most hostile a
judge. But if he thinks that the words of those who correct, such as would
come to be against some, must be true and not falsified, let him not say
that he himself alone has been persuaded, but let him refute through
deeds. And that he will rather write himself in justice, and not us, upon
the myth-fashioning, and that the charge is not without suspicion, we
shall know through what he said again.
{JULIAN} 2.7 And being about to make the word over all the doctrines said
among them, I wish first to say that, that those who meet with it, if they
should wish to speak against, as in a law-court must be curious about
nothing from outside, nor counter-accuse what is said, until they have
made defence over the first things; it is better thus and clearer to set
up a work of their own when they wish to correct something of the things
among us, and in the things in which they make defence toward the
corrections from us not even to counter-accuse. {CYRIL} Speechless then,
tell me, must the one pursued by you be, and do you command the defendant
to be caught silent, and having remembered nothing at all of your things
to choose to confirm the writing against himself? And yet the not wishing
us to say something of your things is of one who fears the refutation and
has not been ignorant of the unpleasantness of the shame in them. If then
he himself, denouncing the religion of Christians, does not think it
worthy of every praise, and with the highest honours crowns the
superstition of the Greeks, let him demand the equal in return; but if he
prides himself in the words against us and fastens the better opinion upon
his own ill-counsels, having

set beside them the things of the Greeks as excelling, how does he think
it right that we be silent, and remember wholly nothing of the things
among them, when wishing to speak with our own doctrines we make the words
upon this very thing?
2.8 If then, having left the need to be set against your words, we have
been turned aside upon the need to remember only the Greek things, I would
say that the word made upon this very thing by him is well and not outside
what is likely; but to those making defence and wishing to meet every
thing of what is said by him how would he still justly find fault, if
having chosen to speak with our own things we are also eager to strip the
shame of the impiety of the Greeks? For as of colours in things of other
kind it is possible to see the look more apparent (For the light shines in
the darkness, he says), in this same manner, I think, also the beauties
grown in the virtues would become apparent to the understandings of the
more ignorant not otherwise except through the shame of the opposites
alone. For the unbeautiful of worthlessness persuades to assign to the
better things the vote of the need to conquer; because of this and very
reasonably he fears his own things and does not allow to come into the
midst the things over which he is ashamed, and he commands even those
being corrected to be silent, and he also blames us in other ways saying
thus:
2.9 {JULIAN} And it is worth taking up a little from where and how a
thought of God first came to us, then to set beside the things said among
the Greeks and among the Hebrews over the divine, and after this to ask
again those who are neither Greeks nor Jews, but of the heresy of Galilee,
instead of what they chose the things among those before our things, and
upon this why ever they do not even remain in those, but having stood off
from those also they turned a road of their own, having confessed nothing
of the beautiful nor of the serious, neither of the things among us the
Greeks nor of the things among the Hebrews from Moses, but from both
plucking the things that have been stuck beside the nations as some
dooms, the atheism from the Jewish knavery, and a cheap and dragged life
from the slackness and vulgarity among us, this they wished to be named
the best god-worship. {CYRIL} He then who says that those who meet with
whatever slanders he would make against us, if they should wish to speak
against, as in a law-court must be curious about nothing from outside nor
counter-accuse what is said, promises clearly in these to set beside the
things said among the Greeks and among the Hebrews over the divine. Does
then the manner of the setting-beside look wherever? And what would be his
aim as he marches the things of the Hebrews or the things of Christians
against the doctrines of the Greeks?
=2.10= For one would not, I suppose, say that having stood off from the
denunciation and having carried himself away from the need to slander he
will use those who will meet with it as straight judges, so as even to
have wished to learn thoroughly from them what is better or what worse;
but he thinks somewhere, as is likely, that there will not otherwise be
some praisers of the opinion concerning God that is in him unless the
things of Christians be made bad, receiving upon themselves by a
setting-beside of the Greek things the more shameful vote. But this would
never come to be among those who know both the weakness of the false and
the power of the truth. Yet consider that; for legislating as it were for
us the need to be silent and to remember nothing of his things when we
make the words over our own religion, he is found caught by what he
himself denied. And since he also brings a question, why ever having left
the things of the Greeks we have inclined toward the Hebrews, come, let us
also say the equal to him; for instead of what, having left the things of
Christians and having run away from the truth, do you love the false, and
the so most abominable will-worship, I mean that of those who idol-worship,
most mindlessly preferring it to the accurate opinion concerning God,

then do you think you have counselled well and not rather to have endured
the last of all evil opinion? And if he should wish to understand the
truest cause upon which, having left the things of the Greeks, we have
honoured the things among the Hebrews, taking his things we say. For he
said thus:
=2.11= {JULIAN} Therefore the Greeks fashioned the myths over the gods
unbelievable and monstrous (for they said Kronos swallowed the children,
then again vomited them) and already unlawful marriages; for Zeus was
mixed with a mother, and having made a child from her, he himself married
his own daughter, or rather he himself did not even marry his own
daughter, but having been mixed simply he handed her over to another; then
the tearings of Dionysus and gluings of limbs. Such things the myths of
the Greeks say. {CYRIL} You have from here a sufficient defence. You are
then at a loss instead of what you also think it right to correct us, that
having all but kicked away the so shameful and unpersuasive idle-myth of
the Greeks we have given the better vote to the true things? For the
divine Moses, and besides the choir of the holy prophets, and apostles and
evangelists, glorify the one God by nature and truly, and they persuade us
also to be disposed thus, carrying us away from myths and all wonder-telling
and shameful mind, and also tuning us into an admirable way of life. And
nothing among them has been fashioned, nor yet does it have an
unpersuasive account of the thoughts. For that the things according to us
agree with the proclamations of Moses and of the holy prophets, and the
force of the evangelical and apostolic teaching is brought together with
the thoughts of those who went before, the argument will show us clearly
in season.
=2.12= And since, I know not what saying, he contends that there is nothing
of the serious or good among us, come, let him show this also; for let him
not make the denunciation bare and undemonstrated. For how is there
nothing of the serious among us? Is not an accurate and filed word
concerning God and the generation of the world found among Christians?
Have we not been supplied from the sacred Scriptures the science of the
things to be done, irreproachable and unblamed? And yet how is it not
clear to all that to philosophize most excellently would be carried
through rightly not in another manner, I think? For contemplative and
practical philosophy besides this would be full of every praise, and it is
marvelled at among those who have been in earnest about the things of the
Greeks; therefore we have not learned atheism from the opinion of the
Hebrews, for he himself said this also, but if someone should wish to
speak the true, from the God-inspired Scripture we have condemned the
ignorance of the Greeks. And that the godless is rather among them who do
not know the one God by nature and truly, how would it not be clear to
everyone? And a cheap and dragged life he says we have chosen from the
slackness among them, naming Greek vulgarity and slackness the
indiscriminate eating of dainties, and declining none of the eatables. For
in this especially they present the power of piety and think to come to be
in the beautiful of every virtue, if they should decline some of the
eatables.
=2.13= And yet why ever do they define in these the manner of
purity? For all things have come to be from God, and the things from a
good one are wholly beautiful; and the all-pure and unprofaned would work
nothing of the things accustomed to defile us. For what would even food
produce in those who have partaken of it? Or of what sort would it let in
the pollution to them? And it is fitting, I think, to decline the things
through which one would reasonably be defiled, and the manners of
worthlessness would do this also very generically, adulteries, fornications,
evil-speakings, false-speakings, slanders, love of money, and the rest.
But they, having taken none of such things into account, pretend to eat
little, and sometimes also

they send away some of the eatables, but they fear nothing of the
outlandish things. And besides this they gladden the highest Zeus, having
chosen to honour his things, and they tend the powers of the Cyprus-born.
Very many then are the things against us from that man; and he speaks very
incorrectly and makes no moderate denunciation also against the all-wise
Moses and his writing. For he says that composing the book of the making
of the world he said wholly nothing of the true things, but simply heaped
together aged idle talk, and the things which it was likely to deem worthy
of every word, all these he made as nothing, and having rhapsodized simply
at random he seemed to say something wise and worth hearing. And he has
marvelled at the opinions of the wise among the Greeks upon this, and most
of all others with praises and applauses he crowns that of Plato.
=2.14= And I, that he is not moderately pompous, in this again I shall
pass by saying, but that he draws up an idle brow upon the inventions of
speech of the Greeks, I shall try to speak through as I am able. And I
think one must set beside again, having chosen from the books among them
the opinion of each, which they thought it right to have concerning the
construction of the world, then to bring against it the cosmogony of
Moses; for thus there will be seen to those who will meet with it both
the nonsense of their narrow chatter and the unmixedness of the writings
of Moses unto truth. Plutarch then, a man who has been not without mark
among them, in the second book of the Collection of Physical Doctrines,
says thus concerning the world: Pythagoras first named the encompassing of
all a world from the order in it. Thales and those from him one world;
Democritus and Epicurus and his leader Metrodorus infinite worlds in the
infinite according to every circumstance; Empedocles that the circuit of
the sun is a description of the limit of the world; Seleucus that the
world is infinite; Diogenes that the all is infinite, but the world has
been limited; the Stoics that the all and the whole differ; for all is
that with the infinite void, but whole without the void the world; so that
the same is both the whole and the world.
=2.15= Then concerning the shape of the world he says thus again: The
Stoics a spherical world, others cone-shaped, others egg-shaped; and
Epicurus that it is possible that the worlds are spherical, and possible
also that they have used other shapings. And he said again, putting
forward the opinions of the philosophers among the Greeks unto
explanation, whether the world is ensouled or not, thus: All the others
said the world ensouled and administered by providence; but Democritus and
Epicurus and as many as introduce the atoms and the void, neither ensouled
nor administered by providence, but by some irrational nature; Aristotle
neither ensouled wholly through wholes nor rational nor noetic nor
administered by providence; for the heavenly things share in all these;
for they contain ensouled and vital spheres, but the things about earth in
none of them, and they share in the good order by accident, not
principally. And these things concerning these. And since it was their aim
to test again whether the world would be by nature corruptible or not,
they have thought thus also concerning this: Pythagoras and the Stoics
both a world generated by god, and corruptible as far as upon the nature;
for it is perceptible because also bodily, yet not indeed going to be
destroyed, by providence and holding-together of God; Epicurus corruptible
because also generated, as an animal, as a plant; Xenophanes an ungenerated
and eternal and incorruptible world; Aristotle the part of the world under
the moon passible, in which also the earthly things waste.
=2.16= Hear, O men, and understand hereafter how much the nonsense in
these is. For rising against one another's opinions, and simply and
unconsidered mixing this and that

belching according to what seems good to each, how would they not be
thought to be guessers rather, and not skilled in the truth? For some one
world, others many, others generated, and there are those wholly standing
against these and different in judgments who say it is incorruptible and
ungenerated; and some administered by providence of God, others even
without providence have assigned the well-ordered motion of the elements
to automatisms and accidents; and some say it has been ensouled, others
neither ensouled nor noetic; and simply one would see as it were drunk the
word upon each tossed this way and that for them. And since, having picked
out Plato from the others, he lingers especially in his opinions, I would
say that Plato and Pythagoras think somehow more reasonably concerning God
and world; and they have collected the training or science unto this
having thrown themselves upon Egyptians, among whom there was much word
concerning the all-wise Moses and the wonder of the doctrines with him
was honoured. Yet they say Plato himself thought the opposites to himself,
and Aristotle who studied with him chose not rather to think his things,
but to be set against and to march against him. For Porphyry says Plato
thought concerning heaven, and said that the bodily of it was put together
from the four elements, having been sounded together with one another by
soul; Wherefore, he says, even now it remains mixed and has been named
according to that which abounds.
=2.17= And he etymologizes, I think, the name and says it has been called
heaven as being some visible, that from being seen some heaven might be
understood. To Aristotle at any rate it did not seem that these things
were thus. From where? For he does not say that heaven has been put
together, nor yet that it is from four elements, and he thinks of some
fifth body without share of the four and wholly having lacked them. And
Plato says the world both ensouled and a noetic animal, and he also sets
providence over it; but his disciple again has not thought thus; for
neither ensouled wholly through wholes nor noetic nor administered by
providence. And again the one defines it generated and corruptible as far
as comes unto its nature, but the other does not even grant it generated
but as incorruptible and ungenerated. And again the clever and
much-spoken-of Plato defines there to be three principles of the all, God
and matter and form; and God he says is the maker, and matter the
underlying, and form the paradigm of each of the things that come to be;
and Aristotle again rises against him and does not agree once for all; for
he does not think it right to think or to say form a principle, and he
says the principles are two, God and matter. And Plato again saying the
principles of all are three, God and matter and form, also adds a fourth,
which he names universal soul. And besides this having said matter
ungenerated, he also says it is generated; then having granted that the
form, whatever it is, exists according to itself, he fights with his own
inventions; for he said it exists in the thoughts of God and does not have
its own existence, or essentializing.
=2.18= To whom then having inclined would the searchers of the truth run
the blameless and most unerring path? Whom of those named shall we free
from speaking false? To whom is it worth assigning the vote of having
stumbled in no way? Or rather how would they be sufficient toward the need
to persuade some, who have so missed the true as not only to be set against
one another but also themselves against their own opinions? And the
all-wise Julian praises these things and is struck with wonder, and he
mocks the things of Moses, and stirring all things up and down he dares to
march the words of Plato against them, and he says: {JULIAN} Here let us
set beside, if you wish, the things of

Plato. What then this man says over the maker and what voices he puts
round him in the cosmogony, consider, that we may set beside one another
the cosmogony of Plato and of Moses. For thus it would appear who is
better, and who more worthy of God; whether Plato who has served the
idols, or he concerning whom the Scripture says that God spoke to him
mouth to mouth? In the beginning God made heaven and earth; and the earth
was invisible and unfurnished, and darkness over the abyss, and a spirit
of God was borne upon the water. And God said: Let there be light, and
there was light, and God saw the light that it was good. And God divided
between the light and between the darkness, and God called the light day
and the darkness he called night. And there was evening and there was
morning, day one. And God said: Let there be a firmament in the midst of
the water, and God called the firmament heaven. And God said: Let the
water under heaven be gathered into one gathering and let the dry appear;
and it was so. And God said: Let the earth shoot forth herb of grass and
fruitful tree. And God said: Let there be luminaries in the firmament of
heaven, that they may be unto lighting upon the earth; and God set them in
the firmament of heaven, so as to rule the day and the night.
=2.19= In these then he says neither the abyss has been made by God nor
the darkness nor the water; and yet it was surely fitting, having said
concerning the light that it came to be when God commanded, to say still
also concerning the night and concerning the abyss and concerning the
water; but he said nothing as concerning things that have come to be at
all, though having often remembered them. Besides these he remembers
neither the generation or making of the angels, nor in what manner they
were brought, but only of the bodies about heaven and about the earth, so
that God according to Moses is maker of nothing bodiless, but an orderer
of underlying matter; for the: And the earth was invisible and unfurnished
is nothing other than of one making the dry essence matter, and bringing
in God as orderer of it.
{CYRIL} Concerning Moses then there would be much and long word for those
who wish to praise; for he has heard God saying clearly: I know thee
beyond all, and thou hast found grace with me. And of the many-formed
virtue that was in him a demonstration would be also very clear, the power
of the wonder-working in Egypt. For he stood under the God of all, and was
a servant as he set the creation against the madnesses of the Egyptians.
Of what sort at any rate Plato was, even if no one should say, the going
away from Athens upon Sicily would cry out; for they say Dionysius, not
having been pleased with the flatteries from him, sold him, as having
surely somewhere set upon an unfree man the penalty most becoming to a
slave. Yet let the word concerning this be left for now, and let it go
again upon what is in hand.
=2.20= For the divine Moses is not seen to have put together idle words
for us, nor yet to have been simply urged into this from love of honour,
but having thought of something of the necessary unto benefit for life.
For he did not set before himself to physiologize thinly or to say
something concerning the so-called first principles or the elements from
them; for these things I think more curious and not graspable to the
understandings of some. And it became his aim to set the mind of those
then upon the doctrines of the truth. For they had gone astray, and each
worshipping what seemed good were caught, and having from very great
ignorance not known the one God by nature they have served the creation.
And some enrolled heaven as god, others the circle of the sun, and there
are those who were eager to assign even to moon and stars and earth and
plants and the nature of the waters, to birds and irrational animals, the
glory of the highest nature. The affairs then having come down to this for
them, and so terrible a sickness

having been distributed to all those upon the earth, Moses was a helper
and was shown introducer of the most beautiful learnings to all, having
cried out expressly that there is one maker of all according to nature,
and standing them off from the others, which also through him were brought
toward being and existing at all. Most needfully then and very clearly,
having passed by the very much thin-speaking, he has gone upon the word of
the more necessary things.
=2.21= For what need was there to say of what sort the nature of the
waters is, or also how it came to be at the beginning, and to measure also
abysses and nature of heaven, and to be curious about the manner of the
existence of the angels? For these things I think it is agreed would
become hard to explain for everyone. But if it was possible at all even to
speak them through, God making wise, who would be the one who would hear
or be able to understand such thin things, or rather even above mind? And
yet we shall find those of that time, in which also the writing of the
all-wise Moses was done, being even behind the ignorance of the Greeks
itself; for those from the things from which it was possible to know
clearly the glory of God, through these themselves would be caught having
fallen into the pit of the last of all folly. For as the God-inspired
Scripture says, it was surely fitting that those of old think the maker
and craftsman of all from the beauty of creatures; but they went down into
this pitch of ill-counsel so as even from the things from which it was
likely to be carried upon the knowledge of the truth, through these
themselves to be caught having suffered the being attached to the false.
And a faithful witness of this also would be the all-wise Paul having
written thus. For his invisible things from the creation of the world are
seen, being understood by the things made, both his eternal power and
godhead, so as for them to be without defence, because having known God
they did not glorify him as God or give thanks, but they became vain in
their reasonings, and their ununderstanding heart was darkened.
=2.22= And this would befit those who have invented the herd-like and
rabble and wholly irrational false-worship, of which sort, as I said, were
those toward whom the word of the all-wise Moses has been made; and that
they would be caught having become full of thunderstruck folly that has
come to the limit, we shall behold without toil having been curious about
the opinion of those after them. For Plutarch, being very thin, said
somewhere concerning them in the first book of the Collection of Physical
Doctrines: And they took a thought of God from this; for always sun and
moon and the rest of the stars, having been carried the under-earth,
rise like in colours, equal in sizes and according to the same places; and
again in the same book: And they define the thought of God thus: a noetic
and fiery spirit, not having form, but changing into what it wishes and
being made like together with all. And they had a thought of this first
from the beauty of the things appearing, having approached as that none of
the beautiful things comes to be at random and as it happened, but with
some art that makes. And I shall bring upon these also what Hermes
Trismegistus has written also To his own Mind (for the book is named thus):
Then, you say, is God invisible? Speak well. And who more manifest than
he? Because of this he has made, that through all someone may see him.
This is the good of God, this the virtue, that he himself appear through
all.
=2.23= And we shall see even Julian himself, the accuser of our holy
religion, agreeing with these. For he contends that to know God is some
untaught matter and self-learned for men, and he says thus: {JULIAN} And
that this belongs to men not as taught but by nature, let the common
eagerness of all men privately and publicly and according to man and
nations concerning the divine be a proof for us first. For all of us
untaught have believed some divine thing, concerning which the accurate
neither for all

is easy nor for those who have known is it possible to say unto all. And
after other things again: To this common thought of all men another also
is added; for all men we have been so naturally attached to heaven and the
gods appearing in it that even if someone supposed God other besides them,
he altogether assigned heaven as a dwelling to him, not having stood him
off from the earth, but as it were having seated the king of all there as
in a more honourable of the whole, supposing him to look upon the things
here from there.
{CYRIL} See then how those who have not endured the thick and beggar-like
and, so to speak, banausic error, and have departed from the opinion of
the herd, have not wholly lacked the true thought concerning God, and they
inferred both who and how great is the superiority of the power that is in
him, so as even to have brought under laws of good order the so great and
admirable creation.
=2.24= But the others, concerning whom the word is, have neither known God
through the creatures, but they were so thunderstruck, and have come to be
outside a mind befitting man, as not only to have chosen to worship heaven
and earth and moon and the other of the stars, but also to set up
many-formed shapes in shrines, and to engrave upon them forms not human
only, but also of irrational animals and birds and creeping things, and to
call these gods and saviours. Then how would one not admire the good craft
of Moses, speaking nothing curious or deep and not easily receivable to
those then, but rather those things through which it was possible for them
to change over upon that which has it rightly and knows how to straighten
into a blameless opinion, that upon, I mean, the God who rules all? Then
one would praise the teachers of lads also very reasonably as they sit
down with their judgments, and lead them by the hand little by little
toward being able to see something of the unspeakable things, and putting
beside them in the beginnings nothing of the things much practised and
somehow having an uphill approach, and shall we not deem Moses the most
holy, who has done this, worthy of every praise? And if it seems to you he
said nothing of the necessary things, do you wish we take hold of the
learnings dearest to you? Let us be curious as we can about the accuracy
of the Theogony of Hesiod.
=2.25= For he pretends to speak of God, and having assigned to himself the
seeming to be Muse-taken as some great matter and worth loving. Tell (he
says) how first gods and earth came to be, and rivers, and the boundless
sea raging with swell, and shining stars and broad heaven above. Then
saying chaos and night came to be and not adding in what manner: And Earth
(he says) first bore equal to herself starry Heaven, that he might cover
her round all. And having shown Heaven a child of Earth, that she also
bore seas having lain beside Heaven, Coeus and Crius and Hyperion and
Iapetus and Theia and Rhea and Themis and Mnemosyne, and Phoebe besides
these, whom he also names gold-crowned, and also Tethys, and he says
Kronos came to be youngest of all; then upon these he brings some rabbles
of idle and inconsistent narrations. But perhaps he will say that the law
of a poet fashioned these myths, Hesiod; for he perhaps blushes at his
narrations. Why then does he blame the hierophant Moses, who has put
together a clear and most unerring writing of true narrations? For he said
that both heaven and earth have been made by God, sun and moon, stars and
light, birds and swimming things, and kinds of irrational animals, and
beauties of plants, and edible fruits, and grasses in the fields.
=2.26= And see how through these words he shears most wisely the error
that struck in among the ancients; for do they not name Zeus heaven, and
Demeter

the earth, and Apollo the sun and the noisy one of the golden distaff,
that is Artemis, the moon? And simply attaching what seemed good to each
of the things that have come to be from God, they worshipped them as gods.
And that the word of the cosmogony came to be for the divine Moses clear
and easily seen together and having nothing over-wrought and with much
accuracy, come, come, let us show. For in the beginning (he says) God made
heaven and the earth. For he does not grant, according to some, that
matter also itself be understood co-unbegun and co-eternal with God and
ungenerated, and concurrent and coexisting with the eternal that which
once was not, with him who always is that which in time and barely was
brought into generation, nor yet with that which is according to the same
and in like manner that which has been moved, with the incorruptible that
under corruption; but rather by time and a beginning that calls toward
generation he bounds the creation, as having been brought from things that
are not according to will of God toward being this which it is. And he
does not surely say that God simply became orderer and craftsman of matter
already underlying and found beforehand, giving form to the shapeless in
whatever manner he would know, and only throwing upon it differences of
qualities, sizes and bulks, but by some unspeakable and unutterable power
to bring that which is not and does not exist at all into beginnings of
being. =2.27= And in what manner he makes is unreachable to see with the
mind according to us, and I say it is also beyond all speech. For how
would one speak the things above mind? And the inventions of the highest
of all essence and the way toward every thing of the things to be done
would, I think, stand so much above the things according to us, as we are
also according to nature worsted by it. When then Moses says: In the
beginning God made heaven and the earth, understand that having brought
together all things as in a word and as in short he recounts the
generation of the whole creation. Then he proceeds with good craft upon
the need to say in what manner they were ordered and how each of the
things made have been well allotted this which they are. And he says he
has made with the most all-powerful Word; for God he is and from God
according to nature, his maker Word of all. For God said, it says: Let
there be a firmament, and see with me at once the firmament fixed by the
power of the Word, which he also called heaven. God said: Let the dry
appear, and the water ran together into one gathering. And he said besides
these that sun come to be, and it came to be, moon, stars, and light and
animals both of land and of water, and birds besides these. And that the
nature of the elements itself would not have from home and from itself the
being able to run through corruption, but rather needs the hand of him who
holds it together toward being well, he taught saying that: A spirit of
God was borne upon the water; for the spirit of God gives life to all
things, itself also being life according to nature, as from the life of
the Father, and all things need it, and it would not otherwise have the
unshaken unto being this which it is.
=2.28= See then, as I said, the firmament being fixed by Word, and the dry
rising when the waters have been gathered into one; see the earth bearing
green with grasses and trees, and seminal words existing in them, that
through them the temporary things, stealing the power of being eternally,
may remain and be saved. See the luminaries in the firmament having come
to be from God not upon some other thing, except only that they may shine
for those upon the earth, and also signify times and days and years. And
he says the earth also was commanded to give forth a nature of irrational
animals, a form to each, the Demiurge distributing both the quantity and
that upon which it would come to be. And since the all had already been
made in order, and nothing at all had been left lacking for the needs of
man, then indeed, then, he himself also was thinking of what manner the
Demiurge would be; for his generation was not improvised in equal with the
other creatures. And since great and excellent, or rather

beyond all mind and speech and wonder the highest nature is understood and
is, it has willed to liken the animal to itself, as it can; in this way
also very reasonably, lest it seem something small and cast down, or also
to differ little from the other animals, the animal that was about to be
according to his image and likeness, that is man, he does not make his
generation unconsidered.
=2.29= And yet someone would say, and would not miss the true, that
nothing would escape the divine mind; and he knows, he knows all things
before their generation. Why then was he considering, though having known
beforehand the nature of man? Economically, as I said, the all-excellent
Moses says man has been honoured as it were even with pre-counsels, not
bringing in his generation simple, as one would say, and in nothing
altered from the others, but as it were even God having set the matter in
thought. And the word is hyperbolic, but it would have, I think, the
seeming to have been well made, if we say man is best of all the animals
upon earth, and has been made also according to image of him who created.
And that the whole creation was brought toward existence by the most
all-powerful nods of God, it is no hardship, as I think, to learn
thoroughly also from what the teachers of his superstition have written;
for it seemed good to all both to think and to say that simply all things
have been made by him, both noetic and perceptible, invisible and seen.
For they have agreed that about the King and Lord of all all things are;
and also Plato contends to say: Gods of gods, of whom I am maker and
father of works. Already then we have brought in the uses of the Greeks,
those upon these I mean, and I shall pass by saying the same, and I shall
remember the words of Hermes Trismegistus. =2.30= For this man said in
that To Asclepius: And Osiris said, it says: Then, O greatest good daemon,
how did the whole earth appear? And the great good daemon said: According
to order and drying, as I said, and the many waters having been commanded
by the Lord to withdraw into themselves the whole earth appeared clayey
and trembling; the sun thereafter having shone forth and unceasingly
burning through and drying, the earth was fixed in the waters, being
contained by the water. And also elsewhere: The Maker and Lord of all
spoke thus: Let there be earth, and let a firmament appear, and at once a
beginning of the making earth came to be. And these things concerning the
earth; and concerning the sun again he says thus: And Osiris said. O
Trismegistus good daemon, from where did this great sun appear? And he
said: Osiris, do you wish us to recount the generation of the sun from
where it appeared? It appeared by providence of the Master of all. And the
generation of the sun is from the Master of all, having come to be through
his holy and maker Word. Likewise also he himself in the first discourse
To Tat in full says. And the Lord of all at once spoke to his own holy and
noetic and maker Word: Let there be sun; and together with the saying,
Nature drew to itself with its own spirit the fire that holds to an
upward-carried nature, I mean the unmixed and most luminous and more
active and more fertile, and raised it into height from water.
=2.31= That then all things have been made by command of God through the
maker Word would befit a man to think and to say truly; but the how or in
what manner, he himself alone would know. And that he distributes to each
of the things that have come to be the being thus according to authority,
and the manner of the existence of all has him as definer, would become
clear also through what Moses said: Let there be a firmament, and it was
so and: Let the water be gathered into one gathering and let the dry
appear. For these things define the nature of each of the things brought
into generation. And Hermes Trismegistus among them remembers this again;
for he has brought in God saying to the creatures: And a necessity

I shall put round you who are under me, this command given to you through
my Word; for this you have as a law. For as I said just now, the Demiurge
defined a natural law for each of the things that have come to be, and by
his nods they appear having been allotted to be thus perhaps or not. And
the word that goes through straight and unadulterated would have it thus;
but he himself is not moderately struck with wonder at the opinion of
Plato, and he says: {JULIAN} Hear at any rate what Plato says concerning
the world. This whole heaven, or world or also whatever else it would most
receive being named, let this have been named by us, whether it always
was, having no beginning of generation, or it has come to be, having begun
from some beginning? It has come to be; for it is visible and tangible,
and having body; and all such things are perceptible, graspable by opinion
with sense. And after other things: Thus then according to the likely
word one must say that this world came to be a living animal ensouled and
minded in truth through the providence of God.
{CYRIL}
=2.32= Behold then clearly even Plato, divine according to him and
all-wise, confirms that the whole world or whatever it would be called is
generated and has begun from some beginning, and also tangible and visible
and embodied and opinable with sense, and has been made according to the
providence of the Demiurge. And that he who has been wholly hung upon the
invention of speech of Plato and crowns it with praises unto the limit has
also gone astray with him and has thought nothing of the blameless things,
and is carried about as by every wind, we shall make clear not at length,
setting beside again the things of Julian himself; for he said thus:
{JULIAN} And let us set beside one with one only, what and of what sort
making the God with Moses makes, and of what sort the one with Plato. And
God said: Let us make man according to our image and according to
likeness, and let them rule the fishes of the sea and the birds of heaven
and the cattle and all the earth and all the creeping things that creep
upon the earth. And God made man, according to the image of God he made
him, male and female he made them, and God blessed them saying: Increase
and multiply and fill the earth and subdue it, and rule the fishes of the
sea and the birds of heaven and all the cattle and all the earth.
=2.33= Hear then also the Platonic oration, which he puts round the
Demiurge of all: Gods of gods, of whom I am maker and father of works,
unlooseable they will be, I at least wishing it; that then which has been
bound is all looseable, yet to wish to loose that which has been well
fitted, and has it well, is of a bad one. Wherefore since you have been
generated, you are not immortal, nor wholly unlooseable; yet you shall not
be loosed, nor shall you obtain a portion of death, having obtained of my
will a still greater and more lordly bond than those with which, when you
were coming to be, you were bound together. Now then what I say, showing
it toward you, learn; three mortal kinds still remaining ungenerated, and
these not having come to be, heaven will be incomplete; for it will not
have all the kinds of animals in itself. And these having come to be by me
and having shared in life would be equalled to gods. That then they may
be mortal, and
this whole may be in truth all, turn according to nature you upon the
making of the animals, imitating my power concerning your own generation.
And as far as it is fitting for them to be homonymously immortal, that
which is called divine and leading in them of those who always wish to
follow justice and you, I having sown and having made exist will hand
over. And the rest you, weaving mortal upon immortal, work animals and
beget, and giving food increase, and receive again those wasting.
=2.34= {CYRIL} This noble one then laughs, and is bold unto the words
against us, at the generation of man announced through, I mean, the
all-excellent Moses

and thinks it has been given small to the nature of man from God that he
came to be from him according to the image toward him and likeness. And
yet how would not everyone of those accustomed to think well agree that
the matter is of the things that know most of all how to beautify? For
what is better, tell me, than to say that the divine likeness has been
sealed in us? For do we not say that the divine essence is the topmost and
highest of all, flashing round with unspeakable glory, and being in very
deed every form and beauty of virtue? Then how is what I said not clear
to all? Why then does he laugh at things so excellent, and why does he
also mock even the need for the minded and rational and most God-like of
the animals upon earth, I mean man, to have been honoured with rule over
all? And the nature of the affairs itself agrees with the words through
Moses; but he makes no account of what is likely, and he has also
unrestrainedly inclined upon this, the need to be attached to the voices
of Plato alone. And indeed he has marvelled, and this unconsidered, at the
oration fashioned I know not how by him, which he says the God of all has
made toward some generated and falsely-named gods.
=2.35= And I think we too must say toward this to him. For if in these
Plato makes character and according to the law of the poets puts round the
person of God words which he would think befit him, he has missed the aim
not moderately, and one would mock him as not knowing how to make
personification in the manner that was needed. But if he pretends to speak
of God, farewell as he talks nonsense; for it is not lawful to say that
the God who has authority over all granted falsely-true gods to have a
share of the glory that befits him particularly and him alone. For he
said that: My glory I will not give to another, nor my virtues to the
graven things. And come, marching the truth against the words of Plato
through short things, let us say that. For let it have been granted, if it
seems good, that the powers above and noetic, having come to be from God,
have been honoured with the calling of god; for we say there are some in
heaven named both gods and lords. But we ourselves also have been crowned
with such a calling, God saying toward us: I said, You are gods, and sons
of the Most High all. But there is in these a most necessary cause of the
matter, and the word unto us of the love of honour from God would have it
rightly as much as possible; for the Demiurge of all, since he worked the
noetic and rational creation according to image and likeness of himself,
inasmuch as he also exists good, has also honoured it with the calling of
god, and there is nothing unfitting. For we ourselves also are accustomed
to name, say, the image of a man homonymously a man. =2.36= The noetic and
rational creation then, inasmuch as it also lies in a greater word with
God than that which is not rational and noetic, appears having obtained
the better glory, gilded with the calling of god. And of the other
creatures none at all has been named god. For that neither is heaven an
animal, or the world simply, nor yet wholly ensouled, even if none of
those who finish among us should choose to say it, Aristotle the disciple
of Plato himself also suffices toward refutation even without the others
whom they say are wise. For he said, as we already having anticipated
contended, neither ensouled wholly through whole the world nor rational
nor noetic. That then to say the world wholly through whole, or whatever
this all is (for he himself said thus), is neither ensouled nor yet noetic,
the power of the truth does not grant him, from home, as I said, and from
those most proper to him as much as possible, he has a most sufficient
standing-against. And that God would not have said to those who are not
gods at all, ensouled or noetic, the need to make, the nature of the
matter itself would show us, going through moderate tests. For what at all
having thought according to himself

did the Demiurge of all hand over to other gods the need to toil concerning
the making of the three kinds? Having hesitated then, they would say, or
having deemed the things according to us worthy of no word; and these
would be, as I think, wholly alien to the highest of all essence.
=2.37= For if the Demiurge is good, how would some hesitation also come to
be in him concerning anything at all? for thus Plato himself somewhere
says he was good, and to a good one no envy comes to be concerning
anything. And to say that he thought it unworthy would be nothing other
than to assign puff to him and to define a writing of disdain. And how
would he choose to rule those whose generation he dishonours? Or in what
manner does he rejoice in the worships from us, if he did not even think
it right to create at all at the beginning? And that he also wishes to be
honoured from us, and demands the well-reined, and wishes the nature of
man to be likened to himself through every virtue, I would set beside,
and very easily, very many and true testimonies of the God-inspired
Scripture. But since his things are more credible to Julian, I say that
Porphyry has written in the second book On Abstinence from Ensouled Things
thus: Let us then also sacrifice, but let us sacrifice as is fitting, to
God over all, as some wise man said, neither burning anything perceptible
nor naming. For there is nothing in matter which is not at once unclean
to the immaterial; wherefore neither is the word according to voice proper
to this one, nor the one within, when it has been defiled by a passion of
soul, but through pure silence and the pure thoughts concerning him let us
worship him. And we must then, having been joined and likened to him, bring
our own leading as a sacred sacrifice to God, the same also hymning and
our salvation. In impassibility then, and in contemplation of God, this
sacrifice is completed.
=2.38= God then wishes to be honoured from us, and through a holy way of
life to be signified toward him noetically, writing his beauty in our own
souls. Then how, tell me, does he ask these things from us, having all but
thrown us aside to other makers and, so to speak, depriving us of the
self-working that has been given to all the other creatures? And why at
all does he provide for those upon the earth, though, according at least
to what seemed good to Plato, they have been given as a plaything to other
gods? For that he provides, and his sparing and gentleness going through
even as far as the cheapest things, he himself who knows his own Father
God will teach saying: Are not two sparrows sold for an assarion, and one
of them will not fall upon the earth without our Father who is in the
heavens? But perhaps he himself, as not being true, will set aside the
voice (for he fights God outlandishly), and he will receive those who have
become proper to him, according at least to the having gone astray I mean.
Alexander then the disciple of Aristotle writes in that On Providence
thus: And to say that God does not wish to provide for the things here is
wholly alien to God; for it is of some envy and of a nature altogether
out of place not to do the better things when able. And since each of
these is alien to God, neither would both be about him nor the one. It
remains then that the divine both is able and wishes to provide for the
things here; and if it both wishes and is able, it is clear that it also
provides; nothing then even of the chance things is reasonable to have
come to be without the divine mind and will.
=2.39= And of this opinion some say Plato also is, and clearly Zeno of
Citium and those from the Stoa are ambassadors of this doctrine. That then
the highest and by nature and only God of all also makes providence of the
things according to us, they themselves have also witnessed. Then what is
the matter, someone would say? And yet it was of one who thinks it right
to provide, not of the best of all, to deprive the human race, and this is
rather to have come to be from him and not that the makers obtain some
generated and from outside having the calling of God bare and only, even
if most of all even of the divine

glory itself it will make war, the thinking that others also are able to
make and to call into existence the things that once were not; for it is
not lawful to say that the proper and excellent things of the divine and
unspeakable nature are able to be in naturally some of the things that
have come to be from it. And the matter would rather fit it and it alone
and will be unto the highest glory for it; for impassable to the creation,
as I said just now, are the things of the highest of all essence only and
particularly. And one of such things we say is also the being able to work
maker-like and to bring into existence the things that once were not.
Then how would a generated and made nature, which the need to be
destroyed would also wholly and altogether follow, I mean according to the
words that are in the nature, work the things of God?
=2.40= For if the making is understood as a manner of science in God, one
would not grant beyond speech that the knowing how to make has been given
by him to the creation, since we ourselves also sometimes make some
things, having the underlying matter and using good craft about these; but
if it is energy and power of a magnificent nature, as I said, and the
making in a manner befitting God is beyond the measures of the creation,
why do they drag down the dignity of the highest nature and grant it to
those in generation and corruption according to what seems good to them?
Then they think they understand something of the necessary things, or
rather they slander the divine voices; for they say the ungenerated
granted to the generated to be able to fill his things and of him alone.
But yes, they say, for it was surely also necessary, if something should
come to be from God, that this be wholly better than to die and be
destroyed. Then, O most excellent, did the Demiurge then grudge some, and
did not grant them to have a share of the most beautiful, and he as it
were injected it into the worse, through not enduring to make them? He
then feared the necessity that does not grant him to be able to make
mortal things, or perhaps he was also wholly ignorant. If then they say he
has been ignorant, the creation knows something more than he; for it
creates, as they themselves say, the things under corruption. But if
leaving this they will confirm that he knows, how is it of a good one not
to do what he knows existing good? and the immortal is surely better than
the mortal.
=2.41= Then besides these it is not ignorant, I think, or rather it is
wise, to say that also. For the gods to whom they say the King and Lord of
all granted the making they say are not immortal, but bound together and
being loosed and remaining outside being destroyed for now, God wishing
them to be in these. When then according to their own nature nothing is
incorruptible or immortal or unlooseable, how would they not be ignorant
who say the God of all did not endure to make the three mortal kinds, but
rather handed this over to others to do? But leaving the nonsense of the
Greeks we say that the Demiurge has made the varied and many-formed in
creation and the otherwise other and not the same as in kind and form,
using the most God-befitting authority. For immortal, as I said, or
unlooseable is nothing; and the will of God upon each of the things made,
this is nature for it, and it reaches without any hesitation even as far
as the things according to us; for he is one and only, not having learned
this from another; for the matter is not a form of science, but a
God-befitting advantage of his own nature, grown upon it and it alone, as
indeed also the other of the dignities, which are least of all passable to
the generated, and have been assigned, as I said, to it and it alone
naturally. And that it is of the most outlandish things to assign us to
other gods of the energy of the making, standing it off from the God who
is by nature and truly, one would see also from his own teachers.
=2.42= For Hermes surnamed Trismegistus writes thus to Asclepius concerning
the

nature of the all: If then two have been agreed, that which becomes and
that which makes, it is one by the union, the one leading, the other
following; leading the making God, following that which becomes, whatever
it be. And do not, because of the variety of the things that become, be on
guard, fearing to attach lowliness and lack of glory to God; for one glory
there is for him, the making all things, and this is as it were the body
of God, the making. And to the making one himself nothing is thought evil
or shameful. For these are the passions that follow generation, as rust
the bronze and dirt the body; but neither did the bronze-worker make the
rust nor those who begot the dirt. And after other things again he goes
through hotter words, setting a clear example, and he says: Then is it
possible for the same painter both to make heaven and earth and sea and
gods and men and all the irrational and soulless things, but for God not
possible to make all things? O much mindlessness and ignorance concerning
God. For such suffer the most terrible of all; for saying they are pious
toward God and bless, by not assigning the making of all to him they do
not even know God; and besides not knowing, they also are most impious
toward him, putting round him a passion, disdain or inability. For if he
does not make all things, he does not make being proud or not being able,
which is impious; for God has one passion only, the good, and the good one
is neither proud nor unable. For this is God, the good, all power to make
all things. And the generated comes to be by the good and by him who is
able to make all things. And if you wish to learn how he himself makes,
and how the things that become come to be, it is possible for you; see a
most beautiful and most like image, a farmer throwing seed into the earth,
here wheat, here barley, here some other of the seeds; see the same
planting vine and apple-tree and the other of the trees; thus also God
sows immortality in heaven, and change in earth, and in the all life and
motion.
=2.43= And these things the men of old of the wise among the Greeks, who
have been not without mark, have both thought themselves, and also thought
it right that others choose to think. But this reasonable and
unmocking one, having said farewell to the opinions of the others, is
worsted by the words of Plato alone, and indeed he tries to make clear, as
at least he thinks, wisely, the oration which he says has been made from
the person of God by him, and he says: {JULIAN} But is this perhaps a
dream, having thought it learn. Plato names gods the manifest sun and
moon, stars and heaven, but these are images of the unmanifest; the sun
appearing to our eyes of the noetic and not appearing, and again the moon
appearing to our eyes and each of the stars are images of the noetic.
Those unmanifest gods then existing in and coexisting and having been
begotten from the Demiurge himself and having come forth Plato knows;
reasonably then the Demiurge with him says gods toward the unmanifest,
of the manifest gods clearly. And common Demiurge of both is this one who
wrought heaven and earth and sea, and stars, having begotten in the noetic
things the archetypes of these. Consider then that also the things upon
these well; for there remain, he says, three mortal kinds, clearly that of
men, and that of animals, and that of plants; for each of these has been
defined by its own words; if then, he says, each of these also should come
to be by me, it is altogether necessary that it come to be immortal. For
also to the gods nothing else is cause of immortality and to the appearing
world than the having come to be by the Demiurge.
=2.44= What then does he say: As much as is immortal, it is necessary that
in these it have been given by the Demiurge? and this is the rational
soul; of these then the forms even you wishing I having sown and having
made exist will hand over; and the rest you upon immortal

weave mortal? Clear then that the maker gods, having received from their
own father the maker power, generated upon the earth the mortal of the
animals; for if heaven was going to differ in nothing from man, and yes by
Zeus from a beast and last of them from the creeping things themselves and
from the fishes swimming in the sea, the Demiurge needed to be one and the
same of all; but if there is much the middle of immortals and mortals,
increased by no addition nor lessened by taking-away toward the mortal and
perishable, it is fitting that of these others be cause, and of others
others. {CYRIL} But in these at least that he confuses all things, and
mixes the natures of the affairs with one another, it is possible for
those who wish to behold, and very easily. For first he says the more
manifest of the gods are images of the more unmanifest, which Plato also
thought it good to call noetic and perceptible, naming the seen things
opinable with sense. And through these the noble Julian seems to us to
wish to make clear the ideas, which Plato sometimes contends are essences
and exist according to themselves, and sometimes also defines as thoughts
of God.
=2.45= Yet however it would have it, even to his own disciples they say
the word upon this is unacceptable, those craftsmen of these things; For
farewell the forms, says Aristotle; for they are twitterings, and if they
are, they are nothing toward the argument. Instead of what then does he
himself think it right to confirm and try to bring in as an unmocked
opinion that which has been supposed not to be well even by his own
teachers? Then besides this we shall find also another word of his not
moderately sick of the ignorant. For he said that Plato knows the noetic
and more unmanifest of the gods both existing in and coexisting and having
been begotten from the Demiurge himself and having come forth. And he adds
that of both the manifest and the noetic the God of all is maker, who
wrought earth and heaven. When then, as he himself also clearly agreed, of
these and of those the unbegotten God is maker, how does he say they have
been generated from him, and coexist and exist in him? How, tell me, will
the generated coexist with the unbegotten God? And in what manner will it
exist in? For we contend that the Word of God, being unbegotten, of
necessity coexists with him who begot and exists in him, and came forth
begottenly from him. But the accurate advocate of the invention of speech
of Plato says the highest God is unbegotten, and that those who have come
to be from him exist in and have been begotten from him and have come
forth, stirring and confusing all things and having deemed the unmixed
unto vision of God worthy of no word.
=2.46= And someone would say to him what is parodied by the tongues of the
many. Apart are the boundaries of the Mysians and Phrygians. Away with so
cold an idle-myth, O man; the word is for you concerning God who is beyond
all, and you are caught having thought quite small things concerning his
glory. Then having yourself shown him maker of all and having sufficed
alone toward the so bright and admirable of the creatures, upon the lesser
of all he says other gods helped him and worked with him toward making,
who would perhaps also say to the God over all: Gather a contribution from
us, and bring the three mortal kinds remaining also to the all; for you
giving soul, we shall add the body, weaving mortal upon immortal. But he
will perhaps also say and will shield his own doctrines, inserting that,
that the coming-together of the elements works the nature of the earthly
bodies. He follows then in this also his own leaders; for they say that
Empedocles son of Meton also thought thus and also chose to hold; for he
says the principles of the things that are are fire, air and water and
earth, and he adds to these Friendship and Strife; and Plotinus also
remembers these in that On Three

principal hypostases, and he says thus: And for Empedocles Strife divides,
and Friendship the one; and this also is bodiless for him, and the
elements as matter.
=2.47= So that even if the coming-together of the elements brings in some
benefit from themselves to the earthly bodies, we shall not receive the
matter untested. For we shall of necessity be curious whose awarding them
the coming-together and having gathered into one the things that fight
with nature, and as it were taming so that the power of each not become
unmixed with the others, but all as it were nod together toward one
another into existence and generation of some one thing. Is it not then of
the God who is best craftsman of all, and this too clearly devising for
the earthly bodies that, having lacked none of the elements, they may have
the benefit from them? For the akin is somehow dear. Therefore there would
not be some others the makers of the three kinds, but rather maker of all
would reasonably be understood the highest and only and beginning of all,
after whom there is wholly nothing beyond, but rather all things under him
and through him and under foot, as having been created. And that he
rejoices upon his works even the words of the Greeks contend, and we also
say, the God-inspired Scripture itself also agreeing this. For Wisdom
says: I was she in whom he rejoiced, and daily I was gladdened in his
face, in every time when he was gladdened having finished the inhabited
world and he was gladdened in sons of men. Then how is it not better that
he rather rejoice in his own and not in the creatures of others?
=2.48= But perhaps he says Plato that he has been held by hesitation, or,
as I said, that he has grudged the better to those upon the earth; and it
is no wonder, when even having set the good itself before the making God
he says it is unmoved, still, stable and standing off from both the need
and the wishing to work something of the things brought into existence,
and having as it were hesitated toward these he assigned the making to the
second god from him and after him. But behold he also sets others beside
him, who know how to work equally, that nothing in him may appear the
excelling. And since he thinks he says something wise and accurate, that,
if one and only Demiurge of all should be understood, heaven will in no
way differ from man and also from a beast and from those whose nature is
to swim through in the sea and also from creeping things, and in this way
one must also bring in the Demiurge analogously having it to the measures
of each of the things made, come, let us also say toward this to him: And
yet what is that which stands against or fights the thoughts that have it
rightly, if someone should choose to say all things of one Demiurge? Would
then the power that sufficed for those so overlying least of all suffice
toward the lesser? Or is this in no way, but the overlying and excelling
for those who have differed as much as possible from the others would be
that and only, the having obtained the self-working from God?
=2.49= Then how is the matter of it not a particular allotment, as it were
having dishonoured the rest of the creatures and all but also said a long
farewell to the earthly? And that these things are nonsense and full of
the last folly we have said having anticipated; yet let him declare that.
He says the generated gods have worked the remaining three kinds; is there
then in them, the things according to us having been excepted, not very
much difference according to the kinds and forms and sizes of bodies
clearly seen? For from the highest even until the cheapest of all their
generation would go through; and also if these things should be said
concerning the plants themselves, one would not lie. Shall we then set the
Demiurge analogously over each, lest that which excels be outraged if the
same should also be said to be maker of those that have settled under?
therefore another of men and very reasonably, and so many of

the others, as many as would be the things made and reaching even as far
as the very cheapest themselves. Are these things not, tell me, a laugh,
and quibbling hereafter carrying from what is likely and from the thoughts
unto truth; then how would it not become clear to everyone? And that he
delights in unsound and strange opinions together with his own leaders
would surely be clear; for contending that heaven is God, he says thus
again:
=2.50= {JULIAN} Why must I call Greeks and Hebrews here as witnesses?
There is no one who does not stretch the hands into heaven praying,
swearing God or gods, and taking a thought of the divine at all is carried
there. And they have suffered this not unfittingly; for seeing neither
anything about heaven being lessened, nor increased nor turned nor
enduring any of the disordered passions, but its motion harmonic, and the
order tuneful, and defined laws of moon, and defined risings and settings
of sun, always in defined times, they reasonably supposed God and a throne
of God. For such a thing, inasmuch as it is multiplied by no addition, nor
lessened by taking-away, and standing outside the change according to
alteration and turning, is clean of all corruption and generation. And
being immortal by nature and indestructible, it is pure of every stain;
and being eternal and unmoved, as we see, either it is carried in a circle
about the great Demiurge by a better and more divine soul dwelling in it,
or having received the motion from God himself (as, I think, our bodies
from the soul in us), it unrolls the infinite circle with unceasing and
eternal bearing.
=2.51= {CYRIL} That then the words are of one playing false, and who has
wholly lacked the thoughts unto truth, is not unclear. And since he has
supposed I know not how that the lifting the hands on high and also the
eyes of those who think to pray will suffice him toward a demonstration
that heaven is God, come, let us say toward this to him. Would then, tell
me, even if it should seem good to someone to behold clouds, and he
himself also send up and upon them the eye of the body, would the matter
suffice him unto full assurance of the need to confess even these
goddesses? Then how would he not be fitting to be laughed at who has been
snatched into deceit by thoughts so cold? And since, he says, from the
things from which heaven is by nature it has reasonably been marvelled at
and supposed among the many both to exist God and a throne of God, let him
come forward and teach if he has thought it the same and sets in equal
measure the things upon both; but I would never think him to have come to
this pitch of apoplexy as to think that to be God and a throne of him
differ in no way. For that the things concerning God would not reasonably
be understood as in sense according to us nor yet as upon bodies, even if
it should perhaps be said less than was needed, one would least of all
doubt. If then a throne of God should be said, it would signify the
kingdom under him, as indeed also upon us it is true to say that of so and
so the throne is bright and surpassing, that is the rule and the kingdom.
=2.52= Then how would that which is ruled share equally with him who
rules, with the smallness of those ruled that which rules them, with those
yoked under that which excels, with that which has more that which has
less? How then and in what manner does he say heaven is either God or a
throne of God? But Yes, he says, for it makes an ordered and harmonic and
tuneful motion, and sun and moon are by nature to make defined
illuminations, and risings and settings of stars and circuits and courses
and the motions according to places the same and unvaryingly and in every
time. Then how was not the divine Paul altogether wise, as showing from
such things the leader, and for the creation the president of the good
order, him who legislates in a manner befitting God for the things that
have come to be by him the how

each would need, and having shown the so much and unmeasured making not
without a president, through the things in which it has it well and is
marvelled at? For he says: For his invisible things from the creation of
the world are seen, being understood by the things made, both his eternal
power and godhead. And it seemed good both to think and to say these
things also to those who have become his leaders; Plutarch then said
concerning them, as we already said having anticipated: And they took a
thought of God from these; for always sun and moon and the rest of the
stars, having been carried the under-earth, rise like in colours, equal in
sizes, and according to the same places.
=2.53= Therefore those of old and who have become inventors of his
doctrines did not come into a thought of heaven having marvelled at the
course of the sun, but having also beheld all the other creation having
obtained a well-ordered and harmonic and not untuneful motion; and they
inferred rather of the strength of him who rules, and they understood his
glorious and lifted-up rule; but he, toward all the opposite, also
transfers the opinion upon this very thing, having endured nothing of the

illuminations of the moon the times becoming to each? Who is he who set
the course of the sun under measures? Is there not then one marshal of
all, as I said? Why then do you carry away from the truer thoughts,
commanding those who marvel at these things not unto vision of God, but
unto only the knowing heaven that it would be God, standing outside
alteration and manners and all change, and being clean also of corruption
and generation, and existing immortal by nature and indestructible. How,
tell me, do you say the generated heaven has lacked turning and alteration
and change? For if it has come to be at all, it has also been moved, and
this is a turning and a standing-over, the not being sometime having been
set beforehand as in a rank of privation. For if from not being that which
has come to be, the having been called toward generation would be
understood as alteration and turning for it, and that it has come to be is
agreed, how would it have come to be outside corruption?
=2.54= And how also is that which is tyrannized by generation and
corruption immortal by nature? And how also is that which has been brought
in time eternal? And yet Plato says clearly that time came to be with
heaven, that having been generated together they may also be loosed
together, if indeed a loosing of them should come to be. How then is that
which is loosed immortal by nature? And how also is that indestructible
which has obtained the not being destroyed in the will of the Demiurge
alone? For nature in these does not have the unshaken, nor yet is the
being well fixed in the things in which each is by nature proper to them;
but it is rather helped toward this by the nods of him who holds through.
Then he says that by a better and more divine soul dwelling in it it is
carried in a circle about the great Demiurge, or it has the motion from
God himself, as, I think, our bodies. But, O most excellent I would say,
the ungenerated according to you, and incorruptible and indestructible,
the unturned and unalterable heaven, which is clean of all stain, which
you also say is eternal, by of what sort of better or more divine soul is
it administered? What is the better in it besides that, or rather than the
God who made? What the great and excellent, I am not able to think. For I
think the unspeakable nature of God has been filled with the highest
glory. If then it should be said by us both to be incorruptible and
indestructible, beyond alteration and turning, ungenerated, eternal, freed
from all stain, having the all-perfect and needing nothing of any thing,
then what have you kept for it, dignifying the nature of the things made
with dignities so bright and surpassing?
=2.55= But the Hebrews, he says, thinking the things concerning heaven,
say it is a throne

of God, and the earth a footstool. Rightly, O noble one; for I shall
remember God himself saying through one of the holy prophets. Heaven is a
throne for me, and the earth a footstool of my feet; what house will you
build for me, says the Lord, or what place of my rest? And yet he does not
say heaven is God, but he likens it to a throne, and also the earth to a
footstool; and if someone should choose to learn thoroughly the cause of
the matter, it is no hardship to speak being true. For the most wise
Solomon raised in Jerusalem that much-spoken-of temple, and the peoples of
the Jews were very gladdened at it, and they thought that the God of all
had dwelt in it having been encompassed; for they are always somehow slow
unto understanding and unskilled in the words concerning God, who having
thought Jerusalem a city of God, contended that he dwelt in it and in it
alone, because of its being said through the voice of David: Glorious
things were spoken concerning thee, the city of God. God then refutes
those who think small things, and he says. What house will you build for
me, who have heaven as a throne, and the earth a footstool? For it was
needful, it was needful to show clearly to those who have supposed his
nature to have been contracted and bounded by places that he would be
everywhere and nothing contains him, but rather he himself comes through
all, and heaven is full of him, and the earth full.
=2.56= And that heaven is generated, it is easy to show through very many
testimonies from the God-inspired Scripture; for God said somewhere. I
with my hand made firm heaven and I commanded all the stars. And also the
blessed David says the heavens have been made firm by the Word of the Lord
and all their power is by the spirit of his mouth; and he knows them also
recounting glory of God. How, or in what manner? from the things which
they are and have come to be very much marvelled at and all but using such
a voice; for the best and excellent of the works of craft, though not
having a voice, have all but also cried through, showing upon themselves
the science of him who wrought them.
[End of the second tome of the refutations against Julian of the all-wise
and blessed archbishop of Alexandria Cyril.]
