HOMILY
ON PARADISE
And God planted a paradise in Eden toward the east and set there the man whom
he had formed. Let us conceive a planting worthy of God and a paradise fitting
the love of beauty of a craftsman of such a kind and of such greatness. In
what was said before it is written, Let the earth bring forth herb of grass
and fruit-bearing tree sowing seed, making fruit. If paradise were of the
common trees, it would clearly have been included in the first generation of
plants, and the trees now being planted in paradise by God himself would have
needed no exceptional planting of God. Now, however, the array of trees
artistically wrought in paradise by God himself is shown by the account as
different from the kinds of plants already existing. For as he deemed man
worthy of an exceptional forming, apart from the rest of the animals, so
also he made the dwelling-place of man 76 a work of his own hand, a place
excelling the whole creation, wonderful in beauty, lying in a commanding view,
unshadowed because of height, lit by all the risings of the stars, illumined
from every side, having the most pleasant temper of the seasons, bathed in air
most translucent. There, then, he planted, where there are no blasts of winds,
no excesses of seasons, no hail, no fiery whirlwinds, no gales, no
thunderbolts, no winter frost, no damp of spring, no summer burning, no autumn
dryness, but a temperate and peaceful concord of the seasons with one another,
each of them both adorned with its own beauty and not plotted against by its
neighbor: neither summer spoiling the beauties of spring, nor the fruits of
summer or of late autumn dripping away under old age. For the seasons danced
round that place <with> one another, but they also ran together at once,
attending it as a guard of honor with the things exceptional to each: the
delights of spring, the nourishments of summer, the gladnesses of late autumn,
77 the rests of winter. The earth fat, soft, truly flowing with milk and
honey, fit for fruit-bearing, all watered with most fertile waters furnishing
a beauty beyond contrivance. For the waters are fine and transparent, having
much delight for the sight, and furnishing more of the beneficial than of the
delightful.
First, then, he created the place itself worthy of receiving the plantings of
God; then he planted in it every kind of beauty of trees, granting through
themselves both a most graceful sight and a most pleasant enjoyment. How shall
I be able to bring the fatherland clearly before your sight, so that you may
recall yourself from this exile? Flowering meadows are a most pleasant
spectacle; imagine in your mind what is more graceful than these. Here the
rose is with the thorn, having the unpleasant mixed with the grace, all but
bearing witness to us that for you, O men, the delights have been mixed with
the painful. 78 For in truth none of human goods is unmixed, but at once the
griefs have been fixed beside the gladnesses: to marriage, widowhood; to the
rearing of children, anxiety; to good offspring, losses; to high repute,
dishonors; to prosperity, damages; to luxuries, satieties; to healths,
sicknesses. The rose is in flower, but it produces dejection in me; for as
often as I see the blossom, I am reminded of my sin, because of which the
earth was condemned to put forth thorns and thistles. And here the grace of
the spring flowers is short-lived, failing us while we still long for it; for
we have not finished gathering before it has withered in our hands. There,
however, the blossom does not shine out for a short while, but has a lasting
delight: graceful the sight, unending the enjoyment, the fragrance unsated,
the fine color flashing out. No blasts of winds distress them, no new moons
wither them, no frost freezes them hard, no flaming of the sun parches them,
but a moderate breeze, furnishing a fine and sweet breath, blowing upon them
lightly and gently, preserves their grace unconquered by time. And the
beauties of the plants are themselves also worthy of the creating and the
planting of the one who made them: all that are of brushwood and all that are
bushy, the single-stemmed, the many-branched, the high-crested, those
flourishing on every side, the leaf-shedding, the ever-leaved, those that are
stripped, the ever-blooming, the fruit-bearing, the 79 barren; some † seen for
use, others contributing toward enjoyment; all excelling in size and beauty,
shady with branches, flourishing in their foliage, teeming with fruits, made
at once for another use and another pleasure for others. For of each of the
fruits there is much that is graceful, and again more of the needful than of
the delightful.
How shall I set before you the excess of that pleasure? If I compare it with
things here, I rather insult by the comparison than set any clarity before you
from the image. All things perfect, all things ripe, not grown little by
little; for they do not grow from blossom to the prime, but appearing at their
own prime, completions of nature, not of human artifice. There also are kinds
of birds of every sort, furnishing through themselves a wonderful enjoyment
both by the flower of their plumage and by the music from nature; so that
through all the senses the man is feasted, seeing some things, hearing others,
touching others, smelling others, tasting still others. And spectacles of
various animals, all tame, all of like temper with one another, both hearing
and uttering things well understood. The serpent was not dreadful then, but
gentle and tame, not swelling fearfully upon the earth and creeping, but
standing high upon 80 feet. There he set the man whom he had formed. He formed
him elsewhere and led him in there. As God made the lights and set them in the
firmament, so also he formed the man, dust from the earth, and set him in
paradise. Note that it is not said whom he made, but Whom he formed. For the
one he made is the one according to the image; and this one is bodiless. And
the bodiless is not circumscribed by place. For the made thing follows the
formed thing, that is, the soul follows the body. For the composite is
together, but place belongs first to bodies, and the soul is in place by
consequence through its relation to the body, not being circumscribed in
places from its own nature [by the body].
Have I gladdened you from the delights in paradise, or have I grieved you more
by the account of perishable things? For surely the mind, thinking high and
lofty and supermundane things, as having its citizenship in the heavens and
looking toward the dignity above, was thinking of the promise and looking
about to hear something of the good things promised [in the promises] to us,
81 Which eye has not seen nor ear heard nor have they gone up upon the heart
of man. If eye has not seen, who is the one narrating? If ear has not heard,
how will yours receive it? If they have not gone up upon the heart of man, how
shall our mind take hold? We do not cast out the bodily, and we seek the
spiritual. For as in the law there was also the sensible, but it made a type
of the intelligible, so also we understand paradise bodily, and we allegorize
it spiritually. God planted a paradise in Eden toward the east. The names of
the plants have been passed over in silence, but the place in which he planted
has been handed down: In Eden, he says, he planted, that is, in delight; for
Eden is named delight. Does he then name delight the enjoyment through the
mouth, given over to the belly whose end is the privy, and has he handed down
a delight that perishes, and is this the grace from God, a filling of the
stomach and well-feeling of the body and a pouring-out that produces pleasure?
Or it is not even pious to say this. For it is a provision of wantonness and
insolence and of all the forbidden things together to overfatten the body and
to sink the soul, making it easily carried toward sin. Let us therefore
conceive a delight worthy of God, so that we may also be taught the planting
fitting the husbandry of God 82. What delight, then, can there be that befits
the saints? Delight in the Lord, and he will give you the requests of your
heart. Since, then, the beauty of virtue is infinite, having its beginning
from the much-varied wisdom of God, for this reason it is written that God
planted a paradise not toward the east, but Toward the easts. For each of the
plants planted by the Lord, adorned in its own light, flashed out. There The
rushings of the river that gladdens the city of God; elsewhere it is named a
torrent of delight, feeding and increasing the heavenly beauties of the
plants, which in what follows is called A river going out of Eden to water the
paradise. In what was said before, then, it was said that having first filled
the creation he thus brought man in to rule and lordship, but now the
contrary: he first formed the man and then planted the paradise. And the cause
is that there, so that he might bring the householder into a house already
full and not, having created him poor, later grant him the abundance, but
here, granting him the perfection that comes from progress, he created him
elsewhere and later settles him in paradise, so that, having learned well the
difference between the life outside and the way of life in paradise, from the
comparison of both he may know the superiority of the beauty, and may fear the
loss of the fall. 83 And that you may understand a planting worthy of the hand
of God, remember what was said in the Gospel by the Lord, that Every plant
which my heavenly Father has not planted will be rooted out. From that
planting are those Planted in the house of the Lord and flowering in the
courts of God. And again in the prophet: I planted you a fruit-bearing vine,
all true. The imitator of Christ also dares and says that We are
fellow-workers of God; you are God's husbandry; I planted, Apollos watered,
but God gave the increase. And the righteous has been likened to the tree
planted by the outlets of the waters, which will give its fruit in its season
and its leaf will not fall away; and The righteous will flower as a palm; and
A vine you removed from Egypt, you cast out nations and planted it. Try, then,
through such teachings to come into understanding of that, so that you may
come to be according to the rays of the divine light, in which the light of
knowledge rises as the paradise of delight is planted. And if you conceive
some place receptive of the saints, in which all who have shone on earth in
good works dwell, enjoying the grace of God and the divine and intelligible
delight, you do not fall away from the fitting conjecture about it. There,
then, certain good powers, rooted and firmly fixed, ministers of the saints,
to whom the man was handed over when newly born and needing much 84 training
together toward perfection. In which the company of the saints is, in which
the risings of the light are, in which the delight of the soul is, there God
set the man. If you are fleshly, you have the sketch of that bodily place and
the most pleasant filling. If you think the enjoyment of the sensible things
great, press on toward this, in which there is an unceasing supply of delight.
If you are spiritual and think things higher than bodily enjoyments, go up
with the mind toward the beauties of the angels and learn well the fruits of
righteousness in them, behold the river of God filled with waters, whose
Rushings gladden the city of God, that city Whose craftsman and maker is God.
This river of God runs through that city, this one that begins from Eden, that
waters the paradise. Having learned all these things well with the mind,
glorify God, because to him belongs the glory unto the ages of ages. Amen.
