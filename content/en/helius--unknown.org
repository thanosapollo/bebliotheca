* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

It is my purpose, Diocletian Augustus, greatest among so many princes, to set
before the knowledge of your divinity not only those who have held the place
of princes in this station which you govern, as I have done down to the
deified Hadrian, but also those who were called by the name of Caesars and
were not princes or Augusti, or who by any other means came into the fame or
the hope of the principate.

Of these I must speak first of Helius Verus, who first received only the name
of Caesar, taken by Hadrian's adoption into the family of the princes,

and since too little is to be said, and a prologue ought not to be more
enormous

than the story, I shall speak of the man himself at once.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Ceionius Commodus, who was also called Helius Verus,

whom Hadrian, as age grew heavy and more grievous diseases pressed him, after
he had already traversed the world, adopted for himself, has nothing memorable
in his life except that

he was the first to be called Caesar only, not by testament, as was formerly
the custom, nor in the way Trajan was adopted, but nearly in that manner in
which in our times, by your clemency, Maximianus and Constantius have been
named Caesars, as if they were true sons of princes and

designated heirs of the August majesty.

And since something must be said of the name of the Caesars especially in the
life of this man, who obtained this name alone,

the most learned and most scholarly men think that Caesar was so called either
from the elephant, which in the language of the Moors is called caesai, slain
in battle by him who first received the name,

or because he was born with his mother dead and her womb cut open, or because
he came from his parent's womb with a great head of hair, or because he was
grey-eyed and flourished beyond human custom. Certainly whatever that was,

it was a happy necessity, from which so famous a name, destined to last with
the eternity of the world, blossomed forth.

This man, then, of whom we are speaking, was first called Lucius Aurelius
Verus, but having been taken by Hadrian into the family of the Helii, that is
into Hadrian's, transcribed 1ueri et Obrecht and others; uiri et P; uirtute
Peter, following Bernhardy. 2 al' adeptus P corr.

and was called Caesar. His father was Ceionius Commodus, whom some have handed
down as Verus, others as Lucius Aurelius,

many as Annius. All his ancestors were of the highest nobility, whose origin
was for the most part from Etruria or from

Faventia. And concerning this man's family we shall treat more fully in the
life of Lucius Aurelius Ceionius Commodus Verus Antoninus, the son of this
man, whom Antoninus was ordered to adopt for himself.

For that book which has a prince of whom more must be said ought to contain
everything that belongs to the family tree.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Helius Verus was adopted by Hadrian at the time when already, as we have said
above, he had little vigor

and was thinking of a necessary successor, and at once he was made praetor and
set as commander and governor over the Pannonias, soon created consul, and,
because he was destined

for the empire,

he was designated consul again. A congiarium was also given to the people on
account of his adoption, and three hundred million sesterces were bestowed on
the soldiers, circus games were exhibited, nor was anything omitted that might
swell the public rejoicing.

And so much did he prevail with Hadrian the prince that besides the affection
of adoption, by which he seemed joined to him, he alone obtained everything he
desired, even by

letters. Nor indeed did he fail the province to which

he had been appointed; for with affairs well managed, or

rather luckily, even if not of the highest, he nevertheless obtained the
reputation of a middling commander.

Yet he was of so wretched a health that Hadrian at once regretted the adoption
and could

have removed him from the imperial family, since he often

thought of others, if by chance he had lived.

It is reported, finally, by those who have related Hadrian's life more
diligently in writing, that Hadrian knew Verus's horoscope and adopted him,
whom he had not much approved for governing the republic, for this reason
only, that he might satisfy his own pleasure and, as some say, an oath, which
was said to have passed between himself and Verus under secret conditions.

For that Hadrian was skilled in astrology Marius Maximus demonstrates to such
a degree that he says he knew all things concerning himself, so that of all
days even to

/these verses Verus's life does not admit,/
adding that:

/Give lilies with full hands; I shall scatter purple flowers and with these

when indeed even that is said to have been said with

/I have adopted a god for myself, not a son./
this man

yet when one of the men of letters who was present was consoling him and said:
/What 3? if his horoscope has not been rightly cast, whom we believe will

Hadrian is reported to have said:
/You say those things easily, you who seek an heir of your patrimony

not of the republic./
From this it appears that he had it in mind to choose another and at the last
time of his life to remove this man from the republic.

But the event aided his plans, for when Helius had returned from the province
and had prepared a most beautiful speech, which is read even today, whether by
himself or through the masters of the bureaux or of speaking, in which on the
Kalends of January he might give thanks to Hadrian his father, having taken a
drink by which he thought himself helped, he perished on the Kalends of
January themselves.

And he was ordered by Hadrian, because vows intervened, not to be mourned.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

He was of a most joyful life, learned in letters, more acceptable to Hadrian,
as the malevolent say, by form

than by character. He was not long in the court; in private life, though less
to be approved, yet less to be blamed

and mindful of his family, elegant, seemly, of regal beauty, of a countenance
that commanded reverence, of rather lofty eloquence,

ready in verse, even in public affairs not useless. Many pleasures of this man
are reported by those who have written his life, and indeed

not infamous but to some extent

dissolute. For the tetrapharmacum, or rather pentapharmacum, which Hadrian
afterward always used, he himself is said to have invented, that is sow's
udder, pheasant,

peacock, ham in pastry and wild boar. Of this kind of food Marius
Maximus relates otherwise, calling it not pentapharmacum but tetrapharmacum,
as we ourselves

have followed in his life. Another

kind of pleasure is also reported, which Verus had invented. For he had made a
bed with four projecting head-rests enclosed on all sides with a fine net and
filled it with rose leaves from which the white had been taken,

and lying with concubines he covered himself with a covering made of lilies,
anointed

with Persian scents. Some also repeat that he made couches and tables of roses
and lilies and indeed purged, which, even if not becoming, are not

yet such as to threaten public ruin. And the same man is related always to
have kept on his bed the writings of Apicius Caelius and Ovid's books of the
Amores, and to have called Martial, the epigrammatic

poet, his Virgil. Lighter still are these things,

that he frequently set wings on his runners after the example of
the Cupids and often called them by the names of the winds, calling one
Boreas, another Notus and likewise Aquilo

or Circius and by the rest of the names, and making them run without rest and
without pity. The same man, when his wife complained of outside pleasures, is
reported to have said:
/Suffer me to exercise my desires through others; for wife is a name of
dignity, not of plea-
sure./
His son is Antoninus Verus, who was adopted

by Marcus, or at any rate together with Marcus, and with him held equal
empire, for they are the first who were called two Augusti, and whose names
are so prefixed in the consular fasti that they are said not

two Antonini but

two Augusti. And so much did both the novelty and the dignity of this thing
prevail that some consular fasti took the order of consuls from them.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

For his adoption Hadrian gave infinite money to the people and the soldiers,

but when he, a man a little more shrewd, saw that he was of most wretched
health, so that

he could not throw a rather solid shield, he is reported to have said:
/We have lost three hundred million, which we paid out to the army and the
people; if indeed we have leaned enough upon a falling wall

and one who can scarcely support not the republic itself, but us

ourselves./
And these things indeed Hadrian spoke with his prefect. When the prefect had
betrayed them, and through this Helius Caesar was from day to day more and
more weighed down with anxiety, as a desperate man is,

Hadrian gave a successor to his prefect, who had betrayed the matter, wishing
it to seem that he had tempered the sad

words. But it availed nothing; for, as we have said, Lucius Ceionius Commodus
Verus Helius Caesar (for he was called by all these names) perished and was
buried with an imperial funeral, nor did he have anything

of royal dignity except that of death. He therefore grieved at that man's
death as a good father, not as a good prince, for when anxious friends asked
who could be adopted, Hadrian is reported to have said to them:
/Even

while Verus was still living I had decreed./
From this he showed

either his judgment or his knowledge of things to come. After this man,
finally, Hadrian, long in doubt what to do, adopted Antoninus called by the
surname Pius, to whom he added the condition that Antoninus himself should
adopt for himself Marcus and Verus and should give his daughter to Verus, not
to Marcus,

nor did he live longer, weighed down with languor and a diverse kind of
diseases, often saying that a prince ought to die sound, not feeble.
