OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA ON THE INSCRIPTIONS OF THE PSALMS
I
I received your command with all eagerness, O man of God, granting the zeal
equally to me and to you, and I attended to the inscriptions of the psalms.
For this you commanded us to investigate, the meaning contemplated in them, so
that what is able through these to lead us toward virtue might become clear to
all. Having therefore associated with the whole book of the Psalms with
greater attention, I thought it necessary not to begin the examination from
the inscriptions, but concerning all the psalmody together to set forth
beforehand a certain technical approach toward the comprehension of the
thoughts, through which from the sequence the discourse about the inscriptions
will also be made clear to us. One must therefore first understand the aim of
this writing =5.25= toward what it looks, then discern the constructions of
the thoughts through sequence toward the subject, which both the order of the
psalms well disposed toward knowledge of the aim shows, and the sections of
the whole book marked off by certain proper boundaries, the whole prophecy in
the psalms having been divided fivefold. When these have been moderately
understood beforehand, the benefit from the inscriptions will become more
familiar to us, being made manifest through the comprehension of the things
previously examined. One must therefore begin the contemplation from here.
CHAPTER I
The end of the life according to virtue is blessedness. For everything
accomplished according to zeal has its reference altogether toward something.
And just as medicine looks toward health, and the aim of farming is a
preparation toward living, so also the acquisition of virtue looks toward the
one living according to it becoming blessed. For this is the chief point and
limit of everything conceived according to the good. That which is truly and
properly contemplated and conceived in this lofty thought would reasonably be
said to be the divine nature. For thus the great Paul names God, setting the
blessed before all the theological names in one of the letters, writing thus
in the words: The blessed and only Potentate, the King of those who rule as
kings and Lord of those who lord it, who alone has immortality, dwelling in
light =5.26= unapproachable, whom no one of men has seen nor is able to see,
to whom be honor and might eternal. For all these lofty thoughts about the
divine would be, according at least to my account, a definition of
blessedness. For if someone were asked what the blessed is, he would not miss
the pious answer if he followed Paul's voice and said that blessed is that
which is properly and first said to be the nature beyond the all; and the
blessed in humans, by participation of that which truly is, becomes and is
named in some measure that which the nature of the participated is. Therefore
the definition of human blessedness is likeness toward the divine. Since
therefore the truly good, or that which is beyond the good, this alone is both
blessed and desirable by nature, of which everything that participates becomes
blessed, well does the divine writing of the psalmody through a certain
technical and natural sequence show us the road toward this, in a teaching
simple according to appearance and unconstructed, variously and in many forms
technologizing the method of the acquisition of the beatitude. It is possible
then


also from the first hymnody itself to take some notion about the matter at
hand, how the discourse, having cut virtue threefold, testifies the blessed to
each section according to a certain suitable analogy, now blessing the
alienation from evil, as becoming a beginning of the inclination toward the
better, and after this the study of the lofty and more divine things, as
already producing a habit of the better. Then the likeness toward the divine
accomplished through these for those being perfected, for whose sake the
things that went before are also called blessed. And this it hints at by the
ever-green tree, to which the life perfected through virtue is likened. =5.27=
CHAPTER II
But so that we may stand more accurately over the teaching about the virtues,
which the discourse traces out through all the psalmic guidance, it would be
well for us first to distinguish on our own, advancing a certain technical
account in order through sequence, how it is possible for the lover of such a
life to come to be in virtue. For thus we would recognize the sequence of the
teaching shown forth to us. One must therefore, if he is going to look toward
virtue, first distinguish by reason the life that is refined and the life that
is blameworthy, marking each with its own tokens, so that their account may be
unconfused, not muddied by anything of the things that mix them. And signs of
the property of each of these there are perhaps also certain others; but the
more generic than the others, according at least to our supposition, are
these: that the cheer that comes to humans from them has been divided toward
sense and toward thought, vice sweetening the sense, and virtue cheering the
soul. Upon these it would be sequential, through praise and through slander,
both to lead the thought of the hearers away from the worse and to attach it
to the better, the slander of the wicked life bringing hatred toward vice, and
the praise of the good things drawing desire toward the more praiseworthy. And
after this, so that the encomium of the good life may become more effective
and the censure of the worse more living, it is proper to make memory of those
themselves who have been wondered at in virtue and of those condemned in vice.
For the examples of the lives, when they have been set forth, produce a
certain intensification of the disposition in the soul and firmness, hope
drawing the =5.28= soul toward equal honor with the best, and the slander upon
the condemned training toward flight and alienation from like practices. And
upon all these it would be necessary to make toward each of these a certain
detailed teaching, which will both show the better and turn away from the
worse, toward this guiding the hearing by certain suggestions and counsels,
and from the worse shutting it off through the deterrent words. And when these
have been thus distinguished, since everything that stands alien toward
pleasure is hard for nature to accept (and I mean pleasure the friend of the
body; for the cheer of the soul has been settled far in the middle from the
irrational and slavish luxury); and we recognized beforehand this as the
proper sign of each of the lives, the one in virtue and in vice, that through
vice the sense-organs of our flesh are flattered, and virtue becomes cheer of
soul for those who accomplish it. And for those now being introduced toward
the higher life, for whom sense still seems to be the criterion of the
beautiful, the soul is not yet sufficiently able to look down upon the good,
being still untrained and unused to such comprehension. And toward everything
not recognized, even if it be as beautiful as possible, our desire remains
unmoved, and if desire does not preexist, neither would any pleasure come to
be of the thing not longed for. For longing becomes a road into pleasure.
Necessarily one must invent something


of this sort for those who have not yet tasted the unmixed and divine
pleasure, through which the teachings of virtue will be received along with
it, being sweetened through one of the things that cheer the sense, just as it
is also customary for physicians to do, whenever they make some bitter and
hard-to-take among the protective drugs =5.29= easy to take for the sick by
seasoning it with the pleasure of honey. And if the introduction of the life
according to virtue has been fully technologized for us through the things
said, so that one must first separate from one another the contrary practices,
then mark each of them with its own token, and after this dignify the one by
the account and make the other to be fled from slander, and by the examples of
notable men strengthen the thought toward each, and by the particular
suggestions both show the road leading toward the good and turn away from the
worse, and make the harsh and manly of virtue sweet for those still infantly,
being sweetened through one of the things that cheer our sense, it would be
time to understand how through this contemplated technology the whole teaching
through the psalms has been brought forth for us, both shutting off from vice
and drawing toward virtue.
CHAPTER III
First then, for we begin the examination from the last things, let us look at
the device through which it made the way of life according to virtue, being so
hard and intense, and the enigmatic teaching of the mysteries and the secret
and hidden theology in hard-to-reach contemplations, so easy to take and
sweet, that not only by perfect men whose sense-organs of the soul have
already been purified is this teaching pursued with zeal, but it has also
become a proper possession of the women's quarters and brings pleasure to
infants as one of the playthings and becomes for those more advanced in age
instead of =5.30= a staff and a rest, and the one who is made bright thinks
the gift of this teaching to be his own, and the one disposed gloomily from
circumstance thinks that such a grace of the writing has been given because of
him; and besides this, people traveling and sailing, or occupying themselves
with certain seated crafts, and all simply in all practices, men and women, in
health and in sickness, make it a loss not to carry this lofty teaching
through the mouth. And already even the drinking-parties and the bridal
brightnesses take this philosophy as a part of cheer in the enjoyments, so
that we may pass over the inspired hymnody through these in the night-vigils
and the philosophy of the churches pursued in these. What then is the device
of this unspeakable and divine pleasure, which the great David poured over the
teachings, through which the lesson has thus become well acceptable to the
nature of humans? Perhaps it is ready for everyone to say the cause according
to which we make the study in these in pleasure; for one would say that
singing the words in melody is the cause of going through these with pleasure.
But I, even if this is true, say that one must not overlook it uncontemplated.
For the philosophy through melody seems to hint at something greater than
according to the thought of the many. What then is it that I say? I heard one
of the wise going through the account about our nature, that the human is a
certain small cosmos, having in himself all the things of the great cosmos.
And the ordering of the all is a certain musical harmony, fitted to itself in
many forms and variously according to a certain order and rhythm and singing
with itself and never torn from this symphony, even if much =5.31= difference
of the things that are is contemplated in the particulars. For as it happens
upon the plectrum that technically touches the tones and leads the melody in
the variety of the sounds, so that if some single-formed sound were in


all, the melody would not have been composed at all; so also the mixture of
the all, in the varied things contemplated in each in the cosmos, through a
certain ordered and untransgressed rhythm touching itself and working the
good-fit of the parts toward the whole, musically works this all-harmonic
melody in the all, of which the mind becomes a hearer, using this hearing in
no way, but peering over the sense-organs of the flesh and having come to be
above, thus hearkens to the hymnody of the heavens. As it seems to me the
great David also heard, when through the technical and all-wise movement
contemplated in them, as they recount the glory of the God who works these
things in them, he listened. For truly a hymn of the unattainable and
unspeakable glory of God, led forth by such a rhythm, is the symphony of all
creation toward itself, mixed through the contraries. For rest and motion have
contrariwise toward one another, and these have been mixed with one another in
the nature of the things that are, and a certain impossible mixture of the
opposites is seen in them, so that both the stationary is shown in the motion
and the ever-moving in that which is not moved. For all the things according
to heaven are always moved, either going around with the unwandering circle or
being unrolled according to the contrary through the planets. And the sequence
in these always stands and remains upon identity, never =5.32= changing from
that in which it is toward some other new thing, but always having likewise
and remaining in the same. The coming-together then of the standing toward the
moving, coming to be in a certain ordered and untransgressed good-fit through
all, is a certain musical harmony, completing a mixed and divine hymnody of
the power that holds the all through. Of which it seems to me the great David
also, having come to be in hearing, said in one of the psalms that both the
other powers all according to heaven praise God, and the starry light, both
the sun and the moon and the heavens of the heavens and the superheavenly
water, whatever he means by the water, and all the things that follow that
creation has. For the co-breathing and sympathy of all things toward one
another, administered by order and cosmos and sequence, is the first and
archetypal and true music, which the fitter of the all technically strikes up
by the unspeakable word of wisdom through the things always coming to be [in
wisdom]. If then the whole ordering is a certain musical harmony, of which God
is craftsman and demiurge, as the apostle says, and the human is a small
cosmos, and this same one has also been made an imitation of the one who
fitted the cosmos, what the account knows upon the great cosmos, this it
reasonably also sees in the small; for the part of the whole is altogether of
the same kind with the whole. For just as in the chip of a small glass it is
possible to see, in the shining part, the whole circle of the sun shown as in
a mirror, as the smallness of the shining admits, so also in the small cosmos,
I mean the human nature, all the music contemplated in the all is seen,
analogizing to the whole through =5.33= the part, as the whole is contained by
the part. And the instrumental arrangement of our body also shows this, having
been crafted by nature toward the working of music. You see the pipe of the
windpipe, the bridge of the palate, the kithara-singing through tongue and
cheeks and mouth, as through strings and plectrum? Since then everything
according to nature is dear to nature, and music has been shown to be
according to nature for us, for this reason the great David mixed melody with
the philosophy about the virtues, pouring a certain pleasure of honey over the
lofty doctrines, through which nature in a certain manner contemplates itself
again and heals itself. For a healing of nature is the good-rhythm of life,
which it seems to me the melody advises through riddles. For perhaps this very
thing becomes an exhortation toward the higher constitution of life, that the
character of those living in virtue must not be unmusical and deviant and
off-sound, nor beyond the measure of the string sounding sharp; for the
well-fitted of the string, overstretched, is altogether broken;


nor again toward the contrary, in lack of measure through pleasure, to slacken
the tension; for the soul becomes mute and voiceless, being made flabby by
such passions; and all the other things likewise, to tighten according to
season and to let go of the tension; looking toward this, how the manner in
the characters may remain for us through all well-melodied and well-rhythmed,
neither being loosed without measure nor overstretched beyond the measure.
Whence also the history testifies to David the accomplishments of this divine
music; that once, having found Saul beside himself and standing out of his
thought, he so healed him, singing down the passion, that the thought returned
again for him toward what is according to nature. It is clear then from these
toward what the riddle of the melody looks, that it advises that the
settling-down of the passions =5.34= that variously come to be in us from the
circumstances of life be made. But this also it is proper not to run past
uncontemplated, that not according to the song-makers outside our wisdom have
these melodies also been made; for the melody does not lie in the tone of the
words, as it is possible to see in those, among whom in a certain composition
of the accents, the tone in the sounds being both made heavy and sounding
sharp and being shortened and stretched, the rhythm is begotten, but having
inserted into the divine words a melody both unconstructed and uncontrived, he
wishes to interpret by the melody the meaning of the things said, uncovering
as far as possible by a certain co-disposition of the tone according to the
voice the mind lying in the words. Such then is the appetizer of the food, by
which the nourishment of the teachings is sweetened as by certain seasonings.
CHAPTER IV
And it would be time already to understand by the account the very feast of
the virtues according to the technical contemplation set forth beforehand for
us. For first it is possible to find virtue distinguished from vice by clear
signs, so that the difference of each of these toward the contrary is
unconfused. For from the cheer that comes to us from them the exceptional of
the property of the practices is shown, vice cheering the senses about the
body, and virtue the soul; so that the nature of the underlying found from
these signs is both unerring and unambiguous. And this it is possible to find
from certain other things both according to what is ready at hand and
according to the contemplation of the thoughts lying in the depth =5.35= in
many places of the psalmody, and especially according to the fourth psalm, in
which he says that those who do not distinguish the false and the vain from
the truth are heavy of heart, but love the non-existent, and overlook what
remains and is worthy of being loved. For he said that piety alone is truly
wonderful, and all the other things that are pursued by humans instead of the
good things lie in supposition, not being upon themselves, but seeming to have
being in the vain thinking of humans. And so that he might uncover the
doctrine about these more clearly, going forward he says that the many define
the good in the things that appear, saying that that alone is good which
someone may show forth to the sense. For many, he says, say Who will show us
the good things? But the one who looks toward virtue overlooks this slavish
judgment of the beautiful. And he sees the beautiful in the light. And thus he
marks the God-formed and lofty cheer; and he calls that light the one shining
from the divine face, which sense does not have nature to reach. For there has
been marked, he says, upon us the light of your face, Lord. For the face of
God contemplated in certain characters does not seem to me the prophet to
think other than the virtues; for by these the divine form is characterized.
And having said this he says the perfect token of virtue: You gave cheer into
my heart, saying the heart instead of the soul and the mind. For it is not
possible for the mind to be pleased by the


baits from vice. And he sets in opposition to the cheer of the heart this
material and worldly plenty, saying that for those who look at the present the
belly becomes the criterion of the beautiful. For having said that grain
=5.36= and wine are multiplied for such, from a part he included in the
account all the pleasures about belly and feast; which rule every material
occupation, about which the zeal is brought to no successful limit, for there
is in nature no storehouse of the enjoyment that comes to humans in a moment,
so as to make stored up for themselves the pleasure which they acquire
according to every zeal, but as one of the deceitful phantasms, having seemed
to have been grasped by the lovers of pleasure, it was immediately made unseen
and passed over into the not-being. And of such an apparition one track is
left after the withdrawal, the shame, having marked in these a deep and
hard-to-wipe type of these, through which it may pass, so that it is possible,
according to the art of those who hunt, to discern the nature of the beast by
the tracks. For they too, whenever the quarry is unseen, recognize the animal
through the track. If then the boar or the lion are indicated by their own
tracks, it is altogether sequential that the nature of pleasure also be
recognized in the track left behind of it. But its track is shame. Therefore
also that which marked such a type in the soul is either altogether shame
itself or productive of shame. But this was contemplated by us in a passing
place; for it was necessary to show according to the principal, through the
psalmody, of what sort the end of each of the practices according to virtue
and vice is. In the aforementioned psalm then, having said the end of virtue
to be peace and relaxation and the single-formed dwelling unmixed toward the
passions, accomplished upon hope of the participation of God, the opposite
=5.37= to this he indicated here through silence, but in many places through
the psalmody he cries saying And the lawless will be utterly destroyed, and
Seed of the impious will be utterly destroyed, and He who loves injustice
hates his own soul; and he will rain snares upon sinners, and other countless
things of like manner to these. And the whole book of the psalmody is full
both of the praises of virtue and of the accusation of those living in vice.
And the memory of the histories, being divided toward the two aims, makes
virtue to be emulated of the well-reputed persons, and wickedness to be fled
through those condemned. For whenever it raises you toward virtue by the
example, it says: Moses and Aaron among his priests and Samuel among those who
call upon him. They called upon the Lord, and he himself heard them. In a
pillar of cloud he spoke toward them. And whenever it shows the wicked end of
vice, it goes through the sufferings of those condemned in wickedness. The
earth was opened and swallowed Dathan, and it covered upon the synagogue of
Abiram. Flame burned up sinners; and Make them as Midian and Sisera; and Set
their rulers as Oreb and Zeeb and Zebah and Zalmunna, all their rulers; and
many other such; and all the particular counsels the psalmody cries to you
from beginning to end, leaving out in no part the exhortation toward the
beautiful, and the things through which one might flee vice; all have been
yoked alongside the judgments that lead toward the beautiful. =5.38= For the
acquisition of the good becomes flight of the contrary and taking-away. And it
would be superfluous to set out all these through accuracy, the zeal of the
discourse about such things being clear to those who encounter this writing.
CHAPTER V
And the whole treatment of the psalms has been cut fivefold, and there is in
these sections a technical arrangement and division. And the boundary of the
sections becomes clear, ending in like manner in certain divine doxologies,
which it is possible


to recognize according to the division in the psalmodies shown by us. And the
number of the psalmodies of each section is this: of the first, forty; of the
second, one and thirty; and seven and ten of the third, and of the fourth so
many; and of the fifth, five and forty. The first part then stands upon the
fortieth from the first, of which the limit is Blessed is the Lord the God of
Israel from the age and into the age, so be it, so be it. The second upon the
seventieth and first, of which the end has thus: Blessed is the Lord the God
of Israel who alone does wonders, and blessed is the name of his glory into
the age and into the age of the age. And all the earth will be filled of his
glory, so be it, so be it. The third upon the eightieth and eighth, and this
too ends into a like limit. For it has thus: Blessed is the Lord into the age,
so be it, so be it. And of the fourth section the limit is the hundredth and
fifth, of which the end is near to the rest: Blessed is the Lord the =5.39=
God of Israel from the age and until the age, and all the people will say So
be it, so be it. And a fifth part is from this into the last, of which the
limit is Let every breath praise the Lord. What then the technical order in
these we have understood, it would be time to say through a few things. In the
first it both stands those living in vice from the out-of-place wandering, and
draws them toward the choice of the better, so as no longer to travel upon the
deceits of the impious, nor to stand through depth in the wicked path of sin,
nor to practice vice as unmoved and established for themselves, but to be
joined to the divine law, having accomplished for themselves through study the
unerring journey, so that the habit toward the better may be rooted like a
plant for them, being watered by the divine teachings. The first entrance then
toward the good is standing-away from the contraries, through which the
participation of the better comes to be. And he who has already tasted virtue
and understood the nature of the good by the experience according to himself
is no longer such as to be drawn away from the attachment toward vice by some
necessity and admonition and to look toward virtue, but he over-thirsts for
the better. For the unrestrained and intense of the desire the discourse
likens to thirst, having sought out the most thirsting nature among the
animals, so that especially the intensified of the desire might be shown
through the animal that comes to be exceedingly in thirst. And this animal he
calls a deer, to which it is nature to be fattened by the eating of the
venomous beasts. And hot and =5.40= burning are the juices of the beasts, of
which the deer, having eaten, becomes drier by necessity, having been drugged
by the juice of the beasts. And for this reason it desires the water more
intensely, so that it may heal the dryness that comes to it from such eating.
He then who has been initiated beforehand into the life in virtue in the first
part of the psalmody and has recognized the sweet of the longed-for by the
taste, and has consumed every creeping form of desire in himself and has eaten
through with the teeth of temperance the passions instead of beasts, thirsts
for the participation of God more than according as the deer longs for the
springs of the waters. And it follows for the one who has obtained the spring
after the exceeding thirst to draw of the water so much as the desire
according to authority draws. And he who has taken in himself the longed-for
is full of what he longed for. For not according to likeness of the fullness
in body is that which has become full emptied again, nor does the drink remain
idle in itself, but the divine spring, in whatever it comes to be, transforms
toward itself the one who has touched and jointly imparts of its own power.
CHAPTER VI
But the contemplative power and activity of the things that are is proper to
the divinity. Therefore he who has in himself what he longed for also himself
becomes contemplative and


surveys the nature of the things that are. For this reason he has made this
the beginning of the third section of the psalmody, in which the discourse
especially examines this, how the just of the divine judgment will be
preserved in the unevenness of life, the good-lot according to this life not
coming to humans for the most part according to the worth of the choices. For
often about =5.41= the same one it is possible to look down upon the two
extremities, the last in vice and the most extreme according to prosperity.
Looking toward whom someone somehow buckles in thought, lest perhaps that
which is called the worse be better for human nature, and conversely that be
evil which is numbered in the portion of the better. For if justice is
praised, and he who has been zealous about this fares badly, and vice is
slandered and grants lasting enjoyments of all the things pursued to those who
have been zealous about it, how is it possible not to think vice more
preferred toward choice of life than virtue, the condemned than the
encomiumed? He then who is lofty in thought and as from a certain outstanding
lookout stretching the eye toward the things standing away saw in what the
difference of vice toward virtue is, that from the last things, not from the
present, the judgment of these comes to be. For having understood by the
contemplative and seeing-through eye of the soul as present what is stored up
through hope for the good, and having passed by in the soul every appearing
thing, and having slipped within the heavenly inner rooms, he lays hold of the
lack of judgment of those who pettily entrust the judgment of the beautiful to
the sense-parts. Through which he says For what is there for me in heaven, and
besides you what have I wished upon the earth? By the same part of the account
both magnificently magnifying and lifting the heavenly by the account, and
contemptuously and mockingly cheapening and loathing that upon earth pursued
by the eyes of the thoughtless. As if someone of those born in a prison,
judging the gloom to be some great good, in which he was nourished and grew
together, then having partaken of the open-air =5.42= grace condemns his
former judgment, saying of what spectacles of sun and stars and all the beauty
about heaven I preferred the accustomed gloom through inexperience of the
better. For whose sake he condemns beforehand by the account the lack of
judgment about the beautiful, saying himself to be beastlike, until he saw the
good in those things. And when he came to be with God, and God is the Word,
and was led toward the right, and the Word becomes a right leader for him
through the counsel, and he saw the glory in virtue, through which the
taking-up comes to be for those who look toward heaven, then he uses those
voices, of which the one makes the good in the heavens in wonder, and the
other spits out the nothing and vain of the deceived zeal about life. And the
whole diction has thus: Beastlike, he says, I became beside you, signifying
the irrational attachment in such things, then he adds But I through all with
you. And having said this he also adds the manner of the joining toward God,
so that we too may learn how he who was formerly beastlike after these things
is joined to God; For you grasped, he says, my right hand. He says a
taking-hold of God, the impulse of the thought toward the right things; And in
your counsel you led me; for not without divine counsel does the leading upon
the beautiful come to be. And with glory you took me to yourself. Well does he
set glory in opposition to shame, which becomes as a certain vehicle and wing
of the one taken to himself by the divine hand, whenever someone alienates
himself from the works according to shame. And thus he added to the things
said: For what =5.43= is there for me in heaven, and besides you what have I
wished upon the earth? Which indeed the many of humans do until now; such
things existing for them according to authority in heaven, nevertheless they
make in the part of prayer that the phantasm-like deceits come to be from God,
some power or honor or wealth or this wretched little glory, about which human
nature is mad. And he who has come to be in these through sequence adds But
for me to cling to God is good, to set in the


Lord my hope. Showing this, that he who is glued toward him through the hopes
grows together in a certain manner with God and becomes one toward that one.
CHAPTER VII
Such then having come to be in the third section the ascent upon the height,
he who has been taken up so far in thought, again taking hold of the overlying
step, becomes himself greater than himself and higher, in the fourth part as
it were having crossed a third heaven according to Paul and having become
higher than the heights previously gone through. For these things no longer
the common human receives, but he who has already been glued and joined to
God. And the discourse says thus beginning the next part: A prayer to Moses,
the man of God; for this one is already such as no longer to be child-led by
law, but to become an introducer of law for others; such as was that lofty
Moses, whom we hear, who voluntarily shook off the royal worth as a certain
dust that had been drawn around the base of the feet; who for forty years
