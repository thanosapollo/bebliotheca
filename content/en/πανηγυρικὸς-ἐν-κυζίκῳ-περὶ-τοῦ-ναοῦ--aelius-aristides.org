For all, as I think, there are sufficient occasions of speaking as in the
present; for the city is worthy of zeal, if any other of the Hellenic ones is,
and the work for which it holds the sacred month is the greatest of those that
have come into the sight of men, and the times are the best and most complete
of any ever; and for me the occasion is especially clear and of the habit of
our life. For Asclepius commands me to speak; so that it is not possible to
take into account weakness of body against the Savior himself, nor to fear the
magnitude of the subject lest it not be easy to succeed; but as Pindar said,
when a god has shown a beginning there is nothing that hinders, especially
since it is not now first that we make trial of him, but in many and great
things before we have learned in ourselves, not from others, how much of ease
is in surplus for him even in the things that seem altogether impossible, and
not merely in those that are hard but that do not necessarily have despair. I
at least have come to such a point of trusting that another will take care,
that I do not know in what way I improvise, except so far as not entirely from
the mouth, but still writing. For neither before did I know what must be said,
until it was already necessary to speak, and he who commanded is greater than
all preparation, at least for me. And I think that great affairs and seas are
not in the same way; for the latter, the greater they are, the more fear they
provide, but the former help in some way, for it is possible as it were to
take hold of a harbor from many places; so that if one is not altogether
unfortunate, at least that of the whole

seeming to miss he will escape. And I think that even someone of those among
the Hyperboreans has already learned the oracle about Cyzicus and the witness
of happiness for the city, who is an interpreter to the other cities, but to
this city also a founder. For the other cities he settled through the settlers
whom he sent each way, but of this he himself became founder directly. So how
is Cyzicus not happy, having begun from such a beginning and referring at once
to such a founder and witness? And one would know even looking at once to its
position and the whole nature, that the god set the name for it as it was
coming to be; so both the things from the land and the things from the sea
coincide. For it lies before Asia, beginning from an island, but being
established into the mainland, having the Euxine Pontus thrown before it on
one side, and on the other the Hellespont, being a certain bond of either sea,
or rather of all that men sail. And sailings past and sailings around and
sailings in of all sailors never leave it, but some are conveyed along the
back, having given themselves with a fair wind into the open sea through
islands of the city, and others run down into it and run up. And yet if it
seemed good to the poets to address the city of the Corinthians as happy,
because lying on the Isthmus it receives those traveling on either side of the
Peloponnese, surely this one splendidly has a claim to the title. For lying in
the middle of the sea it gathers all men into the same, sending both those
from the inner toward the outer and those from without toward the inner, like
some navel of the place between Gadeira and Phasis. And it has happened, I
think, also to other islands to lie in abundant sea all around, and Homer
glorifying Crete said it lies in the middle in the wine-dark sea; but that of
this one is more august. For it has not occupied the middle of one sea; it is
possible

to say that not only is the sea much for it, but also manifold, the part
toward the north a certain spring and starting-point of all that is navigable,
and again the part toward the west the longest and most graceful strait, and a
third in the middle of both these both by nature and by place, this which has
been called Propontis, almost all of which it itself has filled.

And such are the things from the sea; and again those from the land are
sisters of these and a counterweight. For the whole land is much and abundant,
and as one would think fit the mainland to be in size according to the name,
and the natures are many and separated from one another most of all and all
incline toward the best. For here mountains are tamer than plains among
others, and plains sufficient to belong not to a city but to nations, and
rivers and lakes and marshes and glens and certain delights of the blessed. So
that if someone wished to settle all the places, many cities would come to be
around the lakes, many on the shores of the sea, and not a few in the inland,
and it would belong to the many to be ignorant of one another, as many as did
not happen to be cities next of cities. And such are the things from land and
sea for the city, both rivalling one another and not held by many examples.
But indeed the city itself you might address both as island and as peninsula
and if anything is beyond a peninsula toward mainland. For because it is not
passable at a neck nor started from some narrow, but stretching the mainland
along a length, continuous toward it by both legs, it has surpassed the
example of Scyros and if any other city or island is mainland by a little. And
yet Thucydides praises Sicily, because it is twenty stades distant from the
mainland at the least; but this is so distant from the mainland that it is
possible even not to notice that it is not an island, and even if someone the
moles

should take away and the bridges, it is possible to swim to it from the
mainland. Thus both the whole island and the city are no less honorable in
nature than the greatest islands, Crete and Sicily. Beauties then of public
buildings and the whole construction and size of the city neither is anyone so
without resource of speeches as not to be able to praise, nor so sufficient at
speaking as easily to exhibit it. But I, leaving aside all the rest, will
choose this, of which it most belongs to me to make mention. For this seems to
me most beautifully of tokens to show the founder, and that it is a work of
someone of the greater ones; for it seems to be sacred places of all the gods,
like that which they call an agora in this way. For as if by lots the whole
has been set apart and divided for all gods, and the temples have occupied it
as if gods were competing with one another for the salvation of the city. And
sacrifices and processions and approaches and services of gods with the
established laws are not far from the city of the Athenians, except not using
kinship toward the best. And indeed the other power is following upon these.
For so much is in surplus for the city that even of temples elsewhere and
other public adornments if someone should say this is the choragus, he would
not be ashamed; so splendid and common to all are the things it possesses, and
neither Italy overlooks the possessions of this one nor Egypt so far nor the
Tyrrhenian sea nor, a poet might say, the haunts of Syrians and Cilicians, nor
any nation, so to speak, either on mainland or in islands, which has not been
adorned from this city. And Ionians and Aeolians and the islands and cities
before the feet, what would one say? And the most spirited thing, for as the
good runners having given a long start easily catch, so then you too, having
provided all men what they needed, yourselves came last.

I have come to the very surge, how might I say

as much as I wish? But I would say so much as, having said, I would pardon
myself. For I almost need to say that all who attempted the like things you
showed as children, having raised so great a work, which to conceive would
have seemed a work of madness, but to complete greater than according to a
human. One might be at a loss whether most of the island has been transferred
here or remains in place. And I think all would agree that the dedication
would belong neither to another city nor to another quarry than yours; for
nature would not suffice. For formerly those sailing used to take their marks
from the peaks of the islands, this is Cyzicus, this Proconnesus, whichever of
the others one might see; but now the temple suffices instead of the
mountains, and for you alone there is no need of beacons nor fire-signals nor
towers toward those putting in, but the temple filling all that is seen shows
at once both the city and the magnanimity of those who have it, and being so
great it is more beautiful than larger. And if Homer and Hesiod happened to be
passing, they would easily, I think, say the tale about the wall of Troy
having transferred it, that Poseidon and Apollo jointly practicing their art
accomplished the work for the city, the one providing the rock from the depth
of the sea and at the same time making it possible to be conveyed, and the
other as is likely a founder wishing to adorn his own city with so great an
addition. You would say that each of the stones is instead of a temple of the
whole, and the temple instead of the whole precinct, and again the precinct of
the temple becomes sufficient of a city. And if you wish the things of ease
and luxury, instead of the houses of three stories and the triremes it is
possible to see the greatest temple, many times the others, and itself triple
in nature. For some of it is an underground spectacle, and some an upper
story, and in the middle the customary. And courses

under the earth and hanging running through it in a circuit, as if not in the
part of an addition, but having been made on purpose to be courses.

These things then there is no need to adorn with speech, but to set them down
to the geometers and panegyrists, and of these as many as are complete and
sufficient to measure so great a matter; since I fear even this, lest not even
for all these it be possible to find out the exactness. For if someone should
stand away from the speeches about the temple itself, it is sufficient even to
wonder at the machines and the vehicles, which, not existing before among men,
the need of the temple invented. And it is worth also for the sake of fortune
to rejoice with you. For you inscribed the best of the kings up to that time;
and the work has come to you to completion in these times, which have obtained
as their lot the fairest again of fair things and on behalf of whom most
justly would so great a thank-offering be standing to the gods, since it was
not easy to invent a greater. That these provided most kingly ancestors of the
kings before them, and of what sort the construction of the race was for them
from both sides from the beginning, and what nurture they were reared with and
upon what things, and the surplus of the whole education, is a long speech to
go through and by no means easy to fill; but what seems to me most fitting for
the present gathering to hear and their most peculiar encomium and of those
that have come to memory in speech the most marvelous and has gathered the
whole nobility and education, and neither can any private man be silent, and
every city and all races of men are struck with amazement, about this we will
spend time as far as is possible. For these are they who raised the proverb to
the furthest and through the greatest works showed a limit that in truth the
things of friends are common, not having shared a herd of horses, nor vessels,
nor some plethra of land, nor as far as previously longest

it was necessary for partnership to go forward; but having come to such a
point of magnanimity as to occupy all the earth and the sea with this saying
and let nothing of the things that are escape them unshared, having truly
according to Homer made the earth common, by their own presidency, but by the
use of all together; things which before it was possible to pray for on behalf
of justice, but nowhere remained to hope, but the limit of happiness appeared
as some prayer. For generals have already chosen generals in addition in
dangers and cities received one another on occasions and kings made
friendships and treaties toward kings, and these for the most part overcome by
need, and we know partnerships having come to be up to this; but a king
willingly now first chose a king as partner for himself of the whole and alone
of those from all time sought not so much to take on rule in addition as to
take on a king upon all the equal terms. And indeed having set the rule into
the middle he did not make his own part less, but by this very thing he
established both his own and that of the co-ruler greater. For Alexander
astonished men, not having accepted the partnership toward Darius, and perhaps
doing well, inasmuch as he was not going to share Asia toward an equal in
honor; yet it seems to me that not even if someone were much better than
Darius, not even so would he accept to rule all the others with another; but
these made the kingship a token of the most complete justice, as if taking on
the affairs of men for this sake, that they might be common teachers of virtue
to all. This is beyond every circuit of Caucasus, this beyond Indians being
taken and Caspian gates, this beyond all human things, this belongs to those
conquering by the fairest things and what befits nature. For neither if there
is land outside the Atlantic sea, having acquired this in addition, nor
whatever uninhabited of the things within through excess of cold or

heat, if having found these to be tempered from the gods they made them an
addition of the empire privately each for himself somehow, they would not seem
to me to have surpassed all in virtue by so much, as now having made the
things that are common and having made one another of more account than all
the things that are.

O marvelous the one who needs not to be king, unless it should seem good to
the brother, and marvelous the one who does not accept to be king, unless with
the brother. O having exchanged equals with one another, alone most like one
another to the furthest, and alone differing most of all, themselves partners
of the greatest things, but not having others as partners of the equals. For
the king of the Persians seemed to possess something distinctive, the
so-called eye of the king and ears of the king; and this perhaps was providing
even some use, but the most, as I think, a boast, in order that he might seem
to see and hear double of the other men. But these found this indeed fairest
and wisest toward excess; for both became ears and eyes to one another, and
they doubled the use from nature, and they made the guard of the empire not
alien, but truly from home, having shared all. And the good of monarchy has
not been destroyed any the more, but it is a marvelous monarchy of two bodies
and two souls with one mind established, as if some harmony running through
all the strings. What music then would become better than this? What concert
dearer to both Apollo and the Muses? What harmony more common to the race of
men? For the things of Amphion and Zethus came to so much as to wall Thebes,
and here some blame the music of the one, but these having shared the music
over all the cities and all men sing the same, having received the song from
the father somewhere and from ancestors, and still having established it
altogether more splendid, as those

children came to be according to prayer. And they seem to me to have thought
things also fitting to the type of the constitution; for this city alone
changed the law. For all the others both Hellenes and barbarians as many as
laid hold of dynasties even a little, considered this one thing, how no one
would enjoy the same things, but that all would be driven away as much as
possible. But these alone of all set the goods in common into the middle, like
prizes for the best, and neither does it differ to dwell in Europe or Asia or
Libya nor is there any boundary dividing, not Tanais, not Lake Maeotis, not
the Island of Atlas, not whatever one might say, but all men and all races
belong to the city, and it is possible for all to rule the things that come to
be. As then they knew the ancestors established the participation of the
things in part as common for all the worthy, so they themselves far surpassing
all into virtue made the rule of the whole common. And the sweetest of the
conception, for as formerly they provided the annual magistrates according to
a yoke to name for those outside, so now it is possible to name the perpetual
ones together both from the father and with one another. Thus they considered
things following also of the whole constitution and provided the excess better
than hope. And indeed Homer said somewhere glorifying the kings /great is the
spirit of Zeus-nurtured kings;/ but they made neither strength of spirit nor
measurelessness of anger the marks of the rule, but brought in the strongest
things of love of humanity and magnanimity, and truly showed that their
nurture was divine. Their reasoning at least is divine and truly having the
pattern from above and looking toward that constitution, if one must speak a
summary; since they say that the gods themselves and the whole cosmos and
heaven are a friendship and partnership that holds together, and through these
for all time all things safely

proceed, and envy and ill-will in the houses of gods nor in characters neither
ever is nor comes to be.

Those then who take on palisades and walls increase the empires with certain
small and truly mortal things, but those taking on virtue and justice and
friendship, having brought forth so splendid and marvelous examples for all to
see, these have truly obtained the victory from the gods, these have been
adorned with the immortal crown, these it is likely both to be friends of gods
as of one another and to be considered purely benefactors of men. For as those
good at writing through the tablets above the head show the letters with
excess, writing them as greatest and as fairest for the children to imitate
according to their own power, so these revealed the fairest of the things
among men through the greatest of the examples, so that each has it to imitate
according to power and as it were to enjoy the kings as teachers toward his
own affairs. What stelae of Sesostris would be able to surpass these, which
that one set up on both sides as he went around, or rather seem to be near
these? What man at once both conquered so much and so profitably for the
conquered? For indeed for the others fear makes the ruled intimate, but on
behalf of these prayers and common thanks from all, becoming into the middle
not by propriety alone, but from the purest of the purpose. And there come in
to me also the things of the two savior gods, who having occupied all the
earth save in common and work together, sending to one another and making
common both the benefactions from themselves and the thanksgivings from men;
with whom whom would it be more likely for these to be in good repute, or whom
to be in more account of the salvation, who have become as it were prophets
and an imitation of their mind, having composed the fairest friendship for
benefit of the common race of men?

It is worthy then to know gratitude to the gods, and to the

kings both to rejoice with them and to join in prayer, and to count themselves
blessed of the time, and besides counting blessed to enjoy of the empire the
things that come to be. For fair also are these adornments of the buildings
and wonderfully persuasive to the masses, but that is already complete and
truly a gift of some god, whenever both adornments sing together, those in the
souls and those of the buildings. For as in these we praise the harmonies and
that each thing saves the order that comes to be toward one another, so also
this must be thought to be living toward beauty, whenever harmony and order
prevail through all. This is the truly proper adornment of cities, this saves
both a man privately and a city, this one need not buy with expenditure of
money nor waste of times nor servants, nor busy oneself setting up machines,
but each himself must persuade himself to prefer the better things. And do not
wonder if having nothing to censure I then think it right to advise, nor think
that exhortation belongs only to those coming forward upon a charge. But it is
also fitting for those who praise; and this is clear from the old oft-told
speech about the runners. For almost no one, so to speak, exhorts the last of
them nor those wholly left behind, but those whom he sees being near the
victory. And I think also the generals spend the hortatory of the speeches not
toward those of the soldiers for whom it is the same to say many things and
nothing, but toward those for whom it is going to come to be most to the need.
So also we now say nothing intricate nor clever toward you, but nearly these
things which you do and in which you have been reared, and these are respect
of rulers, honor of laws, thinking the same things, a matter always in good
repute, and strongly agreeing with the present times. Now all the cities must
take one another as sisters, now factions and disturbances and

rivalries and being petty about the vain things they must put out of the way,
having thought these are diseases of beasts and that those must have them, and
take true peace and guileless friendship and justice and the partnership
through all if possible as the greatest of gains, knowing in the intercourses

even a defeat is more seemly than victory and of more worth. For these from
the beginning are the strongest and fitting to the present times and having
the benefit at once from home themselves and differing a marvelous amount
toward the honor from the kings. Since there is no way you would more gratify
them than by doing thus, or how what you wish would more come to be for you
from them than if you should present yourselves such as those would most wish.
And nearly, as I said, I advised nothing other than the things you do, but
just as I praised the temple having looked to it and having found nothing from
home, so also now I have neither found nor said anything of my own, but what I
see you doing, these I praised and these for the sake of the good of the
token, in order that nothing of the things that ought to be present might be
lacking to the speech. So much we too contribute to you for the festival as
from the moment, and perhaps it is moderate.
