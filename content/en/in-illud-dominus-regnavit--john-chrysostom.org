* On the Saying "The Lord Has Reigned"
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of the same, on the ninety-sixth psalm: "The Lord has reigned; let the earth
rejoice, and let many islands be glad," that is, man and the Church. And on
the saying, "Day unto day utters speech."

1. Of joy. For if some good thing had not come to pass, the prophets would not
have cried out, "The Lord has reigned." But since, once Christ began to reign,
sin has been loosed, the devil put to shame, the demons have fled, the stings
of death have been broken, the paradise that was shut against Adam has been
opened, the thorns of our sins have withered, my thistle has been rooted out,
and a threefold grace has been set upon my head, my ancient curse has been
loosed. I no longer fear when I hear, "You are earth, and to earth you shall
return." For in baptism I put off the earth and put on heaven, and now I hear,
"You are heaven, and to heaven you shall go. The Lord has reigned; let the
earth rejoice, and let many islands be glad." I no longer lie under the earth.
I am no longer held in those inner, dark, and lightless storehouses and
enclosures of the devil in Hades. I must now mount the clouds and run up into
heaven through the grace of Christ who has begun to reign. The word is not
without witness: "For we shall be caught up in the clouds," Paul cries, "to
meet the Lord, and so we shall always be with the Lord." "The Lord has
reigned; let the earth rejoice, and let many islands be glad." Eve no longer
fears Adam's reproach, for in Mary her defeat has been recovered. And I no
longer fear the serpent that crawls bent to the ground, that turns its head
aside. I no longer fear its venomous bite, for I am no longer earth but salt.
The Lord said to the serpent when he cursed it, "Earth you shall eat all the
days of your life." Before the coming of Christ I was earth, eaten by the
serpent; but the Lord came and changed me into salt. Learn, beloved, how salt
is made, that you may know yourself in that very thing. Salt, then, is made
from water and spirit, and he has begotten us too from water and Spirit.

2. "The Lord has reigned; let the earth rejoice, and let many islands be
glad." Why not all, but many? The many churches of the orthodox. Those that
have no share in right faith are the churches of the heretics. How then should
they rejoice, while they insult King Christ in many ways? Would that one might
even call those churches, beloved, and not dens of foxes, in which there are
foxes not by a change of nature but by a turning of the mind.

3. If Eunomius or Arius or Macedonius should come to my Lord Christ and say to
him, "I will follow you wherever you go," he answers him fittingly and in due
measure: "The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests, but the
Son of man has nowhere to lay his head." Concerning these the Preacher says,
"Catch me the little foxes that spoil the vineyards." Little foxes are the
disciples of the heretics, who slip into our churches and, by their teaching,
ruin the faithful who hang like clusters in the Christ-bearing vineyard. "For
evil communications corrupt good manners." "The Lord then has reigned; let the
earth rejoice, and let many islands be glad."

4. Then what follows: "Cloud and gloom are round about him." The cloud once
covered the Hebrews as they came out of Egypt, checking the burning rays of
the sun and lighting the way with its own brightness. If you wish to see that
cloud more clearly, go up the mountain with Christ and Peter and James and
John, and there you will see the bright cloud overshadowing them, and a voice
coming out of the cloud and saying, "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well
pleased; hear him." What then is "a cloud round about him"? Cloud is one thing
and gloom another. A cloud is a light-casting, most delicate fabric from
luminous air. Gloom is a misty and foggy darkening. How then are cloud and
gloom round about him? And he set the cloud first, and then the gloom. For
first comes the cloud, that is the Godhead, and then the manhood. He said
"cloud," then, that through the cloud he might show the godlike and
light-bearing beauty of his divinity. He said "gloom," that through the gloom
he might signify the human covering. In this gloom the Word of God hid his own
coming.

5. For hear the prophet saying, "Coals were kindled from him, and he bowed the
heavens and came down, and gloom was under his feet." He said "feet of God" to
indicate his journey upon the earth. "Coals were kindled from him, and he
bowed the heavens and came down, and gloom was under his feet." He hid the
journey of his divinity under the covering of the flesh, so that the opposing
powers might not know the manifold way of his wisdom. "For if they had known,
they would not have crucified the Lord of glory." As the prophet also says,
"In the sea is your way, and your paths in many waters, and your footprints
shall not be known." His footprints shall not be known, says the prophet, yet
Eunomius dares to track out the generation of his divinity.

6. "And he bowed the heavens and came down, and gloom was under his feet, and
he mounted upon the cherubim and flew; he flew upon the wings of the winds."
Not that the Lord has wings and flies, but the prophet said "he flew" to show
the swift and keen descent of God the Word from heaven to earth. "And he
mounted upon the cherubim and flew. He flew upon the wings of the winds." Who
are they, if not surely the angels? "For he makes his angels spirits, and his
ministers a flame of fire." But since I called the gloom a misty and foggy
darkening, lest anyone think I am telling a myth and not a fact, let the
prophet himself bear witness: "And he mounted upon the cherubim and flew; he
flew upon the wings of the winds."

7. And "he made darkness his hiding-place," that is, surely, the body. That he
calls the tent of the body darkness, let the next verse of the psalm teach
you. For after saying, "and he made darkness his hiding-place," he added,
"round about him is his tent." Have you seen his cloud? Have you known his
gloom? Have you known what gloom is? Now loose that much-talked-of
interpretation, spoken by many but not truly expounded: "Day unto day utters
speech, and night unto night proclaims knowledge. And he made darkness his
hiding-place." "The darkness," Scripture says, "God called night." Night was
upon the nations. So the Lord put on the night; he wore our tent.

8. "Day unto day utters speech." The Father to the Son, inasmuch as light is
day, and the Son also. "For I am the light of the world." "Day unto day utters
speech." Draw near to the Jordan, and there you will see how the Day from
above utters speech to the Day standing below. For he says, "This is my
beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased." But lest you think the prophet
omitted the Holy Spirit when he said, "Day unto day utters speech," learn,
beloved, that he confessed the Trinity. For the Father is Day; the Son is Day;
the Word flying through the midst of the air in the form of a dove is the
Spirit. That the Holy Spirit is called word, let Paul bear witness: "Take the
sword of the Holy Spirit, which is the word of God."

9. "Day unto day utters speech, and night unto night proclaims knowledge."
"For cloud and gloom are round about him." He himself is Day and he himself is
Night, both God and man. Day according to the Godhead, Night according to the
manhood; for in comparison with the divinity, humanity is night. This is what
is meant by, "He made darkness his hiding-place." "And the darkness he called
night." "Night unto night proclaims knowledge." Christ, according to his
humanity, proclaims to all the night of the earth, that is, to humanity, the
knowledge concerning the Father and himself and the Holy Spirit. To him be the
glory and the power unto the ages of ages. Amen.
