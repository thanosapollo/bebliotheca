A DISCOURSE TO TATIAN ON THE SOUL, ARRANGED BY CHAPTERS, BY OUR FATHER AMONG
THE SAINTS GREGORY, BISHOP OF NEOCAESAREA IN PONTUS, SURNAMED THE WONDERWORKER

You asked us, admirable Tatian, to send you a discourse on the soul, laid out
with effective proofs. You wanted this done without using the testimonies of
the Scriptures, though that is in fact, for those who want to think piously,
true teaching, more demonstrative than any human reasoning. You said you were
not seeking this for your own assurance. You have already been trained to hold
to the divine Scriptures and traditions, and not to let the mind be tossed
about by the twists of human arguments. You wanted it for the overthrow of the
opinion of the heterodox, who cannot bear to be persuaded by the Scriptures
and try, by a certain cleverness, to confute those unused to such arguments.
So we were moved to obey this charge of yours readily, not hanging back as if
we were inexperienced in this kind of argument, but taking courage from your
goodwill toward us. For you know how to set out in public what you judge to be
well said, and to pass over and hide what seems to fall short of what is
needed, doing both out of the favor and friendship you have for us. Knowing
this, I have set out upon the exposition with all confidence. I shall arrange
the discourse in a certain order and sequence, the same that those skilled in
these matters have used toward people who want to inquire into something
intelligently.

First of all I shall set down by what criterion =10.1140= the soul is by
nature grasped; then, by what means it is shown to exist; next, whether it is
a substance or an accident; then, following on these, whether it is a body or
incorporeal; then, whether it is simple or composite; then again, whether it
is mortal or immortal; and last, whether it is rational or irrational. For
these are the questions that most of all tend to be asked in a discourse on
the soul, as the most proper and able to mark its distinctive character. For
proofs to confirm the matters we shall examine, we shall use the common
notions whose trustworthiness the matters at hand naturally confirm. For
brevity and usefulness, we shall for now use only those syllogisms that prove
what the inquiry requires, so that the thoughts, becoming clear and easy to
accept, may give us a certain readiness for answering those who resist. We
shall begin the discourse from here.

What is the criterion for apprehending the soul. 1. Everything that exists is
either known by sense or grasped by thought. What falls under sense has sense
itself as a sufficient proof, for at the same moment as the encounter it
produces in us the impression of the underlying thing. What is grasped by
thought is known not from itself but from its activities. The soul, then,
being unknown from itself, will reasonably be known from its effects.

Whether the soul exists. 2. Our body, when it moves, is moved either from
without or from within. That it is not moved from without is clear from the
fact that it is moved neither by being pushed nor by being pulled, as soulless
things are. And again, when it is moved from within, it is not moved
naturally, as fire is. For fire does not cease from moving so long as it is
fire, whereas the body, once it has become dead, is motionless while still
being a body. Therefore if it is moved neither from without, as soulless
things also are, nor naturally as fire is, it is clear that it is moved by a
soul, which also supplies it with life. If, then, the soul is shown to supply
life to our body, the soul will also be known in itself from its activities.

Whether the soul is a substance. 3. That the soul is a substance is shown as
follows. First, because the definition of substance may reasonably be said of
it as well. And that definition is this: substance is what is the same and one
in number, and capable of receiving contraries in turn. That this soul,
without departing from its own nature, receives contraries in turn is clear to
everyone, I suppose. For justice and injustice, courage and cowardice,
temperance and intemperance, are observed in it, being contraries. If, then,
it is a property of substance to be capable of receiving contraries in turn,
and the soul is shown to admit this definition, =10.1141= the soul is
therefore a substance. Next, since the body is a substance, the soul too must
be a substance. For it is not possible that what is given life should be a
substance, while what gives life should be without substance, unless someone
should claim that even the non-existent is the cause of the existent, or
again, unless a madman should say that what has its existence in another, and
cannot be without it, is the cause of that in which it is.

Whether the soul is incorporeal. 4. That the soul is in our body was shown
above. We must then know how it is in the body. If it lies beside it as pebble
beside pebble, the soul will be a body, and the whole body will not be
ensouled, for it will lie beside some part only. But if it is mixed in or
blended, the soul would become of many parts and not simple, stripped of what
is proper to a soul. For what is of many parts is also divisible and
dissoluble; what is dissoluble is composite; and what is composite is extended
in three dimensions. Body added to body makes bulk; but the soul, being in the
body, does not make bulk, but rather gives life. Therefore the soul will not
be a body, but incorporeal. Again, if the soul is a body, it is moved either
from without or from within. But it is not moved from without, for it is not
pushed or pulled, as soulless things are. Nor is it moved from within, as
ensouled things are, for it is absurd to speak of a soul of a soul. It will
not, then, be a body; therefore it is incorporeal. And again, if the soul is a
body, it also has perceptible qualities, and it is nourished. But it is not
nourished. And if it is nourished, it is not nourished bodily as the body is,
but incorporeally, for it is nourished by reason. Therefore it does not have
perceptible qualities. For neither justice nor courage nor any such thing is
seen; and these are the qualities of the soul. It is not, then, a body;
therefore it is incorporeal. Again, since all bodily substance is divided into
ensouled and soulless, let those who say the soul is a body say whether it is
to be called soulless or ensouled. Again, if every body has color, quantity,
and shape, and none of these is observed in the soul, the soul is therefore
not a body.

Whether the soul is simple or composite. 5. It is shown, then, that the soul
is simple, from the very things by which it was especially proved incorporeal.
For if it is not a body, and every body is composite, and what is put together
is put together from parts, then it would also be of many parts. But being
incorporeal, it is simple, since it is also incomposite and without parts.

Whether our soul is immortal. 6. I think immortality must follow upon
simplicity. =10.1144= And hear how. Nothing that exists is destructive of
itself, since it would not even have been put together from the beginning. For
things that perish perish from their contraries. Therefore everything that
perishes is dissoluble; what is dissoluble is composite; what is composite is
of many parts; and what is put together from parts is clearly put together
from different parts; and what is different is not the same. Therefore the
soul, being simple and not put together from different parts, being
incomposite and indissoluble, will for this reason be incorruptible and
immortal. Again, everything that is moved by something else, not having the
vital principle from itself but from the mover, lasts only so long as it is
held together by the power at work in it. When that which is at work ceases,
that which has its motion from it also fails. But the soul, being self-moved,
never ceases to be. For it follows upon the self-moved that it is always in
motion; what is
always in motion is unceasing; what is unceasing is endless; what is endless
is incorruptible; and what is incorruptible is immortal. If, then, the soul is
self-moved, as was proved above, it will be incorruptible and immortal
according to the syllogism already stated. Again, everything that is not
destroyed by its own evil is incorruptible. Evil is contrary to the good, and
is therefore destructive of it. For the evil of a body is nothing other than
passion, disease, and death, just as its virtue is beauty, life, health, and
good condition. If, then, the soul is not destroyed by its own evil, and the
evil of a soul is cowardice, intemperance, envy, and the like, and all these
do not take from it life and motion, it is therefore immortal.

Whether our soul is rational. 7. That our soul is rational, one could show
from many things. First, from the fact that it has discovered the arts that
are useful for life. For no one would say that the arts have been put together
simply and by chance, since he cannot show them idle and useless to life.
If, then, the arts contribute to what is useful for life, and the useful is
praiseworthy, and the praiseworthy is constructed by reason, and these are a
discovery of the soul, our soul is therefore rational. Next, our soul is shown
to be rational from the fact that our senses are not sufficient for the
apprehension of things. For we are not content, for knowledge of existing
things, with the mere encounter of sense. We do not even want to stop at them.
For things equal in shape and similar in color, yet distinct in their
natures, sensation, being irrational, is too weak to distinguish. If, then,
the senses, being irrational, produce in us a false impression of existing
things, =10.1145= we must consider whether existing things are truly
apprehensible or not. And if they are apprehensible, there is another power,
and a greater one besides the senses, that reaches them. And if they cannot be
grasped, we shall not even be able to grasp what appears otherwise than it is.
That things are apprehensible to us is clear from the fact that we use each
fittingly toward what is useful, and again fashion them into what we want.
Therefore if existing things have been shown to be apprehensible to us, and
the senses, being irrational, judge falsely, it will be mind that
distinguishes all things by reason and truly recognizes existing things. And
mind is the rational part of the soul; therefore the soul is rational. Again,
we do nothing without first sketching it out for ourselves. This is nothing
other than the dignity of the soul. For knowledge of existing things does not
come to it from without; rather it itself, as it were, arranges existing
things by its own conceptions. For this reason it first paints the thing
beforehand in itself, and then puts it into effect. And the dignity of the
soul is nothing other than doing all things with reason. For in this it also
differs from the senses. The soul has therefore been shown to be rational.
