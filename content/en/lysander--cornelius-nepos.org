* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Lysander the Lacedaemonian left a great fame of himself, gotten more by fortune
than by virtue: for it appears that he finished the Athenians waging war against
the Peloponnesians in the twenty-sixth year, nor is it hidden by what reason he
obtained it: for it was done not by virtue of his own army, but by the
immodesty of the adversaries, who, because they were not hearing the word of
their commanders, having wandered in the fields with the ships left, came into
the power of the enemies. By which deed the Athenians gave themselves to the
Lacedaemonians. Lifted by this victory Lysander, when before he had always been
factious and bold, so indulged himself that by his work the Lacedaemonians came
into the greatest hatred of Greece. For when the Lacedaemonians had kept saying
that this was their cause of war, that they might break the impotent domination
of the Athenians, after Lysander obtained the fleet of the enemies at the river
Aegos, he attempted nothing other than that he hold all the cities in his power,
while he pretended to do that for the sake of the Lacedaemonians. For from
everywhere, those who had been eager for the affairs of the Athenians having
been thrown out, he had chosen ten in each single city, to whom he would commit
the highest command and the power of all things. Into the number of these no one
was admitted, unless he was either held by his hospitality or had confirmed by
faith that he would be that man's own.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
Thus a ten-man power having been established in all cities all things were
managed by his own nod. Of whose cruelty and treachery it is enough to bring
forth one thing for the sake of example, lest by enumerating more of the same we
wear out the readers. When as victor he was returning from Asia and had turned
aside to Thasos, because that city had been of special faith toward the
Athenians, just as if the same were not accustomed to be the firmest friends who
had been constant enemies, he desired to overturn it. And he saw that unless he
had hidden his will in that, it would be that the Thasians would slip away and
consult for their own affairs  [omitted]

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
Therefore they took away the ten-man power established by him. Inflamed by which
grief he entered plans to take away the kings of the Lacedaemonians. But he felt
that he could not do that without the help of the gods, because the
Lacedaemonians were accustomed to refer all things to oracles. First he
attempted to corrupt the Delphic. When he had not been able to do that, he
attacked the Dodonaean. Driven back from here too he said he had taken up vows
which he would pay to Jupiter Hammon, thinking he would more easily corrupt the
Africans. With this hope when he had set out into Africa, the priests of Jupiter
greatly deceived him. For they not only could not be corrupted, but they also
sent ambassadors to Lacedaemon, who would accuse Lysander that he had attempted
to corrupt the priests of the shrine. Accused of this charge and acquitted by
the votes of judges, sent as aid to the Orchomenians he was killed by the
Thebans at Haliartus. How truly it would have been judged otherwise concerning
him, an oration was an indication, which after death was found in his house, in
which he persuades the Lacedaemonians that, the royal power having been
dissolved, a leader be chosen from all for waging war, but so written that he
seemed to agree with the sentence of a god, which he, trusting in money, had not
doubted he would have. Cleon of Halicarnassus is said to have written this for
him.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
And in this place the deed of Pharnabazus, a royal satrap, is not to be passed
over. For when Lysander as prefect of the fleet had done many things cruelly and
greedily in war and concerning these things suspected they had been brought to
his own citizens, he asked from Pharnabazus that he give him a testimony to the
ephors, with how great sanctity he had waged war and treated the allies, and
write accurately concerning that thing: for his authority in that thing would be
great. To this man he promises liberally: he wrote a large book with many words,
in which he lifts him with highest praises. Which when this man had read and
approved, while it is being sealed, he substituted another of equal size and of
such likeness that it could not be discerned, sealed, in which he had most
accurately accused his greed and treachery. Lysander, when he had returned home,
after he had said what he wished concerning his own things done before the
highest magistracy, handed over as testimony the book given by Pharnabazus. This
the ephors, Lysander having been removed, when they had known it, themselves
gave him to read. Thus he imprudent was himself his own accuser.
