* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

It had been my plan, revered Augustus, to send each emperor, one by one, to
Your Clemency in a book of his own, as many writers have done.

I had either seen this myself or gathered it from reading.

But it seemed wrong either to split Your Piety's attention with a crowd of
books or to spend my labor on many volumes. So in this book I have bound
the three Gordians together, looking after both my labor and your reading,
lest you be forced to unroll many volumes and still read almost a single
story. But let me, who have fled long books and a crowd of words, not seem to
have run into the very thing I like to think I politely avoid. I will get to
the matter at once.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

There were not two Gordians, as some unskilled writers say, but three. Anyone
who wanted to learn it could have done so from Arrianus, a writer of Greek
history, and likewise from Dexippus, a Greek author, who, even if briefly,
still followed everything with a care for truth. Of these, Gordian the elder,
that is the first, was born of Maecius Marullus as father and Ulpia Gordiana
as mother. He had his father's line from the stock of the Gracchi, his
mother's from the emperor Trajan. His father, grandfather, and
great-grandfather were consuls, and so were his father-in-law, his wife's
grandfather, and likewise another of his wife's grandfathers and two of her
great-grandfathers. He himself was a consul, very rich and very powerful,
owning at Rome the House of Pompey, and in the provinces holding as much land
as no private man.

After the consulship which he had held with Alexander, he was sent to the
proconsulship of Africa by a decree of the senate.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But before I speak of his rule, I will say a little of his character. When
Gordian, the man of whom we are speaking, was a young man, he wrote poems, all
of which still exist, and in fact all those subjects that Cicero treated, that
is, Marius and Aratus and the Halcyons and Uxorius and the Nile. He wrote
them, indeed, so that Cicero's poems would seem too old. Besides these, just
as Vergil wrote an Aeneid, Statius an Achilleid, and many others Alexandriads,
he too wrote an Antoniniad, that is, Antoninus Pius and Antoninus Marcus,
setting out in thirty books of most fluent verse their life, their wars, and
their deeds in public and in private. And this he did as a small boy. Later,
when he grew up, he declaimed controversies at the Athenaeum, even with his
emperors listening.

He held a most splendid quaestorship. In the time of his aedileship he gave
the Roman people twelve shows, that is one each month at his own cost, so that
he sometimes put on five hundred pairs of gladiators, never fewer than a
hundred and fifty. He showed a hundred Libyan beasts in one day, a thousand
bears in one day. There is still a memorable forest-hunt of his, painted in
the Beaked House of Gnaeus Pompey, which belonged to him and to his father and
great-grandfather, and which your privy purse seized in the time of Philip. In
that picture there are still contained two hundred palmate stags mixed with
British ones, thirty wild horses, a hundred wild sheep, ten elks, a hundred
Cyprian bulls, three hundred red-painted Moorish ostriches, thirty wild asses,
a hundred and fifty boars, two hundred ibexes, two hundred fallow deer. All of
this he granted to the people to seize on the day of the show, which was the
sixth he gave.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

He held a famous praetorship. After administering the law he entered his first
consulship with Antoninus Caracalla, his second with Alexander. He had two
children, that son of consular rank who was named Augustus with him and who
was consumed by war near Carthage in Africa, and a daughter, Maecia Faustina,
who was married to Junius Balbus, a man of consular rank. In his consulships
he was more brilliant than the consuls of his time, so that Antoninus envied
him, admiring now his bordered togas, now his broad stripe, now circus games
beyond an emperor's measure. He was the first Roman private man to have as his
own a palm-embroidered tunic and a pictured toga, whereas before even emperors
received them either from the Capitol or from the Palatine.

He divided a hundred Sicilian horses and a hundred Cappadocian ones among the
factions, with the emperors' leave, and by this he was dear enough to the
people, who are always moved by such things.

Cordus says that in all the cities of Campania, Etruria, Umbria, Flaminia, and
Picenum he gave stage plays and youth-games for four days at his own cost.

He also wrote prose praises of all the Antonines who had been before him. He
loved the Antonines so much that he attached to himself as well, as many say,
the name Antoninus, or, as most claim, Antonius.

It is well enough agreed that he marked his son, Gordian by name, with the
name of Antoninus, when, after the Roman custom, he acknowledged the son
before the prefect of the treasury and entered his name in the public records.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

After his consulship he was made proconsul of Africa, with the support of all
who wanted Alexander's rule to be held and to be famous in Africa as well
through the dignity of its proconsul. There is a letter of Alexander himself
in which he thanks the senate for having assigned Gordian to Africa as
proconsul. This is a copy of it:

/You could have done nothing more welcome to me, conscript fathers, or
sweeter, than to send Antoninus Gordian as proconsul to Africa, a man noble,
high-souled, eloquent, just, self-controlled,/

/good/

and the rest. From this it is clear how great a man Gordian was at that time.
He was loved by the Africans as no proconsul before him, so that some called
him Scipio, others Cato, and many Mucius and Rutilius or Laelius. There is
still their shout, which Junius put into writing. For when one day he was
reading an imperial act and had begun from the Scipios as proconsuls, they
shouted,

/To the new Scipio, the true Scipio, the proconsul Gordian./

He heard this and other such things often.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

He was of Roman height, with a handsome grayness and an imposing face,
ruddier than fair, with a good broad visage, and eyes, mouth, and brow that
commanded respect. In the quality of his body he was a little thick. In his
habits he was so measured that you could say nothing he had done greedily,
immodestly, or to excess. He loved his own uniquely, his son and grandson
beyond custom, his daughter and granddaughter with a kind of reverence.

He deferred so much to his father-in-law Annius Severus that he seemed to have
passed over into that man's family as a son. He never bathed with him, never
sat in his presence before the praetorship. When he was consul he either
always stayed in that man's house, or, if he was in the House of Pompey, he
went to him either early or late. He was sparing of wine, most sparing of
food, bright in dress, fond of bathing, so that in summer he bathed four and
five times a day, in winter twice. He slept a great deal, so that in dining
rooms, if he happened to eat at friends' houses, he would sleep even without
shame. This he seemed to do by nature, not by drunkenness and luxury.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

But good habits did him no good. For with this life, a man worthy of respect,
spending his time always with Plato, with Aristotle, with Cicero, with Vergil
and the other ancients, he suffered an end other than he deserved.

For in the time of Maximinus, a savage and brutal man, he was ruling Africa as
proconsul, with his son, already of consular rank, given him by the senate as
legate. And when a certain fiscal agent raged against a great many Africans
more sharply than Maximinus himself would allow, outlawing very many, killing
many, and claiming everything for himself beyond a procurator, and then,
checked by the proconsul and the legate, threatened ruin to those noble men of
consular rank themselves, the Africans could not bear such unheard-of
injuries. First they killed the fiscal agent himself, with a number of
soldiers joining them. Then, when he had been killed, and when the world was
already burning with hatred of Maximinus, they began to think how the rising
that had broken out between Maximinus's men and the country people, or the
Africans, might be quieted.

Then a certain man named Mauritius, a powerful town-councillor among the
Africans, near Thysdrus, in a most famous speech after this to the people of
the town or of the country, in

/Thanks to the immortal gods, citizens, that they have given us a chance, and

/cannot be safe unless we make an emperor. So, if it please you, since not far

shall make them firm by Roman law./

Then they shouted,

/It is fair, it is just. Gordian Augustus, may the gods keep you. May you rule
happily, may you rule with your son./

When this had been done they came in haste to the town of Thysdrus, and found
the venerable old man lying on a couch after administering the law. They
wrapped him in purple; he threw himself on the ground and, still refusing, was
lifted up. And since he could do nothing else, for the sake of escaping a
danger that hung over him doubtfully from Maximinus's men and necessarily from
his supporters, the old man suffered himself to be called emperor.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

He was already an octogenarian and, as we have said, had governed very many
provinces before. He was so recommended to the Roman people by his acts that
he seemed worthy of the whole empire. Of the fiscal agent who had been killed,
Gordian had known nothing beforehand. But when he learned the fact, already
near death and fearing more for his son, he preferred to have honorable
grounds for dying than to be handed over to Maximinus's chains and prison.

So, when Gordian had been named emperor, the young men who were the authors of
this deed threw down Maximinus's statues, smashed his images, and publicly
erased his name. They also named Gordian himself Africanus. Some add that the
surname Africa was given to Gordian not because he had begun to rule in
Africa, but because he drew his origin from the family of the Scipios. In very
many books I find that this Gordian and his son were named emperors together
and surnamed Antoninus; in others, however, Antonius.

After this they came to Carthage with royal pomp and laurelled fasces, and the
son, his father's legate, after the example of the Scipios, as Dexippus, a
writer of Greek history, is the authority, was girt with equal power. Then an
embassy was sent to Rome with letters of the Gordians reporting what had been
done in Africa, and it was received with joy by Valerian, the chief of the
senate, who later ruled. Letters were sent also to noble friends, so that
powerful men might both approve the deed and become still friendlier from
being friends.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

But the senate received those who had been made emperors against Maximinus
with such congratulation that they not only approved these deeds but even
chose twenty men, among whom were Maximus, also called Pupienus, and Clodius
Balbinus. Both of these were made emperors after the two Gordians had been
killed in Africa. The senate had created those twenty, of course, in order to
divide among them the Italian regions to be guarded against Maximinus for the
Gordians. Then embassies from Maximinus came to Rome, pledging a wiping out of
the past. But the embassy of the Gordians won, promising every good thing, so
that the same embassy was believed when it promised a huge bounty to the
soldiers and lands and largesses to the people.

So much more was trust given to the Gordians than to the Maximini that a
certain Vitalianus, who commanded the praetorian soldiers, was killed by a
most daring quaestor and soldiers at the senate's order, because he had
earlier acted cruelly, and then his savagery was feared still more, a thing
friendly and familiar to Maximinus's habits. Of his death this little story is
told. Letters of Maximinus were forged, sealed as if with his own ring, and
soldiers were sent with a quaestor to carry them, adding that certain things
besides the letter were to be said in secret.

So they made for a long portico, and when he was asking after what was to be
told him in secret, they urged him first to look at the seal of the letter.
While he was studying it, he was killed. Then the soldiers were persuaded that
Vitalianus had been killed by Maximinus's order. When the business had been
finished, both the letters and the faces of the Gordians were posted in the
Camp.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

It matters that the senate's decree by which the Gordians were named emperors
and Maximinus an enemy should be spread in writing. On a day of the senate not
lawful but called, the consul, already met at home with the praetors, aediles,
and tribunes of the people, came into the Curia. The prefect of the city, who
had caught some scent of something and who had not received the public
letters, kept away from the meeting. But it did him good, for the consul,
before the usual shouts, before anything lucky was said about Maximinus, said:

/Conscript fathers, the two Gordians, father and son, both of consular rank,
one your proconsul, the other your legate, have been named emperors by a great
assembly of the Africans. Let us therefore thank the youth of Thysdrus, and
thank the people of Carthage, always loyal in their vow. They have freed us
from a monstrous beast, from that wild animal. Why do you hear me timidly? Why
do you look around? Why do you hang back? This is what you have always hoped
for. Maximinus is an enemy. The gods will bring it about that he now cease to
be, and that we gladly try the good fortune and prudence of Gordian the old
man, the courage and constancy of the young one./

After this he read the letters of the Gordians sent to the senate and to
himself. Then the senate shouted:

/Gods, thanks to you, we have been freed from enemies. So may we be wholly
freed. We all judge Maximinus an enemy. We devote Maximinus with his son to
the gods below. We name the Gordians Augusti. We recognize the Gordians as
princes. May the gods keep the emperors of/

/the senate. May we see our noble emperors victorious. May Rome see our
emperors. Whoever kills the public enemies earns a reward./

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

Junius Cordus says there was a secret decree of the senate. What that is, or
why it is so called, I will briefly set out. In general, an instance of a
secret senate decree today is nothing other than that by which Your Clemency,
when the elders have been called to inner rooms, arranges those things that
are not to be published to all. About these you are even accustomed to take
oath that no one, before the business is finished, shall hear or understand
anything. Among the ancients, public needs found this custom, so that if some
force from enemies happened to threaten, forcing them either to take low
counsels or to settle something that ought not to be spoken before it was
done, or if they did not want some things to leak through to friends, a secret
senate decree was made, so that neither clerks nor public slaves nor census
officers were present at those acts. Senators took them down, senators filled
all the duties of the census officers and clerks, lest anything by chance be
betrayed. A secret senate decree was therefore made, lest the matter reach
Maximinus.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

But at once, as men's minds are, at least those who blush if what they know is
not recognized as coming from them, and who think themselves low if they do
not betray what has been entrusted, Maximinus learned everything, so that he
received a copy of the secret senate decree, which had never been done before.
There is, finally, a letter of his to the prefect of the city of this kind:

/I have read the secret senate decree of those princes of ours, which you,
prefect of the city, perhaps do not know was made, for you were not present. I
have sent you a copy of it, so that you may know how you are ruling the Roman
commonwealth./

It cannot be told what a storm Maximinus was in when he heard that Africa had
defected against him. For when he grasped the senate's authority he ran at the
walls, tore his clothes, snatched a sword as if he could kill them all, and
seemed to go wholly mad.

The prefect of the city, when he had received harsher letters, addressed the
people and the soldiers, saying Maximinus had already been killed. From this
the joy was greater, and at once the statues and images of the man who had
been judged an enemy were thrown down. The senate, with the war hanging, used
the power it ought to have used, for it ordered informers, false accusers,
procurators, and all the dregs of Maximinus's tyranny to be killed. And it
was not enough that the senate had judged. This was the people's judgment,
that those killed were broken and thrown into the sewer. Then also Sabinus,
the prefect of the city, a man of consular rank, was struck with a club,
killed, and left lying in public.

When Maximinus learned this, he at once exhorted the soldiers with a speech of
this kind:

/Consecrated fellow-soldiers, or rather my fellow-consecrated, and of whom/

faecem Eyssenhardt, Peter; facem P.

/most truly campaign with me, while we defend Roman majesty from Germany,
while we reclaim Illyricum from the barbarians, the Africans have shown Punic
faith. For they have made us two Gordians emperors, one of whom is so broken
by old age that he cannot rise, the other so ruined by luxury that he has
weakness in place of old age. And lest this be too little, that noble senate
has recognized the Africans' deed, and those for whose children we carry arms
have set twenty men against us and have passed all their votes as if against
enemies.

Come then, as becomes men, we must hurry to the city, for twenty men of
consular rank have been chosen against us, who must be resisted, we acting
bravely, you fighting with good fortune./

By this speech Maximinus himself also learned that the soldiers' minds were
slow and their spirits not eager. So he at once wrote to his son, who was
following far behind, to hurry, lest the soldiers think of something against
him in his absence. Junius Cordus published a copy of the letter of this kind:

/My attendant Tynchanius reports to you what I have learned was done, whether
in Africa or at Rome. He reports what the soldiers' minds are. I ask that you
hurry as much as you can, lest the military crowd, as it usually does, do
something more. What I fear, you will hear from the man I have sent to you./

* Chapter 15
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h15
:END:

While this was going on, in Africa against the two Gordians a certain
Capelianus, always hostile to Gordian even in private life, and already
dismissed as a veteran by the emperor himself when he was ruling the Moors by
Maximinus's order, gathered the Moors and, with a hasty force, after receiving
a successor from Gordian, made for Carthage, to whom the whole people of the
Carthaginians, with Punic faith, inclined. Gordian, however, wanting to try
the fortune of war, sent against Capelianus and Maximinus's men his son,
already older in years, forty-six years old, whom he then had, as we have
said, in the place of a legate, a man of whose character we will speak in its
place. But since in military matters Capelianus was the bolder and the younger
Gordian was less practiced, slowed as he was by the delights of nobility, when
battle was joined he was beaten and killed in the same war.

* Chapter 16
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16
:END:

Such a multitude of Gordian's party is said to have fallen in the war that,
though the body of the younger Gordian was long sought, it could not be found.
There was besides a huge storm, which is rare in Africa, that so scattered
Gordian's army before the war that the soldiers were made less fit for the
fight, and so Capelianus's victory was easy.

When the elder Gordian learned this, since there was no help in Africa, and
much fear from Maximinus and Punic faith pressed him, and Capelianus was
pressing most sharply, and then grief wore out his mind and spirit, he ended
his life with a noose.

This was the end of the two Gordians, both of whom the senate named Augusti
and afterward placed among the gods.

* Chapter 17
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17
:END:

This was the son of Gordian the old man, proconsul of Africa, who with his
father was named Augustus both by the Africans and by the senate. He was
famous in letters and in character besides his nobility, which some derive
from the Antonines, most from the Antonii. Others, indeed, want these things
as proof for testing the quality of the family: that the elder Gordian was
called Africanus, the surname of the Scipios; that he had the House of Pompey
in the city; that he was always named with the surname of the Antonines; that
he himself wished his son to be indicated as Antonius in the senate. Each of
these seems to mark families.

But I follow Junius Cordus, who says that the nobility of the Gordians grew
together from all these families. This same man, then, was born first to his
father from Fabia Orestilla, great-granddaughter of Antoninus, from whom he
seemed to touch the family of the Caesars as well, and in the first days of
his birth he was called Antoninus. Soon the name of the Antonii was published
in the senate. Then among the people he began to be held as Gordian.

* Chapter 18
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18
:END:

In his studies he had a most serious reputation. He was striking in looks, of
singular memory, of notable kindness, so much so that always in the schools,
if any of the boys was beaten, he could not hold his tears. He was overly
welcome and dear to Serenus Sammonicus, who was a great friend of his father's
and his own teacher, so much so that when that man died he left to the younger
Gordian all the books of his father Serenus Sammonicus, which were counted at
sixty-two thousand. That raised him to the sky, since, gifted with the store
and splendor of so great a library, he came into fame among men by the honor
of letters.

He won the quaestorship on Elagabalus's recommendation, because the young
man's playfulness, yet not luxurious or infamous, had been praised to that
luxurious emperor. He held the city praetorship on Alexander's recommendation,
in which he was so great by the grace of his administering the law that he at
once earned the consulship which his father had received late. In the time of
Maximinus or of this same Alexander he was sent as legate to his father's
proconsulship and obeyed, and there those things that have been told above
happened to him.

* Chapter 19
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19
:END:

He was rather fond of wine, yet always of wine flavored somehow, now
with rose, now with mastic, now with wormwood and the other things by which
the palate is most pleased. He was sparing of food, so that in an instant
he would finish either lunch, if he lunched, or dinner. He was most eager
for women. He is said to have had twenty-two concubines assigned to him, from
all of whom he left three and four sons each, and he was called the Priam of
his time, whom people, joking, because he was more inclined by nature, often
named Priapus, not Priam.

He lived in pleasures, in gardens, in baths, in the most pleasant groves, and
his father did not scorn it, saying very often that the young man would
sometime soon die in the highest fame. Yet he never fell away from good men by
the courage of his life, and he was always among the most distinguished
citizens and never failed the commonwealth when counsel was needed.

Finally the senate also named him Augustus most gladly and set the public hope
on him. He was most elegant in dress, dear to his slaves and to all his
household. Cordus says he never wanted to have a wife. Dexippus, against this,
thinks the third Gordian is his son, the boy who after this, with Balbinus and
Pupienus or Maximus, obtained the empire.

* Chapter 20
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20
:END:

When the elder Gordian once consulted an astrologer about this man's nativity,
the man is said to have answered that he would be both the son of an emperor
and the father of one, and emperor himself. And when the elder Gordian
laughed, they say the astrologer pointed out the grouping of the stars and
dictated from old books, so as to prove that he had spoken true. He also
predicted for the old man and the young one, with stubborn constancy from the
truth, both the day and the kind of death and the places where they would
perish. All of this the elder Gordian is said to have told later in Africa,
already emperor and when he feared nothing, and even to have spoken of his own
death and his son's and of the kind of death.

Besides, the old man, when he had seen Gordian his son, used to sing these
verses very often:

The fates will have shown this man to the earth only, and will not let him be
beyond that. Rome's line seemed too strong to you, gods above, if these gifts
had been its own.

There are still sayings of the younger Gordian both in prose and in verse,
which today are often used by his kin, not great, not least, but middle, and
such as show a man of talent, but one living in luxury and abandoning his own
gift.

* Chapter 21
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21
:END:

He was most greedy for fruit and greens, so that, most sparing in the rest of
food, he was always devouring some fresh fruit. He was very eager for cold
drinks, and in summer he did not easily drink anything but cold ones, and as
many as possible. He was of huge body, and so he was driven more toward cold
drinks.

This is what we have found about the younger Gordian worth remembering. For we
should not tell such things as Junius Cordus composed ridiculously and
stupidly about domestic pleasures and other lowest matters. Anyone who wants
to know them should read Cordus himself, who says what slaves each prince had,
and what friends, and how many cloaks or how many military capes. Knowledge of
these things helps no one, since historians ought to put in history what
should be avoided or followed.

What I have decided should not be left out, because it seemed marvelous, I
have put into writing after reading it in Vulcatius Terentianus, who also
wrote a history of his own time: that the elder Gordian so reproduced the face
of Augustus that he seemed to show the man's voice and manner and stature as
well; that the son seemed most like Pompey, although it is denied that Pompey
was of a fat body; and that the grandson, whose images we still see even now,
recalled the face of Scipio Asiaticus. This, for the wonder of it, I did not
think should be passed over in silence.

GORDIAN THE THIRD

* Chapter 22
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h22
:END:

After the death of the two Gordians the senate, frightened and fearing
Maximinus more violently, named as Augusti, from the twenty men it had chosen
to protect the commonwealth, Pupienus or Maximus and Clodius Balbinus, both of
consular rank. Then the people and the soldiers asked that little Gordian be
named Caesar, being, as most claim, eleven years old, as some, thirteen, as
Junius Cordus says, sixteen (for he claims he died in his twenty-second year).
He was seized and taken to the senate and from there set in an assembly,
covered with the imperial garment, and named Caesar.

He was born, as more claim, from Gordian's daughter, as one or two (for I
could not find more) from the son who perished in Africa. Gordian, of course,
once made Caesar, was brought up at his mother's house, and when, after the
Maximini had been destroyed, Maximus also and Balbinus had been killed in a
military sedition, they who had ruled for two years, the young Gordian, who
until then had been Caesar, was named Augustus by the soldiers and the people
and the senate and by all nations, with huge love, huge zeal, and favor. He
was loved by the merit of his grandfather and of his uncle or father, both of
whom took up arms for the senate and the Roman people against Maximinus and
perished either by a soldier's death or by military necessity.

After this veterans came to the Curia to learn what had been done. Two of
them, having entered the Capitol, since the senate was sitting there, were
killed before the altar itself by Gallicanus, of consular rank, and Maecenas,
of the generals. And an internal war arose, since even the senators were
armed, the veterans not knowing that the young Gordian alone held the empire.

Dexippus, indeed, asserts that the third Gordian was born from Gordian's son.
But after it was established among the veterans as well that Gordian alone
ruled, peace was strengthened between the people and the soldiers and
veterans, and this was the end of the internal war, when the consulship had
been conferred on the boy Gordian. But this was a sign that Gordian would not
rule long, that there was an eclipse of the sun, so that it was thought night,
and nothing could be done without lights being lit. After this, however, the
Roman people gave themselves to pleasures and delights, so as to soften what
had been done harshly.

In the consulship of Venustus and Sabinus a faction was begun in Africa
against the third Gordian, with Sabinianus as leader, whom Gordian, through
the governor of Mauretania, so crushed after the conspirators had beset him
that all came to Carthage to hand him over, both confessing their crimes and
asking pardon for their wickedness. So, when the trouble in Africa had been
ended, in the consulship of Gordian, now for the second time, and Pompeianus,
a Persian war arose. Then also the young Gordian, before he set out to the
war, married the daughter of Misitheus, a most learned man, whom, on account
of eloquence, he thought worthy of his kinship and at once made prefect. After
that the empire no longer seemed boyish and contemptible, since he was helped
by the counsels of an excellent father-in-law, and he himself on his side had
some sense, and was not sold through eunuchs and palace servants by his
mother's ignorance or connivance.

There was an earthquake so heavy in Gordian's reign that even cities perished
with their peoples in an opening of the earth. For these, huge sacrifices were
celebrated through the whole city and the whole world. And Cordus, indeed,
says that when the Sibylline books had been inspected and everything that
seemed ordered there had been celebrated, the world-wide evil was quieted.

When the earthquake had been quieted, in the consulship of Praetextatus and
Atticus, Gordian, having opened the twin gates of Janus, which was the sign of
a war declared, set out against the Persians with a huge army and with so much
gold that he might easily overcome the Persians either with auxiliaries or
with soldiers. He made his march into Moesia and, even while under arms,
destroyed, routed, drove out, and pushed back whatever of the enemy was in the
Thracian lands. From there he came through Syria to Antioch, which was already
held by the Persians. There he fought in frequent battles and won, Sapor, king
of the Persians, being pushed back. And afterward he recovered Artaxanses and
Antioch and Carrhae and Nisibis, which

But that good fortune could not be longer. For Misitheus, as most say, by the
arts of Philip, who was made prefect of the guard after him, or as others say,
was extinguished by disease, with the Roman commonwealth as heir, so that
whatever had been his was added to the city's revenues. So great was this
man's arrangement in the commonwealth that there was never a frontier city of
any strength, and one that could bear an army of the Roman people and a
prince, that did not have stores of vinegar, grain, bacon, barley, and straw
for a whole year; the lesser cities, some of thirty days, some of forty, not a
few of two months, those with the least, of fifteen days. The same man, when
he was prefect, always inspected the soldiers' arms, suffered no old man to
serve, suffered no boy to receive rations, went around all the camps and their
ditches, and even for the most part attended the watches at night. He was
loved by all, because he so loved both the commonwealth and the prince. The
tribunes and generals so feared and loved him that they neither wanted to do
wrong nor in any part did wrong.

Philip is said to have feared him violently for many reasons and on that
account to have prepared traps for his life through the doctors, and in this
way: when Misitheus was suffering from a flux of the bowels and was ordered by
the doctors to take a cup for stopping the belly, they say that what had been
prepared was changed and that was given by which he was loosened more. And so
he was drained of life.

When he was dead, in the consulship of Arrianus and Papus, Philip the Arab was
made prefect of the guard in his place, born of a low stock but proud, who did
not hold himself in the newness and hugeness of his fortune, so that at once
he laid traps through the soldiers against Gordian, who had taken him into a
father's place. They were of this kind.

Misitheus had had stores everywhere, as much as we have said, so that the
Roman arrangement could not waver. But by Philip's arts first the grain ships
were turned aside, then the soldiers were led into those places where they
could not be provisioned. By this he at once made the soldiers hostile to
Gordian, they not understanding that the young man had been deceived by
Philip's arts. But Philip added this as well, that he spread a rumor through
the soldiers that Gordian was a youth, that he could not steer the empire,
that it was better that the man who could govern the soldiery, who knew the
commonwealth, should rule. He corrupted the leading men besides, and it was
brought about that Philip was openly demanded for the empire. Gordian's
friends at first resisted most violently, but when the soldiers were overcome
by hunger, the empire was entrusted to Philip, and it was ordered by the
soldiers that Philip, as if his guardian, should rule together with the same
Gordian.

So, when the empire had been taken up, since Philip also bore himself most
proudly against Gordian, and the other recognized himself as emperor and the
offspring of emperors and a man of a most noble family and could not bear the
shamelessness of a man of no birth, he complained on the tribunal among the
generals and soldiers, with the prefect Maecius Gordianus, his kinsman,
standing by, hoping that the empire could be taken from Philip. But by this
complaint he did nothing, when he had charged him with being unmindful of his
benefits and showing himself too little grateful. And when he had asked the
soldiers, when he had openly canvassed the generals, by Philip's faction he
was lesser in everyone's eyes. Finally, when he saw that he was held the
lesser, he asked that at least the empire between them should be equal, and he
did not obtain it. Then he asked to be held in the place of Caesar, and he did
not get that either. He asked even to be in the place of prefect to Philip,
which also was denied. His last prayers were that Philip should hold him as a
general and suffer him to live, to which indeed Philip had almost agreed,
himself silent but doing everything through friends by nods and counsels.

But when he thought with himself of the love of the Roman people and senate
for Gordian, and of all Africa and Syria and the whole Roman world, since he
was noble and the grandson and son of emperors and had freed the whole
commonwealth from heavy wars, that it could happen that, if the soldiers' will
ever turned, the empire would be given back to Gordian if he asked for it, and
since the soldiers' anger at Gordian was fierce because of hunger, he ordered
him, still crying out, to be led out of sight and stripped and killed. When
this had first been delayed, afterward it was fulfilled as he ordered. So
Philip obtained the empire impiously, not by right.

Gordian ruled six years. And while this was being done, Argunt, king of the
Scythians, was laying waste the kingdoms of his neighbors, most of all because
he had learned that Misitheus had perished, by whose counsel the commonwealth
had been governed.

Philip, however, lest he seem to have gotten the empire from cruelty, sent
letters to Rome in which he wrote that Gordian had perished by disease and
that he had been chosen by all the soldiers. And, as could not fail, the
senate was deceived about these things, which it had not known. So, when
Philip had been named prince and called Augustus, he placed the young Gordian
among the gods.

He was a glad young man, handsome, lovable, welcome to all, pleasant in life,
noble in letters, so that nothing but his age was wanting to the empire. He
was loved by the people and senate and soldiers before Philip's faction as no
prince was. Cordus says all the soldiers called him son, that he was called
son by the whole senate, that the whole people said Gordian was their darling.

Finally Philip, when he had killed him, neither took away his images nor put
down his statues nor scraped off his name, but always calling him divine, even
among those same soldiers with whom he had made the faction, he honored him
with a serious spirit and an alien's cunning.

The house of the Gordians still exists, which this Gordian adorned most
beautifully. There is a villa of theirs on the Praenestine Way having two
hundred columns in a four-sided court, of which fifty are Carystian, fifty
Claudian, fifty Synnadic, fifty Numidian, of equal measure, in which there are
three basilicas a hundred feet long, the rest fitting this work, and baths
such as, aside from the city as it then was, existed nowhere in the world.

For the family of Gordian the senate decreed this, that his descendants should
always be free from guardianships and from embassies and from public
necessities, unless they wished.

No works of Gordian exist at Rome except certain fountain-houses and baths.
But the baths were for private men and were fitted by him for private use. He
had begun a portico on the Campus Martius under the hill, a thousand feet
long, so that on the other side a portico of a thousand feet likewise should
be made, and between them a space of five hundred feet should lie open
equally, of which space there should be pleasure-gardens on this side and
that, thick with laurel, myrtle, and box, and in the middle a mosaic pavement
with short columns set on either side and little statues through a thousand
feet, which should be a walk, so that at the head there should be a basilica
of five hundred feet. He had thought besides, with Misitheus, to make summer
baths of his own name behind the basilica, so as to put winter ones at the
beginning of the porticoes, lest either the pleasure-gardens or the porticoes
be without use.

But all this is now taken up by the possessions and gardens and buildings of
private men.

That public vow came to nothing, for Philip showed all this at the Secular

What has been handed down in memory of Gaius Caesar, this also Cordus writes
happened in the case of Gordian. For all who struck at him with a sword (who
are said to have been nine), afterward, when the Philips had been killed, are
said to have killed themselves with their own hand and their own swords, and
with the same ones with which they had struck him.
