* On the Decrees of the Nicene Synod

Of Saint Athanasius: that the synod at Nicaea, having seen the craft of those
around Eusebius, set forth fittingly and piously the things defined against
the Arian heresy.
1.1 You did well making known to me the inquiry that took place from you
toward those who champion the things of Arius, among whom there were also some
companions of Eusebius and very many brothers who think the things of the
church. I approved then your Christ-loving sobriety as having well refuted the
impiety of the heresy, and I have marveled at their shamelessness, that
although the Arian reasonings have been shown rotten and vain, and they
themselves have been condemned by all for every ill-mindedness, still even
after these things they murmured like the Jews, saying: why did those who
assembled at Nicaea write 1.2 unwritten phrases, "from the essence" and
"consubstantial"? You then as a learned man showed them, even when they were
posing in this way, to be talking emptiness no less. But they do nothing
foreign to their own ill-will, fabricating pretexts. For they are various and
changeable in their judgments as chameleons in their colors, and when refuted
they blush, and when examined they are at a loss, and then being shameless
they make excuses. And if someone refutes them in these things too, they toil
until they invent things that are not and 1.3 according to what is written
they reason vain things, so that they may only remain being impious. And such
an attempt is nothing other than a mark outright of their irrationality, and
an imitation, as I said before, of Jewish malice. For those too, being refuted
by the truth and not able to look it in the face, made excuses saying: "What
sign do you do, that we may see and believe you? What do you work?" although
so many signs had happened that even they said: "What do we do, because this
man does many signs?" For the dead were raised, the lame walked, the blind saw
again, lepers were cleansed, and the water became wine, and from five loaves
five thousand were filled. 1.4 And all marveled and worshiped the Lord,
confessing that in him the prophecies were fulfilled and that he is God of God
the Son; but the Pharisees alone, even if the signs were appearing brighter
than the sun, still again murmured as unlearned and said: "Why do you, being a
man, make yourself God?" Foolish and truly blind in 1.5 mind. They ought to
say the reverse: why, being God, have you become man? For the works showed him
God, so that they might worship the goodness of the Father and marvel at his
economy for us. This they neither said, nor did they wish to see the things
that were happening. Or they did see (for it was necessary to see them) but
again they murmured, changing: why do you heal the paralytic on the sabbath,
1.6 and then make the man born blind see again? And this again was a pretext
and only murmuring. For even on the other days, when the Lord was healing
every disease and every infirmity, they blamed as usual and preferred to have
a suspicion of godlessness, 1.7 naming Beelzebul, rather than deny their own
wickedness. But even so, when the Savior was showing his own divinity in many
parts and many ways and evangelizing the Father to all, they no less, as
kicking against goads, spoke against, talking folly, so that only, according
to the divine proverb, inventing pretexts they might separate themselves from
the truth.

2.1 The Jews then of that time, doing such wicked things and denying the Lord,
have reasonably come to be outside both the laws and the promise toward the
fathers. And the Arians now judaizing seem to me to suffer the same as
Caiaphas and the Pharisees of that time; for seeing that their own heresy has
nothing reasonable they invent pretexts, why this and 2.2 not that has been
written. And do not wonder if they now contrive such things. For after a
little they will also turn aside into insults and after these things they will
threaten the cohort and the chiliarch; for in these things is their seeming
constitution of ill-doctrine. For denying the 2.3 Word of God they are
reasonably also empty of every word. Knowing these things, then, I would have
answered nothing to them when they ask. But since your affection demanded to
know the things done in the synod, I did not delay; but at once, as it then
happened, I have made it known, showing through a few things how far the Arian
heresy is stripped of pious thinking and they only 2.4 fabricate pretexts. And
consider you also, beloved, whether it is not so. If they have confidence in
the evils they themselves invented, the devil having sown ill-mindedness in
them, let them defend the things for which, being accused, they were shown to
be heretics, and then at last let them blame, if they can, the things defined
against them. For no one convicted of murder and adultery has room after the
judgment to blame the sentence of the one who judged, why he did not speak
thus but in that way. For this does not free the one judged, but even more
increases his charge through 2.5 rashness and daring. Therefore these too
either let them show their own mind pious (for they were accused and
convicted, they did not first find fault; and it is just that those charged do
nothing other than defend themselves) or if they have a filthy conscience and
see themselves being impious, let them not blame things they do not know, 2.6
so that they may not also carry away a double charge of impiety and a blame of
lack of education. Let them rather examine as lovers of learning, so that
recognizing what they formerly did not know they may wash their own impious
ears with the stream of truth and the doctrines of piety. For so it also
happened in the synod at Nicaea to those around Eusebius.
3.1 As they were contentious in being impious and attempted to fight God, the
things said by them were full of impiety, but the bishops who assembled, they
were more or less three hundred, mildly and humanely demanded of them to give
an account and pious proofs concerning what they said. And as even only
speaking they were condemned and fought among themselves, seeing much the
poverty of their own heresy, these remained speechless 3.2 and through the
silence they confessed the shame at their empty glory. The bishops then,
taking away the phrases invented by them, thus set forth against them the
sound and ecclesiastical faith; and when all subscribed, those around Eusebius
also subscribed to these phrases which these now blame, I mean "from the
essence" and "consubstantial," and that neither a creature nor a thing made
nor of the originated 3.3 is the Son of God, but an offspring from the essence
of the Father is the Word. And the paradox at least, Eusebius from Caesarea of
Palestine, although the day before denying, still later having subscribed
wrote to his own church, saying that this is the faith of the church and the
tradition of the fathers, and he showed plainly to all that formerly 3.4 they
were erring and in vain were contentious against the truth. For even if he was
then ashamed to write with these phrases and as he himself wished he
apologized to the church, yet through the letter, not denying the
consubstantial and "from the essence," he plainly wishes to signify this. And
he has suffered something terrible; for as if defending himself he then
accused the Arians, that writing "the Son was not before he was begotten" they
did not wish 3.5 him to be even before the generation according to the flesh.

And this Acacius also knows, if he himself too, fearing now because of the
time, does not play a part and deny the truth. I have therefore subjoined at
the end the letter of Eusebius, so that from this you may know both of the
Christ-fighters and besides the ingratitude of Acacius toward their own
teachers.
4.1 How then are these not unjust even only thinking to speak against so great
and ecumenical a synod? How do they not act unlawfully, daring to look in the
face the things well defined against the Arian heresy and witnessed by those
who formerly taught them 4.2 to be impious? And if even after subscribing
those around Eusebius changed and like dogs returned to their own vomit of
impiety, how are those who now speak against not even more worthy of hatred,
because pouring out the freedom of their own soul to others they wish to have
these also as leaders of the heresy, men, as James said, "double-minded and
unstable in all their ways," and not having one judgment, but changing now one
way now another, and now praising what they say, after a little blaming what
they have said, and again praising what a little before they slandered. 4.3
And this, as the Shepherd has said, is an offspring of the devil and a mark
rather of hucksters and not of teachers. For there is, as the fathers have
handed down, truly teaching and this proof of true teachers, to confess the
same things to one another and not to dispute either toward themselves or
toward their own fathers. For those who are not disposed 4.4 in this way would
rather be called worthless and not true teachers. The Greeks then, not
confessing the same things but even disputing toward one another, do not have
true teaching; but the holy and truly heralds of the truth both agree with one
another and do not differ toward themselves. For even if they have come to be
in different times, yet they rush to the same with one another, being prophets
of one God and evangelizing the same Word in harmony.
5.1 What things then Moses taught, these Abraham kept; and what Abraham kept,
these Noah and Enoch knew, distinguishing clean and unclean and becoming
well-pleasing to God. For Abel also thus bore witness, knowing these things
which he had learned from Adam, who himself also learned from the Lord, who
also having come at the completion of the ages for the putting away of sin
said: "I do not give you a new commandment 5.2 but an old commandment, which
you heard from the beginning." For this reason also the blessed apostle Paul
who learned from him, arranging the ecclesiastical things, did not even wish
deacons to be double-tongued, much less bishops, and rebuking the Galatians he
declared wholly saying: "If anyone evangelizes you contrary to what you
received, let him be anathema, as I have said before, and again I say: but
even if we or an angel from heaven should evangelize 5.3 you contrary to what
you received, let him be anathema." The apostle then saying these things,
either let these also make those around Eusebius, who change and say other
things contrary to what they subscribed, anathema, or if they know that they
subscribed well, let them not murmur against so great a synod. And if they do
neither this nor that, they are plain even themselves as being carried about
by every wind and wave and being dragged not by their own but by others' 5.4
judgments. And being such they are not even now, putting forward such
pretexts, trustworthy, but let them also cease blaming things they do not
know; and perhaps not even knowing how to distinguish they simply "call the
evil good and the good evil, and think the bitter 5.5 to be sweet and the
sweet bitter." Indeed they wish the things judged evil and rejected to
prevail, and they force themselves to slander the things well defined. It was
necessary then that we no longer make any defense of these things and answer
their vain pretexts, nor that they still be contentious, but that they agree

with what the leaders of their heresy subscribed, knowing that the later
change of those around Eusebius is suspect 5.6 and malicious. But the
subscribing after they were able to defend themselves even the least shows
that the Arian heresy is truly impious. For they did not subscribe before they
condemned it; and they did not condemn it before they were at a loss from
every side and were ashamed; so that the changing is a proof of the
contentiousness toward 5.7 impiety. Wherefore it was necessary, as has been
said, that these too be still. But since they are terrible toward
shamelessness, and they perhaps think themselves more able than those to stand
at the head of this diabolic impiety, even if I already through the former
letter written to you used a broader refutation against them, yet come even
now let us examine these as those, according to each of the things said by
them. For it will be shown no less even now that their heresy is not sound but
some demonic thing.
6.1 They say then, as it also seemed to those, and they have dared to say:
"The Father was not always, the Son was not always; for the Son was not before
he was begotten, but he too has come to be from things that are not; wherefore
God has not always been Father of the Son, but when the Son came to be and was
created, then God also was called his Father. For the Word is a creature and a
thing made and is foreign and unlike according to essence to the Father; and
the Son is neither by nature and true Word of the Father nor is he his only
and true Wisdom, but being a creature and one of the things made he is called
Word and Wisdom by misuse. For by the Word who truly is in God he too 6.2 came
to be, as also all things; wherefore the Son is not even true God." But I wish
first to ask these too this: what at all is a son, and of what is this name
significant, so that thus they themselves may be able even so to understand
6.3 the things said by them. For the divine Scripture signifies to us a double
sense from this name, one concerning those of whom Moses says in the law: "If
you listen to the voice of the Lord your God, to keep all his commandments, as
many as I command you today, to do the good and pleasing before the Lord your
God, you are sons of the Lord our God," as also in the gospel John says: "But
as many as received him, he gave them authority to become children of God";
and another sense, according to which Isaac is son 6.4 of Abraham and Jacob of
Isaac and the patriarchs of Jacob. And they, according to which of these then
having supposed the Son of God to be, mythologize such things about him? For I
know well that they will run together into the same impiety with those around
Eusebius. If then according to the first, of the sort who by improvement of
manners acquire the grace of the name and receive authority so that they may
become sons of God (for this those also said), he would seem to differ in
nothing from us, nor would he be only-begotten, having himself too from virtue
6.5 the appellation of son. For even if, as they say, being foreknown that he
would be such he anticipates and together with coming to be he receives both
the name and the glory of the name, still he will differ in nothing from those
who after the deeds receive the name, as long as he too is thus 6.6 witnessed
to be a son. For Adam also, anticipating the grace and together with coming to
be being placed in paradise, differed in nothing either from Enoch, who after
a time from generation, having been well-pleasing, was translated, or from the
apostle, himself too after the deeds being snatched into paradise, nor even
from the once robber, who through the confession received a promise that he
would be at once in paradise.
7.1 But being chased in these things they will perhaps say what they were
often ashamed saying: "Thus we think the Son has more besides the others and
for this reason is called only-begotten, because he alone came to be from God
alone, but all the other things were created from God through the Son." Who
then suggested to you this foolish and new invention, so that you may say:
"The Father alone

wrought the Son alone with his own hand, but all the other things have come to
be through the Son 7.2 as through an underworker"? For if because of growing
weary God was content to work with his own hand the Son only, but the other
things no longer, it is impious to reason such things about God, especially
for those who have heard Isaiah saying: "Eternal God, God who constructed the
ends of the earth, he will not hunger nor toil, nor is there a finding out of
his prudence." For rather he himself gives strength to those who hunger and
through his own Word he gives rest to those who toil. 7.3 And if as something
lowly he himself disdained to work with his own hand the things after the Son,
this too is impious. For there is no pride in God, who at least goes down with
Jacob into Egypt, and for the sake of Abraham sets Abimelech right for the
sake of Sarah, and to Moses being a man speaks mouth to mouth, and goes down
into the mountain Sinai, and with the 7.4 people he warred by hidden grace
against Amalek. But you lie even so saying this: "For he himself made us, and
not we"; and he himself is the one who through his own Word made all things,
both the small and the great, and it is not possible to divide the creation so
as to say this is of the Father, and this of the Son, but it is of one God as
using his own Word as a hand and in him making all things, and this God
himself showing said: "My hand made all these things." And Paul having learned
this taught: "One God from 7.5 whom are all things, and one Lord Jesus Christ
through whom are all things." He himself then always and now says to the sun
and it rises, and commands the clouds and it rains upon one portion, and upon
which he does not rain, it is dried. And he orders the earth to give back the
fruits, and he forms Jeremiah in the belly. And if he himself now does these
things, it is not doubtful that also according to the beginning he did not
disdain himself to make all things through the Word; for these are parts of
the wholes.
8.1 And if because the remaining creatures could not bear the working of the
unmixed hand of the unoriginate, the Son alone came to be from God alone, but
the other things came to be as through an underworker and helper, the Son (for
this too Asterius who sacrificed wrote, and Arius copying it gave it to his
own, and then they use this little phrase as a bruised reed, 8.2 not knowing
the rottenness in it, the deranged): if it was impossible for the originated
things to bear the hand of God (and according to you the Son is one of the
originated), how was he himself able to come to be from God alone? And if in
order that the originated things might come to be there was need of a
mediator, and according to you the Son is originated, there must have been
also before him some middle one, so that he might be created. And the mediator
again himself happening to be a creature, it is fixed that that one too needed
another mediator toward his own constitution. And if someone invents another,
he will invent beforehand that one's mediator, so as even to fall into the
infinite. And thus, the mediator being always sought, not even the creation
will be able to be constituted, because of no one of the originated being
able, as you say, to bear 8.3 the unmixed hand of the unoriginate. And if
seeing this absurdity you begin to say that the Son, being a creature, came to
be sufficient so as to come to be from the unoriginate, of necessity also all
the other originated things being originated were able to be underworked from
the unoriginate; for according to you that one too is a creature just as also
all these are. And then the coming-to-be of the Word is superfluous according
to your impious and foolish invention, God being self-sufficient to work all
things with his own hand and all the originated being able to bear the unmixed
hand 8.4 of God. Their such mind-harm then being mindless, let us see whether
this so-called sophism of the impious also would not appear more irrational
than their other things. Adam alone came to be from God alone through the
Word. But one would not say again that Adam has something more than all men or
differs from those after him, if he alone has been made and formed by God
alone, but we all are begotten from Adam and are constituted according to
succession of the race, as long as he too was formed from earth, and not being
formerly later came to be.

9.1 And if someone should also give more to the first-formed because he was
counted worthy of the hand of God himself, still let such a one reckon the
more in him in honor and not in nature. For he came to be from earth as also
all; and the hand that formed Adam then, this also now 9.2 and always forms
again those after him and constitutes them. And this God himself says to
Jeremiah, as I said before: "Before I formed you in the belly I know you." And
concerning all he said: "My hand made all these things." And again through
Isaiah he says: "Thus says the Lord who redeems you and forms you in the
belly: I the Lord who complete all things stretched out the heaven alone and
made the earth firm." And David knowing this sang: "Your hands made me and
formed me." And the one saying in Isaiah: "Thus says the Lord who formed me
from the belly a servant for himself" signifies this. Therefore according to
nature he differs in nothing from us, even if he precedes in time, as long as
we too are all constituted and created by the same 9.3 hand. If then thus, O
Arians, you also think concerning the Son of God, that thus he too was
constituted and came to be, according to you he will differ in nothing from
the others according to nature, as long as he too was not and came to be and
from virtue there was co-created for him too the grace of the name. For he too
is one, from the things you say, concerning whom in the Psalms the Spirit
says: "He spoke, and they came to be; he commanded, and they were 9.4
created." Who then is it to whom God commanded, so that this one too might be
created? For there must be a Word, to whom God commands and in whom the things
made are created. But you would not be able to show another besides the Word
you deny, unless perhaps you invent some thought again. Yes, they will say, we
have found it; which I once heard those around Eusebius also saying: "For this
reason we think the Son of God has more than the others and is called
only-begotten, since he alone partakes of the Father, but all the other things
partake of the Son." They have already grown weary changing and exchanging the
phrases as colors, but they will be shown even so as talking emptiness from
the earth and rolling as in mire in their own thoughts.
10.1 For if that one is Son of God, but we are called sons of the Son, their
fiction would be plausible; but if we too are called sons of God, of whom he
too is Son, it is plain that we too partake of the Father who says: "I begot
sons and raised them." For if we did not partake of him, he would not have
said: "I begot"; and if he himself begot, not another but he himself 10.2 is
our Father. And it does not differ again if that one has more and came to be
first; but we, if we received less and came to be later, as long as we all
partake of the same and are called sons of the same Father. For the more and
less does not show another nature; for to each the practice of virtue adds
this. And one is set over ten cities, another over five; and some sit "on
twelve thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel," but others hear: "Come,
you blessed of my Father"; and: 10.3 "Well done, good and faithful servant."
Thinking such things then they reasonably imagine that God is not always
Father of such a son nor that such a son always is, but that he has come to be
a creature from things that are not and was not before he was begotten; for
this one is other 10.4 besides the true Son of God. But since it is no longer
holy for these to say these things, for this mind is rather of Sadducees and
of the Samosatene, it remains then according to the other sense, according to
which Isaac is son of Abraham, to say that the Son of God also is; for what is
begotten from someone by nature and not acquired from outside, nature knows as
a son, 10.5 and this is the signification of the name. Is the generation of
the Son then with human passions? For this perhaps according to those they
themselves too will wish to oppose, not knowing. By no means. For God is not
as a man, since neither are men as God. For they were created from matter and
this being passible,

but God is immaterial and bodiless. 10.6 And if the same phrases are sometimes
laid down in the divine Scriptures upon God and men, still it belongs to men
of insight, as Paul commanded, to attend to the reading and thus to
distinguish and according to the nature of each of the things signified to
discern the things written and not to confuse the sense, so as not to think
the things of God in a human way nor ever to reason the things of men as
concerning God. For this is to mix wine with water and to cast strange fire
upon the altar upon the divine fire.
11.1 For God also creates, and upon men too creating has been said. And God is
as being, and men too are said to be from God and to have this having received
it. Does God then create thus as men? Or is he being thus as a man? May it not
be. We take the phrases otherwise upon God and we think them differently upon
men. 11.2 For God creates calling the things that are not into being, needing
nothing, but men work the underlying matter having first prayed and having
received the knowledge of making 11.3 from God who crafted all things through
his own Word. And again men, not being able to be by themselves, happening to
be in a place, are contained and constituted in the Word of God, but God is as
being by himself, containing all things and contained by nothing, and he is in
all according to his own goodness and power, but again he is outside of all
according to his own nature. As then men do not create in the same way in
which God creates, nor are men thus as God is, so otherwise is the generation
of men and otherwise is the 11.4 Son from the Father. For the offspring of men
are somehow parts of those who beget, since even the nature of the bodies
themselves is not some simple thing but fluid, and has composition from parts.
And men begetting flow off and again there flows onto them the things brought
into them from foods; for which cause men also become fathers of many children
according to time. But God, being without parts, is without partition and
without passion Father of the Son. For there is neither an outflow of the
bodiless nor does some inflow come to be into him as upon men; 11.5 but being
simple in nature he is Father of one and only Son. For this reason he is also
only-begotten and he alone is in the bosom of the Father, and the Father shows
him alone from himself saying: "This is my Son the beloved, in whom I was well
pleased"; and this is Word of the Father, in whom it is possible to think the
impassibility and partlessness of the Father. For not even 11.6 the word of
men is begotten according to passion and part, much less that of God.
Wherefore he himself sits at the right of the Father as Word; for where the
Father is, there also is his Word; but we stand being judged by him as things
made. And he himself is worshiped because he is Son of the worshiped Father,
but we worship confessing him Lord and God because we are creatures and others
besides him.
12.1 These things being so, let whoever of them wishes consider then and let
someone become putting them to shame, if it is lawful to say that the
offspring from God and proper to him is from things that are not, or if there
is even reason for it to come at all into anyone's thought that what is from
God has come upon him as an accident, so that he may even dare to say that the
Son is not always. For in this again the generation of the Son has gone beyond
and risen above 12.2 the thoughts of men. For we become fathers of our own
children in time, since we too, not being formerly, later came to be; but God
always being is always Father of the Son. And of them the generation has its
credit from those who similarly are. But since "no one knows the Son except
the Father, nor does anyone know the Father except the Son, and to whom the
Son may reveal," for this reason the holy ones, to whom the Son revealed, have
given us some image from the things seen, saying: "who being radiance of the
glory and impress of his

hypostasis"; and again: "because with you is a fountain of life, in your light
we shall see light"; when also the Word blaming Israel says: "You have
abandoned the fountain of wisdom"; and this is the fountain that says: "They
have abandoned me, a 12.3 fountain of living water." And the example is small
and very dim toward what is desired, yet it is possible from it to think more
than the nature of man and not to think our generation and that of the Son
equal. For who is able even to reason that the radiance sometime is not, so
that he may even dare to say that the Son is not always, or that the Son was
not before he was begotten? Or who is sufficient to divide the radiance from
the sun or to invent the fountain sometime empty of life, so that he may even,
being mad, say that from things that are not is the Son who says: "I am the
life," or foreign to the essence of the Father is the one who says: "He who
has seen me has seen the Father"? For thus the holy ones, wishing us to think,
have given such examples also, and it is absurd and very impious, when
Scripture has such images, for us to think about the Lord from other things
that are neither written nor have any sense toward piety.
13.1 Let them say then at last from where they too having learned, or who
having handed down to them, they began to suspect such things about the
Savior. We have read, they will say, in 13.2 the Proverbs: "The Lord created
me a beginning of his ways for his works." For those around Eusebius also
seemed to say this, and you writing made known that being overturned by many
proofs these too were condemned, but still they themselves, carrying this
saying up and down, said that the Son is one of the creatures and numbered him
with the 13.3 originated. But this too they seem to me not to have understood
well; for it has a pious sense and very right, which if they too had
understood, they would not have blasphemed the Lord of glory. Let them bring
together the things said before into this saying, and 13.4 they will see much
difference being in them. For who, having a right mind, does not see that the
things created and made are from outside of the one making, but the Son, as
the Word showed in the things before, exists not from outside but from the
begetting Father? For a man creates a house, but begets a son, and no one
reversing would say that the house and the ship are begotten from the
constructor, but that the son is created and made by the same, nor again that
the house is an image of the one creating, but the son unlike the one
begetting. But rather he will confess the son an image of the father, and the
house to be a work of craft, unless perhaps someone is sick in mind and
happens to have gone out of 13.5 his wits. Indeed the divine Scripture,
knowing the nature of each more than all, says concerning the things created

41.τ {Against Eusebius and Theognius.}
41.1 Constantine Augustus to the catholic church of the Nicomedians. You all
know accurately, beloved brothers, the Master God plainly and Savior Christ to
be Father and Son, Father I say unbeginning without end, begetter of his own
age, and Son, that is the will of the Father, which has neither been taken up
through some thought nor has been grasped toward the completion of his works
through some essence sought out. For whoever both thinks and will think this,
this one will have toward every 41.2 kind of punishment an unwearying
endurance. But the Son of God Christ the craftsman of all things and the
furnisher of immortality itself was begotten, as much as belongs to the faith
in which we have believed, he was begotten (or rather he himself who is also
always in the Father came forth upon the ordering of the things that have come
to be by him), he was begotten then by an undivided coming-forth; for the will
has been fixed together both in its own dwelling and these things which need a
different 41.3 care it both does and administers according to the quality of
each. What then is between God and Father and Son? Nothing plainly. For this
completion of the things has taken by sense the command of the will, and has
not stood apart the will as having been partitioned 41.4 from the essence of
the Father. And what follows these: who is it who fears the passion of Christ
my Master through shame rather than folly? Does the divine then suffer,
whenever the dwelling of the august body rushes toward a recognition of its
own holiness, or does what has been separated from the body fall under a
touching? Has not this stood apart which has been taken from the lowliness of
the body? And do we not live, even if toward death the good fame 41.5 of the
soul calls the body? What then here has the harmless and sincere

faith taken as worthy of doubt? Or do you not see that God chose a most august
body, through which he was about to make appear the proofs of the faith and
the examples of his own virtue and to shake off the destruction of the human
race already confused by a destructive deceit and to give a new teaching of
religion and by the example of purity to cleanse the unworthy practices of the
mind, then also to dissolve the torture of death, and to proclaim beforehand
the prizes of immortality? 41.6 But you, whom the communion of the love
reasonably makes to be addressed by me then as brothers, you are not ignorant
of me your fellow-servant, you are not ignorant of the fortification of your
salvation, whose care I have genuinely taken up, and through which we not only
fought down the weapons of our enemies, but also still living we shut together
the soul toward showing forth the true faith of humanity. 41.7 But I rejoiced
at these good things especially because of the renewal of the inhabited world.
For it was truly worthy of wonder to lead so many nations into
like-mindedness, who a little before were said not to know God. Yet what were
these nations about to know, who have taken up no care of contentiousness?
What then do you think, beloved brothers, as I blame 41.8 you yourselves? We
are Christians and we disagree with a pitiable disposition. Is this then our
faith, is this the teaching of the most holy law? But what is the cause
through which the destruction of the present evil has been raised? O the
absurdity, O excess of hatred exceeding the greatness of all indignation. What
is the terribleness of this robber-band that has been shown forth, which
denies that the Son of God has come forth from an undivided essence of the
Father? Is God not everywhere, although we perceive this one to be present to
us always? Has not the good order of the wholes been constituted through the
power of this one, although it has been deprived of the standing-apart of the
separation? 41.9 Has something then been done by you? Beloved brothers, learn
thoroughly now, I ask, the tortures of the present pain; you promised to be
confessors of this one whom you deny to be God, the all-destructive teacher
persuading you of this. I entreat, who is it who taught these things thus to
an unhurt multitude? Eusebius plainly, the fellow-initiate of the tyrannic
cruelty. For that he has become everywhere a refugee of the tyrant, it is
possible to see from many places. For this the slaughters of the bishops bear

Christians shouts expressly. =41.10= For I will say nothing now about the
insults that have come to be toward me, through which, when especially the
