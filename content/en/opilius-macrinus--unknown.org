* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

The lives of those princes or tyrants or Caesars who did not reign long
lie in obscurity, because there is nothing worth telling of their private
life, since they would not even have been known at all unless they had
aspired to empire, and of an empire which they did not hold long not
much can be said. We will nevertheless bring to light what we have dug
out from various historians, and those things, to be sure, which will be
worthy of record. For there is no one who in his life does not do
something every day; but it is the duty of him who sets out to write the
lives of others to write out at length what is worthy of knowledge.

Junius Cordus, indeed, had a zeal for publishing the lives of those
emperors whom he saw to be more obscure, and he did not profit much; for
he found both little and things unworthy of record, asserting that he
would pursue even the smallest matters, as if it were necessary to know
of Trajan or Pius or Marcus how often he went out, when he varied his
foods, and when he changed his clothes, and whom he promoted and when.
By following all these things he filled his books with mythical
histories, writing such stuff, whereas of cheap matters either nothing
should be written or very little, if nevertheless from these the
character can be observed, which truly ought to be known, but only in
part, so that from that the rest may be gathered.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

When Antoninus Bassianus had therefore been killed, Opilius Macrinus,
his prefect of the guard, who had previously managed the privy purse,
seized the empire, born of a low station and of a shameless mind and
face, and he named himself now Severus, now Antoninus, although he was
hated by all, both civilians and soldiers. And at once he set
out to the Parthian war and took from the soldiers the power of judging
him and from the rumors by which he was pressed the chance to grow;
although the senate received him as emperor gladly out of hatred of
Antoninus Bassianus, since in the senate there was one voice for all:
/Anyone rather than a parricide, anyone rather than an incestuous man,
anyone rather than an impure man, anyone rather than a murderer of both
senate and people./

And it may perhaps seem wonderful to all why Macrinus wished his son
Diadumenus to be named Antoninus,

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

although he is said to have been the author of Antoninus' death. Of him
I will set forth what has been related in the annals. The prophetess of
Caelestis at Carthage, which goddess, when filled, is accustomed to sing
true things, under Antoninus Pius, when the proconsul, as was the
custom, inquired about the public state and she was predicting the
future of his own command, when it came to the princes, ordered in a
clear voice that they count how often she said Antoninus, and then, to
the amazement of all, she uttered the name of Antoninus eight times.
But while all believed that Antoninus Pius would reign for eight years,
he both exceeded this number of years, and it was agreed among those who
believed, either then or afterwards, that something else had been
signified through the prophetess.

Finally, when all who were called Antonini have been counted, this
number of Antonini is found: Pius first, Marcus second, Verus third,
Commodus fourth, Caracallus fifth, Geta sixth, Diadumenus seventh,
Heliogabalus eighth were Antonini; nor are the two Gordiani to be
reckoned among the Antonini, who either had only the praenomen of the
Antonini or were even called Antonii, not Antonini.

Hence it is that Severus also called himself Antoninus, as most report,
and Pertinax and Julianus and this same Macrinus; and by the Antonini
themselves, who were the true successors of Antoninus, this name was
kept more than their own. So say some. But others report that Diadumenus
was called Antoninus by his father Macrinus in order that the suspicion
of Antoninus' murder might be taken from Macrinus among the soldiers;
others, however, say there was so great a longing for this name that,
unless the people and the soldiers heard the name of Antoninus, they
would not think a man imperial.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

And of Macrinus indeed, when it was announced that Varius Heliogabalus
was emperor, after the senate had already named Alexander Caesar, many
things were said in the senate so that it appears he was ignoble,
sordid, and filthy.

The words, finally, of Aurelius Victor, whose surname was Pinius, were
these: that Macrinus was a freedman, a man of the brothel, occupied with
servile offices in the imperial house, of venal faith, of a sordid life
under Commodus, removed by Severus even from the most wretched offices
and relegated to Africa, where, to cover the infamy of his condemnation,
he gave himself to reading, pleaded petty cases, declaimed, and at last
gave judgments; but that, presented with gold rings, with his
fellow-freedman Festus as his patron, he was made advocate of the
treasury under Verus Antoninus. But these things too are set down as
doubtful, and others are said by others, which themselves also we will
not pass in silence. For most have said that he exhibited a gladiatorial
fight and, having received the wooden sword, went to Africa; that he was
first a hunter, afterwards even a clerk, then advocate of the treasury.
From which office he came to the highest things; then, when he was
prefect of the guard, his colleague having been sent away, he killed his
emperor Antoninus Caracallus with so great a faction that he did not
seem to have been killed by him; for, having bought his groom and held
out a huge hope, he so managed that he was said to have been killed as
if by military plots, because he was disliked either for parricide or
for incest.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

At once, then, he seized the empire, having taken his son Diadumenus
into partnership, whom he immediately, as we have said, ordered to be
called Antoninus by the soldiers; then he sent Antoninus' body to Rome
to be laid in the tombs of his ancestors. He charged his late colleague,
the prefect of the guard, to see to his duty and especially to bury
Antoninus honorably with a royal funeral, because he knew that Antoninus
had been much loved by the commons on account of the garments given to
the people as a congiarium. There was added also this, that he feared a
military rising, lest, if it intervened, his own empire, which he had
gone to snatch, but had received as if unwilling, should be hindered; as
men are who say they are forced to those things which they even procure
by crimes. He feared also his colleague, lest he too should desire to
rule, since all hoped that, if the concession of a single unit were
added, he would not refuse, and all would do it most eagerly from hatred
of Macrinus, either for his wicked life or for his low birth, since all
previous emperors had been noble. He moreover thrust himself into the
name of Severus, although he was touched by no kinship with that man.
Whence a jest arose:
/Macrinus is Severus in the same way that Diadumenus is Antoninus./
He nevertheless at once, to wipe out the soldiers' rising, gave a
donative both to the legionaries and to the praetorians more generously
than usual, inasmuch as he wished to lessen the crime of the slain
emperor; and money, as usual, profited him whom innocence
could not profit, for a man of every vice was retained for some time in
the empire.

He then sent a letter to the senate about the death of Antoninus,
calling him divine and excusing himself and swearing that he had known
nothing of his murder. Thus, after the manner of ruined men, he joined
perjury to his crime, with which it was fitting that a wicked man should
begin.

When he wrote to the senate it is of interest to know of what sort the
speech was by which he excused himself, so that both the impudence of
the man may be known and the sacrilege with which the wicked emperor
took his beginning. Heads from the speech of the emperors Macrinus and
Diadumenus:
/We would wish, conscript fathers, both with our Antoninus unharmed and
returned with a triumph, to see your Clemency; for then at last, with
the state flourishing, we should all be happy, and we should live under
that prince whom the gods had given us in the place of the Antonini.
But because this could not come to pass through a military tumult, we
announce first what the army has done concerning us, then we decree
divine honors, which is the first thing to be done, to that man in whose
words we have sworn, since the army thought no one worthier to avenge
the murder of Bassianus than his prefect, to whom he himself would
surely have entrusted the faction to be punished, if he had been able to
detect it while alive./
and below:
/They have conferred the empire on me, of which I, conscript fathers,
have meanwhile received the guardianship; I shall hold the government if
what has pleased the soldiers shall please you also, to whom I have
already ordered both a donative and all things after the imperial
custom./
likewise below:
/The soldier has given my son Diadumenus, known to you, both empire and
name, calling him, to be sure, Antoninus, that he may be honored first
by the name, so also by the honor of the throne, which we ask you,
conscript fathers, to approve with a good and fortunate omen, lest you
lack the name of the Antonini, which you most love./
cohonestetur Jordon, Petschenig; quo honestetur P; cohonestaretur Peter.
likewise below:
/To Antoninus, moreover, the soldier has decreed divine honors, and we
decree them, and we ask you, conscript fathers, to decree them; although
we could command by imperial right, nevertheless we ask, dedicating to
him two equestrian statues, two on foot in military dress, two seated in
civil dress, likewise to the Divine Severus two triumphal statues; all
which things, conscript fathers, you will order to be fulfilled, we
striving religiously on behalf of our predecessors./

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

When the letter had therefore been read in the senate, contrary to the
opinion of all, the senate both received the death of Antoninus with
gladness and, hoping that Opilius Macrinus would care for the public
liberty, first enrolled him among the patricians, a new man and one who
a little before had been procurator of the privy purse; the same man,
though he had been a pontifical clerk, whom today they call the lesser
pontiffs, it named pontifex maximus, with the name of Pius decreed. For
a long time, nevertheless, after the letter was read, since no one at
all believed in Antoninus' death, there was silence. But after it was
established that he had been killed, the senate inveighed against him as
against a tyrant. Finally they at once conferred on Macrinus both the
proconsular command and the tribunician power.

His son, indeed, when he himself had received the name of Felix, in
order to remove the suspicion of Antoninus killed by him, he called
Antoninus, previously named Diadumenus, which name indeed Varius
Heliogabalus also, who said he was the son of Bassianus, a most filthy
man and conceived of a harlot, afterwards received. Finally there exist
verses of a certain poet, by which it will be shown that the name of
Antoninus began from Pius and little by little through the Antonini came
to the last filth, if indeed Marcus alone seems to have increased that
holy name by the manner of his life, while Verus degenerated, and
Commodus even polluted the reverence of the consecrated name. What now
can be said of Caracallus Antoninus, or of this man? Finally even what
of Heliogabalus, who, last of the Antonini, is recorded to have lived in
the highest impurity?

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Having therefore been named emperor, after taking up the empire he set
out against the Parthians with a great apparatus, striving to wipe out
the meanness of his birth and the infamy of his former life by the
greatness of a victory; but after a conflict was held against the
Parthians, by the desertion of the legions, which had fled to Varius
Heliogabalus, he was killed. But he reigned more than a year.

Indeed, since he was inferior in that war which Antoninus had waged,
Artabanus gravely avenging the slaughter of his own citizens, Macrinus
at first fought back; afterwards, however, sending envoys he asked for
peace, which the Parthian granted with a willing mind after Antoninus
had been killed. Then, when he had withdrawn to Antioch and given
himself to luxury, he furnished the army a just cause of killing him and
of following the son of Bassianus, as was thought, that is Heliogabalus
Bassianus Varius, who afterwards was called both Bassianus and
Antoninus.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

There was a certain woman, Maesa or Varia, from the city of Emesa,
sister of Julia the wife of Severus Pertinax the African, who after the
death of Antoninus Bassianus had been expelled from the palace by the
pride of Macrinus; to her indeed Macrinus granted all that she had long
collected. She had two daughters, Symiamira and Mamaea, of whom the
elder had a son Heliogabalus, who received the name both of Bassianus
and of Antoninus; for the Phoenicians call the sun Heliogabalus. But
Heliogabalus was conspicuous for beauty and stature and priesthood and
known to all men who came to the temple, especially to the soldiers.
Maesa or Varia told these that Bassianus was the son of Antoninus, which
little by little became known to all the soldiers. Maesa herself,
moreover, was most rich, from which even Heliogabalus was most
luxurious. When she promised the soldiers, the legions were drawn away
from Macrinus. For when she had been received by night into the town
with her own, her grandson was named Antoninus, the insignia of empire
having been conferred.

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

When these things were announced to Macrinus, stationed at Antioch, he
wondered at the woman's boldness, and at the same time even despised it,
and sent Julianus the prefect with the legions to besiege them; when
Antoninus was shown to these, all having inclined to him with a
wonderful love, the prefect Julianus was killed and all went over to
him. Then, a part of the army having been joined, Antoninus came against
Macrinus, who was hastening against him, and when battle was joined
Macrinus was defeated by the treachery of his soldiers and by love of
Antoninus. Macrinus, fleeing indeed with a few and his son, was killed
in a certain village of Bithynia with Diadumenus, and his head was taken
and brought to Antoninus.

It should be known besides that the boy Diadumenus is said to have been
Caesar, not Augustus, whom most have handed down as having been of equal
empire with his father. The son too was killed, to whom empire brought
this alone, that he should be killed by a soldier; for there will be
nothing worthy in his life that may be said, besides this, that he was
set beside the name of the Antonini as a kind of bastard.

* Caput 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

He was nevertheless in his imperial life a little more rigid and
austere, hoping that he could give all his former deeds to oblivion,
although that very severity of his opened an occasion of blaming and
abusing him; for he had wished to be named both Severus and Pertinax,
which two names seemed to him names of harshness, and when the senate
had named him Pius and Felix, he received the name of Felix, and did not
wish to have that of Pius. Whence an epigram not inelegant of a certain
Greek poet seems to exist against him, which in Latin is contained in
this sense:

An actor already old, base, heavy, harsh, unjust,
impious and fortunate, so wishes to be at once,
that he will not be pious, yet wishes to be blessed,
which nature denies, nor does reason admit;
for he could be called and seem pious and fortunate;
impious, he is unhappy, and will be so to himself.

These verses some one of the Latins set up in the forum next to those
which had been put up in Greek; when Macrinus had received them, he is
said to have answered with these verses:

If the fates had brought forth such a Greek poet
as that Latin gallows-bird was, the people would have known
nothing, the senate would have known nothing, no huckster
would have written foul songs against me.

By these verses, far worse than those Latin ones are, Macrinus believed
he had answered; but he was held no less in ridicule than that poet who
tried to write in Latin from the Greek.

* Caput 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

He was therefore proud and bloodthirsty and wishing to rule in military
fashion, even blaming the discipline of former times and praising
Severus alone above the rest; for he both raised soldiers on the cross
and always afflicted them with servile punishments, and, when he
suffered military seditions, he often decimated the soldiers, sometimes
even centesimated them, which word is his own, since he said he was
merciful when he punished every hundredth of those who were worthy of
decimation and of the taking of every twentieth. It is long to disclose
all his cruelties; I will nevertheless show one, not great, as he
himself believed, but sadder than all tyrannical enormities. When
certain soldiers had assaulted a maidservant of their host, a woman long
of bad repute, and he had learned this through a certain secret
agent, he ordered them to be brought and asked whether it had been
done; when this was established, he suddenly ordered two oxen of
wonderful size to be opened alive and the soldiers to be inserted one
into each, with their heads thrust out so that they could talk with one
another; and so he afflicted them with this punishment, although not
even for adulterers had such penalties been established among the
ancients or in his own time. He nevertheless fought both against the
Parthians and against the Armenians and against the Arabs, whom they
call the Fortunate, no less bravely than fortunately.

A tribune who allowed the watches to be deserted he dragged, bound under
a wheeled carriage, through the whole journey, alive and then lifeless.
He also restored the punishment of Mezentius, by which that man bound
the living to the dead and forced them to death, wasted by a long decay.
Whence also in the Circus, when the public favor had stretched itself
forth toward Diadumenus, it was shouted:
/A youth of outstanding beauty,/
/whose father was not Mezentius./

He also walled living men into buildings; those guilty of
adultery he always burned alive together, their bodies joined; slaves who
had fled their masters, when found, he assigned to fight as gladiators.
Informers, if they did not prove their charge, he punished with death; if
they proved it, he dismissed them infamous, a money reward having been
given.

* Caput 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

He was not unskilled in law, so much so that he had resolved to abolish
all the rescripts of former princes, that business should be done by law
not by rescripts, saying it was a crime that the wills of Commodus and
Caracallus and of unskilled men should be regarded as laws, since Trajan
never answered petitions, lest those things which seemed composed for
favor should be carried over to other cases.

In granting grain-allowances he was most lavish, in gold most sparing,
but in beating the palace staff so impious, so persistent, so harsh,
that his slaves did not call him Macrinus but Macellinus, because his
house was stained with the blood of home-born slaves after the manner of
a butcher's stall. He was most greedy of wine and food, sometimes even
to drunkenness, but in the evening hours. For if he had lunched, even in
private he was most sparing; at dinner most lavish. He invited men of
letters to his banquet, so that, speaking of liberal studies, he was of
necessity abstemious.

* Caput 14
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14
:END:

But when men thought of his old cheapness, saw the cruelty of his
character, they could not bear so offensive a man in the empire; and
especially the soldiers, who remembered many of his most deadly and
sometimes most shameful deeds, entered a faction and killed him with his
boy Diadumenus, surnamed, to be sure, Antoninus, of whom it was said
that he had been Antoninus in dreams, whence also verses of this kind
exist:

We have seen in dreams, citizens, unless I am mistaken, even this:
that boy bore the name of the Antonini,
who was born of a venal father but a modest mother,
for she suffered a hundred adulterers and asked a hundred,
he himself too was a bald adulterer, thence a husband;
behold Pius, behold Marcus, for he was not Verus.

And these verses were translated from Greek into Latin, for in Greek
they are most eloquent; they seem to me, however, to have been
translated by some common poet. When Macrinus had heard this, he made
iambics which are not extant. They are said, however, to have been most
pleasant. These indeed perished in that tumult in which he himself was
killed, when all his things also were seized by the soldiers.

The manner of his death, as we have said, was such: when the army had
inclined to Antoninus Heliogabalus, he fled and was defeated in war and
killed in a suburb of Bithynia, his men partly surrendered, partly
killed, partly put to flight. Thus Heliogabalus was believed famous,
because he seemed to have avenged his father's death; and from there he
came to the empire, which he dishonored with huge vices, luxury,
foulness, gluttony, pride, enormity. He himself also obtained a like
end of his life.

These things about Macrinus are known to us, some varying in many
points, as a man's history is wont to do, which, collected from
very many sources, we have brought to your Serenity, Diocletian
Augustus, because we have seen that you are eager for the old emperors.
