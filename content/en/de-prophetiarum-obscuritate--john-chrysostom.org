* On the Obscurity of the Prophecies
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Proof that the prophecies concerning Christ and the nations and the fall of
the Jews are usefully obscure.

1. Today I wish to set a prophetic table before you, and I am preparing to
launch the discourse toward the sea of Isaiah's wisdom. But what am I to
suffer? I hesitate and fear lest, once we have left the harbor and come to the
depth of the prophetic thoughts, we grow dizzy: which is what those unused to
sailing suffer. For they, when they have left the land and see sea on either
side of the ship, and nothing else but sea and sky, are seized with vertigo,
and think the vessel is being spun round them together with the sea. But the
dizziness comes not from the nature of the sea, but from the inexperience of
those who sail. Other sailors, at any rate, =56.164= dive with bare bodies
down upon the waves and suffer nothing of the kind, but going down into the
depth itself they spend their time more securely than those who live on dry
ground, and receiving the brine with mouth and eyes and the whole body they
are not distressed. So great a good is practice, so great an evil is
inexperience: thus the one persuades us even to despise fearful things, while
the other prepares us even to suspect and fear what is safe. For those sitting
on the high decks of the ship grow dizzy even at the sight; but the others are
not troubled even in the midst of the waves. This happens also in the case of
our mind. For waves of passions often overtake it, wilder than those of the
sea, a storm of anger twisting the heart from below, =56.165= winds of evil
desire producing much confusion in the mind. But the inexperienced and
unpracticed man, when the winter of wrath begins, is at once troubled, shaken,
tossed, and looks on while the soul is being sunk under the passions and
suffering shipwreck. The experienced man, who has practiced bearing such
things nobly, seating reason above the passions as a helmsman on the tiller,
does not leave off doing everything until he has steered the vessel toward the
calm harbor of philosophy. What happens then on the sea, and what befalls the
mind, this also occurs in the interpretation of the Scriptures: we must be
troubled and shaken when we go out onto the sea, not because the sea is
fearful, but because we who sail are inexperienced. That a discourse easy by
nature can become hard through the inexperience of the hearers, I will present
Paul as a witness to you. For having said that Christ became a high priest
according to the order of Melchizedek, and seeking who Melchizedek is, he
added: Concerning whom we have much to say, and it is hard to interpret. What
are you saying, O blessed Paul? Hard to interpret for you, who have spiritual
wisdom, who heard the unspeakable words, who were caught up to the third
heaven? If it is hard to interpret for you, for whom is it graspable? For me,
he says, it is hard to interpret, not because of its own difficulty, but
because of the weakness of the hearers. For having said, Hard to interpret, he
added: Since you have become dull of hearing. Do you see that it was not the
nature of the discourse, but the inexperience of the hearers, that made what
was not hard into something hard? And the same cause makes not only the hard,
but also the short into something long. Therefore he said it was not only hard
to interpret, but also much, attributing the cause both of the length and of
the difficulty to the dullness of hearing. For just as with the sick it is not
necessary to set a short and hastily prepared table, but one must prepare

the foods in variety, so that if the sufferer does not wish to take this, he
may take the other; and if he does not admit that, he may receive the other;
and if he pushes this away, he may lay hold of the remaining one, and by the
variety of foods we may overcome the difficulty, and by the many forms of the
table we may treat the displeasure of the mind: so often one must do also in
the spiritual feast. When we are weak, we must prepare the discourse long and
varied, having parables and examples, arguments, and periods, and many other
such things, so that from all of them the choice of what is profitable may
become easy for us. Yet even if the discourse is much and hard to interpret,
he did not deprive them of the teaching about Melchizedek. For by saying, Much
and Hard to interpret, he raised their zeal, so that they might become more
eager about the hearing; and by providing the table, he granted their desire.

2. Let us also do this. Even if the sea of the prophets is boundless, and many
are the depths there, =56.166= let us dare the sea according to our power. Or
rather, not according to our power, but according to the grace given us from
above, not because of our own boldness, but because of your benefit, let us
dare the sea, imitating Paul in this too. That he did not deprive them of the
discourse concerning Melchizedek, hear what follows. Having said, Concerning
whom the discourse is much and hard to interpret, he added: For this
Melchizedek, king of righteousness; then also king of Salem, which is king of
peace, without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither
beginning of days nor end of life; but made like the Son of God, he remains a
priest continually. Did not Paul disturb your hearing, discoursing about a man
and saying, Without father, without mother? And why do I say, about a man? For
if this were said about Christ, would it not even so furnish us much inquiry?
For if without father, how a Son? if without father, how Only-begotten? For
the son ought to have a father, since otherwise he would not be a son. But the
Son of God is both without father and without mother. How? Without father as
to the birth below, without mother as to the birth above. For he had neither a
father on earth nor a mother in heaven. Without genealogy. Let those who busy
themselves about his essence listen. And yet some think that this Without
genealogy has been said about the birth above. For the heretics do not even
want this: they busy themselves about that too and pry into it. Those more
moderate than these yield that one, but no longer think that Without genealogy
has been said also about this lower birth. Let us show then that Paul said
this about both births, both the one above and the one below. For that one is
fearful, and this one is most mystical. Therefore Isaiah also says, Who shall
declare his generation? But he said it, someone says, about that one above.
What then shall we say to Paul, who named both births and then added, Without
genealogy? For having first said, Without father, without mother, he then
added, Without genealogy, so that you might believe him to be without
genealogy not only according to that birth in which he is without mother, but
also according to this one in which he was without father, I mean the lower.
Therefore having set down both, he then said, Without genealogy. For this
lower one too is incomprehensible, so that we may not even dare to peek toward
that other. For if the forecourts of the temple are so fearful and
unapproachable, how will anyone attempt to enter the inner shrine? That he was
begotten of the Father, I know; the how, I do not know. That he was born of
the virgin, I understand; but the manner I do not grasp even here. For the
birth of each nature is confessed, and the manner of each has been left in
silence. And just as here in the case of the virgin, not knowing how he was
born of a virgin, I confess that he was born, and I do not do away with the
fact because of ignorance of the manner: so you also do in the case of the

Father. Even if you do not know how he was begotten, confess the, He was
begotten. And if the heretic says to you, How =56.167= was the Son begotten of
the Father? drag his pride down to the earth, and say to him: Come down from
the heavens, and show how he was born of the virgin, and then ask those other
things. Hold him, and stand round him, and do not let him leap away, nor
withdraw into the labyrinth of reasonings; but hold him, and choke him, not
with the hand, but with the word. Do not give him the distinctions and escapes
he wants. From that point they throw the disputants into confusion, because we
follow them and do not lead them under the laws of the divine Scriptures.
Fence him then with a wall on every side, the testimonies from the Scriptures,
and he will not even be able to gape. Say to him, How was he born of the
virgin? I do not leave off, nor do I withdraw. But he would not have the
manner to tell, even if he contended ten thousand times. For when God has
shut, who will open thereafter? Such things are acceptable by faith alone. But
if you do not endure this, but seek reasonings, I will say to you what Christ
said to Nicodemus: If I told you earthly things, and you do not believe, how,
if I tell you heavenly things, will you believe? I spoke about the birth from
a virgin, and you do not know, nor do you dare to gape, and you pry into
heaven? Would that it were heaven, and not the Lord of the heavens, that you
were busying yourself about. If I told you earthly things, and you do not
believe. He did not say, you are not persuaded, but, You do not believe,
showing us that even earthly things need faith. And if earthly things need
faith, much more the heavenly. And yet then he was discoursing with Nicodemus
about a far lesser birth: for the discourse was about baptism and the
spiritual regeneration; but he was saying that even these are graspable by
faith. And he called them earthly, not because they are earthly, but because
they are accomplished on earth, and by comparison with the birth above, which
is unspeakable and surpasses every mind, these are earthly. If then it is not
possible to know how I am born again from the waters, but one must receive
what happens by faith alone, and not pry into the manner: of what madness
would it be, in the case of the birth above of the only-begotten Son, to set
human reasonings in motion, and to demand an account of the manner of
begetting?

3. How then the Only-begotten Son of God is without father and without mother
has been sufficiently shown, and how he is without genealogy as to both
births. Let us now lead the discourse away to what is pressing, postponing the
word about Melchizedek to another day, and let us rouse the hearing, since we
are about to hear prophetic riddles. For the prophetic things are like
riddles, and there is much difficulty in the Old Testament, and the books are
hard to grasp; but the New is clearer and easier. And why, someone says, have
these things been so arranged, although the New discourses about greater
matters, about the kingdom of heaven, and the resurrection of bodies, and the
unspeakable goods which even surpass the human mind? What then is the cause of
the prophecies being obscure? They foretell many evils to the Jews: and that
those will be cast56.168 out, while we will be received in; and that the
temple will be destroyed, and will no longer rise again, and Jerusalem will
fall, and will be passable to all; and the Jews, becoming wanderers, will be
scattered everywhere in the world, and having fallen from the city, they will
no longer even have the ancient constitution, but all their former things will
be taken from them, both prophecies, and sacrifices, and priesthood, and
kingdom. And not only these things, but the prophets also foretold many other
such things, mixing countless tragedies into their own books. So that the
Jews, hearing clearly at the beginning, might not murder those who said these
things, they hid the predictions by the difficulty of the interpretation, and
poured much obscurity over the matters,

preparing safety for the speakers by the obscurity of what was said. And from
where is this clear? For we must also be required to give account, even if we
speak among friends: for perhaps many who are not friends are also present.
Let those too learn these things then, so that they also may become friends. I
said that if the Jews had heard the evils that were going to overtake them,
and that Jerusalem would be taken because of Christ with this immortal capture
that has no change, if they had heard these things plainly from the prophets,
they would at once have killed those who said them. From where then is this
clear? First from their manners: for they were mad and savage. The people is
one always thirsting for prophetic blood; their hands practiced themselves in
the slaughters of the saints. The great Elijah cries out against them saying:
Lord, they have killed your prophets, they have dug down your altars. Christ
again: Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who killed the prophets, and stoned those sent to
her. And Isaiah, accusing them in harmony with these, cried: Your hands are
full of blood. And again Christ: Your fathers killed the prophets, and you
build their tombs; fill up the measure of your fathers. Do you see how both
the Master and the servants bear witness to their bloodguilt? And what is:
Fill up the measure of your fathers? Kill me too, he says; add the Master's
slaughter to the servants' blood. For if they slaughtered ten thousand men,
they were all fellow servants; but when they stretched out their hands against
the Master, then the measure was filled; and reasonably. For as long as they
had not killed the Master, they had a hope of salvation, and they expected
that the Lamb of God coming would take away the sin of the world; but when
they murdered the physician, and insulted the mercy seat itself, and turned
away from the one who came to forgive sins, they fell thereafter from all
hope. Therefore he says: Fill up the measure of your fathers. Yes, someone
says; but that they are bloodguilty and accursed has been shown from many
testimonies; from where is it clear that they would not have spared the
prophets, if they had heard that Jerusalem would be over56.169 thrown, that
the law would cease, that the Old would be changed? Especially it is clear
even from what has been said; yet I will make the proof clearer again from the
Scriptures themselves. For if ever they heard a prophet saying that Jerusalem
would be overthrown with a temporary overthrow, when they ought to have
changed and beaten back the wrath of God, they let their anger fly at the
prophet. And that this is true, hear the histories themselves. The Persians
once besieged their city, and a barbarian camp sat round outside; and the
dangers were not unclear, but the city of Jerusalem was then in the midst of a
snare, all being armed and standing round from without. Yet though the terrors
were so plain, when Jeremiah came and said to them that the city would be
delivered into the hands of the Chaldeans; and this was not even a prophecy,
for their eyes saw what would happen; yet because he said what was plain and
before their eyes, the polluted and senseless men, ungrateful even toward
their benefactors, raged so as to think him a traitor and a destroyer of the
city, and to say that He weakens the hands of this people. And yet he was
strengthening them, and yet he was waking their eagerness, and leading them to
God, putting round them an unbreakable and unconquerable wall; but they,
thinking none of these things, ordered him to be killed. For they always gave
such returns to their benefactors, and though the king pardoned, they did not
even so yield, but when they could not kill him they threw him into the pit of
mire.

4. And if they did not bear the hearing of a temporary captivity, how were
they going to listen to the prediction of the

perpetual slavery? If Jeremiah said, You will go away to Babylon, and they did
not bear to hear, but punished the one who said it: if they had heard the
prophets saying, Not to Babylon, but you will be scattered everywhere in the
world, and you will no longer return, would they not also have drunk the blood
of those who said it? And if this still seems to be a conjecture, I furnish
you a plain proof that it was not safe for them to speak the things to come, I
mean our honor, their fall. Tell me, why did they stone Stephen, the
firstfruits of the martyrs? Was it not charging him with these things and
saying, This man speaks blasphemous words? He said, they say, that Jesus will
destroy this temple, and change the customs which Moses delivered, and for
this they stoned him. And if they did not then endure to hear these things,
and that when the facts were already persuading them, how would they have
endured the prophets proclaiming these things beforehand? You heard, beloved,
that they stoned him because of the temple and the change of the constitution;
hear how they also bring this charge against Christ. For this man said, they
say: Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it. Did you see how
everywhere the destruction of the temple, and the trans56.170 fer of the
constitution, produced the wrath in them? Therefore the prophets said these
things, but they did not say them plainly. Therefore they also wanted to kill
Paul, because he was persuading them to change from the constitution. From
where is this clear? You see, brother, he says, how many myriads there are of
Jews who have believed, and they have all been informed about you, that you
teach apostasy from the law. The faithful did not endure to be taught apostasy
from the law; and how would those not yet believing have endured to hear that
the law would someday cease? That the Jews would have killed the prophets, if
they had clearly foretold any such thing, we have shown from the testimonies,
both from the blessed Jeremiah, and from the first martyr Stephen, from Christ
himself, and from the apostle Paul. For charging them all with the same
things, they killed them thus. And that they would also have burned the
prophetic books themselves, if they had understood what was said, this too I
will try to establish from a certain history, hidden from you, but about to be
clear immediately; for so I will try to report it. What then is the history,
hear. And it came to pass in the fourth year of Jehoiakim son of Josiah king
of Judah, the Lord said to Jeremiah: Write all the words which I have spoken
to you from the day of Josiah until this day; that is, all the evils which I
intend to do to them. See how loving toward man God is, and caring. For since
they would not endure to hear them bit by bit, Gather them all, he says, and
increase the fear, so that even thus they may become better. Remember the
promise: what we seek is to show that they would also have cut up the books
themselves, if they had understood all the things that have come out today.
Perhaps they will hear evils, for we must hold to the history, which I intend
to do to them, and they will turn from their wicked way. Perhaps, God says?
Tell me, does he not know the things that will be? Does he not know whether
they will hear, he who knows all things before their generation, who searches
hearts and kidneys, who is a discerner of thoughts and intentions, before
whose eyes all things are naked and laid open? And for what reason did he say,
Perhaps they will hear? For you must learn these things too, because of those
who charge the Only-begotten with ignorance. For see, the Father also utters
words of one who does not know: for the, Perhaps, is of one who does not know;
but he is not ignorant. Whenever then you also hear something of this kind
said by the Son, accept the same verdict. For being Son, he imitates the
Father everywhere. But let the times of those contests wait, so that we may
not fall away from the present subject; and let us say why he says, Perhaps

they will hear. If he had said that they will hear, without the Perhaps, it
was false: for they were not going to hear. If he had said the truth, that
they will not hear, he was sending the prophet in vain to those who would not
hear. And not only for this, but also so that his foreknowledge might not be
thought some compelling cause of the disobedience, for this reason he has set
the words ambi56.171 guously, so that some may not say that God foretold it,
and it had to happen in any case: which they also say about Judas. He
foretold, they say, that he would be a traitor, and therefore he became a
traitor. O the folly, O the shamelessness. For the foreknowledge, O man, is
not the cause of the wickedness; God forbid. For it is not compelling of the
things that will be, but only knowing beforehand. It was not because Christ
foretold that that man became a traitor; but because he was going to become a
traitor, therefore Christ foretold. So that they may not say here too that he
said they will not hear, and shut the way of repentance to them, he
anticipated and pulled up their pretext, saying to the prophet, Perhaps they
will hear.

5. Remember the promise: for this is why I remind you continually, so that
when I bring on the conclusion you may not forget the chapter that was sought
by us at the beginning. And what was this? That if the Jews had perceived that
these terrible things which have overtaken them, I mean those that now hold
them, were going to happen to them, they would have cut up the books
themselves, and would not have spared even the divine letters. But let us come
to the history. Hearing these things, Jeremiah calls his disciple Baruch son
of Neriah, and says to him: Write in a book all the terrible things that are
going to happen to them. What has happened? You were commanded by God, and you
send the disciple? Are you shrinking? Are you in agony? Are you afraid? If you
are afraid, how will the disciple dare? But none of these things: for the
cause is attached. For having said, Write and read, he added: For I am held in
the prison. O greatness of soul. He was in the jail, and he did not leave off
the prophecy. Let us attend to the courage of the righteous man, and to the
philosophy of his mind. He did not say to himself: So many terrible things
have happened to me because of this boldness; I have spent ten thousand words,
and I have gained nothing, nor has anything more come to me except only being
bound; and God has not yet released me from the bonds, and again he sends me
to those beasts? He said none of these things, nor did he think them; but he
looked to one thing only, how the Master's command might be accomplished; and
since he could not do it through himself, he arranged it through the disciple.
For read to them, he says, and tell all the evils. For I am held in the
prison. And Jeremiah was speaking, and Baruch was writing in a book. It was a
time of fasting when these things were happening; a feast had come on, calling
all to the mother city; for the assembly had to become common, since the
meeting was going to be about necessary matters. Baruch went in to the rulers,
and read into the ears of the rulers all these words; and he also said the
cause: For perhaps, he says, your mercy will fall before the Lord; so that
they might not think he was speaking as an accuser, but, learning that he had
come to treat them, might become more moderate. What then did those men do?
When they ought to have given thanks, when they ought to have praised, when
they ought to have wondered, they do none of these things, =56.172= but going
away they reported to the king what was written in the book, and they laid the
book up in the house of Elishama. And the king, he says, sending Jehudi, one
of those standing by him, ordered the book to be brought. And the king was
sitting in a winter house; for it was the ninth month, that is November; he
means the ninth counting from Xanthicus; for these things too must be known
accurately. For if he were counting from Dius, it would not have been winter
then. For what

reason is this also added? From what follows you will know clearly. And a
hearth of fire was before him: a brazier, he says, because it was cold. Do you
see how nothing is omitted in the divine Scripture? And a brazier of fire was
before him; and the satraps stood round; the book was brought, full of ten
thousand goods, for the prophecy of evils was a destruction of evils, and it
was being read. Remember the promise for me, I beg. And as he was reading
three pages, taking the penknife he cut it, and threw it onto the hearth of
the fire, until the whole book was consumed. Did you see how they do not spare
even the books? how they do not keep off even from the divine letters? Because
it contained a captivity of Jerusalem, he cut it up; and not finding the
prophet, he let his anger go upon the letters. He then who warred thus against
lifeless things, if he had found the one who had a soul, what would he not
have done? For just as the savage among beasts, when they have seized those
who box with them, and then those men escape leaving in the mouth of the
beasts the skins they wear, thereafter gnaw those skins, comforting their
anger: so indeed the king also did. He did not find the one who had the book,
and he cut up the book itself; and he did not only cut it up, but also threw
it onto the hearth of the fire, so that not even a remnant of those letters
might remain. But you have not yet known all his madness; you will know it
clearly, if you attend to the narrative with accuracy. For it did not say that
having read the whole book he thus burned it; but, As he was reading three
pages or four, he cut it out. He did not even wait for the end of the reading,
but from the very prefaces he was at once made savage. For these reasons it
was not safe for the prophets to tell the Jews with clarity all the terrible
things that would be. For if he did not bear to hear a temporary captivity,
how would he have endured to learn the perpetual one? And the king did not
even stop at this; but he sent, he says, to seek the prophet everywhere, but
he did not find him; for God hid him. That man he then hid by a place, but the
other prophets by the obscurity of what was said.

6. And it will be clear to us not from these things only that it was a daring
thing among the Jews, and of a soul that throws itself into danger, to speak
the honor and glory that would come to the nations, and the dishonor that
would succeed those men, but also from the words of Paul. He at any rate,
seeing a certain prophet opening a little of this prediction, and speaking
more clearly than the others, both our =56.173= goods and their evils, was
struck and wondered at him for the daring, speaking thus: But Isaiah is very
bold, and says: I was found by those who did not seek me, I became manifest to
those who did not ask for me. I said, Behold I am, to the nation that did not
call my name. And yet, if danger had not followed from the prediction, for
what reason did Paul say, But Isaiah is very bold, and says: I was found by
those who did not seek me? A very great accusation of the Jews indeed. Those
who did not seek found, and those who sought failed; those who had not heard
believed, and those who had heard crucified. For these reasons he called
Isaiah daring. For it was indeed of very great daring, standing in the midst
of those being accused, to make the accusations unsparingly, and by the
prophecy to cast those men out of the honor plainly, and to bring others into
the glory owed to them. The court of the hearers had all as accusers. And who
among hostile judges can escape danger? Therefore, he says, He is very bold
and says. But I still want to show this to you more clearly. Therefore it has
been said obscurely in the Scriptures about Jews and about us, so that the
Jews might not understand what was said before the time. And for this I bring
as witness the most loud-voiced Paul, the one who speaks from above, the
trumpet of the heavens, the vessel of election, the bridesman

of Christ. For I betrothed you, he says, to one husband, to present a chaste
virgin to Christ. This man I bring you as a witness, saying plainly that for
this reason some of the things in the Old have been shadowed, not all. For if
all were going to be obscure, it would have been said in vain to those then.
For the prophecies have also the temporary wars of that time, and plagues, and
famines; and they also have the things that have come out today, the calling
of the Church, the rejection of the synagogue, the abolition of the law. These
things he did not want them to know; but those things that happened in their
own times he did want. And this itself I will try to show: that he made only
these things obscure, the things about us and about the synagogue, the things
that have now come out, and the abolition of the law; and this they ought not
to have known from then. For if from the beginning they had learned that the
law was temporary, they would certainly have despised it; therefore he
shadowed this only. That not every prophecy was obscure, but this part only
was wrapped, hear Paul clearly indicating both these things to us, both that
the law was shadowed, and that it was according to this part only. For writing
to the Corinthians he speaks somehow thus: Having then such a hope, we use
much boldness, and not as Moses put a veil on his face, so that the sons of
Israel might not gaze at the end of what was being abolished, but their
thoughts were hardened. For until today a veil remains upon the reading of the
Old Testament, not being unveiled, because it is abolished in Christ. Perhaps
what has been said is obscure; so it is necessary to make it clearer,
reminding you of the history itself. For when Moses, having taken the tablets
on the mountain, was about to come down, a certain =56.174= unspeakable and
wonderful glory shone from that holy face, such that none of the many could
approach him and converse. So that he might not be unapproachable to the
people throughout, putting a veil on the face, he allowed the Jews to be with
him without fear. And when speaking with the people he had the veil, but
turning toward God he put it off again. And this happened, both so that the
lawgiver might appear trustworthy to those about to receive the law brought
through him, and so that the type of the truth might be drawn beforehand in
him, and the cause of the economy according to Christ might be drawn from
above. For since some were going to say, For what reason did Christ not come
with the godhead bare, but put on flesh? from above, through the face of the
servant, he answered all these. For if the Jews did not bear to see the glory
of the servant that came upon him later, how would they have endured
thereafter to see the godhead bare?

7. And that veil does not indicate this only to us, but also that the Jews
suffer these same things in the case of the law which they then suffered in
the case of the face of Moses. For just as they did not see the glory of the
lawgiver's face, that curtain having been put in between, so neither can they
now look down upon the glory of the law. And this is necessary for us also
against heretics. For thinking what was said to be an accusation of the law,
they accepted this passage of the Epistle; and when they heard that the law
has a veil, and that it is abolished, thinking it to be a slander, they left
the letters, so that they might be caught by their own attempts. For this very
thing shows the law to be great. For just as then it was not an accusation of
Moses to have the veil on the face, but a weakness of the Jews, and it was
even a very great praise of Moses, that he had such glory on the face as to
need a curtain toward his fellow servants: so it also happened in the case of
the law. For if the law had not had an unapproachable glory, it would not have
needed a veil. When then he says that A veil remains upon the reading of the

Old Testament, he means its obscurity; and when he says, Not being unveiled,
because it is abolished in Christ, the part which is obscure, the law showed
us. For that part of the law was not obscure which contributed to our life and
constitution; since it would have been given in vain; but only those parts
were shadowed through which we could learn that it is abolished through
Christ. For this too is a work of the wisdom of God, that the law itself
should be brought in saying about itself that Christ coming will abolish it,
and that in him it will cease. This very part of the law then, the one saying
that the law is abolished in Christ, this only was obscure. And making this
itself clear the great Paul added: Not being unveiled, because it is abolished
in Christ. For lest, hearing that A veil remains upon the reading of the Old
Testament =56.175=, you should think the whole of it obscure and shadowed, by
the addition he corrected this suspicion. For having said, A veil remains upon
the reading of the Old Testament, he added, Not being unveiled, because it is
abolished in Christ. This itself, he says,

was not unveiled, that it was going to be abolished in Christ; and it was not
unveiled to those who have not come to the faith; since the one who has come,
and has enjoyed the grace of the Holy Spirit, no longer looks at the law with
a curtain, but sees its glory bare. And the glory of the law is to be able to
teach that it is abolished in Christ, so that you may learn this too. Did you
see the glory of the law? And the true glory of that is when it can train you
toward Christ; and it trains, whenever it shows him being abolished. So that
from this too a mortal blow has come to the heretics. For if the law were
contrary and an enemy of Christ, and not given by him, Paul ought not to have
called it his glory, that it could teach those who approach it that it is
abolished through Christ. If again the law were wicked, its veil ought not to
have been taken away, but it ought to have remained shadowed even after the
grace. But if this is a work of the grace, to make those who approach more
clear-sighted toward the grasp of the law, so as to take from there all the
beginnings and starting points of the faith toward Christ: what greater proof
than this of the kinship of the law with the grace could anyone name, except
that when Christ opens the eyes of those who approach him, so that they can
look down upon the leading of the law, that leading, having appeared and
become clear, will be able with much ease to send those who understand on to
the things of Christ? For these things show neither Christ fighting with the
law, nor the law warring against Jesus, but the whole contrary, the one making
a way beforehand for this great philosophy, the other taking them from there
and leading them to the highest peak. And for all these things let us give
thanks to the God who loves mankind, who manages each thing at the fitting
time, and in many ways works our salvation, and let us show a way of life
worthy, according to our power, of his love of mankind and of so great a
providence, so that we may also obtain the goods to come: which may it be that
we all obtain, by the grace and love of mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ,
through whom and with whom to the Father, together with the Holy Spirit, be
glory, honor, power, unto the ages of ages. Amen.


Still on the obscurity of the Old Testament, and on the love of God toward
mankind, and concerning not accusing one another.

1. The herdsman rejoices when he sees the herd of

cattle flourishing for him and in good condition; and the farmer too rejoices,
seeing the cornfields waving; but neither does the farmer rejoice so over the
cornfields, nor the herdsman over the cattle, as I now rejoice and exult,
seeing this fair threshing floor filled with these spiritual sheaves. For when
the word of piety is sown into so many and such hearings, =56.176= it is
necessary that the ear of obedience should quickly sprout, much and ripe. For
when someone cuts a furrow in deep and rich soil, even if he does not cast the
seeds with a lavish hand, he will receive a great crop, the nature of the
place multiplying the scarcity of the seeds through its own power: so also
when someone sows into souls that are obedient and full of reverence, even if
he casts few seeds of teaching, he will see a rich harvest, the wisdom of the
hearers hiding the poverty of the speaker. This happens also in the fishing of
fishes. For even if the fishermen happen to be inexperienced, yet if they cast
the nets into a gulf that has many fishes, they easily succeed in the catch,
the multitude of the fishes running together overshadowing their inexperience.
And if in that hunt of those about to be fished the multitude often comforts
the ignorance of the one who draws the seine, much more will this happen in
this spiritual fishing. For those fishes, when they see the traps thrown in,
at once leap away and jump out; but you do the contrary: when you see him
standing up and spreading the seine of the teaching, not only do you not leap
away and jump out, but you even walk further in, running together from every
side, and each pushing and pressing the neighbor, himself hastens to be first
to leap and fall into the nets. Therefore we have never drawn up the seine
empty, not because of our experience, but because of your desire.
Sufficiently, then, did that tongue which flows with proven and pure gold, and
has fountains of honey in the mouth, feast us even the other day, I mean the
tongue of the blessed Paul; or rather, even hiding the pleasure of every
honeycomb by the sweetness of the spiritual teaching. But since, according to
the philosophy that befits you, you do not even dishonor the things of poor
and beggarly me, but you wonder at the things that have much superiority, and
you also admit the words of our cheapness, I have stood up with eagerness to
pay you the debt which I promised the other day, but did not lay down, the
length of the teaching having struck us away from the end. What then was that
debt? For we must remind you of the beginning of the loan, so that the
explanation may become clearer to you from the subject. We inquired then why
the Old Testament is more obscure than the New; for perhaps you remember; and
we have said one cause so far, the savagery of the hearers, and we brought
Paul as a witness saying that The same veil remains upon the reading of the
Old Testament, not being unveiled, because it is abolished in Christ. We
showed that just as Moses the lawgiver had a veil, so also the law had a veil,
the obscurity; but the veil is neither a charge against the lawgiver, nor an
accusation of the law, but of the weakness of the hearers. For Moses did not
have a veil for himself, but because they could not bear the glory of his
face. When at any rate he turned toward the Lord, the veil was taken away. So
also the law, since it could not yet learn the perfect teachings full of
philosophy, both those about Christ and about the New Testament (for all those
things were stored, as in a treasury, in the =56.177= old Scripture), had a
veil, condescending to those men, but keeping all the wealth for us, so that
when Christ is present, and we turn to him, the veil may be taken away. Do you
see then to how great a dignity the

coming of Christ has brought us, leading us up into the rank of Moses. But
perhaps someone will say: And for what reason was it said then, if what was
said was not going to be clear to them?

So that it might become useful to those after these things. For this is the
dignity of prophecy, not when it reports present matters, but when it
proclaims beforehand the things to come. And a prophecy, when it is said in
shadow, becomes clearer after the outcome of the facts, but before the outcome
not at all. So that, since it was then said in shadow, it was obscure; but
when the facts have come out, for this reason what was said was clearer. And
so that you may learn that a prophecy, even if it is said long before the
time, but is said in shadow, becomes more obscure, waiting for the outcome
from the facts, I will make this plain to you from the disciples themselves.
Destroy this temple, Christ was saying to the Jews. For when he was driving
out those who were insulting the temple through the trading, those men say:
What sign do you show us, that you do these things? And to this he says:
Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it. But he was speaking
about the temple of his body. This is a certain prophecy: for the cross had
not yet come out, nor the destruction of the temple, nor the three-day
resurrection, which he himself accomplished. And see how he riddled both
things with accuracy, both their daring, and his own power. Yet they did not
know what was said. And that the Jews were ignorant is no wonder; but he says
that not even his disciples understood, until he rose from the dead; And then
they believed the Scripture, and the word which Jesus said.

2. Do you see that they needed the outcome through the facts, so that the
prophecy might become clearer, and that the Jews had no charge for not
understanding those prophecies about Christ before the coming of Christ? For
by the coming they were going to become evident and clear. Hear Christ himself
saying: If I had not come, and spoken to them, they would not have sin. Why
would they not have sin, if the prophecies foretold? Because they foretold,
but they were not clear, nor evident before the coming of the one foretold.
For if they were even then clear and evident to them, it is plain that even
before his coming they had sin; but if they did not have sin, it is quite
clear that it was because of their obscurity and because what was said was
shadowed. For they were not required to have the faith toward Christ before
the coming of Christ. For what reason then was it foretold then? So that when
he is present they may have the teachers from home leading them on, and may
know that the things happening are not a novelty, nor is the economy recent,
but these things were proclaimed beforehand from above and many years before;
and this is no small thing toward drawing them to the faith. This then is one
cause of the obscurity, which we also showed through more testimonies in the
former discourse. So that we may not again trouble you saying the same things,
we must leave this, and say another which makes the Old Testament not obscure
and unknown, but more difficult for us. For it is one thing to know nothing at
all of what is contained, and to see a veil ly56.178 ing upon it, and another
to find what is said, but to find it with toil. What then is the second cause,
through which the Old Testament is more difficult than the New? We do not have
the Old Testament written among us in the native voice; but it was composed in
one tongue, and we have it being read in another. For it was written at the
beginning in the voice of the Hebrews, and we received it in the tongue of the
Greeks; and when a tongue is interpreted into another tongue, it has much
difficulty. And those who are experienced in many tongues know accurately how
it is not possible, when we change into the other tongue, to carry over all
the clarity of the voice that lies in its own nature. This

then has been the cause of the difficulty in the Old Testament. For three
hundred years before the coming of Christ, in the time of Ptolemy the king of
the Egyptians, the Old Testament was carried over into the Greek tongue, very
usefully and necessarily. For as long as it was discoursing with one nation of
the Jews, it remained in the Hebrew voice. For no one then was going to attend
to it, the rest of the race of men having been driven into extreme savagery.
But when Christ was going to be present, and to call the whole world to
himself, not through the apostles only, but also through the prophets (for
they too lead us by the hand to the faith of the knowledge of Christ), then at
last, as if certain entrances and roads, the prophecies, formerly shut by the
obscurity of the tongue, he prepared to be opened from every side through the
interpretation, so that all who flow together from the nations from
everywhere, and traveling these roads with much ease, might through them be
able to come to the king of the prophets, and to worship the only-begotten Son
of God. Therefore all were interpreted before the time of the coming of
Christ; since if they had remained in the Hebrew dialect only, and David was
saying, Ask of me, and I will give you nations as your inheritance, and the
ends of the earth as your possession, from where was the Syrian going to know
what was said, or the Galatian, or the Macedonian, or the Athenian, if the
Scripture remained in obscurity? Again Isaiah cried: He was led as a sheep to
slaughter, and as a lamb before its shearer is voiceless. And again: There
shall be the root of Jesse, and he who rises to rule nations; upon him nations
will hope. And again: The earth will be filled with knowing the Lord, as much
water to cover seas. And David again was saying: God has gone up with a shout,
the Lord with the voice of a trumpet; and again: The Lord said to my Lord: Sit
at my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool of your feet.

3. Since then the Old Testament foretold about the passion, and about the
resurrection, and about the ascension, and about the sitting at the right
hand, and about his second coming, and about all these things simply that lie
in the =56.179= New, so that these things might not remain unknown to the
nations that were going to be after these things, and they might be ignorant
of the force of the prophecy, the grace of God managed that the Scriptures
should be interpreted before the coming of Christ, making them useful not only
to those from the nations, but also to those of the Jews scattered everywhere
in the world who had thereafter thrown off the Hebrew tongue. For see, the one
from the nations believed, seeing the signs of the Jews. How again were the
apostles to bring over the Jew, if they were not going to furnish him the
prophet as a teacher from home? For if Paul, going into Athens, needed an
inscription carved on an altar, and made the teaching toward them from that,
expecting to take them more easily with their own weapons, which also
happened: much more, discoursing with Jews, he needed the alliance from the
prophets, so that those men too might not charge that he was bringing some new
and strange things into their hearings. And why, someone says, was there not
one voice, and we would have been released from all the difficulty? There was
one voice of old, O man, and just as there was one nature of men, there was
also one voice of all. There was not a difference of tongue from the
beginning, there was not a difference of voice, there was not an Indian, nor a
Thracian, nor a Scythian, but all conversed in one tongue. What then has been
the cause? someone says. We appeared unworthy of this one voice, we who are
always ungrateful toward the benefactor. What are you saying? We appeared
unworthy of a voice? And the irrational creatures all have their own voice:
sheep bleat, goats bleat, a bull bellows, a horse neighs, a lion roars, a wolf
howls, a serpent hisses; each of the

irrational creatures has kept its own sound, and I alone have been deprived of
my own voice? And the wild and the tame, both the tractable and the untamed
animals, each remained having the voice allotted to them from the beginning;
and I their ruler have been dishonored? and for them the honors are unmoved,
and I have fallen from the gifts of God? And what so great thing have I
sinned? Were the former punishments not enough? He gave me paradise, and he
cast me out of paradise; I was living a life without hardship and released
from toils, he condemned me to sweat and labor; the earth provided me all
things unsown and untilled, he ordered it to bring up thorns and thistles, and
he turned me back to it again; he punished me with death; he punished the race
of women by subjecting them to pangs and toils. Were these not enough for
punishment, but he also cast me out of the voice, and deprived me of this
honor, so that I turn away from those of the same tribe and the same race as
if they were savage, the voice walling off association from me? I have
increased the objection for this reason, so that when I bring on the solution
the victory may become more brilliant. If he wanted, someone says, to cast me
out of all these things, for what reason did he give them to me at the
beginning? Do you want me to bring on the solution from this very thing, from
the bare contradiction itself? For so great is the abundance of the
justifications on behalf of God, that the contradiction of the one who
objects, taking nothing from us, suffices for the solution of the charges. If
he wanted to cast me out of all these things, for what reason did he give them
to me at =56.180= the beginning? For I too say the same: if he wanted to cast
you out of all these things, for what reason did he give them? So that, since
he did not want to cast you out, therefore he gave you these things at the
beginning. What then happened? God did not cast you out, but you destroyed
what was given. Wonder at him for the love of mankind, that he granted; blame
yourself for the slackness, that you did not keep the gift. So that it is
clear that not the one who deposited is the cause, but the one who betrayed
the deposit is liable to charges. For that he loved, and was loving toward
man, and wanted to grant, he showed, with no one compelling, nor putting on
force, not wondering at your successes, not having a return of toils to give
you; but at once having formed you, at once he transferred you into this rule,
so that he might show that the gift was not a repayment of a return, but the
grace was bare. But if you did not keep what was given, blame yourself, not
the one who gave the gift. Do we then have only this to say on behalf of the
Master? This apology then is sufficient; but his boundless goodness and his
unspeakable love of mankind furnishes us also another abundance of
justifications. For it is not only this that one can say, that he gave, and
you destroyed; for even thus the giver has been released from the charges, or
rather he is even worthy of very great wonder, that even knowing beforehand
that you would destroy, he did not deprive you of the gift; yet I have also
another thing much greater than these to say. And what is this? That even
after you destroyed from your own slackness, he has given you back again what
was lost; or rather not only what was lost, but also things much greater than
these. For you lost paradise, and he gave you heaven. Do you see how the trade
is greater than the loss, how the wealth is more? He gave you heaven, both so
that he might show his own love of mankind, and so that he might bite the
devil, showing that even if he plot ten thousand times against the race of
men, nothing more will come to him, God always leading us up to a greater
honor. You lost paradise then, and God opened heaven to you; you were
condemned to temporary toil, and you were honored with eternal life; he
ordered the earth to bring thorns and thistles, and the soul sprouted for you
a fruit of the Spirit.

4. And consider with me the love of God toward mankind, to how far it came
down. Among those who lose some of their own possessions, even if they receive
greater and more costly things, those lost things

they are eager to seek, and not to be satisfied before, until they receive
them back. Since then you lost paradise, he has not given you heaven only, but
both paradise and heaven. Today you will be with me in paradise, he says: so
that he may comfort the pained soul not only by the addition of the greater
things, but also by the recovery of what was lost. But, if it seems good, let
us come to this present subject itself, and let us see how we lost the voice.
For the history is no small thing toward a word of safety. For the one who has
learned the manner of the former safety will be safer toward the rest. And we
must say all things to you: for example, that of old there was one voice of
all men, that later it fell into many; until when it was one, and when it fell
into many; and whether that one itself vanished, and others were brought in,
or while that one remained the rest were intro56.181 duced; why it was
confused, and from what cause; then in which of these many voices the Old
Testament was composed; for it was because of that that we moved all these
things; whether in the ancient and older, or in those brought in later. But do
not fear: for even if we cannot pay all today, yet we will certainly pay you
later. For what reason then, not going to pay all today, have we read you the
subject of all the debts? So that, expecting the payment, you may have us
continually in mind. For the one who has lent gold to someone, and has him
liable for the debt, both at table, and in the house, and in the marketplace,
and on the bed, and everywhere imagines and dreams the one who owes him; and
the love of the money, together with the money, makes that man too be carried
about continually upon the soul of the lender. So that we too by the hope of
the payment may be continually in your thoughts, both in the house, and in the
marketplace, and wherever you are, for this reason we have confessed the
debts, but we do not make the whole payment today, so that by the expectation
of what remains we may leave among you a subject of memory concerning us. For
it is a great safety for us to enjoy your love continually, of such and so
great a people. For the one who enjoys love will certainly also enjoy prayer.
And how great a good this is, is clear from there: that Paul, the one caught
up to the third heaven, the one who heard unspeakable words, the one who
trampled all the necessities of nature, the one who was thereafter in perfect
safety, needed also the prayer of the disciples, and said: Pray for me, that I
may be delivered from those who are disobedient; and again, Pray, that a word
may be given me in the opening of my mouth. And everywhere you see him both
asking the prayers of the disciples, and giving thanks to them after
receiving. For lest someone say that through humility he takes refuge in the
prayers of the disciples, he shows their power also, speaking thus: Who
delivered us from so great deaths; and we have hoped that he will also
deliver, you also helping together by the petition for us, so that the gift
toward us may be thanked for through many on our behalf. And if the prayer of
the multitude released Paul from dangers, how ought we not to expect to reap
great things also from this protection? For since when we pray by ourselves we
are weak, but when we are gathered we become stronger, we put God to shame by
the multitude and by the alliance. So also a king often, having sent someone
to death, did not nod to one man begging about the condemned, but was put to
shame by a whole city entreating, and through the multitude of those
entreating he snatched from condemnation the one being led to the pit, and
brought him back to life. So great is the power of the supplication of the
multitude. Therefore here too we are all gathered, so that we may draw God
more greatly into pity. For since when we pray by ourselves, as I said before,
we are weak, from the

bond of love we put God to shame to give us what we ask. And I do not say
these things simply, nor for myself only, but so that you may always hasten to
the assemblies, so that you may not say: What then, can I not pray in the
house? You can pray, but the prayer does not have so much power as when
=56.182= it happens with one's own members, as when the whole body of the
Church sends up the petition with one mind with one voice, priests being
present, and offering up the prayers of the common multitude.

5. Do you want to learn how great is the power of the prayer that happens in
the church? Peter was once bound in the prison, and many chains were about
him; But prayer was being made intensely by the Church concerning him, and at
once it released him from the prison. What then could be more powerful than
this prayer, which helped the pillars and towers of the Church? For Paul and
Peter are towers and pillars of the Church; and it loosed the bonds of the
one, and opened the mouth of the other. And so that we may show the power as
double, not only from the things that happened then, but also from the things
accomplished each day, let us remind you of the prayer itself that happens
from the people. And yet if one of the many of you ordered a man to pray by
himself for the safety of the bishop, each would decline, as the burden being
greater than his power; but in common all, hearing the deacon commanding this
and saying, Let us pray for the bishop, and for old age, and for help, and
that he may cut the word of truth rightly, both for those here, and for those
everywhere, you do not decline to do the command, but with intensity you offer
up the prayer, knowing the power of your assembly. Those who have been
initiated know what is said; for this has not yet been entrusted to the prayer
of the catechumens, because they have not yet arrived at this boldness; but
you are exhorted by the one who ministers these things to make the petitions
both for the world, and for the Church stretched to the ends of the earth, and
for all the bishops who administer it, and you obey with eagerness, bearing
witness by deed that great is the power of the prayer offered up in church
from the people in agreement. But let us return to the present subject, that
of old there was one voice. From where then is it clear that there was one
voice? And the whole earth, it says, was one lip. What has been said is
obscure. Does the earth have a lip? By no means. What then does it say, and
about what? It does not speak about this earth that is without sense, that is
unmoved; but it called the common race of men thus, reminding them of their
own nature, bringing them to a reminder of the mother from which they came.
For this animal is double, I mean man, composed of two essences, one
perceptible, the other intelligible, I mean soul and body, and having kinship
both in heaven and on earth. For through the intelligible essence he shares
with the powers above, and through the perceptible he is joined to the things
of the earth, being a certain exact bond of either creation. When then he does
something of the things that seem good to God, he is called spiritual, and he
is not even named from the soul, but from another greater honor, from the
working of the Spirit. For the soul is not even enough for us toward
successes, if we do not enjoy that help. And so that you may learn that not
even the soul is enough for us toward successes; and =56.183= why do I say,
toward successes? not even toward being able to understand what is said; The
natural man, it says, does not receive the things of the Spirit. For just as
he calls fleshly the one who is a slave to the flesh, so he calls natural the
one who entrusts the matters to human reasonings, and does not receive the
working of the Spirit. But as I was saying, that when we succeed we are called
spiritual; but when we sin and are tripped and do something unworthy of our

nobility, he calls us from the cheap nature, naming us earth. Since then here
too he is going to accuse some of those who built the tower, of those lifted
up into arrogance, of those who took a thought greater than their own worth,
and he is going to accuse them of arrogance, therefore he called them from the
cheap essence saying: And the whole earth was one lip. And so that you may
learn that he calls us thus when we sin, he called Adam thus after the sin
saying: You are earth, and to earth you will go; and yet he was not earth
only, but also had an immortal soul. For what reason then did he call him
earth? Because he sinned. When at any rate he was forming him, he did not call
him thus; but what? Let us make man according to our image and likeness; and
let them rule the fishes of the sea, and the beasts of the earth. And their
fear and their trembling shall be upon all the earth. Do you see what the
privileges of the nature are? what the honors? what the praises? But before
the sin; and after the sin thereafter, You are earth, and to earth you will
go. And hear also Malachi riddling this, or rather God through the prophet:
For behold I send you, he says, Elijah the Tishbite. For what reason then does
he send? So as to turn the heart of a father toward a son. For since that
fearful and awful court is going to stand over, so that the judge, taking some
without defense under the accounts, may not condemn them; so that that man
coming and saying beforehand that the coming is near at the doors may make men
more sober; for the things said many years before are accustomed to fall into
neglect; renewing our memory then that prophet comes. But that those who sin
are called earth, this must be shown now.

6. Having said then, So that he may turn the heart of a father toward a son,
he added: Lest coming I strike the earth utterly; and he strikes those who
sin. Do you see those who sin called earth? Again elsewhere the prophet,
narrating about Christ, spoke thus: He will be girded with righteousness about
his loin, and grasped with truth about his sides; not that there is a side and
a loin about God; for the Divine is bodiless; but through these he indicates
to us the unbribable and undeceived judgment of the judge; and that nowhere
thereafter are there slanderers, nor those who spite, nor the corruption
through money, nor ignorance of what is just. For in these courts both the one
not liable is punished, and the one liable escapes; for justice has been
corrupted in many places; but when the just and undeceived judge is present,
the one girded with righteousness about his loin, and wrapped with truth about
his sides, all will obtain the just things with accuracy. And =56.184= he will
strike the earth with the word of his mouth. And so that you may learn that he
does not speak about the earth, but about sinners, he added, that And with the
breath through his lips he will slay the ungodly. Do you see that here too he
called the sinners earth? Knowing these things then, when you hear that The
whole earth was one lip, think again of the human nature; for he reminds us of
our own cheapness; for it is a great good to inspect one's own kinship, and to
know from where we have been put together. This inspection of the nature is a
sufficient teaching of humility; it can settle all the passions, and make a
calm in the mind. Therefore someone was exhorting saying: Attend to yourself;
reckon up your nature and your construction, and this is enough for you toward
being settled continually. Therefore that righteous Abraham had this reasoning
in himself continually, and never thought highly. He at any rate who was
discoursing with God, and enjoying so great a boldness, and having been
witnessed to by him for virtue, said: But I am earth and ashes. And another

wishing again to settle the puffed-up man does not move long discourses, but
reminds him of the nature only, and rebukes him fiercely, speaking thus: Why
is earth and ashes proud? Do you tell me the things that appear after death?
Settle him while he is living. He does not know now that he is earth and
ashes. He sees the comeliness of the body, he sees the power, the attendance
of flatterers, the parasites following. He wears costly garments, he has been
clothed with a great bulk of office, the fantasy deceives him and makes him
forget the nature. We know that we are earth and ashes; but we who are sober;
but that man does not wait for the proof from the end, nor does he come to the
coffins and the tombs of the ancestors, but he looks toward the present
things, and thinks nothing of the things to come. Teach him already from here
that he is earth and ashes. Wait for me, he says, and I do not teach this, but
something much cheaper; so that when he imagines, he may recognize his own
cheapness; so that while living he may take the medicine. For having said, Why
is earth and ashes proud? he added: Because even in his life his inwards have
been set at nought. What is, Because even in his life his inwards have been
set at nought? Perhaps what has been said is obscure. By inwards he means the
entrails, he means the bowels, the belly full of filth and much uncleanness
and stench, not accusing the nature, but leading on to humility; Because even
in his life they have been set at nought. Did you see the cheapness and
perishableness of the essence? Do not wait for the day of the end, so that you
may learn your weakness; while the man is still living for me, search him, and
pass through into his entrails by the word, and you will see all his
nothingness. But do not fall; for God did not make us thus hating us, but
sparing us, furnishing us great pretexts of humility. For if a man being earth
and ashes dared to say that I will go up into heaven; if he did not have the
bridle of the nature, where would he not have fallen in his mind? When then
you see someone puffed up, =56.185= stretching the neck, drawing up the brows,
being carried on the chariot, threatening, throwing into prison, handing over
to death, doing spite, say to him: Why is earth and ashes proud? Because even
in his life his inwards have been set at nought. These things one can say not
about a private man only, but also about the very one sitting upon the royal
throne. For do not look at the purple, nor the diadem, nor the golden
garments, but search the nature itself, and you will see it having nothing
more than the many; or rather, if you wish, go round also the purple, and the
diadem, and the garments, and all that fantasy, and of these very things you
will see again the earth being the subject. For all the glory of man is as the
flower of grass. See, that whole adornment appeared even cheaper than earth.
Do you see how it settles the puffing? how the thought of the nature itself
takes down all arrogance? It is enough only to reckon who we are, and from
where we have been put together, and all arrogance of reasonings will be gone.
For this is why God constructed us from two essences, so that when you are
lifted up into arrogance, the cheapness of the flesh may settle you; and when
you think something unworthy and ignoble of the honor given you from God, the
nobility of the soul may lead you up toward the zeal of the heavenly powers.

7. And the inspection of the nature is fitted not only toward the taking away
of arrogance, but even if some other desire troubles, whether that of money,
or that of bodies, this unseemly one that calls out into wantonness, this is
enough for a comfort of the passion. When then you see a well-formed woman,
having a shining eye, bright, gleaming from the cheek, bearing a certain
impossible bloom upon the face, inflaming your

reasoning, and increasing the desire, think that what is wondered at is earth,
that what kindles is ashes, and the soul raving will stop; uncover the skin of
her face, and then you will see all the cheapness of the well-formedness; do
not stop at the surface, but pass further in with the reasoning, and you will
find nothing else but bones and sinews and veins. But these are not enough?
Reckon her for me being altered, growing old, being sick, eyes sinking, and
cheeks hollowing, that whole flower flowing down; think what you wonder at,
and be ashamed of your judgment. You wonder at clay and ashes, and dust
kindles you and cinder. I do not say these things accusing the nature; God
forbid; not blaming, nor throwing into cheapness, but preparing medicines for
those who are sick. Therefore God made her such, so cheap, both so that he
might show his own power, and the care concerning us, leading on to humility
by the cheapness of the nature, and settling all our desire, and showing his
own wisdom, as being able even in clay to work out so great a beauty. So that
when I cheapen the essence, then I uncover the art of the Creator. For just as
we wonder at a sculptor more, not when he shows us the statue beautiful from
gold, but when from clay, the material lying underneath, he works the image
complete and accurate: so also we are struck and glorify the =56.186=
master-craftsman God, because in ashes and clay he deposited in our bodies an
impossible beauty and a showing of a certain unspeakable wisdom. And he did
this not in our body only, but also in all the creation. For having brought in
cheap essences in many places, he both deposited a clear sample of his own
art, and mixed in a certain refutation of the weakness of the essences; so
that through the art and the beauty you may wonder at the Creator, and through
the weakness and the cheapness of the nature and of the essence you may not
worship the things that have come to be. Bright is the sun shining, and it
lights up the whole world; but when night has come it fails. For what, someone
says, is brighter than the sun? But this too fails; and not in night only, but
also in day. And therefore often even in day the sun has failed, so that you
may wonder at the Creator through the art, but may not worship what has come
to be through the weakness. Do you see this heaven, how great a body it is?
how beautiful? how it gleams, and is better than our bodies according to the
surface? But it is without soul. Did you see both the showing of the art, and
the refutation of the weakness? do you see the fortifications lying for you
from either side? For so that you may not condemn the Creator as weak, he made
them beautiful; and so that you may not worship the things that have come to
be as gods, he worked these weak in part. Remember these things continually.
Therefore we interpret Scriptures, not so that you may learn Scriptures only,
but so that you may also correct the manners. For if this does not happen, we
read in vain, we explain in vain. For just as an athlete going into a
wrestling-school, being anointed and enjoying the hands of the trainer, if
when the contest has come he refutes that art, was brought into the
wrestling-school in vain: so if coming here, and learning all the holds and
the grips of the devil, when the time of the contests has come you are
tripped, either seeing a well-formed face, or being lifted up into arrogance,
or being mastered by some other wicked reasoning, you came in here in vain.
Remember then the words, not those against our nature, but those against the
unchaste desire. What has been said is not a charge of nature, but what is
said is of desire. With this settle anger, thus soothe desire, thus pull down
arrogance. And the whole earth was one lip, and one voice for all. See, the
thing sought is still here. The discourse is not about this, but that all men

had one voice. And why did he call the voice one lip? It is a custom of
Scripture to call the words thus, a tongue. And we must know this too because
of the heretics, who accuse the creation of God, who say that the body is
wicked. For since Scripture calls the wicked motions of the mind by the
members of the body; I mean something of this kind: They sharpened their
tongue like a serpent; Their tongue is a sharp sword; some then think that it
has been said about the tongue. But it has not been said about the tongue; God
forbid; for it is a work of God; but about the murderous words, those that
kill men, those that fall more ter56.187 rible than a sword. Their tongue is a
sharp sword; and again: Deceitful lips in heart, and in heart he spoke evils;
not speaking about the member of the body, but about the underhanded words. So
also here, having said, And the whole earth was one lip, he did not teach
this, that all men had one lip, but he called the voice a lip. For having
said, The whole earth was one lip, he added, And one voice for all. Thus when
he says, Their throat is an opened tomb, he does not accuse the throat, but
the wicked words that go up from there, the dead doctrines. For a tomb is
this, a receptacle of dead bones and bodies. Such also are the mouths of those
who accuse the Creator; such are the mouths of men who utter shameful things,
of those who revile, of those who throw out stinking and wicked words from
their own throat.

8. Fill it with fragrance, O man, not with stench; make it a royal treasury,
not a satanic tomb. And if it is a tomb, at least shut it, so that the stench
may not go forward. Do you have wicked thoughts? Do not bring them out through
words; let them lie below, and they are quickly choked. We are men, often
planning many wicked and unseemly and shameful plans; but let us not allow the
thoughts to go forward into words, so that being pressed below they may become
weaker and perish. For just as if someone, having thrown all kinds of wild
beasts into a pit, if he blocks the pit from above, he chokes them easily; but
if he furnishes some exit and a small breathing, he gives them much comfort,
and does not let them perish, but makes them more savage: so also it happens
in the case of the wicked reasonings, when they are born within, if we shut
their going out to the outside, we quickly make them vanish; but if we bring
them out through the words, we have both made those stronger, having given
them to breathe through the tongue, and from the practice of shameful words we
quickly fall toward the pits of unseemly deeds. Therefore the prophet did not
simply call it a tomb, but An opened tomb, charging this very thing which I
said. For the one who utters shameful words does not shame himself only, but
also fills those near, and those who associate with him, with much ruin. And
just as if we open the tombs, we will fill the cities with plague: so if the
shameful mouths should be opened without fear, they will fill all who
associate with a most terrible disease. Therefore one must put a door and a
bar and a bridle on the mouth. That there was then one voice has been
sufficiently shown us from these things; and thereafter it is necessary to say
for what reason the many were brought in. But for now let us exercise the more
moral discourse; let us train our tongue to have a bridle, and not to bring
out simply all things from the mind; not to speak evil of the brothers, not to
bite and eat one another. Worse than those who bite the bodies are those who
do this through words. =56.188= Those bite the body with the teeth, this man
bites the soul with the words, wounds the reputation, makes the wound
incurable. Therefore he will receive a greater chastisement and punishment,

inasmuch as he puts in a more terrible bite. And not for this only will the
accuser fall from pardon, but because he has neither a just nor an unjust
pretext to name for his own wickedness. For the other sins, even if they have
irrational causes, yet they have them; for example, the fornicator fulfills
desire, the thief looses poverty, a man-slayer stops anger; the accuser has no
pretext to name. For what abundance of money, tell me, does he put round
himself? what desire does he fulfill? It is nothing else but a matter of envy,
having neither a just nor an unjust cause. Therefore he has been deprived of
all pardon. Do you want to speak evil? I give you a useful subject. Do you
want to speak badly? Speak your sins badly. For say you, it says, your sins
first, so that you may be justified. Did you see an evil-speaking that has a
crown and praise and righteousness? Again, A righteous man is an accuser of
himself in first speech; of himself, not of another. If you become an accuser
of another, you have been punished; if of yourself, you have been crowned. And
so that you may learn how great a good it is to accuse one's own fall, A
righteous man is an accuser of himself in first speech, it says. And yet if
righteous, how an accuser? if an accuser, how righteous? The righteous man is
not liable to accusation. But so that you may learn that even if someone is a
sinner, but accuses the sins, through the accusation he becomes righteous,
therefore it said, A righteous man is an accuser of himself in first speech.
And what is, In first speech? Attend accurately: In the courts there are two
parts, of those bringing suit, and of those being sued; of those accusing, and
of those being accused; of those liable, and of those not liable; and the
first speech is given to the accuser, to the one not liable. But here the
contrary. You the one liable, snatch the first speech, so that you may become
not liable; do not wait for the accuser. Even if you are of those liable, yet
before you hear any such thing from that man, yourself accuse what has been
sinned. The tongue is a sharpened sword; but let us not bring wounds upon
others, but let us cut out our own rottenness. Do you want to learn that it
was a custom of the righteous not to speak badly of others, but of themselves?
Hear Paul crying: I thank Christ who strengthened me, that he counted
me faithful, putting me into a ministry, the one who was formerly a
blasphemer, and a persecutor, and an insolent man. Do you see how he speaks
badly of himself? And again, Christ came into the world to save sinners, of
whom I am first. And again elsewhere, I am not worthy, he says, to be
called an apostle, because I persecuted the Church of God.

9. Did you see how everywhere he speaks badly of himself? For he knows the
gain of this accusation, that it works righteousness. When then he must accuse
himself =56.189=, he uses the accusation unsparingly; but when he also sees
others judging the evils of others, see with how much austerity he stops their
mouths, speaking thus: So do not judge anything before the time, until the
Lord comes, who will also light the hidden things of the darkness, and will
make manifest the counsels of the hearts. Keep the court for that one, who
knows the unspeakable things of the mind. And even if you think you know
accurately the things of the neighbor, your judgment has failed. For who
knows, it says, the things of the man, except the spirit of the man that is in
him? How many of the rejected and cheap have it to shine brighter than the
sun! how many of the great and conspicuous will be found being ashes and a
whitened tomb! You heard how Paul speaks badly of himself, with intensity and
excess of sins, of which he was not going to undergo accounts, remembering
them continually. For he was a blasphemer and a persecutor and an insolent man
before the baptism, and baptism loosed those sins; yet he remembers them, not
because he was going to undergo a word for them, but so that he might show the
love of God toward mankind, and what sort of man he was, what sort he made
him, from

a persecutor making an apostle. And if that man remembers the sins that have
vanished, much more must we remember those after the baptism. For what apology
will there be for us, or what pardon, when he remembers continually those for
which he is not liable, and we do not even remind ourselves of those for which
we are going to undergo a word, but leaving our own, we busy ourselves about
the evils of others? [Hear Peter saying: Go out from me, because I am a sinful
man.] Hear how Matthew also accuses his former life, and calls himself a tax
collector, and is not even ashamed, parading the former life. For since they
had nothing to accuse themselves of after the baptism, they remembered the
former things, training us to make no account of the evils of others, but to
care for our own and to turn them over continually. For there is not, there is
not any other such medicine toward the taking away of sins, as the continual
remembrance of them, and the perpetual accusation of them. Thus the tax
collector was able to put off the countless evils, that he might say: God, be
merciful to me the sinner; thus the Pharisee fell from all righteousness,
because leaving off reckoning the things he himself had offended, he was
condemning the world, saying: I am not as the rest of men, rapacious and
greedy, nor as this tax collector. Therefore Paul also was exhorting saying:
And let each prove his own work, and then he will have the boast toward
himself only, and not toward the other. Do you want to learn also in the Old
how the righteous accused themselves? Hear these too speaking in harmony with
those. For David was saying: My lawless deeds have gone over my head; as a
heavy burden they were heavy upon me. And Isaiah cried: Woe is me the
wretched, because being a man I also have unclean lips. And the three children
spending time in a furnace, and handing over the bodies to death because of
God, numbered themselves among the last of the sinners, We have sinned, we
have been lawless, saying; and yet what was more splen56.190 did than those,
what more pure? For even if they had done some sins, the nature of that flame
wiped them all away. But they did not look toward their own successes, but
they reckoned up the sins. Thus also Daniel after the pit of the lions, after
the countless punishments which he endured, himself also accused himself, and
no one the neighbor. Why ever? Because the one speaking badly of others
provokes the Master. But the one speaking badly of himself propitiates and
reconciles; and he works the righteous man more righteous, and he snatches the
sinner from the charges and makes him worthy of pardon. Knowing these things
then, let us not busy ourselves about the evils of others, but about our own;
let us unfold our conscience, let us remember all our life, let us search each
of the things sinned by us, and let us not only not speak badly of others, but
let us not even hear others speaking badly. For a charge lies for this too,
and a very great punishment; For a vain hearing, it says, you shall not
receive. It did not say, Do not believe a vain hearing, but, Do not even
receive it; block the ears, wall off the entrance to the accusation, show that
you the hearer are no less an enemy and a foe to the one speaking badly than
the one being accused. Imitate the prophet speaking thus: The one who slanders
his neighbor in secret, this man I chased out. He did not say that I did not
believe, nor that I did not receive what was said, but I even drove him away,
as an enemy and a foe.

10. But there are some who comfort themselves with a cold comfort, speaking
thus: Lord, do not set this sin down to me, because I owe a word of hearing.
What need of the apology? what need of this pardon? Be silent, and you have
been released from the charges; say nothing, and you are clean of the agony.
Why do you weave troubles for yourself, both with God, and with men? Why do
you make yourself liable to charges? why the burden

heavier do you bind? Is it not enough for you to undergo accounts about your
own evils, but you also heap other sins upon yourself? This apology is
superfluous; you do not owe a word of hearing, but you owe a word of
accusation. For when having heard you are not silent, you owe not only a word
of the hearing, but also of accusation. For from your words you will be
justified, it says, and from your words you will be condemned. These things I
say and testify fearing not on behalf of those who are spoken of badly, but on
behalf of those who speak badly. For the one who has been spoken of badly has
suffered no spite, nor been harmed; but if the things said about him are
false, he even has a reward; and if they are true, not even so has he suffered
any spite from your accusation. For the judge will not then bring the verdict
for him from your reviling. And if one must also say something wonderful, he
will even gain the greatest things by bearing the blasphemy nobly, as indeed
the tax collector also; but the one speaking badly, whether he speaks the
neighbor badly with true things, has been most greatly wronged. And that the
slanderer has perished, there is not even need of a proof; and that, even if
he speaks true things, he makes the court more burdensome for himself,
parading the misfortunes of the neighbor, and becoming a cause of scandals,
uncovering to all what one ought to hide, and proclaiming the sins =56.191= of
the neighbor, is surely clear to everyone. For if someone who has scandalized
one will give an inescapable punishment, the one who scandalizes ten thousand
through the wicked rumor, how great a chastisement will he not undergo? For
the Pharisee was not lying, but was speaking true things, calling the tax
collector a tax collector, yet he paid the penalty. Knowing these things then,
beloved, let us flee accusing. For there is neither a more terrible, nor an
easier sin than this. For what reason? Because it is done more swiftly than
every lawlessness, and quickly snatches the one who does not attend. For the
other sins need both time, and expense, and delay, and helpers, and often in
the spending of the time they are cut off. I mean something of this kind:
Someone chose to murder, someone chose to seize and to be greedy; he needs
much business, and in the delaying he often loosed the anger, stood off from
the wicked impulse, dissolved the corrupted mind, did not add the deed to the
plan; but in the case of speaking badly it is not so, but if we are not very
sober, we are easily snatched; and we need neither time, nor delay, nor
expense, nor any business, so as to speak badly; but it is enough only to
choose, and at once the wish has gone out into deed. For it is a tongue that
serves only. Since then the evil is also quick to turn, and the sin easy to
stand round, and the chastisement and the punishment terrible, and there is no
gain, not small, not great, let us flee the disease with much accuracy, and
let us cover the evils of others, and let us not parade them; let us exhort
those who sin, just as the Lord also says: If your brother sins against you,
go, rebuke him between you and him alone. So that the unpublished character of
the rebukes will make the treatment easier =56.192=. Let us not bite, nor eat
the wounds of others; let us not imitate the flies, but let us emulate the
bees. The flies sit in the wounds, and bite; the bees fly upon the flowers.
Therefore these fix honeycombs, and those bring diseases upon whatever bodies
they sit on; and the ones are abominable, and the others longed for and in
good repute. Let us too then prepare the soul to fly upon the meadow of the
virtue of the saints, and let us continually stir the fragrance of the
successes among them, and let us not bite the wounds of the neighbors; but if
we also see some doing this, let us stop their mouths, walling off against
them the fear of the chastisement, reminding them of the kinship toward the
brothers. And if they yield to none of these, let us call them flies, so that
even the reproach of this name may stand off from the wicked practice

them; so that, being released from this evil leisure, they may spend all the
leisure upon the search of their own evils. For thus both those who have
fallen will rise, the memory of the things sinned by them not being paraded,
and those who continually reckon up their own evils will easily put them off,
becoming more hesitant toward the doing of the rest by the memory of the
things sinned, and those who continually look upon the virtue of the saints
will take a very great zeal toward the imitation of the same; and through all
these things, the whole body of the Church having been set right for us, we
shall be able with this fullness to enter into the kingdom of the heavens;
which may it be that we all obtain, by the grace and love of mankind of our
Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom to the Father be the glory,
together with the Holy Spirit, now and always and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
