OF GREGORY BISHOP OF NYSSA ON ECCLESIASTES HOMILY I Ecclesiastes is set before
us for exposition, having the toil of contemplation equal to the greatness of
the benefit. For the proverbial thoughts having already trained the mind
beforehand, of which the dark sayings and the wise utterances and the riddles
and the varied turns of words, as the prologue of that book contains, ... then
for those who have grown toward the more perfect of the lessons the ascent
comes to this lofty =5.278= writing, truly and God-inspired. If then the
proverbial study that prepares us for these lessons is so toilsome and hard to
contemplate, how much toil must one look upon in these lofty thoughts now set
before us for contemplation? For just as those who have worked out the
wrestling-art in the trainer's school strip for greater sweats and toils in
the gymnastic contests, so the proverbial teaching seems to me to be a certain
practice, training and softening our souls toward the ecclesiastical contests.
If then the practice is accomplished with so many sweats and toils, what must
one reckon concerning the contests themselves? Surely even if someone
conceived every hyperbole he would not present worthily in speech how many
toils the stadium of this writing shows to those contending toward the
security of the thoughts through athletic experience, so as not to show the
argument in a fall, but in every wrestling of thought to keep the mind upright
through the truth. But since this too is among the master's commands, that one
must search the scriptures, there is every necessity, even if our mind be
found behind the truth, not reaching the greatness of the thoughts,
nevertheless to accomplish the not seeming to overlook the Lord's commandment
through zeal according to our power about the word. Therefore let us search
the writing set before us =5.279= as we are able. For altogether he who has
given the command to search will also give the power toward this very thing,
as it is written that The Lord will give a word to those who evangelize with
much power. And first let the title of the book be set before us for
contemplation. In every church Moses and the law are read, the prophets, the
psalmody, all the history, and whatever belongs to the old and the new
covenant, all is proclaimed in the churches. How then is this alone
exceptionally adorned with the title of Ecclesiastes? What then is it that we
have supposed about these things? That in all the other writings, both
histories and prophecies, the aim looks also toward certain other things not
very useful to the church. For what does it matter to the church to learn
accurately the disasters of wars, or who have become rulers of nations and
founders of cities, and who colonists of whom, or what kingdoms will appear in
the time that follows, and how many marriages and childbearings have been
carefully recorded, and all such things as can be taught through each writing;
what of such a kind would contend along with the church toward the aim of
piety? But the teaching of this book looks toward the ecclesiastical way of
life alone, =5.280= guiding these things through which one might accomplish
the life in virtue. For the aim of what is said here is to lift the mind above
sense and to persuade it, having left behind everything that appears great and
splendid among the things that are, to look up through the soul toward the
things unattainable by sense-grasp and to take desire of those things which
sense does not reach. And perhaps


also the title looks toward the leader of the church. For the true
Ecclesiastes, who gathers the scattered into one fullness and who assemblies
into one gathering those who have wandered in many places according to the
varied deceits, who else would he be if not the true king of Israel, the Son
of God, to whom Nathanael said that You are the Son of God, you are the king
of Israel? If then these words are of the king of Israel, and this same one is
also Son of God, as the gospel says, then the same one is also named
Ecclesiastes. And perhaps it is not outside what is likely that we refer the
meaning of the title to this sense, so that through this we may learn that the
force of these words is referred to him who through the gospel founded the
church. For Words, he says, of Ecclesiastes son of David. And thus also
=5.281= Matthew in the beginnings of the gospel names him, calling the Lord
son of David. Vanity of vanities, said Ecclesiastes, all things vanity. Vain
is understood as the insubstantial, which has being only in the utterance of
the word; but a thing that exists is not co-indicated by the meaning of the
name, but it is a certain idle and empty sound, brought forth through
syllables in the form of some word, falling in vain upon the hearing without
meaning, such as some people invent in play, whose signification is nothing.
This then is one form of vanity. And another vanity is called the uselessness
of things done with a certain zeal toward no aim, as the buildings of children
in sand and shooting at the stars and hunting the winds and the runner's
contest against his own shadow, when he contends to step upon the crown of the
shadow, and whatever other such thing we find among things done in vain, all
of which are brought under the word of vanity. And often that too is called
vain in common usage, whenever someone looking toward a certain aim and
pursuing each thing with zeal as something profitable for what is set before
him, then when something contrary has come about the toil comes round to no
profit, and then that the zeal has advanced =5.282= upon no accomplishment is
indicated by the word of the vain. For common usage says of such things I
toiled in vain and I hoped in vain and For a vain thing I underwent the many
labors. And that we may not go through each of the things of which the name of
vanity is said in the proper sense, we will take in brief the notion of this
utterance. Vanity is either a word without thought or a thing without profit
or a counsel without substance or a zeal having no end or, in general, the
nonexistent in the case of everything profitable. If then the notion of the
vain has already been understood by us, it would be necessary to examine what
the vanity of vanities means. And perhaps the thought sought would become more
known to us, if we should examine together the scriptural usage in the case of
things understood toward the better. The action of things necessary and
advantageous is named a work by scripture, but the things that surpass among
those pursued, as many as look toward the worship of God itself, are called a
work of works, as the history makes clear, the word showing, I think, through
a certain analogy to us from the work of works, what is more honorable among
the things pursued. For the relation that zeal about works holds toward
idleness in general, the same relation the activity toward the loftier and
more honorable of the things pursued has toward the remaining works. Thus also
something holy is said by scripture; and again =5.283= holy of holies, so that
by equal measure the holy exceeds the unholy in holiness and again the holy of
holies exceeds this, that which is contemplated by hyperbole in
sanctification. What things then we have been taught in the case of the better
word of the scriptural usage, such a form signifying the intensification of
the underlying thought, this we will not miss if we understand also in the
case of the vanity of vanities. For he says that the things appearing among
the things that are are not simply vain, but that they are such according to a
certain hyperbole of the meaning according to the vain, as if someone should
say of the


dead more dead and of the soulless more soulless. And yet the comparative
intensification has no place in such things, but nevertheless it is said by
this word toward the clearness of the excess of what is indicated. As then
there are works of works and holies of holies understood, through which the
superlative indication toward the better is interpreted, so also the vanity of
vanities shows the unsurpassable of the excess in the vain. And let no one
suppose that what is said is an accusation of the creation. For the charge
would pass over to him who made all things, if he who constituted all things
from things that are not should appear to us a craftsman of such things, if
all things should be vanity. =5.284= But since the constitution of man is
double, soul running together with body, and the form of life has been divided
correspondingly to each of the things contemplated in us; for there is one
life of soul and another of body; for the one is mortal and perishable, and
the other impassible and uncorrupted, and this one looks only to the present,
but the aim of the other is stretched to the continuous; since then there is
much difference of the mortal toward the immortal and of the temporary toward
the everlasting, the voice of Ecclesiastes bears toward this, that one must
not look toward this sensible life, which compared with the true life is a
certain nonexistent and insubstantial thing. But no less someone might say
that this argument too is not outside the accusation of the creator, because
both the soul and the body are from him, so that if the life through flesh is
accused, and God is maker of flesh, such blame would <would> have its
reference to him. But these things at any rate will be said by one who has not
yet come outside the flesh nor has accurately peered toward the loftier life.
Since the one educated in the divine mysteries is not at all ignorant that the
life assimilated to the divine nature is proper and according to nature to
humans, but the sense-life carried on through the activity of the sense-organs
has been given to the nature for this, that the =5.285= knowledge of the
things that appear may become a guide of the soul toward the recognition of
the invisible things, as Wisdom says from the greatness and beauty of created
things the generator of all is seen analogously. But human folly did not see
the thing wondered at through the things that appear, but wondered at what it
saw. Since then the activity of the sense-organs is temporary and short-lived,
we learn through this lofty voice that the one looking toward these things
looks at nothing. But the one guided through these toward the understanding of
that which is, and having understood through the things that run past the
standing nature, and having come into comprehension of that which is always
the same, both saw the truly existing good and acquired what he saw; for the
possession of this good is the knowledge. For What surplus, he says, is there
for the man, in which he toils under the sun? He named the life in the body a
toil pursued without profit upon no accomplishment. For What, he says, surplus
is there for the man? that is, what comes about for the soul through the toil
of living for those who live toward the appearance? And in what also is the
life, or what of the appearing =5.286= goods remains in identity? The sun goes
round its own course shining in turn and being darkened, both lighting the air
that lies over us, when it shows itself above earth, and drawing darkness on
through the west. And the earth stands and remains unmoved in the fixed, and
what stands is not moved, and what is moved does not stand, and all things are
shown through the same things in every temporal interval, being changed in
nothing through the change toward the newer. The sea is a receptacle of the
waters flowing together from everywhere, and neither does the inflow cease nor
does the sea increase. What is the aim of the course for the waters always
filling what is not filled? And for what does the sea receive the inflows of
the waters, remaining forever without increase through the addition? These
things he says, so that from the elements themselves, in which the life of
humans is, he may interpret beforehand the insubstantiality of the things
pursued among us. For if this stretched course of the sun has no bound, nor
does the succession in turn of light and


darkness receive a standing, and the earth condemned to standing remains
unmoved in the fixed, and the rivers toil without accomplishment being spent
on the insatiable nature of the sea, and in vain the sea receives the inflows
of the waters, taking into its gulfs to no more the thing poured in through
all; if these things are in these, in what is it likely that the human is,
which has its life in these, and why are we surprised, if A generation goes
and a generation comes, and this course does not fail =5.287= nature, the
generation of humans always coming in both thrusting out what has entered
before and being thrust out by what comes in after? What then does the word
shout through these things to the church? that, O humans, looking away to the
whole, understand your own nature. The things you see about heaven and earth,
the things you behold in the sun, the things you understand in the sea, let
these interpret your nature also for you. For there is a certain rising and
setting according to likeness of the sun and of our nature. One way for all,
one circle of the journey of life. When we rise through generation, again we
are dragged down into our kindred place. For when life has set upon us, our
light too becomes underground, when the sense that receives the light becomes
earth; and altogether the earth is dissolved toward the kindred, and this is a
certain circle continuously winding in the same things. Just as in the case of
the sun the word says that rising according to the upper part of the earth it
travels through the southern parts, and underground according to the opposite
it comes under the northern part and in this manner forever going around it
circles its own course and again cycling round it goes about; For it circles,
he says, circling; so then also your spirit goes (naming from a part the whole
human spirit), =5.288= running round this circular motion through like things.
For The spirit goes, he says, and the spirit returns upon its circles. And the
one who has understood these things would be benefited not a little toward his
own life. What is more brilliant than the light? What is more manifest than
the rays? But nevertheless if the sun should come under earth, the light is
hidden and the ray disappears. Looking toward these things let someone pass
through his own life more soberly, despising the conspicuousness here, having
learned from the things seen that the notable does not suffice toward the
continuous, but the successions of the contraries are swift to turn; and
nothing remains forever such as it is in the present, not youth, not beauty,
not the conspicuousness from powers. And these things for those living in some
good fortune; but for as many as the life toward virtue seems toilsome, let
the soul be trained by the example of the earth toward enduring the terrible
things. The earth stands unto the age. What is more miserable than this
unmoved fixedness? And yet the standing is stretched until an age. But you,
for whom the time of the contest is little, do not become more soulless than
the earth, do not become more mindless than the unfeeling things, you who have
been allotted reasoning and are administered toward life by word, but Remain,
as the apostle says, in the things you learned and were assured of, in a firm
and unmoved standing. Since this too is among the divine commands, that Be
firm and unmoved =5.289=, let sobriety remain unshaken in you, faith fixed,
love unalterable, the standing in every good unmoved, so that the earth in you
too may stand unto the age. But if someone gaping toward greed, having spread
out the unmeasure of desire like a certain sea, should be insatiable toward
the profits flowing in from everywhere, let this one, looking toward the true
sea, be healed of the disease. For as that does not pass its own measure in
the countless inflows of waters, but remains in the equal fullness, as if no
addition from waters were coming about for it, in the same manner also human
nature, being marked off <by> its own measures in the enjoyment of the things
that come, cannot widen together with the multitude of revenues the greedy
appetite of enjoyment, but what flows in does not cease, and the power of
enjoyment is kept in its own bound. If then the


enjoyment cannot increase beyond the measure of nature, for what do we draw
the inflows of revenues, never overflowing from the things coming in toward
doing good to others? And since according to the account of vanity given us
vanity is either the mindless word or the unprofitable thing, he well begins
the word from there, so that we may reckon neither anything of the things that
come to be nor anything of the things said, if it looks toward the aim here,
=5.290= as existing. For every zeal of humans occupied toward something of the
things beside life is outright the playthings of infants upon sands, for whom
the enjoyment of the things coming to be ceased together with the zeal about
the things coming to be; for at the same time they ceased from toiling, and
the sand flowing together toward itself left no trace of the things toiled at
for the children. This is the human life, sand is ambition, sand is power,
sand is wealth, sand is each of the things enjoyed through flesh according to
zeal; in which now the infantile souls, being vain among the insubstantial
things and undergoing many toils about each of these, if only they should
leave the place of the sand, I mean the life in flesh, then they will
recognize the vanity of the sojourn here; for the enjoyment also remained
behind with the material life, and they draw along with themselves nothing
except the conscience alone. As it seems to me the great Ecclesiastes also, as
if already having come outside these things and boarding the immaterial life
with naked soul, said those things which it is likely that we too will
sometime say, when we come outside this seaside place, about which is the sand
spat out by the sea of life, and are separated from all the waves that crash
around us and boom against us, bringing from the sea that has been understood
only =5.291= the memory of the things pursued here, saying Vanity of vanities,
all things vanity, and What surplus is there for the man, in which he toils
under the sun. For truly, according to my word at least, this word belongs to
every soul, when stripped of the things here it is relocated toward the
hoped-for life. For whether it accomplished something of the loftier things in
this life, it accuses that in which it was, dishonoring the past by the
comparison toward what has been found; or also, having been disposed with
passion about matter, it should see the unexpected things and should learn by
experience the unprofitableness of the things pursued by it beside life, then
lamenting it will send forth this voice, such as we humans do from repentance
narrating our follies in the wailings, Vanity of vanities and the things that
remain. All words, he says, are toilsome, and a man will not be able to speak.
And yet nothing is thought from the ready more easy than speaking. For what
toil is there for the one speaking whatever one wishes? The tongue is moist
and easily turning and shaping itself without toil toward whatever form of
words it should wish; unhindered is the drawing of the aerial breath, using
which it works the sounds; painless is the service to the cheeks and together
to the lips the cooperation toward the utterance of what is said =5.292=. What
toil then does he look upon for the word, the bodily toil not making the toil
for the word? For we do not go through the word digging earth or rolling rocks
or bearing burdens on the shoulders or working some other of the toilsome
things, but the thought constituted in us, uncovered through voice, became
word. But since such a word has no toil, it would be necessary to understand
which are the toiling words, which a man will not be able to speak. Let the
elders, he says, be deemed worthy of double honor, especially those who toil
in word. And elder according to common usage is said of the one who has gone
out of the unordered age and has come into an aged condition, so that if
someone should be unstable in reasoning and should have life in disorder, such
a one is not yet an elder, even if he should happen to appear in gray hair,
but still a man. Therefore the words, the ones that are truly words, those
coming to be toward the soul-profitable and useful of humans, these are full
of sweats and toils and bring on much toil, so that they may become words. For
the toiling farmer must first


partake of the fruits, says the craftsman of such words, as it is necessary
that the word not be understood as an utterance, but that the virtue in the
works, set forth toward teaching of life for those who see, become instead of
word for those being taught. All such words then are toilsome, of those who
guide virtue first accomplishing in themselves the things they teach. For this
is the needing first to partake of the fruits, which =5.293= before the others
we farm for ourselves through virtue. Or perhaps the word also interprets the
weakness of the rational nature. For whenever the mind, having come outside
the sense-organs, which indeed have been named vanity, and having somehow
slipped through into the contemplation of the invisible things, should attempt
to present to the word what has been thought, then much toil comes about for
the word, this interpretive voice finding no device about the clearness of the
unutterable things. We see heaven, we receive the gleams of the lights with
sense, we have stepped upon earth, we draw the air with the mouth, we use the
water toward what seems good to nature, we receive fire into the communion of
life; and if we should wish to think something about these, what each of the
appearing things is by nature in the account of essence or how it had its
subsistence, A man will not be able to speak, even if he should happen to be
above the others, all grasping knowledge being exhausted toward the
declaration of the unattainable things. If then the word about these things is
a toil exceeding the human power and nature of speaking, what would one say
the words about the Word itself or about the Father of the Word suffer? Surely
all lofty speech and loud-voicedness is a certain speechlessness and silence,
if it should be judged toward the true meaning of what is sought, so that this
alone is properly to say the utterance upon =5.294= it, that even if someone
should move all reasonings and should leave out nothing of the thoughts
fitting to God, if the voice should be judged toward the very worth of what is
set before, whatever he should say is not a word; for a human will not be able
to speak. For sight did not stop the contemplation coming about through eyes
for the soul about the appearing thing, but always looking as if we had not
yet seen we still have in ignorance what is taken through sense. For sight
cannot pass through the color, but has as measure of its own activity what
appears beforehand to it according to the surface of the things that are.
Therefore he says: An eye will not be filled of seeing, and an ear will not be
filled from hearing; of which the hearing-power, receiving the word about
each, does not have a nature to be filled. For no word will be found
distinguishing through accuracy in itself what is sought. How then will
hearing be filled of the hearing about the things sought, when that which
fills is not? Then after these words he asks himself and answers himself. For
having asked: What is that which has come to be? that, he says, which will
come to be; and what is that which has been made? that, he says, which will be
made. What then does the question mean? As from sequence we oppose to him,
from the things we have learned, =5.295= and say: if all things are vanity, it
is clear that not even one of these things which does not subsist has come to
be; for the vain is altogether nonexistent, and the nonexistent one would not
reckon among the things that have come to be. If then these things are not,
say what is that which has come to be, which will remain in being? Shortly
then the answer has come about for him toward what was asked: do you wish to
know what is that which has come to be? understand what is that which will be,
and you will recognize what has come to be. And this is Understand for me, he
says, O human, of what sort you will become having raised yourself through
virtue. If in all things you should be formed in the soul through the good
characters, if you should come outside the stains of vice, if you should wash
off all filth of the material pollutions from your own nature, what will you
become being adorned through such things? What form will you put around
yourself? If you should understand this by reasoning, you have been taught
that which came to be among the first things, which truly is what will come to
be, the According to image of God and likeness. And where now is that, I will
say to the one teaching these things, which once came to be and is hoped for
again later, but now is not? But he answers at any rate =5.296= the same word


to us, the one teaching the lofty things, that for this reason the present
things have been named vanity, because that is not in the present things. And
what, he says, is that which has been made? that which will be made. Let none
of the hearers think there is wordiness and a certain vain repetition of words
in the difference of that which has come to be and that which has been made.
For the word shows through each of the utterances the difference of the soul
toward the flesh. The soul has come to be and the body has been made. Not
because the emphasis of the words signifies one thing and another does the
word use this difference of words upon each of the things signified, but so
that it may give you the things fitting to reckon about each. That the soul
has come to be at the beginnings, which later having been purified will appear
again; that the body being molded has been made by the hands of God, which the
resurrection will show itself at the due times; for of whatever sort you
should see after the resurrection, of such a sort it has at any rate been made
at the first. For the resurrection is not anything else, if not at any rate
the restoration to the ancient. Therefore he adds to these also the following,
saying that nothing is outside the ancient. For There is not, he says,
anything recent under the sun, as if he were saying that if something is not
according to the ancient, it is not even at all, but is thought. =5.297= For
there is not, he says, anything recent under the sun, so that someone should
speak and show something of the things that have come on after, that this is
new and in being subsists. This is the sense of the things said, and the
wording has this manner: And there is not anything recent under the sun, who
will speak and will say, see this is new. And he contends for the things said
through the words next: if anything has truly, he says, come to be, that is it
which came to be in the ages before us. For the very words of scripture having
thus indicate this sense: Already it has come to be in the ages that have come
to be from before us. And if forgetfulness has prevailed of the things that
have come to be, you would wonder at nothing; for also the things now being
will be covered over by forgetfulness. For when nature inclined toward vice,
we came to be in forgetfulness of the good things; when the return toward the
good comes about for us again, again the evil will be covered over by
forgetfulness. For this I think is the sense in the things said, in which he
says: There is not memory for the first things, and also for those who have
come to be last there will not be memory of them, as if he were saying that of
the things that came on after the good fortune from the beginning, through
which the human came to be in evils, the things coming on again in the last
things will wipe out the memory; For there will not be memory of them with
those who have come to be unto the last, that is the last condition =5.298=
will produce for nature a complete disappearance of the memory of the evils,
in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the glory unto the ages of the ages.
Amen. HOMILY II I, he says, the Ecclesiastes. And we have learned who is the
Ecclesiastes who gathers the things that have wandered and been scattered into
one and makes all things one church and one flock, so that nothing may be
unhearing of the good voice of the shepherd that makes all things live. For
The words, he says, which I speak, are spirit and are life. This one names
himself Ecclesiastes as physician and life and resurrection and light and way
and door and truth and all the names of loving-kindness. As then the voice of
the physician has what is proper toward those who are weak, and the word of
life becomes active upon the dead who, whenever they hear the voice of the Son
of man, no longer remain in the ancient deadness; and those who are in tombs
seek the voice of the =5.299= resurrection; and the word of the light is
fitting for those who have been darkened, and the way for those who have
wandered, but also the door for those who need to enter; so also the
Ecclesiastes at any rate converses with those who assemble. Therefore the
Ecclesiastes speaks toward us. And indeed let us hear his words, we the
church. For as the


chorus looks toward the chorus-leader, and the sailors toward the helmsman and
the battle-line toward the general, so also those who are in the fullness of
the church toward the leader of the church. What then does Ecclesiastes say? I
became king over Israel in Jerusalem. When was this? Or at any rate when He
was appointed king by him upon Zion his holy mountain announcing the ordinance
of the Lord? to whom the Lord said that You are my Son, and that Today I have
begotten you; for he said that he had begotten today the maker of the all, the
Father of the ages, so that through setting the temporal name beside the time
of the generation the word may present not the pre-eternal existence, but the
generation through flesh in time for the salvation of humans. These things
then the true Ecclesiastes goes through teaching, I think, the great mystery
of salvation, for the sake of which God was manifested in flesh. For I gave,
he says, my heart to seek out and to survey in wisdom about all the things
that have come to be under heaven. This is the cause of the =5.300= Lord's
sojourning through flesh among humans, the giving his heart to survey in his
own wisdom about the things that have come to be under heaven. For the things
above heaven did not need the one surveying, as neither does that which is not
held by the disease need the one healing. Since then the evils are about
earth; for the creeping beast the serpent that is dragged upon the chest and
the belly makes the earth its food, feeding on nothing of the heavenly things,
but being dragged in what is trodden always looks toward what treads, watching
the heel of the human journey and injecting the venom into those who have lost
The authority of treading upon serpents; for this reason he gave his heart To
seek out and to survey about all the things that have come to be under heaven;
for in the things above the heavens the prophet sees the divine magnificence
unlowered, saying that Your magnificence was lifted up above the heavens. And
since the under-heaven part was lowered through vice; for thus the psalmist
says that they were lowered because of their sins; the Ecclesiastes came to
survey this, what has come to be under heaven which was not before, how vanity
entered, how the nonexistent prevailed, what is the power of that which does
not exist. For evil is insubstantial, because it has its subsistence from that
which is not, and that which is from that which is not is not even at all
=5.301= according to its own nature, but nevertheless vanity prevails over
those who have been made like to vanity. He came then to seek out by his own
wisdom what has come to be under the sun, what is the confusion of the affairs
here, how that which is was enslaved to that which is not, how the
insubstantial holds power against that which is. And he saw, That God gave an
