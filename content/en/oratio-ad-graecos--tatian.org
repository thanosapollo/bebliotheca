Of Tatian to the Greeks.
1.1 Do not be disposed in a wholly hostile way toward the barbarians, O men
of Greece, and do not grudge their doctrines. For which pursuit among you did
not get its constitution from barbarians? The most approved of the Telmessians
invented the divination that is through dreams, the Carians the foreknowledge
that is through the stars, flights of birds the Phrygians and the most ancient
of the Isaurians, the Cyprians the art of sacrifice, the Babylonians astronomy,
the Persians magic, the Egyptians geometry, the Phoenicians the education that
is through letters. Therefore stop calling your imitations discoveries. For
Orpheus taught you to practice poetry and to sing, and the same man also to be
initiated; the Tuscans to model, and the records of times among the Egyptians
to compose histories. From Marsyas and Olympus you carried off the art of the
flute; 1.2 and both were Phrygians; rustics established the harmony that is
through the pipe. The Tyrrhenians the trumpet, the Cyclopes bronze-working, and
the woman who once led the Persians to compose letters, as Hellanicus says; and
her name was Atossa. Throw down, therefore, this conceit, and do not put
forward elegance of words, you who, praised by yourselves, have obtained
advocates at home. A man of sense must wait for the testimony from others, and
must be in accord also in the utterance of speech. But as it is, it has turned
out for you alone not even to speak with one voice in conversation. For the
diction of the Dorians is not the same as that of those from Attica, and the
Aeolians do not sound like the Ionians; and when there is such discord among
those among whom 1.3 there ought not to be, I am at a loss whom I must call a
Greek. And the most absurd thing of all is that you have honored interpretations
not even akin to you, and by sometimes abusing barbarian voices you have made
your dialect a jumble. For this reason we have taken leave of the wisdom among
you, even if someone was very august in it. For according to the comic poet
these things are chatter, leftover-grape museums of swallows, mutilators of
art, and those who pursue this gasp in the throat and let out a voice of
crows. For you established rhetoric for injustice and slander, selling for pay
the independence of your speeches and often presenting what is now just as in
turn not good; and poetry, that you may compose battles of gods and loves and
1.4 corruption of soul. 2.1 For what august thing have you brought forth while
philosophizing? And who of those altogether in earnest has stood outside
boastfulness? Diogenes, making self-sufficiency grand by the boast of a tub,
was seized by a passion through raw-eating of octopus and died of ileus through
incontinence. Aristippus, walking about in purple, played the prodigal in a way
that won credit. Plato, while philosophizing, was sold by Dionysius through
gluttony. And Aristotle, setting a limit to providence ignorantly and marking
off happiness in the things that pleased him, flattered, in a very uneducated
way, Alexander, that maddened stripling, who in a thoroughly Aristotelian
manner shut up his own friend for not being willing to do him obeisance and
carried him about like a bear or a panther. He was thoroughly persuaded, then,
by the doctrines of his teacher, displaying courage and virtue at drinking
bouts and running through his own and altogether 2.2 dearest with the spear,
and again weeping and starving himself on a pretext of grief, in order that he
might not be hated by his household. I would laugh also at those who up to now
abuse his doctrines, who, saying that the things after the moon are without
providence, being nearer the earth than the moon and lower than her course,
exercise providence over the unprovided; and among those for whom there is no
beauty, no wealth, no strength of body, no noble birth, among these according
to Aristotle there is not the happy.


And let such men philosophize. 3.1 For I would not accept Heraclitus, who said
I taught myself, because he was self-taught and arrogant, nor would I praise
him for hiding his poem in the temple of Artemis, in a mystic way, so that
later the publication of it might come about. For those who care about these
things say that Euripides the tragic poet, going down and reading, delivered
the Heraclitean darkness little by little from memory to those in earnest. His
ignorance, then, death convicted; for being seized by dropsy and practicing
medicine as philosophy, and plastering himself with cow-dung, when the dung
hardened and produced contractions of the whole body, he was convulsed and
died. And Zeno, who declares through the conflagration that the same men rise
again 3.2 upon the same things, I mean Anytus and Meletus upon accusing, and
Busiris upon killing strangers, and Heracles again upon laboring, is to be
declined; he who in the account concerning the conflagration introduces the
wicked as more than the just, Socrates being one and Heracles and certain
others of that kind, few having come to be and not many. For the bad will be
found altogether more than the good, and God will be shown according to him a
maker of evils, occupying himself in gutters and worms and doers of unspeakable
things. For the boastfulness of Empedocles the fire-blasts in Sicily proved,
that not being a god he lied in claiming to be what he said he was. I laugh
also at the old-wives' talk of Pherecydes and 3.3 at Pythagoras' inheritance
concerning the doctrine, and at Plato's, even if some do not wish it, imitation
concerning these men. For who would bear witness to the dog-marriage of Crates,
and would not rather decline the conceited tongue-madness of those like him and
turn to seeking what is in earnest according to truth? Therefore let not the
festal assemblies of the fault-lovers and not philosophers drag you aside, who
dogmatize things contrary to themselves, and each has cried out according to
what occurred to him. And there are many clashes among them too; for one hates
the other, and they hold opposite opinions among themselves through
boastfulness, choosing places *** the eminent. And they ought not even by a
prejudice of kingship to flatter the rulers, but to wait until 3.4 the grandees
come to them. 4.1 For why, O men of Greece, do you wish to clash the
constitutions against us as in a boxing-match? And if I do not wish to use the
customs of certain people, for what reason am I hated as most polluted? The
king commands to pay tributes; I am ready to furnish them. The master to be a
slave and to serve; I know slavery. For the human being is to be honored in a
human way, but God alone is to be feared, who is not visible to human eyes, not
comprehensible by art. Ordered to deny this one alone I will not be persuaded,
but I will rather die, in order that I may not be shown a liar and ungrateful.
The God according to us does not have constitution in time, being alone without
beginning and himself being the beginning of the whole.
God is spirit, not pervading 4.2 through matter, but the constructor of
material spirits and of the shapes in it, both invisible and intangible,
himself become father of things perceptible and visible. This one we know
through his making, and the invisible of his power we grasp by the things made.
I do not wish to worship the craftsmanship that has come to be under him for
our sake. Sun and moon have come to be for us; then how shall I worship my own
servants? And how shall I declare woods and stones gods? For the spirit that
pervades through matter, being less than the more divine spirit, and as it were
made like a soul, is not to be honored equally with the perfect God. But
neither is the unnameable God to be bribed; for the one in need of nothing of
all 4.3 is not to be slandered by us as in need. And I will set out our things
more plainly. 5.1 God was in the beginning, and we have received the beginning
as a power of the Word. For the master of the whole, himself being the
subsistence of the all


according to the as yet unmade making, was alone; but inasmuch as he himself
was subsistence of every power of things visible and invisible together with
himself, he himself and the Word, who was in him, established all things
together with himself through rational power. And by the will of his simplicity
the Word springs forth; and the Word, not having gone into a void, becomes a
firstborn work of the Father. This one we know as the beginning of the world.
And he has come to be by distribution, not by cutting off; for what is cut away
has been separated from the first, but what is distributed, having taken on the
division of an economy, 5.2 has not made needy that from which it has been
taken. For just as from one torch many fires are kindled, and the light of the
first torch is not lessened through the lighting of the many torches, so also
the Word, having come forth from the power of the Father, has not made the one
who begot him without word. For I myself also speak, and you hear; and I do not
surely through the passage of the word become empty of the word as I converse,
but putting forth my own voice I have chosen to order the unordered matter in
you, and just as the Word, begotten in the beginning, begot in turn our making
for himself, having crafted the matter, so I also 5.3, regenerated according to
the imitation of the Word and having made the grasp of the true, reshape the
confusion of the kindred matter. For neither is matter without beginning as God
also is, nor, through being without beginning, is it itself equal in power to
God, but it is generated and not having come to be by another, but projected by
the craftsman of all alone. 6.1 And for this reason we have also believed that
there will be a resurrection of bodies after the consummation of the whole, not
as the Stoics dogmatize, the same things always coming to be and passing away
according to certain periods of cycles to no useful end, but once, when the
ages according to us have been bounded, and the constitution will be unto
completeness through human beings alone, for the sake of judgment. And neither
Minos nor Rhadamanthus judges us, before whose death no soul, as they tell in
myth, was judged, but the craftsman God himself becomes the examiner. For even
if you think us altogether babblers and seed-pickers, it is no concern to us,
since we have believed this word. For just as, not existing before I 6.2 came
to be, I did not know who I was, but existed only in the subsistence of fleshly
matter, and having come to be, I who was not of old have believed that I am
through birth; in the same way the one who has come to be and through death is
no longer, and again is no longer seen, will be again just as one not having
come to be of old and then born. Even if fire make my little flesh vanish, the
world has contained the matter evaporated; even if I be spent in rivers or in
seas, even if I be torn by beasts, I am stored in the treasuries of a rich
master. And the poor man and the godless one does not know the things stored
up, but God who is king, when he wishes, will restore the subsistence visible
to him alone to the ancient state. 7.1 For the heavenly Word, having become
spirit from the Spirit and word from rational power, according to the imitation
of the Father who begot him made the human being an image of immortality, in
order that, just as incorruption is with God, in the same way a human being,
having taken a share of God's portion, may have also the immortal. The Word,
then, before the construction of human beings becomes craftsman of angels, and
each of the two kinds of the making has become free-willed, not having the
nature of the good, which *** except only with God, and being perfected by
human beings through the freedom of choice, in order that the worthless may
justly be punished, having become wicked through himself, and the just, for the
sake of his deeds of valor, 7.2 may be praised worthily, not having transgressed
the will of God according to free will. And the things concerning angels and
human beings have this manner; and the power of the Word, having with itself
the foreknowing *** the future coming out not according to fate but by the
free-willed judgment of those who choose, used to foretell the outcomes of the
things to come, and became a preventer of wickedness through prohibitions,


and a praiser of the goods that remain. And when some who was more prudent than
the rest, because of being first-born, was followed, and human beings and
angels proclaimed as god the one who rose up against the law of God, then the
power of the Word declined both the beginner of the madness and those 7.3 who
followed along with him from the common dwelling with him. And the one who had
come to be according to the image of God, when the more powerful spirit was
separated from him, becomes mortal; and through the transgression and the
ignorance the first-born is shown a daemon, and those who imitated this one,
and the phantoms of this one have turned out a camp of daemons, and through
free will they were handed over to their own silliness. 8.1 And human beings
become for them the occasion of the apostasy. For having displayed to them a
diagram of star-placing, as those who play with dice, they introduced fate, a
thing altogether unjust. For both the one who judges and the one who is judged
have come to be according to fate, and those who murder and those who are
murdered and those who are rich and those who are poor are offspring of the
same fate, and every birth furnished amusement as in a theater for those among
whom, as Homer says, unquenchable laughter arose among the blessed gods. For
those who look at the single-fighters, and one taking sides with the other ***
and the one marrying and corrupting children and committing adultery, both
laughing and being angry, both fleeing and being wounded, how will he not be
thought 8.2 to be mortal? For through the things by which they have made plain
to human beings of what sort they have been born, through these they urged
those who hear on to the like. And have not the daemons themselves, with Zeus
their leader, fallen under fate, overpowered by the same passions as human
beings also are? And otherwise how are these to be honored among whom there is
much opposition of doctrines? For Rhea, whom those from the Phrygian mountains
call Cybele, has legislated cuttings of private parts because of her beloved
Attis; but Aphrodite delights in the weavings of marriage. Artemis is a
magician, Apollo heals. And after the beheading of the Gorgon, the beloved of
Poseidon, from whom the horse Pegasus and 8.3 Chrysaor sprang up, Athena and
Asclepius divided the drops of the bloods; and he from them used to save, but
she from the like gore used to become a man-slayer, the war-maker. This one the
Athenians, as it seems to me, not wishing to slander, attach also the one who
came to be from the mixing with Hephaestus to Earth, in order that Athena may
not be thought, just as Atalanta through Meleager, so also through Hephaestus
to have been deprived of her courage. For the lame in both legs, as is likely,
the maker of brooches and bent spiral ornaments, deceived with girl-ornaments
the motherless and orphan child. Poseidon sails, Ares delights in wars,
Apollo is a lyre-player, Dionysus plays the tyrant over Thebans, Cronus slays
tyrants. Zeus even 8.4 has intercourse with a daughter, and the daughter
conceives from him. 8.4 Eleusis will now bear me witness, and the mystic
serpent, and Orpheus saying put doors against the profane. Aidoneus seizes
Kore, and his deeds have become mysteries; Demeter weeps for her daughter, and
some are deceived because of the Athenians. In the precinct of the son of Leto
someone is called a navel; and the navel is a tomb of Dionysus. I praise you
now, O Daphne; having conquered the incontinence of Apollo you convicted his
divination, because, not having foreknown the things concerning you, he got no
benefit from his own art. Let the far-shooter tell me now how Zephyrus made
away with Hyacinthus. Zephyrus has conquered him; and though the tragic poet
says breeze, 8.5 most honored vehicle of the gods, conquered by a short breeze
he lost his beloved. 9.1 Such are these daemons who defined fate. And their
element-system was the making-into-animals. For the creeping things upon the
earth and the swimming things in the waters and the four-footed things upon the
mountains, with whom they used to make their dwelling after they had become
cast out from the dwelling in heaven, these they deemed worthy of heavenly
honor,


in order that they themselves might be thought to spend time in heaven, and
that they might show the irrational citizenship upon earth to be rational
through the star-placing. So that the passionate and the toiling and the
self-controlled and the incontinent and the one who is poor and the one who is
rich belong to those who legislated the birth. For the drawing of the zodiac
circle is a poem of gods, 9.2 and the prevailing light, as they say, of one of
them awards the prize against the more, and the one now conquered is accustomed
to prevail another time; and the seven planets please them *** as those who
play at pebbles. But we are both above fate and, instead of wandering daemons,
have learned one unwandering master, and not being led according to fate we
have declined the legislators of it. Tell me, by God: Triptolemus sowed the
wheats, and after the mourning Demeter benefits the Athenians; but why, when
her daughter had not been lost, did she not become a benefactress of human
beings? The dog of Erigone is shown in heaven, and the scorpion the helper of
Artemis, and Chiron the 9.3 Centaur, and the halved Argo, and the bear of
Callisto; then how, before these came to be about the aforesaid stations, was
the heaven unordered? And to whom will it not seem laughable that the Triangle
is placed among the stars according to some because of Sicily, but according to
some as the original letter of the additional name of Zeus? For why have not
also Sardinia and Cyprus been honored in heaven? And for what reason were not
also the brothers of Zeus, who divided the kingships, *** star-set by a
shape-working of letters? And how is Cronus, who was bound and became cast out
from the kingship, established as steward of fate? And how does the one who no
longer rules give kingships? Throw down, therefore, the 9.4 nonsense, and do
not, because you hate us, transgress unjustly. 10.1 A change of form has been
told in myth for human beings; and among you the gods also change form. Rhea
becomes a tree, and Zeus a serpent because of Persephone, and the sisters of
Phaethon poplars, and Leto a cheap animal, because of whom Delos, as it is now
called, has been named Ortygia. Does a god, tell me, become a swan and take on
the form of an eagle and make pederasty august through the cup-bearing of
Ganymede? Why should I reverence gods who take gifts and grow angry if they do
not receive? Let these have fate; I do not wish to worship the planets. What is
the lock of Berenice? And where were her stars before the aforesaid woman died?
And how has the dead Antinous, a youth in bloom, been established in the 10.2
moon? Who was it that brought him up, unless someone, mocking God, has been
believed when he said that this one too, as the kings, had gone up into heaven
for pay through perjury, and then, having theologized the like, has been deemed
worthy of honor and a gift? Why have you robbed me of my God? And why do you
dishonor his making? You sacrifice a sheep, and you worship the same; there is
a bull in heaven, and you slaughter the image of it. The Kneeler squeezes a
wicked animal; and the eagle that ate Prometheus, the maker of man, has been
honored. Fair is the swan, because he was an adulterer; fair also are the
Dioscuri of alternate days, the seizers of the Leucippides. Better is Helen,
having left fair-haired Menelaus and 10.3 followed the mitre-wearing and
much-gold Paris. Just and temperate is the one who transferred the woman who
had played the harlot into Elysian fields. But neither has the daughter of
Tyndareus been made immortal, and Euripides wisely introduced the killing of
the aforesaid woman by Orestes. 11.1 How then shall I accept a birth according
to fate, seeing such stewards of it? I do not wish to be king, I do not want to
be rich, I have declined the generalship, I have hated fornication, I do not
practice sailing because of greed, I do not contend to have crowns, I have been
freed from glory-madness, I despise death, I become above every kind of
disease, grief does not consume my soul. If I am a slave, I endure the slavery;
and if I am free, I do not make the noble birth grand. I see the sun the same
for all, and one death over all through


pleasure and defect. The rich man sows, and the poor man takes a share of the
same sowing; the richest die, and those who beg have the same outline of life.
Those who are rich 11.2 need more, and through credit they become, with their
reputation, poor; but the poor man also, even the most moderate, aiming at what
is his own, comes through more easily. Why do you lie awake according to fate
through love of money? And why, according to fate, reaching often, do you die
often? Die to the world, declining the madness in it; live to God, declining
the old birth through the grasp of him. We did not come to be toward dying, but
we die through ourselves. Free will destroyed us; we who were free have become
slaves, we were sold through sin. Nothing worthless has been made by God; we
displayed the wickedness; and those who displayed it are able again to decline
it. 12.1 We know differences of two spirits, of which the one is called soul,
and the other greater than the soul, image and likeness of God; and both were
present with the first human beings, in order that they might be in one respect
material, and in another above matter. And it is thus. It is possible to see
the whole construction of the world, and the whole making, as having come to be
from matter, and the matter itself as projected by God, in order that one part
of it may be thought without resource and without shape before receiving
distinction, and the other ordered and well-arranged after the division in it.
There is, then, in it the heaven from matter, and the stars 12.2 in it; and the
earth also, and everything brought to life from it, has the like constitution,
so that there is a common birth of all. And these things being so, there are
certain differences of the things from matter, so that one thing is more
beautiful, and another itself also beautiful, yet lessened by something
stronger. For just as the constitution of the body is of one economy, and about
it there is the cause of its having come to be, and these things being so there
are certain differences of glory in it, and one thing is an eye, another an
ear, another an ordering of hairs and an economy of inward parts and a compact
of marrows and bones and sinews, 12.3 and being different from one another
there is a harmony of agreement according to economy; similarly also the world,
according to the power of the one who made it, having acquired some things more
bright, and some unlike these, has by the will of the one who crafted it taken
a share of material spirit. And the particulars it is possible to understand
for the one who does not vainly reject the most divine interpretations, which,
having been proved through writing according to time, have made those who
attend to them altogether dear to God. Nevertheless the daemons also, whom you
so name, having taken a compact from matter and having acquired the spirit from
it, have become prodigal and greedy, some of them having turned toward the
purer, and others having chosen the lesser of the matter 12.4 and conducting
themselves according to what is like it. And these, O men of Greece, you
worship, who have come to be from matter, and have been found far from good
order. For the aforesaid, having turned through their own silliness toward
vainglory and having thrown off the reins, were eager to become robbers of
godhead; and the master of the whole allowed them to revel until the world,
having taken an end, is dissolved, and the judge is present, and all human
beings, being sent forth through the uprising of the daemons from the knowledge
of the perfect God, receive through the contests on the day of judgment a more
perfect testimony. There is, then, spirit in lights, spirit in angels, spirit
in plants and waters, spirit in human beings, spirit in animals; and being one
12.5 and the same it has acquired differences in itself. And when we say these
things we are not using words from tongue, nor from probabilities, nor from
thoughts and sophistic composition, but from a more divine utterance, which
those who wish, make haste to learn; and you who do not reject the Scythian
Anacharsis, do not now take it ill to be educated among those who follow a
barbarian legislation. Use our doctrines even as the


Babylonian art of foreknowledge; listen to us speaking even as to an oak that
divines. And the things aforesaid are counter-sophistries of deranged daemons,
but the things of our education are above the worldly grasp. 13.1 The soul, O
men of Greece, is not immortal by itself, but mortal; yet the same is also able
not to die. For it dies and is dissolved with the body when it does not know
the truth, and it rises later at the consummation of the world with the body,
receiving death through punishment in immortality; and again it does not die,
even if it is dissolved for a time, when it has made the recognition of God.
For by itself it is darkness, and there is nothing luminous in it, and this
then is what has been said: the darkness does not comprehend the light. For the
soul itself did not save the spirit, but was saved by it; and the light
comprehended the darkness, inasmuch as 13.2 the light of God is Word, and the
unknowing soul is darkness. For this reason, dwelling alone, it inclines down
toward the matter, dying together with the flesh, but having acquired the
yoking of the divine spirit it is not without help, and it goes up toward the
regions to which the spirit leads it; for of the one the dwelling-place is
above, and of the other the birth is from below. The spirit, then, came to be
from the beginning a fellow-dweller with the soul; but the spirit has left this
one when it was not willing to follow it. And she, having acquired as it were a
spark of its power and through the separation not being able to see the perfect
things, seeking God according to error stamped many gods, 13.3 following the
daemons who play counter-sophist. And the spirit of God is not with all, but
residing with certain who conduct themselves justly and being intertwined with
the soul it announced through predictions to the remaining souls what was
hidden; and those that were persuaded by the wisdom drew to themselves the
kindred spirit, but those that were not persuaded and declined the servant of
the God who suffered were shown fighters against God rather than reverers of
God. 14.1 Such also are you, O Greeks, glib in words, but having a strange
judgment, and you have practiced many-rule rather than monarchy, thinking to
follow the daemons as strong. For just as the one who robs, being inhuman, is
accustomed through daring to prevail over those like him, so also the daemons,
having run aground into much vice, have deceived the isolated souls among you
through ignorances and appearances. They do not die easily, for they have no
share of flesh; but living they practice the pursuits of death, themselves also
dying as often as they train those who follow them in the sins, so that what is
in excess for them in the present, not dying in like manner with human beings,
this, whenever they are about to be punished, 14.2 is bitter to them *** they
will not share in everlasting life, taking instead of death a portion in an
immortal one. And just as we, for whom dying comes about easily now, hereafter
take on either the immortal with enjoyment or the painful with immortality, so
also the daemons, using the present life throughout toward transgression and
dying through the living, will hereafter have the same immortality similar to
that during the time they lived, similar according to constitution to human
beings who by judgment practiced the things which they legislated for them
during the time they lived. And perhaps for those who follow them the kinds of
the sin bloom out as less, because they do not live for a long time, but for
the aforesaid daemons 14.3 the transgression has turned out greater because of
the boundless of the lifetime. 15.1 And we must hereafter seek now this which,
having, we have lost, and yoke the soul to the holy spirit and work the yoking
according to God. The soul of human beings, then, is many-parted and not
one-parted. For it is composite, so as to be apparent through a body; for
neither would it itself ever appear without a body, nor does the flesh rise
without a soul. For a human being is not, as the crow-voiced dogmatize, a
rational animal capable of intellect and knowledge;


for according to them even the irrational animals will be shown capable of
intellect and knowledge; but the human being alone is image and likeness of
God, and I mean a human being not the one who does things like the animals,
15.2 but the one who has gone far from humanity and toward God himself. And
concerning this it has been composed by us more accurately in the work On
Animals, but what now holds together must be said, of what sort the image and
likeness according to God is. The incomparable is nothing other than being
itself, and the compared is nothing other than the similar. Fleshless, then, is
the perfect God, but a human being is flesh; and the bond of the flesh is soul,
and the flesh is holding of the soul. And such a kind of constitution, if it
should be as a temple, God wishes to dwell in it through the presiding spirit;
and if such 15.3 a tent is not, the human being excels the beasts according to
articulate voice alone, and the rest is of the same dwelling as those, not
being a likeness of God. And all daemons do not possess little flesh, but their
compact is spiritual, as of fire and air. To those alone, then, who are guarded
by the spirit of God the bodies of the daemons also are easy to see, but to the
rest not at all, I mean to the soulish. For the lesser does not have strength
to make a grasp of the stronger. For this reason, then, the subsistence of the
daemons does not have a place of repentance. For they are radiances of the
matter and of wickedness, and matter wished to have authority over the soul;
and according to free will 15.4 some have handed down laws of death to human
beings; but human beings, after the loss of immortality, have conquered death
