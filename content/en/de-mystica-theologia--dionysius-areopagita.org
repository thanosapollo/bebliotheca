ON MYSTICAL THEOLOGY, TO TIMOTHY
<I.>
<1.> O Trinity superessential and superdivine and supergood, guardian of
the theosophy of Christians, guide us to the superunknown and superbright
and highest summit of the mystical oracles, where the simple and absolute
and unchanging mysteries of theology lie veiled in the superluminous gloom
of the secretly mystic silence, there in the most dark shining forth what
is most superapparent, and in the wholly intangible and invisible
overfilling with splendors beyond beauty the minds that have no eyes.
Let this, then, be my prayer. But you, beloved Timothy, by intense
practice concerning the mystical visions, leave behind both the senses and
the intellectual activities, and all things sensible and intelligible, and
all things that are not and that are, and stretch yourself up unknowingly,
as far as is possible, toward the union of him who is above all being and
knowledge. For by the unrestrained and absolute ecstasy of yourself and of
all things, you will be raised purely toward the superessential ray of the
divine darkness, having stripped away all things and been released from
all.
<2> See to it that none of the uninitiated hear these things. And by these
I mean those who are entangled in the things that are, and imagine that
nothing is superessentially beyond the things that are, but suppose that
they know, by the knowledge that is according to themselves, him who has
made "darkness his hiding-place." And if the divine initiations are above
these, what would one say concerning those who are still more uninitiated,
whoever characterize even from the last among the things that are the
Cause that lies above all, and say that it in no way surpasses the godless
and many-shaped forms that they fashion for themselves? It is necessary
both to set upon it and to affirm all the positions of the things that
are, as Cause of all, and more properly to deny them all, as being
superessentially above all, and not to think that the negations are
opposed to the affirmations, but that far rather it itself is above the
privations, being above every both removal and position.
<3> Thus then the divine Bartholomew says that theology is both much and
least, and that the Gospel is broad and great and again concise. He seems
to me to have conceived that supernaturally, that the good Cause of all is
both many-worded and brief of speech at once, and without speech, as
having neither word nor intellection, because it lies superessentially
above all things, and is revealed without covering and truly only to those
who pass through all things both unclean and clean, and go beyond every
ascent of all holy summits, and leave behind all the divine lights and
sounds and heavenly words, and enter into "the gloom" "where" he truly
is, as the oracles say, who is beyond all. For not simply is the divine
Moses commanded first to be purified himself, and then to be separated
from those who are not such, and after every purification he hears the
many-voiced trumpets and sees many lights flashing forth pure and widely
poured rays; then he is separated from the many and with the chosen
priests he reaches the summit of the divine ascents. And even in these he
does not come to be with God himself, but he contemplates not him (for he
is unseen), but the place where he stood. (And this I think signifies that
the most divine and highest of the things seen and conceived are certain
hypothetical reasons of the things that lie under him who surpasses all,
through which his presence that is above every conception is shown,
visiting the intelligible summits of his most holy places.) And then he is
released even from the things seen and from those who see, and enters into
the gloom of unknowing that is truly mystical, in which he shuts off all
the cognitive apprehensions, and comes to be in the wholly intangible and
invisible, being wholly of him who is beyond all and of nothing, neither
of himself nor of another, but being united, by the complete inactivity of
all knowledge, to him who is wholly unknown, in a way that is better, and
knowing beyond mind by knowing nothing.
<II.>
According to this we pray to come to be in this superluminous gloom, and
through unseeing and unknowing to see and to know him who is beyond sight
and knowledge, by the very not seeing and not knowing (for this is truly
to see and to know), and to hymn the superessential superessentially
through the removal of all the things that are, just as those who make a
statue that is of itself, taking away all the hindrances that overlay the
pure vision of what is hidden, and by the removal alone revealing the
hidden beauty itself in itself. And it is necessary, as I think, to hymn
the removals in the opposite way to the positions. For those we set down
beginning from the first and descending through the middle things to the
last; but here, from the last toward the most principal, we remove as we
ascend beyond, that we may know that unknowing which is veiled by all the
known things in all the things that are, and that we may see that
superessential gloom which is hidden by all the light that is in the
things that are.
<III.>
In the Theological Outlines, then, we hymned the most principal things of
cataphatic theology: how the divine and good nature is called unific, how
triadic; what is the fatherhood and sonship spoken of according to it;
what the theology of the Spirit wishes to make clear; how from the
immaterial and undivided Good the inmost lights of goodness sprang forth,
and by the outgrowth have remained ungone from the coeternal remaining in
it and in themselves and in one another; how the superessential Jesus was
substantiated in the truths of human nature, and whatever else has been
made manifest from the oracles has been hymned according to the
Theological Outlines. And in On the Divine Names, how he is named Good,
how Being, how Life and Wisdom and Power, and whatever else belongs to
intelligible divine naming. And in the Symbolic Theology, what are the
transferred names from the sensible things upon the divine, what the
divine forms, what the divine shapes and parts and instruments, what the
divine places and worlds, what the angers, what the griefs and the wraths,
what the drunkennesses and the debauches, what the oaths and what the
curses, what the sleeps and what the wakings, and as many other sacredly
fashioned formations as belong to the symbolic divine typing.
And I think you have also seen how the last things are more many-worded
than the first. For the Theological Outlines and the unfolding of the
Divine Names had to be more brief of speech than the Symbolic Theology.
Since the more we look up toward the upward, so much the more are the
words contracted by the synopses of the intelligibles; just as even now,
entering into the gloom that is beyond mind, we shall find not brevity of
speech, but complete speechlessness and mindlessness. And there, the word,
descending from above toward the last things, was broadened according to
the quantity of the descent toward a proportionate multitude; but now,
ascending from below toward that which lies above, it is contracted
according to the measure of the ascent, and after every ascent it will be
wholly voiceless and will be wholly united to the unutterable.
And why at all, you say, having set the divine positions from the first,
do we begin the divine removal from the last? Because, setting that which
is above every position, it was necessary to set the hypothetical
affirmation from that which is more kindred to it; but removing that which
is above every removal, to remove from those things that stand more
distant from it. Or is it not more Life and Goodness than air and stone?
And is it not more that he does not revel and does not rage, than that he
is not spoken and is not conceived?
<IV.>
We say then that the Cause of all, being also above all, is neither
without being nor without life, neither without reason nor without mind;
nor is it a body, nor shape, nor form, nor has it quality or quantity or
bulk; nor is it in a place, nor is it seen, nor has it sensible contact;
nor does it perceive, nor is it perceptible; nor has it disorder and
disturbance, being troubled by material passions, nor is it powerless,
being subject to sensible accidents, nor is it in want of light; nor
alteration or corruption or division or privation or flux, nor anything
else of the things of sense, neither is it nor has it.
<V.>
And again ascending we say that it is neither soul nor mind; nor has it
imagination or opinion or word or intellection; nor is it word nor
intellection, nor is it spoken nor conceived; nor is it number nor order,
nor greatness nor smallness, nor equality nor inequality, nor likeness or
unlikeness; nor does it stand nor is it moved nor does it keep quiet; nor
has it power, nor is it power, nor light; nor does it live nor is it life;
nor is it being, nor age, nor time; nor is there intelligible contact of
it, nor knowledge; nor is it truth, nor kingship, nor wisdom; nor one, nor
unity, nor divinity or goodness; nor is it spirit as we know it, nor
sonship nor fatherhood, nor anything else of the things known to us or to
any other of the beings; nor is it anything of the things that are not,
nor anything of the things that are; nor do the things that are know it as
it is, nor does it know the things that are as they are; nor is there word
of it, nor name, nor knowledge; nor is it darkness nor light, nor error
nor truth; nor is there of it altogether position nor removal, but making
the positions and removals of the things after it, we neither set it nor
remove it, since the complete and unific Cause of all is above every
position, and the preeminence of that which is simply released from all
and beyond the wholes is above every removal.
