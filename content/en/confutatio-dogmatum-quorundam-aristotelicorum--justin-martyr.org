OF THE HOLY JUSTIN, PHILOSOPHER AND MARTYR
A REFUTATION OF CERTAIN ARISTOTELIAN DOCTRINES.
Of the goods that come about by human effort toward the service of God,
nothing is so acceptable to God as to strive according to one's power to make
men better. Seeing therefore this zeal in you, O most honored presbyter Paul,
to be exceedingly fervent, I readily obeyed you concerning the things you
commanded me, to make some brief selection of the Greek doctrines concerning
God and creation, not in order that you might know anything true from them
(for how could he who has from above the teaching concerning these things
through the prophets from God himself who made the creation?), but in order
that you might know that they have not done this according to demonstrative
science, according to which the Greeks profess to make their accounts
concerning God and creation, but have determined what seemed good by
conjecture. Of the men who have made accounts concerning God and creation,
some received the teaching concerning God and creation from God himself who
made the creation, through the prophets, who ministered to God for this, whom
he first showed worthy of being believed by the divine works that came about
through them, and then in this way furnished the teaching concerning the
things unclear to those being taught; but others, disbelieving the words
through the prophets, entrusted the discovery of the knowledge of God to their
own reasonings. And according to those who have known the distinction from the
teaching of God and of creation, God is one, being ungenerated according to
either manner of ungeneratedness, having neither God nor gods before himself
nor after himself, having no co-eternal subject nor opposite, having
incorruptible essence and unimpeded activity, being craftsman of the whole
world, whose will is the beginning of its being and of its being such and of
its remaining as it does. For either the parts of the world remain in
incorruption, as heaven and the heavenly things and the invisible powers, or
in coming-to-be and passing-away, as the animals and plants upon earth. And
just as what has come to be would not have come to be unless he had commanded
Let it be, so neither would it have remained unless he had set the command,
for the incorruptibles that they stand unto the age of the age, and for those
in generation and corruption Grow and multiply and fill the earth. And toward
a showing of his divine power and that he is not a slave to a law of nature,
but works what seems good by authority of will, he made at the beginning those
of the nature in generation and corruption from the earth and the waters,
commanding: Let the earth bring forth living soul according to kind and plants
having in themselves the seed and trees making fruits; and again: Let the
waters bring forth living soul according to kind; but those generated from the
race-beginners he ordained to come to be from the sowing of the seed.

And concerning these things all the prophets sent from God to all men
continued thinking the same things, and disagreement among them has come about
none; but according to those who disbelieved the words of the prophets, and
determined what seemed good concerning God and creation by their own
conjecture, there is some great disagreement both toward one another and
toward themselves in the things concerning the beings and their principle
according to essence and number and motion and limit, as is shown from the
first book of the Physics of Aristotle, together with his saying nothing true
concerning the matters he has undertaken to determine.
α. From the first book of the Physics of Aristotle.
For there is always something that underlies, from which what comes to be
comes to be, as plants and animals from seed. And the things that come to be
come to be simply some by reshaping, as a statue, some by addition, as the
things that grow, some by subtraction, as Hermes from the stone, some by
composition, as a house, some by alteration, as the things that change
according to the matter. And all the things that come to be thus it is clear
come to be from underlying things. And this is twofold: for either the
underlying or the opposite. And I mean by opposite the unmusical, and by
underlying the man.
If first is the sowing seed and later what comes to be from seed and both are
generated, then to the generation of what is laid down as coming to be from
seed the seed underlies, but to the generation of the sower it is not possible
for the seed to underlie; therefore it is not always that animals and plants
come to be from seed. Further if each is a generated animal, the sower and
what comes to be from seed, and each is not from the same underlying, but the
one from the seed, the other not from the seed, how is it not false that
animals always come to be from seed? And concerning plants the same account.
Further if the principle of what comes to be from seed is the sower, and this
is unknown and undefined in the treatise of the Physics of Aristotle, for the
sake of what was this treatise written, the principle of the first animal and
plant being unknown? For ignorance of the things from a principle follows upon
ignorance of the principle. If the animal that comes to be from seed comes to
be according to nature, it is clear that of the same animal coming to be, but
not from seed, it does not come to be according to nature. Let the
Aristotelians say then according to what the animal comes to be that does not
come to be according to nature. Further if a difference according to
generation of the animal has been given, both of that from seed and according
to nature and of that coming to be neither from seed nor according to nature,
it is necessary that generation be limited, how then do Greeks say that
animals and plants are co-unbegun with God, those that have come to be
according to nature, which can be neither contemporaneous with the first
animal and plant? Further if everything that comes to be is always composite,
how have you distinguished the manner of the things coming to be according to
composition by the proper difference of the manner from the other manners of
generation? For if every generated thing is com

posite, it is necessary that it come to be according to composition; but if
the manner of generation according to composition fits every generation, it
has been distinguished superfluously from the other manners of generation by
its proper difference. Further if the things underlying the said generations
are essences and in them the twofold of the underlying is observed, that is
the underlying and the opposite, but matter is not essence, how does the
manner of generation of the things coming to be from essences fit the manner
of generation of the things not coming to be from essence? It is necessary
therefore either that matter be essence, or that the things coming to be from
matter come to be in another manner besides the said manners of generation.
But since in the treatise of the Physics matter has been determined not to be
essence and the manner of generation of the things coming to be from it has
not been handed down, how is this treatise not superfluous, in which the clear
is aporetic? Further if, as it is necessary for nature and art not to make
chance things from the chance but the determined from the determined, so it is
necessary also for God to make the determined from the determined, who is it
that set this limit for God? And that God set this limit for nature and art is
clear. But if God is not under the limit, but makes chance things from the
chance, not being hindered by the necessity of the limit from making what he
wills, how has he not been freed also from the limit of making from underlying
things by necessity?
β. Of the same Aristotle from the same book.
And the underlying is one in number, but two in form. For the man and the gold
and matter in general is countable; for it is rather a this something, and not
according to accident does what comes to be come to be from it; but the
privation and the contrariety is accident; and the form is one, as the order,
the music, or some other of the things thus predicated. Through which it is in
a way to be said that the principles are two, and in a way three; and it is in
a way the contraries, as if one should say the musical and the unmusical or
the hot and the cold or the attuned and the unattuned, and it is in a way not;
for it is impossible for the contraries to be acted on by one another. And
this too is solved by the underlying being something else; for this is not
contrary. So that neither are the principles more than the contraries in a
certain way, but two as one might say in number, nor again altogether two
because of the other thing existing as their being, but three.
If the underlying is one in number, but two in form, from which it is
necessary that one underlie the one, how is the underlying not itself
underlying to itself? But if this is impossible, how is it not false that the
underlying is two in form? Further if something of the things coming to be
must always underlie, and this is matter, how is matter not always generated?
For it always underlies the one of the contraries. For either it underlies the
privation or the form that is contrary to the privation; and by the form it
becomes formed, and by the privation formless. How then is matter not
generated essence, always having its being generatedly? Further if privation
is nothing

else besides the taking-away of every being, and matter has this, how has not
even its being matter been taken away? Further if when the form of matter is
absent there is absent from it also its being essence and the this something
and the being, then matter is nowhere when its form is absent. Further if
matter is countable, but the contraries that have happened to it are not
countable, how are there three principles, both matter and the things that
have happened to it? If nothing of the things we observe coming to be in the
constitution of natures is from matter, neither the qualitative nor the
quantitative, how do the things coming to be come to be from it in themselves?
If it is impossible for something to be and not to be a this something, how
through the privation does matter, not being a this something, have being? If
the privation and the form have happened to matter, but matter is not essence,
how have the contraries happened to it? For accidents happen to essence. If as
order is in the ordered, so also form is in matter, how do the unordered
things before the order have the being a this something, but matter before the
form, not having the being this, is the analogy of order toward form saved in
matter? If in the composition of the things coming to be two principles only
are observed, both matter and form, but apart from composition there are three
principles, both matter and privation and form, it is clear then that when
what comes to be was not, three principles were laid down; but when what comes
to be is, not three but two. How then is what comes to be not a principle of
the destruction of the one principle, which if it had not been destroyed, what
comes to be would not have come to be? If as the man is acted on by the
musical and the unmusical and the body by cold and hot and the voice by
harmony and disharmony, so matter is acted on by form and privation, let
matter be essence, as man and the body, or accident, as the voice, in order
that the consequence of the analogy be saved. But if matter is neither essence
nor accident, then neither is it acted on by the contraries.
γ. Of the same from the same book.
How many then the principles of the physical things concerning generation are,
and how, has been said; and it is clear that something must underlie the
contraries and that the contraries are two. But in some other manner it is not
necessary; for the one of the contraries is sufficient to make the change by
presence and absence. And the underlying nature is knowable according to
analogy. For as bronze toward a statue or wood toward a bed or matter and the
formless toward some other of the things having shape, before it takes the
shape, so this stands toward essence and the this something and the being. One
principle then is this, not being one in this way nor one as the this
something, but one in account, and still the contrary to this, the privation.
But whether form is essence or the underlying is not yet clear.
If form by its presence informs matter and by its absence makes it formless,
and there are two principles, both the form and the formless, and privation
also makes these very things by its

presence and its absence, the principles will therefore be coming to be and
being taken away by one another and not eternal; for the eternal has no need
of some presence and absence toward being. If the change of the principles
comes to be by the presence and absence of the contraries, it is clear that
the change comes to be from their being principles. How then can they remain
principles, who have cast off being principles? If the principle that has a
principle is not a principle, how can matter, having form as principle of
being, and privation of not being, be a principle? To name nature that which
underlies privation, being neither essence nor this something nor being, how
is it not superfluous? If principles are neither from others nor from one
another, but privation is from the absence of form and form from the absence
of privation, how are form and privation not principles from one another? For
by the absence of one another they are productive of one another.
If the account that bronze has toward the statue that is going to come to be
from it, this matter has toward essence and the this something and the being,
how can bronze, if it is not essence and this something and being, not have an
account toward the statue, but matter, being neither essence nor this
something nor being, can have an analogy toward bronze? If all the things
observed in constitutions of physical things are knowable by sense, how is
matter, from which the sensibles are, knowable by analogy and not by sense, or
how is it possible at all for the sensibles to come to be from a non-sensible?
If being a this something is also being one principle, how does matter have
being one principle, but not have being a this something? And if being a this
something and not being a this something is essence, how is matter
not-being-a-this-something, but is not essence? And how can anyone cast matter
out of being one, who has divided it into two forms, into that which underlies
what comes to be and into the opposite? If it is not yet clear from the things
said whether form is essence or the underlying, how has he said assertorily
concerning matter that it is not essence nor this something nor being? For he
has said that the underlying is matter. At one time that it is not essence nor
this something nor being, and at another that it is unclear whether it is
essence, how is this not of those who do not know what they say?
δ. From the same book.
Those first according to philosophy who sought the truth and the nature of the
beings were turned aside as if pushed off some other road by inexperience, and
they say that none of the beings either comes to be or passes away because it
is necessary that what comes to be come to be either from being or from
not-being, and from both of these it is impossible; for neither does being
come to be (for it already is) and from not-being nothing would come to be
(for something must underlie). And so then increasing the next consequence
they say that there are not even many things but only being itself.
These too are sufficient to bear witness to the truth of what was

said by us concerning the philosophers among the Greeks, that they have not
made their accounts concerning the beings according to demonstrative science,
but have determined what seemed good by conjecture.
ε. From the same book.
But we say that to come to be from being or not-being, or that not-being or
being does something or is acted on or that anything becomes this, in one
manner does not differ at all from the physician doing something or being
acted on or something being or coming to be from a physician; so that since
this is said in two ways or in more, it is clear that also from being and
being either doing or being acted on. The physician then builds, not qua
physician but qua builder, and becomes white, not qua physician but qua black;
but he heals and becomes unphysician, qua physician. And since we say most of
all that the physician properly does something or is acted on or comes to be
from a physician, if he does or suffers these things qua physician, it is
clear that also not coming to be from being signifies this, qua not-being.
Which those men, not having divided, stood off, and through this ignorance
they were so further ignorant as to think that nothing of the others comes to
be nor is, but to take away all generation. But we ourselves also say that
nothing comes to be simply from not-being, yet nevertheless it comes to be
from not-being, as according to accident; for from privation, which is in
itself not-being, not being present in it, it comes to be. And this is
wondered at and seems thus impossible, that something come to be from
not-being. Likewise neither from being nor does being come to be, except
according to accident; and thus this too comes to be in the same manner, as if
from animal an animal would come to be and from some animal some animal, as if
a dog should come to be from a horse. For the dog would come to be not only
from some animal, but also from animal, but not qua animal; for this already
belongs; but if something is going to come to be an animal not according to
accident, it will not be from animal, and if something being, not from being
nor from not-being. For from not-being has been said by us what it signifies,
that qua not-being. And further we do not take away that everything either is
or is not.
If Neither from simply being nor from simply not-being is it possible for
something to come to be is common to those who from inexperience and ignorance
take away generation, and to this one who has corrected those who err, how has
generation not been taken away according to the one who corrected, as it has
been taken away according to those who err? If it is impossible for something
to come to be according to accident from being and from not-being, the
generation of what comes to be in itself not being thought beforehand, as it
is impossible for the physician to become white unless the black becomes
white, and it is impossible for the physician to build unless the builder
builds, if the physician is black and a builder, how does something come to be
according to accident from being and from not-being, nothing previously coming
to be in itself? If first the animal, and later the animal from animal and
some animal from some animal, and to the generation of the animal from animal
the seed underlies, in power an animal, but in actuality not an

animal, but to the generation of simply animal the underlying was not seed,
how is the animal coming to be from not-animal not coming to be contrary to
nature? If before what comes to be came to be, matter was, but what comes to
be from it was not, what was matter? As if there was matter of the horse
before the horse came to be, if all the things observed in the essence of the
horse are generated, how was matter ungenerated, from which a horse has come
to be? If in itself neither privation is being nor matter, but accident
happens to what is being in itself, how is it possible according to accident
for something to come to be from privation, which is neither in itself nor
according to accident? Of things that being disjoined it is impossible for
something to come to be from none of them, of these neither when they have
been combined is it possible for something to come to be; as from simply being
it is not possible for something to come to be, likewise also from simply
not-being, that we may use the voices of Aristotle. How have both become
possible, both coming to be from being and coming to be from not-being? If the
underlying, from which what comes to be comes to be, must underlie the
contraries, then does not something come to be from privation according to
accident when it does not underlie the contraries? He who does not take away
the all either to be or not to be, but grants the all to be and not to be, how
can he speak truly saying that matter is neither essence nor this something
nor being? If what comes to be comes to be, not from simply not-being nor from
simply being, but from both, then it comes to be both from matter as from
being and from not-being, and as from being a this something and not being a
this something. But if these things are so, then what was said concerning
matter is false, that it is neither essence nor this something nor being. As
matter is formless, so also form is matterless. But if matter and its
formlessness are two principles, then form also and its matterlessness will be
two principles. How then, the principles being four, were three only set down
as principles.
. From the same book.
For we say that matter and privation are other, and of these the one is
not-being according to accident, matter, but privation even in itself, and the
one near and somehow essence, but the other in no way. But they say that
not-being is, the great and the small likewise, the both-together as one or
each separately. So that this manner of the triad is altogether other than
that one.
If according to accident it belongs to matter to be not-being, it is clear
that according to accident matter is neither matter nor a principle; for being
matter and being a principle belongs to being. And if according to accident it
belongs to matter to be not-being through the privation, it is clear that
according to accident it is being through the form. Therefore it will belong
to matter never to be being in itself, but always according to accident, at
one time to be not-being, at another to be being. How then is it not absurd to
say that that which is never being in itself is near essence and somehow
essence? If it is one thing to be matter and another to be matter of
something,

and matter is deprived of being matter of something, but is not deprived of
being matter, then matter will be being and not-being, being through its being
matter, not-being through its not being matter of something. But if matter is
matter of nothing, then matter is nothing of formlessness. But if matter is of
formlessness, then matter never was qua matter, but always matter of
something, at one time of formlessness, at another of form. And that
formlessness is something is clear from its having been set as a principle of
the things coming to be. But if these things are so, then matter is never
not-being, but always being, at one time formless, at another formed;
wherefore also always generated. If matter can cast off its co-eternal
formlessness, then it can also cast off its co-eternal ungeneratedness. But if
not the second, then not the first. How then does what comes to be come to be
from matter, matter not casting off its co-eternal formlessness? If, as the
manner of the triad according to Aristotle is other besides the manner of the
triad according to Plato, so also the triad is other, how is the hexad not a
principle of the things coming to be but the triad? And if the triad is not
other, how is its manner other?
ζ. From the same book.
For the remaining one is co-cause with the shape of the things coming to be,
as a mother; but the other portion of the contrariety would often be imagined,
by one fixing his thought toward its evil-doing, not even to be at all. For
there being something divine and good and to-be-desired, we say that the one
is contrary to it, and the other is of a nature to desire and reach after it
according to its own nature. But to these it happens that the contrary reaches
after its own destruction. And yet neither can form desire itself because it
is not in need, nor the contrary; for the contraries are destructive of one
another. But this is matter, as if female of male and ugly of beautiful;
except not ugly in itself, but according to accident, nor female, but
according to accident. And it is destroyed and comes to be in a way, and in a
way not. For as the in-which, it is destroyed in itself; for what is destroyed
in this is the privation; but as according to power, not in itself, but it is
necessary that it be incorruptible and ungenerated. For if it came to be,
something must first underlie, that from which being present in it; and this
is the nature itself, so that it will be before it comes to be. For I call
matter the first underlying to each, from which something comes to be being
present in it not according to accident. And if it is destroyed, it will
arrive at this last, so that it will be destroyed before it is destroyed.
If, as matter and form are a principle of the things coming to be, so also
privation, how is matter co-cause with the shape of the things coming to be,
but privation not co-cause? If form is a principle for this reason, since from
it being belongs to what comes to be, it is clear that privation also is a
principle for this reason, since through it not-being belongs to what comes to
be. How then is privation called a principle of the things coming to

be? If, when form is present, then privation does not remain, it is clear that
when form is not present privation remains. How then is it not absurd to say
that what remains is not? For remaining belongs to being. If privation is the
other portion of the contrariety, and the evil-doing of this is the form, how
is form not both good and evil, good to matter, as through it there belongs to
matter being essence, but evil to privation, as being taken away through it?
If there is something divine and good and to-be-desired, and it has the
contrary which is neither good nor to-be-desired, then the divine will be both
good and not good, both to-be-desired and not to-be-desired, wherefore also
both divine and not divine. But if this is absurd, then the divine the good
and to-be-desired has not something contrary; for the simply good is good to
all, not to some only. If the contrary of the good does not desire the good
(for it desires destruction of its own nature, if it desires the good) and
form does not desire itself (for form is without need), and the divine is
to-be-desired and to-be-reached-after, but to what is it to-be-desired and
to-be-reached-after if not to matter? Then matter is ensouled; for to desire
and to reach after belongs to what is ensouled. But if matter is ensouled, how
is it not false that matter is not-being according to accident? For the soul
is being, and what has soul is being. If, as female desires male and as ugly
the beautiful, matter desires the good, and matter is not in itself female and
ugly, but according to accident, it is clear that neither in itself does it
desire the good, but according to accident. Then the divine also will not be
in itself good and to-be-desired and to-be-reached-after, but according to
accident; which it is absurd to say. If matter is generated through the form,
but corruptible through the privation, and ungenerated through itself and
incorruptible, then matter will always be corruptible or generated, but
incorruptible and ungenerated never; for it always either underlies privation
being destroyed in it, or the form coming to be in it. The ungenerated in the
case of generated things signifies the privation of generation that is able to
be, but in the case of eternal things it signifies the negation of all
generation both that has already come to be and that is going to be; as the
bronze that is going to be a statue, before it becomes a statue, is called
ungenerated according to privation, because it can become a statue; but in the
case of God the ungenerated signifies a negation of all generation according
to essence both that has already come to be and that is going to be. There
being then two manners of ungeneratedness, according to which is matter
ungenerated? If according to privation, then matter is ungenerated as the
bronze, but if matter is ungenerated according to negation, then matter
becomes nothing; nor can matter have toward essence the account that bronze
has toward the statue, according as Aristotle said. If matter is ungenerated
as God is, and God can make something from the ungenerated, it is clear that
God can also make something from simply not-being. For the unfitness that
not-being has toward something not coming to be from it according to nature
and according to art, the

same the eternally ungenerated also has toward something coming to be from it
according to nature and according to art. How then was God able to make
something from the eternally ungenerated, but from simply not-being was he not
able? But clearly the disbelief that God can make something from not-being
pushed the first philosophers into a denial of all generation of the beings
from matter, and Aristotle who corrected this into a taking-away of the
generation of matter. If there is something coming to be and there is
something which this comes to be, according as he said in the things above,
then matter is none of these, if it is ungenerated and incorruptible. If
matter is ungenerated and incorruptible, of necessity it is ungenerated
according to either manner of ungeneratedness; then neither does something
come to be from it nor is it destroyed in it. How then is privation destroyed
in an incorruptible? If matter is one, but the things coming to be from it are
many and different, how is it necessary that matter remain ungenerated, its
always-divisible not being able to remain undivided? If matter is one in
number, but two in form, and is ungenerated and incorruptible, then either it
is wholly ungenerated and incorruptible or the half of it. But if it is wholly
ungenerated and incorruptible, it is clear that the half of it also is such;
but if the half of it is ungenerated and incorruptible and the half of it is
not such, then the contradiction concerning the same will be true; which is
impossible. If that which receives the form either becomes that or comes to be
from that, how is matter ungenerated receiving the form? If to be ungenerated
and incorruptible belongs to beings, how is matter both not being and
ungenerated and incorruptible? If the ungeneratedly being in the ungenerated
is ungenerated also this, how does the privation that is ungenerated in matter
come to be and pass away? If, as has been said, the one of the contraries is
sufficient by its own presence and absence to make the change, what is it that
is changed, matter being ungenerated and incorruptible? If the ungenerated and
incorruptible is observed about the essence of the ungenerated and
incorruptible being, where is the ungenerated and incorruptible of matter,
matter not being essence? If bronze were not generated according to its
generation that has already come to be, neither would a statue have come to
be. How then does matter come to be or something from matter, matter not
having come to be beforehand? If bronze were not destroyed, neither would the
brazen be destroyed. How then is the material destroyed, matter being
incorruptible? If a body is not completed from the incorporeals, how has a
body come to be from matter and form, being in themselves incorporeal? If by
physical necessity the undivided and the unchangeable follow the ungenerated
and incorruptible, how is the ungenerated and incorruptible matter in the
forms divided according to kinds, and how do the things that change according
to the matter come to be according to alteration, as he determined in the
setting-out of the manners of generation in the things above?
η. From the second book of the Physics of Aristotle.
In one manner then nature is thus said, the first matter underlying each of

either those in which there is something contrary to nature are not eternal,
or it is false that among the eternal things nothing is contrary to nature. If
none of the ungenerated either what it has or what it does not have has or
does not have according to providence of someone, how, if heaven and the stars
are ungenerated, according to providence of nature do they not have an organ
of motion, but have a spherical shape instead of the organ, being more fit
toward the continuous motion than the organs of going?
ξα. From the same book.
The first being one moves many of the divine bodies, but the many being each
only one; for of the wandering any one is carried more locomotions. In this
then nature equalizes and makes some order, assigning to the one many bodies,
and to the one body many locomotions. For in many spheres the one sphere being
bound is carried, and each sphere happens to be a body.
If of the ungenerated and eternal essences there is no maker, how are these
eternal and ungenerated, of whose order nature is maker, assigning to the one
many bodies, and to the one body many locomotions, equalizing the multitudes
of the locomotions of the one body to the multitude of the bodies. If these
ungenerated are also unmade, how is it possible that among the ungenerated
there be a made physical order? A sphere bound to a sphere, having the same
essence which the sphere has to which it is bound, how does it not appear
having both the absurdities that do not exist among the eternal things, the
having in vain the movable essence according to which it is not moved, and the
being moved contrary to nature according to accident?
ξβ. From the third book On the Heavens.
Of the things said by nature some are essences, and some works and affections
of these; and I mean by essences the simple bodies, as fire and earth and
those coordinate with these, and as many as from these, both the whole heaven
and the portions of this, again both the animals and the plants and the
portions of these.
He who has said that heaven is from some simple element being other besides
the elements here, and that it is not possible that another heaven come to be
because this has embraced all the matter, how now does he say that heaven is
from the same elements, from which also the animals and the plants? But if he
says another heaven has come to be from other elements, how does he not lie
saying: It is not possible that there be another heaven besides one? But if he
says the same heaven at one time from another more divine element

besides those here and at another from the same elements, how is he not lying
in the one or the other?
ξγ. From the same book.
Concerning then the first of the elements it has been said, and of what sort
in nature, and that it is incorruptible and ungenerated.
If an element is that from which what comes to be comes to be being present in
it, how is it possible that the same be both element and ungenerated? If the
element is first and that from the element later, how is the element
ungenerated, from which heaven is, and heaven co-ungenerated with it? If the
element is ungenerated for this reason that it is not from an element, how is
heaven ungenerated which is from an element?
ξδ. From the same book.
Then to be eternal is impossible; for we see also fire and water and each of
the simple bodies being dissolved. So that it is necessary that the elements
of the bodies be generated and corruptible.
If earth also is an element of the bodies, how have you said the earth
co-eternal and co-ungenerated with heaven, but the other elements generated
and corruptible?
ξε. From the same book.
The generation of the elements is from one another.
How do the elements come to be from one another first not coming to be
according to the generation from one another? For this generation of necessity
leads the way of that generation; for they being they are changed into one
another and come to be from one another.
TO THE GREEKS.
α. The nature that makes the physical things, if it does not previously come
to be by that which makes not according to nature, neither exists nor makes.
β. That not simply every generated and corruptible thing has in generation the
four-part time, beginning and increase, prime and decline, but that which
comes to be by nature or art.
γ. If God is called by our names whatever he is called, it is not necessary
that there follow God through the name as many as follow our name. And God is
called both truth and good; and to truth the false is opposed, and to the good
the evil, but to God nothing is opposed. Plato not having understood this
dogmatized something contrary to God, an evil both necessary and eternal.
δ. Being is not not being this something, as a body is not not being this
body. But if matter does not have the being this, then neither does it have
the being; for the being is inseparable from the being this.
ε. Nature and art make from essences whatever they make, as from seed nature
makes the man and from man the seed; and essences are both the seed and the
man. Likewise also the arts make from the bronze a statue and from bricks a
house; and essences are both the bronze

and the bricks. Then matter not being essence, who is it that has made from it
the things that have come to be from it, nature and art being unable to make
something from not-essence?
. If, as Aristotle says, there is a body neither heavy nor light nor one of
the four elements nor from the four elements, it is clear that, if this is
true, there will be a body neither hot nor cold nor according to nature nor
according to affection; for the hot or cold according to nature or according
to affection is either of the four elements or is from the four elements. But
if there is not a body neither hot or cold according to nature nor according
to affection, then neither is there a body neither heavy nor light nor being
of the four elements nor being from the four elements. How then does he say
that the aether is hot through the motion of the heavenly body and of the
bodies being moved in it? For the bodies heating by the motions are heated
before those heated by them; but if it is heated, it is clear that it is
heated according to affection; and if according to affection, it is clear that
according to alteration; and if according to alteration, it is clear that
according to change; and if according to change, it is clear that it is
changed from the contrary into the contrary. And contrary to the cold is the
hot; then it is changed from the cold into the hot. But since there is not a
body being changed from the cold into the hot according to affection, not
being either of the four elements or from the four elements, through this the
heavenly body is not another element besides the four elements; for it is
heated according to affection being changed from the cold into the hot.
ζ. If the bodies heated from the motion are heated more by the more of the
motion, how, if according to Aristotle the sun is a body not hot according to
nature but according to motion, by the more of the motion more neither is it
heated nor does it heat, but always by the unequal of the motion it has the
equal of the heat?
η. If according to Aristotle the definition of essence is that which is
receptive of the contraries in turn, how does the same Aristotle say again
that matter is not essence, being receptive of the contraries in turn, for of
privation and of habit?
θ. If he who is deprived of the being this, this one has also been deprived of
the being, how does Aristotle say that it is impossible for something to come
to be from the altogether not-being, matter according to him being deprived of
both, both of the being and of the being this?
ι. If from the altogether not-being something does not come to be, it is clear
that from being something comes to be. But if matter is this, how does
Aristotle speak truly saying that matter is not-being?
ια. If matter is not altered, it is not possible for something to come to be
from it. But if everything being altered is altered from this into this, and
matter is not this, then it is not altered; wherefore neither does something
come to be from it.
ιβ. If from the chance into the chance there does not come to be

change, but into the contrary in the same kind, as in quality there does not
come to be change from the white into the great but into the black, according
to what then is matter changed in the generation of the essences coming to be
from it, having nothing able to be changed?
ιγ. If, as in the animal-things the animal and in the color-things the color,
so also in the material-things the matter, how when the animal-things are
taken away the animal is taken away together and when the color-things are
taken away the color is taken away, and when the material-things are taken
away the matter is not taken away? But if the matter is taken away together
with the material-things, how does matter preexist the material-things?
ιδ. If the standing-off and the partaking belong to the beings, it is clear
that the altogether not-being neither stands off from something nor partakes.
How then does matter partake of privation, and is not of the beings?
ιε. If it is impossible that the same in the same be moved both the motions,
both that according to nature and that contrary to nature, how is the sun
moved according to nature from rising to setting, but contrary to nature from
setting to rising through the sphere? For it is not possible that the same in
the same be moved the contrary motions according to nature.
ι . Of the sun not being the eye was superfluous, and of the eye not being the
sun was superfluous. But since through each the other is necessary, then each
is generated; for the ungenerated is ungenerated through nothing, but through
itself only, or rather not through itself; for the uncaused is uncaused.
ιζ. If it is impossible time being that what has come to be come to be
according to cause, but not according to time, how do the Greeks say that the
world has come to be according to cause and not according to time?
ιη. If it is impossible that the world be without a year, how is the world
eternal and ungenerated, the year not being eternal but having its being from
a period of more days?
ιθ. If the year is generated, how are the things in the year eternal and
ungenerated?
