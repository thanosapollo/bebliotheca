OF JOHN THE CLERIC AND CUBICULARIUS KAMINATES ON THE CAPTURE OF THESSALONICA
1.1 How excellent among goods is love of labor, witnessing in itself the
acquisition of every virtue. 1.2 And in you, among your other advantages, this
love of labor has been made plain even in this, O best and most learned of
men, Gregory: both in what you write to us and in what you seek to learn from
us by letters. 1.3 For it is a display of no small virtue and laborious zeal
to wish to have received knowledge of many affairs, and especially of those
through which the soul conceives the fear of God and, as it were, brings some
needful starting-points from this into the contemplative part of the mind,
from which it is taught to turn aside from sin as the maker of death, and to
cling to virtue, whose end is eternal life for those who choose it. 1.4 But I
for a time thought I should put off fulfilling the command, for two reasons:
judging it burdensome to write to you about such things, a man of such
stature, whose words steer his deeds, and whose deeds, agreeing with his
words, give exactness to one another (for already rumor cries your perfection
in all things, and has made you known to our ears even while you are silent);
and because I could explain well nothing of the matters you inquired into,
putting forward my own ignorance as a seemly defense, so as not to insult or
diminish by speech the splendor of the affairs you asked after, and of those
affairs that have all but filled with the hearing the whole world under the
sun. 1.5 But since this too came upon me, along with what has already been
said, that I might be required to pay the penalty of disobedience in addition
to my other debts (disobedience being by nature a terrible and destructive
passion, and the very thing that from the beginning persuaded our first
parents to despise the divine law), I come to fulfill what was asked, taking
courage from your fair-minded and most compassionate friendship. 1.6 For I
know well that neither by the technical methods nor indeed by the scientific
balances of your pure and great wisdom will the ungraceful and ill-fitted
quality of this discourse, or the sense of what is said, be rejected; but
rather that the history of what will be told, going forward true and alien to
every falsehood and fiction, will be released from all blame. 2.1 You asked to
learn through the letter the manner in which we inhabit the prison, having
been given over into barbarian hands, and how we exchanged a foreign land for
our own, of what fatherland we are, and what things belong to it. 2.2 For you
said you inferred from that brief converse, through which we were together
with you a little while as you were passing through Tripolis, that we were
full of a long narrative, and that both what had already befallen us and what
was expected later were great things and beyond all tragedy. 2.3 For you
yourself happened then to be wandering in the same misfortune as we, and were
taking along with you certain captives there, whom you said were Cappadocians
of the same fatherland as yourself, with whom you were also being sent toward
the city of Antioch, relating that your pains had nowhere come to a stand. 2.4
At which time you also saw us, as you yourself testify, still having the
freshness of grief and the traces of the former fear seated on our faces, so
that even in silence we cried out the disaster from our color. 2.5 But if I
should wish to go through these things one by one, I would make the discourse
long and not suited to the need, overstepping the law of due measure, not to
say that I would also weary your unwearied hearing, even though you have a
desire to listen to what is said. 2.6 But since, then, you promised this at
the outset, to listen with understanding, I begin our affairs thus. 3.1 We, O
friend, are of Thessalonica as our fatherland; for I will first make her known
to you, through whom


I came to know many things I had not yet known. 3.2 And having only just
become known to you, I am now required to give knowledge concerning that very
city, a great city and first of the Macedonians, conspicuous in the other
things by which a city is glorified, and yielding to none of those nearest
even to appear equal to her, but more conspicuous still in the piety which she
received from the beginning and, having received it, has preserved until now.
3.3 For Thessalonica boasts that she has Paul himself as teacher of piety, the
vessel of election, who, having embraced the inhabited world with the gospel
preaching in a circuit from Jerusalem even as far as Illyricum, sowed the seed
of the knowledge of God more in her and was eager that the fruit of faith be
rendered abundant. 3.4 And after him the great among martyrs and admirable
among prize-winners, Demetrius the myrrh-gusher, himself also laying down a
great contest on behalf of piety (for besides the other boasts of virtue he
still shone in divine teachings and was adorned with exactness concerning the
doctrines, from which the glory concerning him was spread still more to the
ends of the earth). 3.5 Then all the archbishops in succession, clinging
firmly to the word of salvation, by whom she, being always fitted and schooled
in the more divine instruction, sent away demonic and abominable errors and
opinions and idolatrous idle-talk to those who honor the demons, and having
sealed the faith with orthodox teaching, and never at all mixing any weedy and
heretical and alien plant with the divine seed, she preserved piety pure and
unadulterated. 3.6 Of which there were many other marks as well, from which
rumor, taking its starting-points, proclaimed her name brilliant, but this one
thing was shown first and most peculiar: that she both is and is named
orthodox, and is glorified by this more than by the rest. 3.7 But since you
also sought laboriously to learn the things belonging to that city itself, and
this is hard to undertake both for me and, to the present impulse of the
discourse, all but inconsistent, I will add a few things, and those by which
it is most possible for you to seem to see the facts of what is said, and then
I will turn to our own disaster. 4.1 The city, then, as has been said, is both
great and broad, fortified with walls and frequent outworks and furnishing
safety to the inhabitants so far as comes from the building of these, having a
sea gulf spread out from the south, by which, being washed along its side
face, it offers the approach to itself easy of access to the merchant ships
flowing in from every quarter. 4.2 For a marvelous harbor is hollowed out
there, safely laying the entrance on its back for sailors, wholly untried by
disturbances from winds and rendering the anchorage waveless. 4.3 This the
craftsman cut off from the rest of the sea; for, having barred the inflow of
the waters in the middle by a certain wall, he barred off together, from the
seaward side, the surge of the winds as well. 4.4 For the sea, being blown in
the deep and vomiting its run toward the dry land, being hindered by the
barrier of the wall there, having nothing on which to let loose its threat,
goes to the parts on either side of the wall, and the water, having slipped
somewhat gently, keeps such an anchorage outside every squall. 4.5 And the
gulf, cut off from the great sea by a certain neck, thrown forward in the
manner of an elbow and reaching a very great length through the waters, which
those of the country name Ekbolos because it has been thrown out far into the
waters, and being narrowed toward the opposite dry land by this cutting of the
neck, becomes another open-sea harbor. 4.6 From which elbow as far as the very
wall of the city the water imitates a most seemly circle, stretching far on
the parts on either side and contracting little by little toward the city. 4.7
And the gulf looks from the south toward the rest of the sea, and the harbor
likewise looks toward it, and the city toward the harbor. 4.8 But the harbor
is enclosed in four corners, while the gulf is rounded, having its hollow
going through a very great depth, and the extremities even before


much of the dry land having drawn in most of the waters. 5.1 But in these
things is the southern part of the city; the northern is exceedingly rough and
hard to travel. 5.2 For a mountain peeps over, standing up above the hills,
lifting not a little part even of the city itself above the rest of the land,
so that one part of it is level and suited to the needs of the inhabitants,
while the other is stretched up in the knolls and the heights of the
mountains. 5.3 But the extension of the mountain does no harm to the city so
as to give enemies leave to come upon it from a height and fight down from the
wall; for the knoll there has given a little leisure toward being guarded
against every plot, having beaten off easy assault by the space in between.
5.4 From there the mountain is stretched up a long way and is seated above,
growing rough with hills and ravines, looking toward the midmost east, but as
far as it extends, pressing toward the east, so far it flees the lands lying
to the side on either part. 5.5 For on the two flanks of this mountain, I mean
both the southern and the northern, plains are spread underneath, passable and
useful, granting every starting-point of good living to the citizens. 5.6 Of
which the one toward the south of the mountain, and toward the east of the
city, is exceedingly all-beautiful and lovely. 5.7 For it is adorned with
spreading trees, varied gardens, countless waters, some of springs, some of
rivers, which the thickets of the mountain grant to the plain and also greet
the sea itself. 5.8 For vines planted beside one another crown the fields and
urge the beauty-loving eye toward gladness by the multitude of the fruits. 5.9
And many and frequent monasteries of monks, inhabiting both the reclining of
the mountain and what lies under of the rest of the land, and seated in
certain most delightful places, have come to be a novel thing both for
travelers and for the citizens themselves. =5.10= And the plain on the left
flank of the mountain is itself also stretched to a great length, and is
widened as far as other mountains; in the midst of which two broad lakes lie
like a sea, occupying most of it and themselves also contributing a great use.
=5.11= For they nourish fish both small and great, various in kinds and many
in multitude, filling a most abundant table both for the neighboring villages
and for the city itself. =5.12= For the lakes, as it were, contend with the
sea by their gifts of these, and they vie which of them may furnish the more.
=5.13= Yet each is beaten by each, and the winner has no one on whom to vote
the defeat. =5.14= And the rest of the plain is given over to the farmers, is
yielded to the animals, both those that obey the arts of men and those that
send a free track into the mountains. =5.15= For deer, having left the
mountains and as it were delighting in the waters at the lakes, both have
drink in plenty, and, herding together with the cattle in it, they bring
forward the food in common. 6.1 But having sufficiently in these things
described both the east and north of the city, and the south as well, let us,
as far as we can, recount the position of the land toward its west. 6.2 There
is another plain, beginning from the very wall of the Ekbola, touching the
mountain on the right, bordering the sea on the left, lying under the eyes of
those who see it with unspeakable beauty. 6.3 For one part of it is soaked
with waters, as much as boasts that it has both the city and the sea as
neighbor, crowned with vines and leafy trees and gardens, and adorned with
dwellings and very many venerable precincts, most of which flocks of monks
have divided among themselves, practicing every form of virtue and living for
God alone, toward whom they press and for whom, having left the civic tumults,
they have chosen the path that leads to him alone. 6.4 And from there it is
spread on the dry land a long way, having its growth for the most part without
wood, but furnishing the practice of every farming care. 6.5 And it extends
looking toward the setting of the sun, stretching as far as certain other


high and great mountains, where a city called Beroea has also been settled,
itself most conspicuous both to its inhabitants and in all the other things by
which a city boasts its constitution. 6.6 And this plain contains in the space
in between also certain mixed villages, of which some pay dues toward the
city, being named Drugubites and Sagudates by title, while others render the
tributes to the neighboring nation of the Scythians, which is not far off. 6.7
Yet the villages neighbor one another in their dwelling, and this too, among
the other things, contributes not a little to the Thessalonians: mixing with
the Scythians through mercantile methods, and especially whenever they are on
good terms with one another and do not move weapons that savage the fight;
which, having been practiced by both sides for a long time of old, they
exchange their needs with one another in a community of life, keeping among
themselves a marvelous and deep peace. 6.8 And certain very great rivers,
setting out from the Scythians' land and dividing the aforesaid plain among
themselves, themselves also furnish much abundance to the city, both by the
supplies from the fish and by the runs of ships from the sea through them,
through which a varied revenue of needs is devised, flowing down together with
those waters. 7.1 But I have escaped my own notice, stretching the discourse
about these things long, or longer than I promised from the beginning. 7.2 And
love of the fatherland has done this, being sweetly drawn along with the
memory, and in the images seeming as it were to be present with what is said,
and also the eager and inquiring quality of your knowledge-loving hearing,
which from the beginning you promised to set wholly beside us, nothing else
distracting it or dragging it aside from this narrative. 7.3 And these things
are so. For the things longed for among affairs always love to hold both the
one who speaks about them and the one who hears, with bonds of necessity; and
neither will sight so readily and easily stand off from the sweetest
spectacles, nor hearing from delightful things heard, nor will a sweet
discourse be checked from its impulse, until it reaches the end toward which
it presses from the first. 7.4 Yet if it seems good, let us run back again to
that city itself; for how long shall we shape the statue as in a mirror, and
try to portray the archetype by the outer varieties of colors, when it is
possible to meet through timely narratives with the facts themselves, so that
what is somehow dimly indicated may be more known? 7.5 For having promised at
the outset to describe Thessalonica in discourse, of what sort 7.5 she happens
to be, as far as possible, and what she being formerly has come to later,
having been as it were stolen away by the things around her, and having until
now given leisure, as one might say, to the outer narratives, we run the risk,
by delaying, of putting off the explanation of the timely things. 7.6 So it is
needful to go back again to her in the discourse, and first to learn
thoroughly the beauty about her, and then thus afterward to speak out the
greatness of the suffering, so that as much as she may appear incomparably
superior to the other cities, so much she may be judged worthy by all to be
lamented and pitied. 8.1 We said that the city is broad and great and has shut
up much space in between by what surrounds it. 8.2 But as much of the wall as
looks toward the dry land is most strong and fortified by the thickness of the
building, keeping safety from every side by the outer forewall, packed
throughout with outworks and battlements, and furnishing no necessity of fear
to the inhabitants. 8.3 But the southern part of it is altogether low and
unprepared for war; for I think that, because no assault of harm from the
seaward side was suspected, the craftsman of old neglected the building. 8.4
And a certain old account is also carried, having reached even to us, that the
city was unwalled from that part for many years, until, by the fear once of
Xerxes the Mede, who carpentered a sailing land and campaigned against Greece
and brought a multitude of many ships, the one who steered the


scepters of the Romans in those times put a small and, as it were, incidental
barrier around the part there. 8.5 From which time even until now this same
thing has remained, the harm from there never, as he thought, being even in
suspicion among any that it would come to be. 8.6 For even if the city often
endured many wars and very violent ones, both those from the barbarians and
from the neighboring Scythians themselves, who devised every form of defensive
engines against her and flowed in like sand by the multitude of the army and
contended that she should bear nothing of their threat (for they were truly
ungovernable in their impulses and fenced with a varied panoply), 8.7 yet the
fight was, as it were, on land, and she escaped the plots, slipping aside from
the attack by the safety of the wall and trusting her own salvation to the one
who customarily and warmly fights for her, I mean the all-glorious martyr
Demetrius. 8.8 For this savior of the fatherland delivered her from many
dangers and often compassionately granted her the victory even when she had
not yet been tried in the war. 8.9 But yielding these things to be told in the
books of his miracles, let us go forward from what has already been said to
what follows. 9.1 So by the causes spoken the city was above every danger. 9.2
For from the time when the font of divine baptism conformed the Scythian
nation to the Christ-named people and divided the milk of piety in common to
both, the faction of wars had ceased, and the sword that practices slaughters
had been released from its working, and the things foretold by Isaiah, the
most loud-voiced of the prophets, were clearly fulfilled among us. 9.3 For our
swords passed over into sickles and the spears into ploughs, and there was war
nowhere, and peace held the presidency over the whole region, and there was no
starting-point of good living of which we did not enjoy to satiety, from here
the abundances of farming, from there the supplies of trade. 9.4 For earth and
sea, appointed from the beginning to serve us, brought gifts rich and without
expense concerning each thing. For of those things of which the land was in
want, or even unsuited toward fruit-bearing, the sea, devising the possession
from herself and bringing it in with the merchant ships, gave what was lacking
without want to those who needed it. 9.5 And what of those sojourning from
every quarter should I speak of first, and especially the things with which
they honored us citizens, giving what was from themselves and receiving in
turn what was from us? 9.6 For a public highway leading toward the east from
the west going through the middle of this city, and of necessity persuading
those who pass by to spend time among us and to furnish the things toward
need, we both reaped and acquired in addition whatever of good things one
might name from them. 9.7 Hence also a mixed crowd always surrounded the
streets, both of the natives and of those otherwise coming as strangers, so
that it was easier to number beach sand than those going through the market
and making the method of exchanges. 9.8 From this there came to be for the
many overflowing treasures of gold and silver and precious stones, and the
weavings from the Seres were devised among them as woolen ones are among
others. 9.9 For of the other materials, bronze and iron and tin and lead and
glass, by which the arts through fire hold life together, I think it even
superfluous only to mention them, they being so many that another city could
be built and completed through them. 10.1 Being thus prosperous and abounding
from every side, beautified by the technical methods and exulting in the
splendors of the dwellings, did she appear to be in want, compared with the
others, in the observations from laws, or in the orders concerning the polity,
or in the boasts concerning letters? Not at all. 10.2 For knowledge was
pursued by her as seeing is by the eyes, and good law as the business of life.
10.3 And you would have seen the youthful crop of the children giving leisure
to nothing else than to letters, from which sciences and arts have their
power. 10.4 And the things


concerning the harmonious music of the songs, or the soul-delighting melodies
of those who sing, and the pursuits of those allotted to God, how shall I
signify in the discourse? 10.5 And how shall I describe the showing of these?
For up to this point of the narrative I do not know how, having been carried
along with the rush of the discourse and as it were forgetting my own
rusticity, I was trying, moderately somehow, and as no other of the very
ignorant and rustic would, to describe the showing of the things asked by your
virtue. 10.6 But from this point, and especially because I mentioned the
well-rhythmed sweetness of voice of the songs, I do not know what I am to
become or where I am to go with the discourse, or which of those sweetest and
well-ordered melodizings I am to leave out, with which men sang and kept
festival together with the heavenly powers. 10.7 For if someone should wish to
portray that muse, the hymns sent up to God from every mouth as one in the
public assemblies, by the sound of the angels keeping festival, where the
dwelling of all who rejoice is, he will miss nothing of what is due. 11.1 For
certain very great and all-beautiful temples with varied adornment, thrown
forward through the middle of the city as certain common propitiations toward
the divine, and of these especially the house of the all-working and divine
wisdom of the superessential Word, and that of the ever-virgin all-holy and
Mother of God, and also that of the aforesaid all-glorious and fair-victorious
martyr Demetrius, where he accomplished the divine contests and received the
prize of victory, which also assembling the whole people in themselves on the
periodically returning days of the feasts, awarded unspeakable gladness and
spiritual joy to those who came together. 11.2 For in each of these there had
been allotted orders of priests, through whom the mystical 11.2 worship is
performed, and companies of readers, through whom the hymnody of the songs is
pursued, shouting the verses by turns, and disposing the sound by the
hand-gestures of the melodies, and constituting a great and worth-seeing
dance, charming the eyes of those who see by the form of the flashing robe,
and delighting the hearing by the crafted lyre of the psalms. 11.3 For what
was the mythical Orpheus toward this hymn, or the Homeric muse, or the
babblings of the Sirens, written up in the falsehood of fictions, for which
there is no true word of praises, but false rumors that lead men astray and
enslave them toward deceit. 11.4 In vain, then, did the Hellenes beat a false
drum in these things, having been stripped of the true knowledge of affairs
and arming themselves against themselves with the empty-talk of their
superstition. 11.5 But for us both what was worshipped was true and the thing
hymned itself. 12.1 Here indeed wonder comes upon me, and shuddering and
astonishment, whenever I take to mind how such and so great a hymn, not yet
likened in greatness to any of the other sacrifices brought forward, so
suddenly fell silent and was counted as nothing and withdrew like a dream.
12.2 Yet I reckon this: that being in sins we were recounting, according to
the psalmist, the divine ordinances, and the wretchedness of our ways rendered
this conspicuous and venerable thing unapproved, God having judged it unworthy
to be hymned and honored by profane and accursed lips. 12.3 For how long shall
we not set in the midst the true cause of every danger being brought upon us,
so that those afterward may know through the example concerning us how one
must worship the living and true God and keep themselves above every
destruction, lest, slipping into like faults of wickedness with us, they draw
the divine indignation upon themselves. 12.4 For it was for no other reason
that we came to trial of that great threat, at which even in remembering I am
beside myself, but through a wretched and evil choice, 12.5 whether through
the altered and various quality of the polity and of the manners, of the men
flowing in together to the city from every nation and place, others
contributing some other evil and of this to the neighbor


giving a share 12.6 (for through the abundant supply of needs which we
mentioned, the men of the neighboring lands and cities were exchanging their
dwelling in this one, and especially as many as were left from the already
captured islands from the raid of the ill-named Agarenes, fled to this city,
thinking they would have no care of enemies in it for the rest), 12.7 or again
through the relaxed and carefree quality of luxury and the varied and
convenient quality of need toward every pleasure, we had suffered as it were a
forgetfulness of God, wandering a long and unpassable wandering, and as the
apostle says, each of us was going after his own desires, advancing without
fear against every sin and making no care of the way that leads toward virtue.
13.1 For what form of wickedness, if one must speak the truth, was not
practiced among us? Fornications, adulteries, uncleannesses, hatreds, lies,
thefts, strifes, contentions, railings, angers, greedinesses, injustices; and
envy, the leader of evils, was a common practice each day and a thing of the
marketplace. 13.2 For it was not whoever of us was eager that his neighbor
should fare well, but whoever was mind-struck in carpentered bringing some of
the unwanted things upon him. 13.3 Nor whoever had wished to fulfill
compassion by sharing from his own with the one in need, but whoever fought
against his kinsman to increase his own from what was another's, dissembling
against one another, striking one another, slandering one another, finding out
every evil among themselves, choking orphans, trespassing on widows'
boundaries, joining enmities, stitching plots. 13.4 And what then was the end
of all these things? I shrink from letting the tongue loose at the greatness
of the affairs, and I would not have endured even to remember again those
painful things which we have already suffered. 13.5 But since it is not
possible to slip aside from the memory of such narratives, through which we
have been left a novel and fearful thing heard for life, I come to speak the
greatness of the danger, so that you too may know of what sort the wages of
sin are. 13.6 For while we were living in luxury, while we were living
wantonly and passing life in all unseemly practices, when, according to what
is sung in the psalm, all had turned aside, they had together become useless,
and there was none that sought after God, then we were tried by that fearful
threat, or rather overthrow, that I may speak more properly, or just
recompense. 14.1 And consider here too the loving-kindness of God, who does
not wish the death of the sinner so much as that he turn and live. 14.2 For
knowing the ungovernable and wretched quality of our judgment, and that our
mind lies carefully upon the evil things, what does he devise toward checking
the impulse of wickedness and casting into us causes of turning? 14.3 First he
caused the already mentioned neighboring city, I mean Beroea, to be shaken
down upon those who dwelt around, so as to consume many of those seized there:
administering what from this? 14.4 That we, all taking perception of the
things threatened against others, might even late make an abstinence from our
own evils and steer our deeds toward virtue. 14.5 Then, since when this had
come to be we did not yield from the old custom that ruled in us, he passes
over to another form of device, having shown beforehand in others the disaster
that was going to be even for us ourselves if we did things not to be repented
of. 14.6 For another city of Greece called Demetrias, settled not far from us,
exceeding those nearest in a great multitude of inhabitants and in the other
things in which cities boast greatly, not long before our capture became a
work of the barbarians. 14.7 For having been besieged so that all but all of
those under her fell by the sword, she furnished nothing else to think than
that we, remaining upon evils, should await a like trial of affairs and, as
upon a living stele, read in her the sufferings owed to us. 15.1 But let no
one think that I, in declaring, say these things, that it was because of us
that these cities received the trial of the fearful things. For the danger was
owed to them, as I think, even through their own wretchedness, as


also to us after them, God bringing upon each the punishments worthy of the
things sinned. 15.2 But in these things it was easy for us too to find no
ordinary starting-point of turning toward the better and of salvation, making
their overthrow our own safety. 15.3 Yet since the one who searches the hearts
saw our mind holding with all zeal to the way that leads toward destruction,
and that no saving commandment and educative working had been left, from which
a perceiving and sober soul relearns the good, fleeing the deeds of the worse
things, because of which the wrath comes upon the sons of disobedience, as the
apostle says, and wickedness had to be rubbed out, lest by its further advance
it should also ruin others, 15.4 for this reason he allowed that fearful and
shuddering blow to be brought upon us, so that what we were not taught when
others suffered, this we, having suffered, might make an example for others.
16.1 But why must one speak at length? For it is already time to take hold of
the timely narratives, and to set forth in the discourse how we underwent that
painful siege, so as to be for those afterward a subject of admonition and an
exhortation of clear teaching. 16.2 For while we were in these things there
came a swift-running messenger from the one who holds the scepters of the
Romans, Leo the piety-loving sovereign, announcing the attack of the
barbarians, I mean the ill-named Agarenes, and that we should arm with speed
and make ready toward the fight, saying that certain deserters had arrived
from those very barbarians, and had told the emperor beforehand what their
counsel is, and that they had made their whole impulse upon this city, having
already been confirmed by many of those previously taken in hand by them that
it is unwalled from the seaward part and easy to attempt with fights from the
ships. 16.3 When, then, this evil message had been announced, there came to be
a murmur throughout the whole city and fear and tumult, an unaccustomed and
fearful thing having fallen at first upon our ears, yet we resolved to procure
our own salvation, and to fit whatever was toward defense against the
opponents, and to be stirred toward the war against them. 16.4 Yet the
reasoning of each was weary, both with the other cares and with inexperience
of fighting, not yet being in trial of the thing, nor having been taught how
it takes its beginning. 16.5 And more, that the wall was not fit where we also
looked for the assault, sank all souls. 16.6 For it was truly suspect, not
only to us inexperienced in war and unarmed, but already even to those
themselves who had been trained and have the causes of such affairs laid up
beforehand. 17.1 We resolved, then, if anything even needed to be done, to do
it in the wall itself and to hold first to its safety and change toward the
better. 17.2 But the one who had brought us this message from the emperor did
not yet advise us this (and this man was called Petronas, numbered in the roll
of the protospatharioi, who had been entrusted to spend a little time in the
city and to give it some help and due cooperation in the affairs), but said he
had found out another most wise device, exceedingly effective and saving, if
the sins that make a way for the destruction against us had not subjected even
this to removal. 17.3 For being a man of letters and having had much trial of
affairs, having seen by himself that if he should wish to take care of the
building of the wall, he would both give toils to the city and would benefit
nothing more than those who had taken thought about this before him, he
transferred his mind to another form of safety (and consider how naturally
suited and fitting). 17.4 For knowing that the whole southern part of the city
is washed around by the sea waters, and that with the war falling from there
the barbarians would easily have to accomplish in it whatever they might wish,
nothing hindering them from getting above the building of the wall because of
its being near the ground and most low, and that the ships rather


overtop from the stern part, and from on high can wound those on the
battlements, 17.5 he resolved to hide a certain barrier and crafted ambush in
the waters themselves, so that both safety for the city and a hindrance for
the enemies might come to be in the same thing. 17.6 For there were many tombs
carved from a single stone both on the eastern and western part of the city,
in which of old the Hellenes inhabiting this place buried their own dead. 17.7
Which, gathering and by a certain unexpected method, which he alone found out,
sinking in the sea, and hiding these under the waters in a row as from a small
interval, he was in truth crafting a marine and strange defense, both stronger
and safer than the wall thrown forward toward height on the dry land. 17.8 And
indeed it would even have come into work, procuring every safety for the city,
so that the ships could not even approach at all and give a share of their
harm, if this too had not been hindered through our wretchedness and gone
forward into nothing. 18.1 For already, when such a through-the-sea barrier
had reached somewhere about the middle of the dangerous place and was cutting
away every fearful and evil suspicion of ours, there came another, himself
also sent from the emperor, summoning in haste Petronas who had been appointed
to this and transferring all the care of the city upon himself. 18.2 And this
man was called Leo, appointed general of the whole region and having taken up
all care of the affairs being prepared toward the fight. 18.3 To whom it also
seemed good to let the care of this work be idle for the time and to have the
building of the wall accomplished. 18.4 For as soon as he came, he at once
shifts the whole people appointed to this toward bringing the needful
materials to the craftsmen, so that perhaps by many hands and by the abundant
supply of the things due the thing pursued might be brought into work.
Already, then, the things of the building were again in working. 18.5 But as
much as the wall being raised toward height seemed to keep off the ill will of
the opponents, so much what was left appeared to us citizens to hint a
suspicion of every danger. 18.6 For it was not possible for the whole to be
set right, stretching in a great length, toward our no longer suspecting the
disaster from it, and the time bore witness beforehand that the attack of the
enemies was at hand, the building not yet having touched the middle space,
from which we also suspected the harm. 18.7 For messages more fearful than the
former and more frequent buzzed around all our ears, and that the barbarians
were already drawing near, having accomplished most of the voyage. 18.8 For
there was nowhere for them even to delay a little, because most of the islands
had already been sacked by them, and the cities having their dwelling toward
the sea had been turned toward flight by the shock of the hearing of them
alone, and there was nothing that sufficed to endure their impulse or the
continuity of their weapons. 18.9 For they foretold the barbarian ships to be
four and fifty together, each of them being no less than a city in size and in
the other fittings, in which there was a mixed crowd of desperate and mad men,
both the Ishmaelites inhabiting Syria and the Ethiopians neighboring the
Egyptians, all blood-eating and beast-minded men, having practiced much
science in murders and having studied slaughters with pirate devices, so that
no one could even by hearing await trial of these, but each hastened to
withdraw from the land that bore him, thinking it better to dwell with wild
beasts in the mountains than to be taken by them and carry off a many-pained
death. 19.1 Yet while these things were in this state there came again a
messenger more fearful than those already spoken, making plain to all that the
attack of the barbarians was nearer. 19.2 And this man was called Niketas, a
general in rank and in good order in counsels. 19.3 This man, having come,
pledged to help the


city (for he said he had arrived for this), and he put it that he was ready,
if anything needed to be done. 19.4 Yet that it happened 19.4 that something
of this sort also came to be in the midst of the expectation of the enemies,
from a device of the scandal-working and most wicked demon. 19.5 For the
already mentioned general, meeting this newly arrived Niketas somewhere about
the building of the wall at first, seated on a horse, as he thought to fulfill
the customary address toward the friend with the greeting, the other also
seated on a horse, having neglected the bridle, suffered a terrible thing and
worthy of many tears. 19.6 The horses having been thrown into confusion, and
more this one on which the general had sat, struck by the natural madness,
having arched the neck and bristled the mane, having been raised upright,
threw him from the seat, and having somersaulted from the tail side and been
thrown to the ground, crushed in the right thigh and the parts toward the
hip-socket, he was pitiable and giving up even life itself. 19.7 Whom the
spear-bearers of the soldierly order at once taking up in their hands led
home, as was likely to take thought no longer about the affairs at hand. 19.8
So the man lay struck with unbearable pains, not having to whom he should
first let his mind go. 19.9 For the care of the expected danger drew him, how
and in what manner he should save the city from the raid of the barbarians.
=19.10= But the sharp pain drew him more toward itself, and the suffering of
the struck limbs threatening death, and persuaded him to prefer nothing else
to his own salvation. =19.11= And thus lying on the border of two hard things
and being beaten from each, he was unfit toward every working of the things
due. =19.12= Yet because of the urgency of the time we, having built certain
wood-composed towers and brought them near to the crumbled part of the wall,
furnished ourselves a little cooling of good hope, as if from a height to keep
off those coming from the sea and not yield to them to go near the wall and
work their ill counsels. =19.13= But these were inventions of men in haste,
the necessity of fear suggesting the devices of vain things, even if, as a
certain wise man said, everything that is pressed is resourceful. =19.14= For
there was no one among us who would firmly consider against what was to come
or the outcome of the things hoped. 20.1 Yet since, when the general had been
struck, all care concerning us passed over to the said Niketas, he too began
to show the things from himself according to power, and he said that a great
multitude of the neighboring Sklavenoi, both those paying dues under us and
those under the general of Strymon, experienced in archery, had been ordered
to be driven together to the city, so that we perhaps might not even be found
wanting the panoply of the opponents, but might have those with whom we would
beat off their first assault. 20.2 Which he was also eager to bring into work.
20.3 For having written letters he sent them out through the whole region,
hastening the men about the Sklavenoi to come to us with speed, each of them
armed as it was possible. 20.4 From which indeed a few simple men flowed
together, not as many as filled the need, but even very easily numbered and
altogether unprepared toward the fight. 20.5 And this happened from the rulers
entrusted with them being certain clumsy and most wicked men, looking rather
to their own advantage than that of the common, and being by custom to plot
against the neighbor, and raging after taking of gifts, and having practiced
to prefer nothing else to the acquisition of this. 20.6 And though twice and
thrice and often the aforesaid Niketas tried through letters to frighten the
general of Strymon and accused him of slowness, and that if the city should
suffer anything with danger present, he would vote the cause upon him alone
before the ruler, 20.7 he had it no less than before, holding to his customary
ill counsel; having first overlooked the divine and then the human fear, and
having set the destruction of


so great a city at nothing, he resolved that neither he through himself nor
any of those under him should come and help us surrounded by so great a
disaster, but having led all of us astray by the expectation of always being
present until the last day of the war, he escaped notice having completed the
plot against us and having laughed a great and clever laugh at the destruction
by which we were held. 21.1 And having thus been cheated of the hope from the
allied Sklavenoi, although we ourselves were not easily numbered, but filled
the need with a great multitude and even greatly exceeded the army of the
barbarians, yet that no trial of war existed in us nor that we had the
practice concerning this laid up beforehand made their attack fearful and full
of astonishment. 21.2 And otherwise also that nothing had been found out
beforehand toward defense against this appeared evil to us. 21.3 And to use
flight for the time was burdensome; for if we turned aside, both the city was
going to be captured and the adornment through gold and silver and remaining
needful material of the aforesaid venerable houses was going to be taken away,
or even the all-holy houses themselves were going to become burnt with fire.
21.4 And at the same time, slipping aside from the harm of the barbarians, we
did not have how we should dispose the indignation of the ruler against us.
21.5 But these things were bearable and holding to every salvation, even if to
come only to mention of such things, not yet having had trial of the evil, was
harder than every death. 21.6 For a people to go to so great an affair, having
been accustomed beforehand to nothing else except pursuing the delicate and
luxurious life and not yet trained beforehand in warlike contests, made
astonishment and shuddering in us. 22.1 Yet wrestling in our reasonings with
one or the other of these, we thus spent the following days. 22.2 And what
alone is left to those at a loss, this we too were already pursuing, calling
upon the unspeakable mercies of the divine compassion and the intercessions of
the saints. 22.3 For having occupied the aforesaid all-seemly house of the
all-glorious martyr Demetrius, as many of us as inhabit the city and as many
as were newcomers from abroad, every rank and age, and having constituted
mournful choruses, we cried to the martyr to become a champion for us against
the expected threat of the barbarians, 22.4 saying, "You have shown, O noble
martyr, the warmth of your help in many dangers that have often come upon your
city, and you have scattered every plot of opponents, and have kept her
untried by every destruction. 22.5 Show even now, most compassionate one, your
measureless care concerning us, and let not barbarian and alien nations that
do not know God boast against us. 22.6 And do not give this house of yours,
which the whole inhabited world has acquired as a common healing-place and
refuge, to be profaned by accursed and godless men, who sneer at our faith and
set at nought the worship, and bring this alone as a charge against us, the
reverence of God, and threaten an untimely and destructive death. 22.7 For
even if we are worthy of countless punishments through the sins we have
practiced upon the earth, and have been set down as liable to this coming
overthrow, 22.8 yet we have not known another God except the one who crowned
you, through whom you also accomplished the divine contests, whom you also
glorified exceedingly by the imitation of the passion, from whom you have also
received the grace of the many miracles, and you have been granted to us as a
strong wall and unshaken support, making the supplications on our behalf to
him on each occasion and asking for us the things that are advantageous. 22.9
Look then even now upon the want and helplessness of this people, and having
heard our petition stand with a free-spoken intercession on behalf of us your
servants, and snatch us from the expected gathering, lest the children of the
maidservant Hagar boast against us, and lest they say, Where is their
champion? =22.10= For you yourself also see, most gracious one, that we have
not trusted in spears nor in shields, but have laid up the whole to your
powerful intercession, hoping again to


obtain your providence." =22.11= Bringing these supplicatory things with a
holding of heart to the martyr night and day, all of us, and washing the floor
of the temple with tears, we continued in the expectation of the opponents,
our sin standing against us as a certain most strong fence and hindering the
intercessions of the martyr toward bending the divine favor to us. =22.12= For
it is likely that even to him it was said, just as of old to the prophet
Jeremiah making the petition on behalf of Israel, not being worthy to obtain
the divine compassion, that "Do not pray for this people; for I will not hear
you." =22.13= For it was necessary, it was necessary that the destruction
yielded against us be worked, so that all who are in sins may know that
nothing so draws the divine ear toward the petitions brought by the saints as
a right cutting of life and care of best deeds. 23.1 For while these things
were being pursued by us in vain, there came one announcing that the barbarian
ships had already drawn near somewhere by the neck of the said Ekbolos, a
Sunday of days dawning, the ninth and twentieth of the month of July being
led, of the year 412. 23.2 When, then, this rumor had suddenly run through the
whole city, a tumult was raised among all, and shaking and confusion, others
shouting one thing and another and taking counsel about what was at hand, and
all arming as it was possible, and hastening down toward the wall. 23.3 And
not yet having been scattered on the battlements of the wall, behold even the
ships of the barbarians appeared from the said prominence, having their sails
spread. 23.4 For by a certain coincidence it happened at this time that a wind
had entered upon them from the stern, so that it seemed to the many that they
were being carried not even on waters but raised through air. 23.5 For the
month of July was being led, as has been said, when also more than the other
days the gulf-through wind there comes in, having its beginning from the
prominences of Olympus of Greece, and from early morning until the ninth hour
on each of the summer days falling in together upon the city and
differentiating the air. 23.6 Finding that, then, as a fellow-worker, the
enemies, while the day was still in its beginnings, broke near. 23.7 And first
they slacken the sails, having come somewhere there by the wall, and they
carefully observed of what sort the city happens to be. 23.8 For they did not
begin the fight at the same time as being moored, but they gave a little
leisure both to try beforehand our strength, how we might have it toward the
grappling through war, and to train themselves toward this very thing. 23.9 So
they stood for the time full of fear, having nothing among the things seen
with which to liken what they looked at; for they observed a city broadened
with a great size, and the whole wall surrounded by a multitude of people.
=23.10= From which, having been still more struck, they held the fight a
little, so that from this a short courage also came to be in us, and in the
midst of such leisure to recover our souls. 24.1 While we were thus, it seemed
good to the leader of the barbarians' army to go through the whole wall, as
much as is washed around by the sea. 24.2 And this man was clumsy and
all-wicked, and showing the deed also corresponding to the homonymy of the
beast, and disposed no better than that one in the wildness of manners and the
ungovernable quality of the impulse. 24.3 And you yourself have certainly
known him from the rumor, which has the wickedness of the man cried abroad, so
that never has any of the impious who are spoken of driven to such madness as
to have it insatiably to see outpourings of human bloods and to prefer nothing
else to the destruction of the Christians. 24.4 For this man, himself also
having once been and having been formed by the regeneration of saving baptism
and having been taught our religion, having been overpowered by the barbarians
themselves, exchanged their impiety for the pious faith, and he always has it
through zeal to grant them nothing more than to confirm the name by the deeds,
and showing dramas of a transgressor and pirate, from these both to be
ambitious and to be lifted above. 24.5 This untamed one, then, and
transgressor


Leo went around in the ship looking at the wall through the sea and studying
from where, having set out, he might assault with ill working. 24.6 And the

see of some wrestler's agony in the counter-speeches of the body toward the

25.1 But when that beast had seen the whole wall, and still also that the
strait of the harbor was fenced with an iron chain and certain sunken ships,
he rather planned to make the assault from there, from where he discerned that
neither the ambushes of the previously sunk single stones from outside hinder
the attack of the ships, nor indeed that they would receive an intensification
of war from the high already-built wall. 25.2 But where the water of the sea,
being deepened, breaks against the lower wall, having marked the places and
turned back toward his companions, he gave over the contest. 25.3 They then
with speed, having been scattered with the ships at the said places, and
having used a barbarian and harsh shout, rushed at the wall, driving with the
oars, and striking a stunning beat with the drums made from hides, and
throwing those on the battlements into confusion with many other scare-things.
25.4 But those standing toward the wall shouted against them still more and
with a louder voice, calling upon the saving weapon of the cross toward
alliance against the opponents, 25.5 and so much that the barbarians, having
heard that many-peopled and more fearful than every thing heard war-cry, grew
dizzy for the time and expected to accomplish nothing, having reckoned the
multitude of the people from the shout, and that it would not be easy, having
stripped toward fight against so many, readily to sack so great a city, which
they had no example with which to compare. 25.6 Yet lest they should seem to
have cowered at the first assault, not without fear, nor again with the
madness which they used later, but with a certain rage mixed with fear, having
come near they warded off those opposite with the snows from the missiles.
25.7 Then they attempted something even more shameless and contended to rush
still nearer, like certain barking dogs stirring their own anger, growing wild
at those carried against them from the wall. 25.8 For those of the city did
not neglect archery, but used it rather clearly and fitly, having stationed
all the Sklavenoi who had flowed in from the neighboring lands at those
places, of whom nothing is so easy of attempt among the others as to shoot at
a mark and for nothing of their missiles to bear the rush in return. 26.1 But
while both sides were thus shooting and being shot, and showing the fight
falling upon them as equal, certain of the barbarians, having divided off,
bolder and more reckless than the others as was likely, and having hurled
themselves from the ship into the sea, and having brought down with themselves
a wooden ladder, pushed it along in the waters, trying through it to go up the
wall, making no account of the things sent against them from there. 26.2 For
until they came near they covered their bodies, both swimming in the water and
fencing their heads with the shields. 26.3 And when they came close, having
stripped off the water they held more stoutly, wrestling with the shots,
carrying the shields alone upon the head, then having more quickly drawn the
ladder up against the battlements they attempted through it to go up and to go
inside. 26.4 But death anticipated what had been resolved, and before they
accurately


considered how they might complete the device, they were released from life.
26.5 For only that they moved their tracks on the steps, and the stones let go
against them like a dense hail together packed them off both to the sea and to
destruction. 26.6 In these things all the ships go back-rushing, no one else
having it as yet to attempt such a thing boldly; but with the snows from the
missiles alone, even shadowing the air itself, they shot from a length,
themselves being no less shot both by the missiles sent with good aim and
missing few and by the downfalls from the stone-throwers, of which even the
rush alone coming to be through the air worked astonishment in the barbarians.
27.1 For already the said Niketas too, the one sent from the emperor, ran
through the whole wall encouraging the people, 27.2 saying, "Men of
Thessalonica, I had other opinions concerning you before this, and I would not
have judged you to be so noble and bold toward the work of war, having neither
been tried of old in any such thing nor having worked it; but now the peak of
the affair has furnished hopes concerning you that are not ignoble. 27.3 For I
see all of you swelling in bodies and being men in souls and being wholly of
the things at hand, laughing down the opponents and turning their engines
nobly aside. And you do nothing unseemly. 27.4 For the contest is about
yourselves, men honorable both in forms and in the advantages of soul, and
about the remaining city, of which nothing of the noteworthy things contends
toward conspicuousness. 27.5 For if you get above the danger here, the praise
among all is owed to you; but if you suffer something of the unwanted things,
and of those with which the barbarians have threatened, there is nowhere to
liken the disaster or the greatness of the shame. 27.6 For this reason stand
nobly, procuring the victory both for the fatherland and for yourselves, and
do not give backs to the hands of enemies, nor leave some novel narrative to
life, having exchanged so great a danger for a small slackness." 27.7
Anointing the people with these hortatory things and putting no little courage
into all souls, he made the circuit. 27.8 And the general, as it were having
forgotten his own suffering, though it was hard from the fall we spoke of
before, and furnishing a pain greater than the power, having mounted a certain
mule, not astride but along the side and as the pains of the crushed limbs
permitted, himself also went around, seating the more eager of the taxeis at
certain needful places of the wall, so that perhaps both what was according to
them and those near, stirring toward imitation, might dispose the things
toward the war. 28.1 And the barbarians, having rushed together not once only
but even often through that whole day, more struck than before, having gone
backward, leaving the fight toward the waters at one signal, withdrew in the
ships, and were moored at a certain shore being toward the east of the city.
28.2 Then, having gone out of the ships, they again used the missiles against
those who had been allotted the stand there on the high wall, where a certain
gate also stands out, called Rome, neighboring the sea. 28.3 Where, having
fought until deep night, and as it were having grown weary with the agony,
they grew quiet in the ships, perhaps considering how they might set upon us
on the next day, and making ready toward other engines. 28.4 And already
another care again came in upon us too, having been a little released from the
war: to keep watch those on the battlements through the whole city from every
side around, and to look askance at the barbarians, lest they escape notice
having made some night ambush or snare and, having come to be in the wall,
accomplish the whole. 28.5 For they are naturally suited toward these things
especially, and as soon as they have thought they go toward the work, setting
every danger at nothing, and attending to one thing only, to have begun what
has been resolved. 28.6 And even if perhaps the end of the affair comes out
contrary to what they spied, they think they have glory in attempting boldly
for the time things impossible.


28.7 For this reason we completed that whole night keeping watch, blaming
ourselves in nothing of the things previously contested. 28.8 For we came to
such courage that even the leader of the barbarians himself came to be in
wonder and made much of learning later the cause, how we stood so manfully
against each assault, and how the opposite of what he had heard concerning us
came out, his hope having been overturned. 29.1 But when dawn stood over as
herald of the second day of the war, and things harder than those already done
had been devised against us by the barbarians, the generals again grew weary
in cares, stirring the anger of each and introducing the eagerness according
to themselves. 29.2 When, then, the sun's ray had shone around the air, the
barbarians, having gone out of the ships, rushed again at the wall, having
been scattered and having divided themselves in the phalanxes at certain
places, and having flowed together more at the outlets of the gates they moved
the whole panoply against us ourselves. 29.3 For some of them used the bows,
others a handmade thunder of the stones; others, seated at the stone-throwers,
sending the oversized hails of those rocks, raised them on high. 29.4 And the
murder threatened against us was of many forms, and falling from every side it
made the trial fearful to those who happened upon it. 29.5 For against the
said gate alone they set seven stone-throwers fenced from every side, which,
going through Thasos, they had prepared beforehand toward such a use. 29.6 And
indeed, having brought certain wood-composed ladders opposite these near to
the wall, they tried to go up through them, keeping safety for themselves by
the stones let go from the stone-throwers. 29.7 For those, working
unceasingly, did not allow anyone to lean out unharmed from the high wall.
29.8 And indeed what they had resolved would even have reached into work for
them, they having already drawn the ladder up to the battlements of the
forewall, if a certain divine power had not strengthened certain bold men to
leap down to the place, who also, having wounded the barbarians with spears,
hurled them backward together with the ladder. 29.9 And thus, they having seen
even this device gone forward into nothing and having used flight so as even
to leave the ladder, we came to such courage as even to laugh against them,
and more eagerly than the other day to use both the missiles and the stones
from the stone-throwers, and no longer to allow them even for a short time to
come near the wall, even though they, having been kindled toward a more mad
quality, themselves also like wild swine sharpened the teeth, and if somehow
it were possible, wished to burst us living among themselves. =29.10= For of
what sort it was to hear them fearful, raging against us! How they made the
showing of the exceeding anger, whenever they growled from the deep and by the
foams sent from the mouth the demonic quality in them was shown, =29.11= not
even wishing to take a share of food through that whole day, but having it
insatiably of the war in so raging a heat, and not perceiving at all even that
they wear bodies both being tamed by toil and being burnt by the sun above the
head, having this alone through care, either to sack the city and fill up the
anger against us, or, this not going forward, even to give up life and to do
themselves to death with the weapons. =29.12= For a barbarian anger once
kindled would not sooner cease from the unreasonable impulse that moves it,
until it sees the outpouring either of its own blood or of that of the one
acting against it from the opposite. 30.1 But since it was not without danger
for them to draw near the wall, they entrusted everything to the missiles and
the stone-throwers. 30.2 For having arranged themselves in a row, and having
stood off so much that their shots, the impulse not yet yielding, fall upon
the city, and having fenced themselves with the shields and having become
wholly of the agony, they stood like certain statues, having bodies compacted
from bronze or some other more firm material, with a countless and unspeakable


toil enduring and vying toward the war. 30.3 For when the sun was leading the
day according to the middle, when also by the shots of the rays it sets the
air more on fire than in the other hours, having as it were burnt out the
natural rage by the peak of the heat, and having further provoked that
irrational anger with desperations, they transferred the impulse to another
form of siege (and consider how hard). 30.4 For four gates leading out toward
the eastern part of the city, they planned to reduce two of them to ash with
fire, both the said Rome and the one called Kassandreotic, 30.5 having set
this in the mind, that if they should be able, while the outer gates were
burning, to go inside the forewall and slip under the high wall, they would
have no fear toward ill-working even the inner ones and driving all together
under one into the city, having set opposite well-aimed and terrible archers,
so that by the continuity of the missiles they might not allow anyone of those
inside to lean out without danger. 31.1 And indeed they thus began the craft.
31.2 Having found wagons they set upon them, prone, the smallest ships, which
the fishermen among us used toward the hunting of the fish, and still also a
multitude of certain other woods and a heap of brush; which, having sprinkled
with pitch and sulphur and having slipped under the wagons, they turned the
axles, leading them with the hands, until they reached as far as the gates
themselves. 31.3 Then having kindled that material, and going back-footed,
having fenced themselves with the shields, having gone toward the archers,
they escaped notice bringing what had been resolved into work. 31.4 For the
