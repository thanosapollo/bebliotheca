<ON THE REIGNS I> CONCERNING LEO THE EZ AMALEK Having completed the book from
history as you commanded, emperor, with great studies and labors, I have set
it as a gift upon you, so that it may be known to have been composed from
loyalty to my lord and from longing. Preface The distinction of what was
labored over of old, presenting truly the greatest benefit, stirs those who
have laid hold of it toward the writing of those things. Hence I too now,
having taken up in many ways the written zeal concerning these matters, both
from those who lived then and knew them in some manner and from rumor that
has, as it were, run abroad, have been spurred to this contest, on the one
hand if I might somehow be seen of some use to those after, and on the other
that I might harvest this for myself as well, inasmuch as I have been
commanded to judge these very things by Constantine the emperor, a lover of
the beautiful both in nature and in purpose, and the most learned of emperors
that ever were, son of Leo the all-wise and ever-remembered lord, so that I
may record what has not been handed down in the history-book, from the reign
of Leo of Amalek, who was brain-struck in impiety, and what follows. 1.1 In
the second year of the reign of Michael, son-in-law of the emperor Nicephorus,
in the year of the world's creation 6322, seventh indiction, when a defeat had
befallen the Romans at the hands of Krum, chief of the Bulgarians, Leo being
then general of the Anatolics and not having met with the clash of the war,
but having withdrawn with ill counsel together with the tagma under him,
watching his time for an attack on the empire, the emperor Michael became a
fugitive from there together with the people left to him. 1.2 But as some have
said, that Leo alone, attacking the Bulgarians on one part, fought them down
nobly, so as to carry off from there the greatest glory. And the emperor set
his course on the road of return, accusing his subjects of much, as not having
taken thought for their written oaths, but going over into factions, from
which defeats come about, and he ordered Leo with the army under him to sit
outside the city because of his partial superiority against the Bulgarians, so
that they should not thereafter so much suspect Roman want of men and flight.
From which the military assembly abused the emperor's simplicity, and voted
him slack, and with one voice preferred the general Leo for the empire. And
when he had fallen into a misty and great agony at the word of the
acclamation, as once at the bridge of Gaius Caesar, Michael of Amorion swore
he would kill him if he did not consent to it. And he asked only his consent,
and promised to make easy the entry as far as the city and the palace itself,
which was brought to completion by the army itself. 1.3 Before these things,
when the emperor Michael had come near the city, there met him a certain man
of the notable, John, entrusted with the care of the walls, of the family of
the Hexaboulii, paying the customary homage to the emperor, and sharing the
pain of the misfortune he asked: who among the generals, O master, has been
left in the remaining army? And he said: Leo, who leads the first theme, a man
quick-witted and holding to uprightness. And he answered that it was
unprofitable if he alone should command such a thing, without one of your



household servants. And since the emperor suspected nothing contrary, certain
defenses were put forward by him to Hexaboulios, that Leo would never wish to
play the tyrant. So the emperor went off to the palace, where not long after
the acclamation of the tyrant Leo was also announced. At this the emperor
Michael being distressed, some advised him to stand firm against the tyrant by
every means; but he, being calm in mind and not bloodthirsty in purpose, kept
all from bloodshed, bending favorably to the command of God, and so much so
that he even sent one of his household service, the exarch, to Leo with the
imperial tokens as well, and urged the senate too to go off to meet him, so
that no drop of Christian blood be poured out for his sake. At which his wife
Procopia, grown jealous, said such a thing: a terrible thing, terrible, if the
wife of the tyrant Leo should put on the modiolos, naming her Barka. And he,
turning her back with prudent words, said: it is good that what happens should
follow the Lord's will. 1.4 And when the tyrant had reached through the Golden
Gate the church of the Forerunner, which Studius built, many of the senate, or
even almost all, came to him, and escorting him with the crowd that followed
they led him to the palace. And entering and standing before the
bronze-wrought gates, on the marble that was circular to the eye and purple
all round, about to offer a prayer of thanksgiving to the image of Christ God
there, whether in truth or in pretence, he strips off the garment he had put
on, a dress eagled in rose-colored hues, which they name kolobion in the local
speech, and this, ungirt of a belt according to the custom long kept for the
generals, he gives conspicuously to Michael, first of his grooms. And when
that man put it on at once, it was reckoned by those who knew as an omen of
imperial ascent after Leo. And about a certain place of the palace named the
Skyla, Michael insulted the edge of Leo's garment by treading on it, whether
he did this from rashness, or because the providence above was sketching the
future in advance. And Leo, coming to awareness of this, took it as an omen
that some innovation would come from him <against> himself. 1.5 And when Leo
had gone into the inner parts of the palace, at once, having shorn their hair,
both the emperor Michael and his consort with their children appear as
pitiable suppliants in the oratory of the all-holy Theotokos, which is named
Pharos, asking to obtain some kindness. And he, become obedient to the
entreaty, granted them a respite of life, and sends Michael to spend his time
in a certain monastery, fenced in with the monastic life, to whom he lavished
a gift of gold each year at a set term; but he separated the wife and the
children of this man, of whom he condemned Ignatius to castration. 1.6 And
when Leo had seized the reins of empire, the things that had been revealed to
Bardanes the patrician, whose surname was Tourkos, concerning him came to
light. This Bardanes, then, when he led the theme of the East, had in himself
a very great concern for an attack on the empire so as to lay hold of the
throne; hence he laid this matter carefully before a monk of those who
possessed foreknowledge, who made his dwelling at a certain place of
Philomelion, having as partner in such counsel Leo, a man distinguished in
many advantages of nobility, manly in look and grim in bearing, middle-aged
and easy in company, and reared of the same theme at a certain place named
Pidra, and with him also Michael of Amorion, in whom the defect of speech had
come about, and with them also Thomas from the lake of Gazouros, he too
drawing his race from Armenians, and moreover brave and most inquisitive. So
when Bardanes came to the monk, all the wishes of his mind are said to have
been uncovered to him, and



so much so that there was foretold to him both the taking away of his
substance and the blinding of his eyes. And though he was vexed at the things
said before as not to his mind, nevertheless, when he had left off the
customary prayer, the things of his departure were made ready for him. And
when those who served him brought up horses for him, the monk, looking at
these, with the greatest urgency advises Bardanes to turn back. And when he
came back with much joy, expecting some omen of the things he desired,
contrary to his hopes the prophetic man says again: let nothing be a care to
you concerning the plot, which has not settled well in your mind. For of your
eyes, as I said to you before, and of your substance you will undergo the
alienation; but of the men who brought the horses to you for service, the
first of them and the one after him will wear the diadem, and the third,
having obtained acclamation, will quickly be overthrown by death. So Bardanes,
groaning hard at these things, made known the things of his thought to Leo and
Michael and Thomas, those under his hand, together with some abusive
irritation against the monk, saying such things: you, O Leo, having become
emperor, and Thomas, having been acclaimed, will be killed by Michael, while
he will be kept altogether unharmed in the empire. 1.7 From here Bardanes,
being hard in soul, and returning to the theme entrusted to him with the camps
under him and certain other like-minded men of his arrogance, encamps vainly
in Bithynia against Nicephorus who was then emperor. The emperor having heard
of these things and having put together a considerable army sends it against
him. And he, struck with dismay at the expedition, becomes submissive to the
emperor, asking a pledge of pardon, and indeed he is granted this readily,
bringing the security by the symbol of the cross, and he obeys, condemning
himself by the act to be sent to the island of Prote to the estate he had
laboriously made there. And when not much time had yet passed, certain men
from Lycaonia, whether acting on their own, or with imperial consent, or
wishing to please the emperor Nicephorus himself, fall suddenly on Bardanes
and blind his eyes, and they came to the church asking forgiveness. 1.8 And
when the things of the revolt had been puffed up by Bardanes, Leo from the
Armenians and Assyrians flowed over to the emperor, then also Michael of
Amorion, of whom the one is entrusted with the command of the foederati, the
other with that of count of the tent; but Thomas even to the point of dangers
keeps the things of faith toward his own lord. And when some time had gone
forward, Leo, having become hypostrategos of the Anatolics, does great deeds
of valor against the Ishmaelites and is honored by the emperor Michael after
Stauracius with the magnificent rank of the patricians. 1.9 And this Michael
possessed a household girl, in whom a spirit of python was at work through
certain turns. Who, coming quickly in a stone place which is called Boukoleon,
cried out with a piercing voice to the emperor: come down, come down, withdraw
from what is another's. So when such an address had been uttered many times
the emperor, reasonably struck and no less cowering, confers about this with
Theodotus the spatharocandidatus and a familiar acquaintance, the son of
Michael the patrician of the family of Melissenos, whose fair name Cassiteras
was heard, that if the girl should become possessed, he should by all means
hold her in a certain house and search out and say whose the house is and what
the man's mark of recognition is. And when she had uncovered the name and
character sought, she urged him to come opportunely to the acropolis, at which
two men would coincide, of whom the rider of a mule would be mounted on the
imperial throne. And such were the things of the frenzied girl, but the things
of the emperor's exactness were, as was likely, more searching, as of one
anxious about his empire. For Theodotus, being closely questioned by him,
denied that he



had known nothing of the pythoness's babblings; but going off to the appointed
place as had been announced beforehand he saw, and taking the man with him he
goes off with him to the church of Paul the apostle, in which the things for
the nurture of orphans are stored. And there having pledged one another with
covenants, Theodotus takes courage to say such things to Leo, that from a
divine vision there has been made known to me concerning you things both great
and that will come to pass, through which you would altogether take over the
hegemony of the Romans. =1.10= And a war having occurred between Romans and
Bulgarians, and their chief Krum saying that the Bulgarian refugees should be
given back, and likewise also those of the Romans, the emperor was agreeing
upon these things, even if such a judgment was much in faction among the many
of the magnates, and not least also with Theoctistus the magistros; from whom,
disagreement having been brought about, both sides go toward opposition. So
Romans and Bulgarians march out against one another, and a warlike encounter
having been set, the Roman army is defeated with all its strength, as it seems
the providence above was about to conduct our affairs otherwise, with a view
to a better issue. =1.11= But from where we turned aside, let us now return
again. And Leo the emperor, having been acclaimed in public, set Thomas his
own age-mate as tourmarch over the foederati, and Michael, whose child he had
adopted as a son from the spiritual font, he distinguished with the honor of
patrician and excubitor. =1.12= So the hostile Bulgarians having been puffed
up by the former victory, no ordinary desolation followed among them in many
lands and dwellings through burning and captivity, so that it was reasonable
that an embassy be sent by the emperor to the <chief> of the Bulgarians, as he
inclined toward peace, to work out a truce. And the ruling Bulgarian not
having consented to this, being barbaric in manner and insolent, Leo the
emperor is eager to march out against them. And a mighty battle having been
joined, by imperial ordinance our people is subjected to a rout, so that the
emperor himself is turned with them. Yet when the Bulgarians were pressing the
pursuit, the emperor from a turning back with the remaining people stands
against them and, throwing himself among them, is shown altogether a champion
against them. And not only so, but he even overpowered the chief of the
Bulgarians himself, and of their offspring dashed some against the rocks, and
punished others harshly on a threshing-floor, so that from here in these
things the works of recompense were brought to an end according to the poem.
=1.13= Then the emperor came to thought concerning the monk at Philomelion,
and he sends him the offerings for need according to prayer, as to a true
oracle-singer of the things that came to pass. And in the time between the
same monk had died, and it happened that another monk, Sabbatius, slipped into
his little tent in his place, who secretly stood against the veneration of the
venerable icons. And seeing the man sent by the emperor he made him a thing to
be sent away, judging the sender unfit for the purple, as giving himself to
idols, the icons venerated by the august Irene and by Tarasius the famous
patriarch, foolishly insulting her as Pardo and Thyad, and Tarasius the high
priest of God, the wretched man; in return for which he both announced by
letter that the Lord would overthrow his empire, if he should not overthrow
these. The crazed emperor, thunderstruck by these things, summons Theodotus
and shows him the things sent by the monk. Then indeed then Theodotus, seizing
such a purpose and taking heart, says to the emperor, adding evil upon evil,
that wonders were being worked by a certain monk, who had his dwelling in the
parts of Maurianus, which he made clear neighbored Dagisteus, naming this man
another great Antony; and whatever he should choose to say to your empire, be
eager to accomplish this also. Having thus ill advised the emperor he goes off
to the monk



and in a fabricated way says these things, that on the coming night, at an
appointed hour, I will bring the emperor to you in a cheap condition to
recount concerning faith and certain notable affairs; whom I counsel you to
curse and to declare that he will pass from life the sooner, unless he should
yet be formed to the dogma of Leo who sprang from Isauria, and that if he
should keep this, he would drive on holding power of the empire for two years
and seventy, as if to hunt him toward his own will by love of life and love of
glory; for he was held by desire for these, and especially being lightened by
youth. These things were carefully prepared for the emperor by both Theodotus
and the darkened monk in the Mauriana, as is the way with heresiarchs. So the
emperor entering with Cassiteras to the monk and having prefaced some words
toward him is struck by him, as if he had foreknown his pretence, saying thus,
that being emperor you ought not so to play a part in a private condition and
think somehow to be unknown. By which the emperor, struck at the machinated
foreknowledge, becomes obedient to him. For this, being desirable to Theodotus
from providence, had been crafted beforehand, as these things were also
pilloried through metrical poetry by the blessed Theophanes the confessor.
=1.14= Therefore the taking down of the venerable icons is triumphed from the
emperor. Toward which he also forced the high priest Nicephorus to subscribe,
and when he in no way obeyed he unreasonably punishes him as an exile (to
whom, when he was setting the diadem on Leo's head, it seemed that he felt
pain as from the rubbings of thorns and thistles). And as he was being led
away the confessor Theophanes <the> one from Agros joined him at a certain
stretch of the shores, assigning honors with candles and incense together, and
receiving blessings from there he enjoined his own disciples, who had then
heard these things, to do this very same thing as he was departing from the
Propontis. Whom the patriarch seeing, and bending his own neck and raising his
hands, called back with a last prostration the acceptable intercessions of the
all-pious Theophanes; concerning whom those present inquired: when you
<raised> your hands aloft, O master, toward whom did you intend, by way of
commission, to send forth the last long greeting? And he said: to the holy
father and confessor Theophanes of the monastery of Agros, who received us
with lamps and frankincense. And the thing prophesied went out into deed; for
not long after he himself, with many others set apart from the church, with
countless ill-treatments bound on the crown of confession, no longer seeing
the patriarch according to what was said before. So Theodotus, having received
the patriarchal throne beyond his desert on the day of the life-giving
resurrection of Christ our God, held to the heresy at his ease and
communicated it to many as a matter of eye-rheum. =1.15= So the emperor Leo,
having played the youth against both Bulgarians and Hagarenes and being held
by an unconquered conceit, appeared more arrogant to all, so as from here to
decline also toward the more savage. For he was fearful both in bearing and in
deeds, breaking nothing of the austerity that was in him, nor of envy and
boldness, and so much so that of many even of those caught in small charges
the hands of some, the feet of others, and certain of the vital members were
continually hung in the highways of the city, so that from these things he had
become hateful to all under his hand. And as time went forward he did not
remain harsh only as far as these things, but he also raved against the pious
faith. For he found also a certain helper of the wickedness lurking in him, a
cunning man, who happened to be overseeing the sacred order in the palace and
had the piety that appears outside. So guessing a time at which the message of
Isaiah saying "to whom have you likened the Lord, and to what likeness have
you likened him? has a craftsman made an image, or a goldsmith melting gold
gilded him, or has he prepared a likeness



of him?" and the rest, he says to the emperor in secret: understand what is
said, O emperor, and know the truth which the prophet made clear to us, and
hold to such worship, and do not be thunderstruck at all by the venerations
from likenesses. Having said such things he struck into him still more the
poison of the heresy, through which he imparted corruption to the pious
companies, from whom he unsparingly maltreated those who did not obey. =1.16=
And this emperor Leo, even if impious, <was> most capable of taking hold of
public affairs, so as to leave nothing of the things that bring benefit
unforeseen, so that even after his death Nicephorus the patriarch said such
things, that the polity of the Romans, even if it had an impious man,
nevertheless has lost a great provider. Besides this he was also justly most
burdensome to those who did injustice. So that at a certain customary time as
he was going out of the palace, a certain man came to him setting up a charge,
that this man's wife had met with seizure by one of the senators, who had
obtained such freedom as to hold her without fear for a long time; and though
I laid a charge about this also to the eparch, he accomplished nothing for me
toward vindication. So the emperor, having heard these things, quickly ordered
one of the attendants that on the return the accused together with the eparch
should be set before his face. So when the emperor had come home the thing
commanded was set into deed. And he says readily to the man who made the
charge: what is the drama concerning you? And the one used the same speeches,
and the man charged confessed the charge as undisputed, and the examiner bore
witness with the deposition, even if he had not made the punishment against
the condemned. At once he removed the judge from office as not bringing the
right, and he punished according to the laws the one caught in adultery. And
the greater part of the administrations he accomplished transacting in the
Lausiacus, calling in the best of secretaries, to whom the writing of the
issuances had been entrusted, and he attached to himself generals and rulers
of the notable, and otherwise those trained in ungiftedness, himself being not
less than money, even if more a cause of heresy, so as even to punish most of
the orthodox in cruelty. And he was very ambitious of honor, even if shouting
in a haughty way and ill-rhythmed. Which also at the church of the Pharos this
too was plain to those who would come upon him as he was throat-singing in
psalmody. For he sometimes led the singers, and especially then, crying: they
made light of it through longing for the all-king, of which slipping he then
drew the divine justice conspicuously upon himself by the hymn, a prophecy
being fulfilled in this. =1.17= And when some time of these things had run
ahead, Michael of Amorion, advancing in the advantages according to bravery,
on which account also the regiment of the excubitors had been entrusted to
him, is struck with a slander of treason; who, having shaken this off in many
ways, is sent out by the emperor to govern the levy in him. Whom, envying, as
some said, he had wished to put to death, if God, in the ways he approved, had
not preserved him paradoxically and marked him out for the empire. Even if
some have taken this, that there was in Michael also a daring of tongue
greater than the boldness according to nobility in every way, speaking defects
and threatening the ruler with total deposition, and to insult his wife with
unholy marriages. Leo was filled with the sound of these things, and at first
he tried skillfully to lull the impulse (for he knew him a talker and most
shameful, even if he thought him brave), but he does not persuade with words
either advising at all or being stern; for Michael held likewise to such
shameful daring. So the emperor sets hearers of his daring deeds, on the one
hand as if pressing down his boldness and tongue-sickness, and on the other
choosing also to eavesdrop the things about this man. For he had also
carefully instructed Hexaboulios, as being a sensible and much-experienced
man, and moreover an acquaintance of his own people, not to allow him when he
was eager to have an unbridled mouth, but



to keep silence, from necessity being very cautious of the things against the
emperor. But these things worked nothing for him toward amendment, as he was
borne along without restraint from lack of training; and since once and twice
and many times it was made clear to the emperor concerning them, and he
himself was found hard to heal or incurable, he pledges Hexaboulios by a
handwritten note that he should not at all hereafter keep silent any of his
ravings, but should set all things in the open for himself. And as the
arrogant man did not give way in his talking, at once he makes him plain to
the emperor as taking over the same shamelessness. And on a stated day of
those before the birth of Christ our God according to the flesh, the proofs of
the things moved against him entered into the open, the emperor examining the
things upon this with much testing about the place of the asecretis. So
Michael is convicted of tyranny, he is deposed openly, being pricked by the
evidence of the accusers, and the emperor orders certain of the shield-bearers
to take him with both hands and lead him away to the bath of the palace, and
to bind †a little ape† and thread him toward a pole and set him as fuel for
the furnace fire. =1.18= And the imperial ordinance having been thus brought
forward, the emperor had been eager to see what would be completed. But the
augusta Theodosia had met him without counsel, the daughter of Arsaber the
patrician and quaestor, about to accuse the emperor of bloodshed and
barbarity, and that he had not at all the fear of God in his heart, and that
he had in no way blushed at the festal day, on which he had chosen to receive
the divine communion with stained hands, and about to advise that Michael be
kept shut up, and then that the things concerning him obtain more examination,
and those who had shared the signal with him, and at that time be brought
under punishments. So the emperor, having perceived such words, ordered the
accused to be freed from the immediate punishment, and he addresses such
things to his wife: O woman, you have today released my soul from the justice
from there, and you will perhaps shortly deprive me of life; but you and our
children will see what will come to pass. And in this he prophesied the
future. For the time of his killing had been revealed to him from a certain
symbolic book, riddling that between the chi and phi of the letters the lock
of a lion beast had been pierced through with a sword, that is <between> the
birth of Christ and the Lights the beast-mannered Leo himself would be killed;
and not only so, but also the calling of the one who would kill from a
god-sent dream (for he had seen a little before the patriarch Tarasius
speaking ill with freedom of the wages of his wickedness, and strictly
ordering a certain Michael to consume him, who, having obeyed the one
commanding, struck him and having thrown him half-dead left him. And having
become awake and being held by much fear he made known the thing seen to his
own wife), and likewise also from what was said to him through the patrician
Bardanes by the monk at Philomelion, and from the changing of dress and the
treading on the garments. Nevertheless, having summoned the papias, he gave
this man over to keep him safely toward a certain dwelling of those who sweep
the palace, having secured him with iron fetters, whose key he guarded stored
in his undergarment, having entrusted this to none of the others. =1.19= So
about the third night hour the emperor, having risen from the bedchamber,
stoutly broke down all the doors leading toward the tent of the papias; for he
was very stout and bold-hearted. And entering he finds Michael sleeping on the
bed of the papias, and the papias lying beside him on the ground. And bending
about Michael he tested with his palm his breast, what impulse it sent forth,
an anxious or a carefree one. And when he recognized that this man had not
perceived the touch, he had wondered greatly at his uncaring sleep, nodding
his own head and inferring hard things, and going through, having contracted
the anger greatly with his hand against the papias, he fanned the



threat upon both. These things a eunuch boy attending on Michael had seen in
secret, who recognized the emperor entering from no other thing than from the
imperial boots of scarlet, creeping under the bed (for there he had been
hidden from the emperor); so after the arrival of the emperor he recounted all
the things that had happened both to his own master Michael and indeed also to
the papias. The papias having become a hearer of these things and being held
by measureless fear, together with Michael himself they prepare an approved
counsel. Michael pretends to have a very great care of the debts according to
the soul, so that he might lay these before one of the holy men through a
certain Theoctistus, most faithful of those serving him, who after these
things was honored with the distinguished rank of the patrician and was
entrusted with the care of the imperial reed, through which he was glorified
as kanikleios, upon whom also the things of the guardianship of Michael were
confirmed as to a well-disposed man. But then the end according to the request
of the pretext was hung from an imperial ordinance, and the plot went forward
into deed. For Michael, having set out exactly to Theoctistus the things of
the attack, and having ferried across in secret-mystic fashion a deep
consideration to those who had shared the ambush with him, insists that he
will detect their partnership to the lord, if they should now neglect the
undertaking. =1.20= From here having prepared a common counsel they compose
the undertaking thus, to slip in by night through the Ivory Gate, at which the
things of the doxology had been dedicated to God, as about to play priests in
the vestments and to handle swords secretly, and then to make the turning
toward the right part and attack the emperor. For a certain old type had run
through, that the priests should wait out the night outside the palace until
the entry appointed for them toward dawn. Hence the rebels entered as priests,
following the true ones; and the priests went into the oratory church, but
those left over sat in a dark place unnoticed, so that they might attack the
emperor opportunely. And when the dawn hymn had set in, the emperor according
to custom comes down to the church. The hour toward dawn was putting the
ambush into the open, if the daring of the attackers had not fanned the
triumph. For at once with boiling and sharp courage, going over sword in hand
beside the mark, they stretched the swords against the leader of the priests,
guessing this man to be the emperor, led astray from the form that then fell
upon them; for a most bitter winter having been set, the emperor used upon the
head a standing-up covering, and in the same manner also the one leading the
catalogue of the priests; from which it happened that the rebels truly
displayed the undertaking against him. Who, struck at the sudden rush of the
attackers, cried out strongly that the emperor was another, toward whom they
had hung the onset. Wherefore he also stripped the head as a plain mark (for
baldness stood about him), so that he might not draw the cut from what was at
hand. So the emperor went into the mercy-seat happening upon it, and having
seized the cross of this, or according to some the hanging of the censer,
being at a loss for some defensive instrument, he was about to stand against.
Nevertheless having become plain to those who would put him to death, at once
they chopped him with the swords as he held a cross in his hands, so that from
this at least he sent off the cut. For which cause he spoke toward one of the
