16 TO NOMUS THE CONSUL. 17 TO DIONYSIUS, COUNT OF THE EAST.
Many wish to rule others, but few know how. For the care of men needs, beyond
every art and science, prudence, so that the one who cares for them, adorned
with this, may find what must be done; and it needs also temperance and
justice, so that what has been found by the former, ratified by these, may
become a deed and not be corrupted by the contrary dispositions. For this
reason such a man needs both courage and prudence; for this scatters the
opposing phalanx. Your Magnificence, adorned with these, has accomplished the
science of rule, [and] if the head of these, piety, had been present (for I
cannot bear to be silent, having become an intimate, but I will counsel the
greatest things), blame would not have been able to weave even a chance
slander. But concerning this I need a longer discourse. Now I entreat your
Greatness to use in season both your nobility toward those ruled and your
care for the common things, and to set Cyrrhus on its feet, which is more
useful to the public revenues than many splendid and famous cities. For it
contributes many times more than those, and it is not just that it become a
bywork of slander, which through the favor will be shown at once. For our
admirable son Philip has reached the imperial city and will teach the highest
authority the sufferings of the city and will receive in every way


remedies against evil, dissolving the danger that hangs over it. For this
reason I announce a small delay; for we hope that our Master who loves
mankind will scatter this gloomy and shrieking cloud as quickly as possible.
18 TO AREOBINDUS THE MASTER OF SOLDIERS.
When I was pursuing the solitary life I preferred quiet above all things
together; for this is what the law of that life commands. But since God
ordered me, though unworthy, to shepherd souls, I am compelled, often even
against my will, to take care of affairs whose concern is pain[ful] to me,
but lawful <and> useful to those who make use of it; for the apostolic law
cries: If one member suffers, all the members suffer with it; and again: he
that rules, with diligence. Serving these laws, I address your Highness by
letter and I entreat that the estate Sergitheum (and this is of our diocese),
which pays tribute under your authority, enjoy humanity. For the oil canon
which they are charged wears down those who farm it, the earth having borne
no fruit last year nor this year, or very little. And it befits you, who
shine with understanding, with which you also adorn your courage, † the
things supplied from God by the husbandmen, even if it should at some time
hold back the yield of the fruits †, to think the tillers of the land worthy
of sparing, who cannot pay the appointed revenue; for not even the springs
can offer the streams of waters when they do not receive them from the
fountains. This entreaty we offer, having been often troubled by the farmers.
And your Magnificence, having considered what must be done and what is
reasonable in what has been said, I well know will impart humanity, inasmuch
as you long to obtain this from the God of all.
19 TO CONSTANTINE, FORMER PREFECT.
The God of all adorned the nature of men with reason; for which cause it also
finds by itself what is needful; and witnesses are those of old, who did not
receive laws from others, but themselves found laws and introduced the
constitution that fits rational beings. And if anyone besides nature should
also take on education and be carefully reared on the labors of others, he
offers to nature the manifold teaching as a kind of material. And she, as one
skilled, rejects the superfluous, receives what is profitable, and fashions
the love-worthy statues of virtue; for she does not receive the lessons from
the poets or from the philosophers or indeed from the other prose-writers
simply and at random, but imitating the bee she gathers what is useful and
lets the superfluous go. Your Magnificence, embracing this life, has
increased the reason implanted in nature, or rather has shown it when it was
hidden; for the passions make it submerged; but when it obtains wings it
swims over and comes up and lives as in the light. For this reason I
especially entreat your Highness on behalf of those who need forethought; for
moving more rationally it cares for them with greater eagerness. I ask that
the most admirable and most splendid Dionysius also enjoy this care, who held
office not according to his will, and lives with poverty, and embraces a
decent and moderate life, but is charged with things which not even a slave
become instead of a free man would be able to pay. And if he should also be
allowed to teach the tragedy of his own misfortunes (and you will allow it in
every way, shining with gentleness), he will enjoy pity and sparing not only
from you who are


ready for mercy, but also from those who choose the opposite. So pitiable is
the drama of his sufferings.
20 TO PROCLUS, BISHOP OF CONSTANTINOPLE.
By other letters also I brought to the right hand of your Holiness our most
magnificent son Philip and begged that he enjoy your forethought; but the
most God-beloved and most holy man of God, lord James, ordered a second
letter to be added to the former, charging me to add this also to the letters
and to teach your Holiness that he too asks the favor through us. And he is
worthy of love to God because of the excess of virtue, and surely worthy of
admiration to those of God, and especially to your Piety, who both lately
cheered him with letters and crowned him with many praises. For this sacred
head, then, most dear to God, let him enjoy your care and let the dignity be
saved for our city.
21 TO AREOBINDUS THE PATRICIAN. 22 TO EUSEBIUS, BISHOP OF [AN]CYR[A].
If the nature of men kept the divine laws, it would have enjoyed lasting and
enduring goods. But since we turned aside from the straight path and despised
the divine commandments, we have of necessity been allotted a painful life,
so that searching out the causes of the grievous things, and finding sin
their mother, we may flee her as an enemy and embrace the divine laws. For
this reason not only some men fall from good fortune, but cities and nations
also cast away prosperity and pass over into utmost misfortune. This the
Libya of old, now called Africa, has also suffered; for being full of all
goods, she suddenly became empty of them. And men who have nothing gentle,
but live with cruelty and savagery, drive and carry her; for which reason
most of the most God-beloved bishops have preferred a foreign land instead of
their fatherland, having as consolation the hope toward God, and as income
for the necessary need of the body the hands of God-beloved men. One of these
is the most God-beloved bishop, lord Florentius, who is eager to reach the
greatest city, and makes the journey through your Holiness; and I know that
he will put in at a harbor fair for mooring and fair-mouthed and windless and
full of goods of every kind. And I, having enjoyed his company for a little,
benefited † also the greetings of your [grav]ity †, which I make much of, and
I am eager both to send at once, both procuring cheer from it, and stirring
the worthy old man to the prayers on my behalf.
23 TO AERIUS THE SOPHIST.
Of old the Athenians lamented the Sicilian disasters; but those of Libya and
Carthage the present time has brought forth. And these are more grievous and
far harsher. Barbaric cruelty and boldness brought them on. Of these the most
well-born Maximianus has also become, and tragically telling the sufferings
he stings the hearers and channels the stream of tears. And the most
God-beloved and most holy bishop, lord Juvenal, has borne witness to the
truth of what is said by him, putting the testimony in letters. Let him then
enjoy the hospitality of Alcinous, since he too has escaped many towering
waves. For the shipwrecks of the mainland are harsh as well.

28 TO THE SAME.
If I had more sons, I would have sent you more students. Those then whom I
have, I both send and think worthy to share in your education. One of these
is our most well-born son Theodotus, both proud of his ancestral nobility and
adorned with manners of his own. And for these reasons then and for my sake I
entreat your Wisdom to take up this form of benefaction; for it is a
benefaction, at least according to the aim of your Magnificence, even if to
those who know the affairs of cities it is thought a harm. For your
Magnificence, doing this, will show again the fatherly affection.
29 TO CYRUS THE PRESBYTER.
Having been asked for a letter to your Godliness by so-and-so and having done
this, I thought I had received a favor rather than given one; for both are
dear to me, both the one who asked and your Godliness to whom he asked. I
then have written being so disposed; and let your Reverence both look on him
with pleasure and gladden us with a reply. For in this way you will increase
the friendship.
30 TO ARCHELAUS, BISHOP OF SELEUCIA.
The sea is thought to cut the two continents, having been appointed to occupy
the space between them. But if anyone should wish to search out the truth, it
joins the bounds rather than cuts them; for by granting merchants an easy and
swift conveyance of necessities, it prepares the continent opposite to run
here, and this one to rush toward that, and to spread the canvas, and to move
the helms. And if the sea were also mainland, which of the merchants would
have been able, with so great a road lying before them, either to bring what
each land lacks, or to carry out the surplus? But now she, offering her back
to those who wish to sail, fulfills the needs of both sellers and buyers. I
praise her then for these things too, but I praise her more because she has
become for me a sponsor also of the letters to your Piety, and through the
need of the necessities

31 WITHOUT HEADING.
I am both without share in splendid words and empty of good deeds. But you
fulfill the laws of friendship, weaving praises for friends, and by the power
of words raising them on high, and showing how much strength words have, that
they are able to show even the very small things as great; gathering the
beauty and the power of these from every side, you offer their fruits, now
conversing in company, now speaking through letters, and setting Attic
eloquence in the letters, and charming those who meet them more by the
pleasure of the words than the Sirens of the myth charmed Odysseus with their
songs. And I write these things trusting experience rather than report; for I
have often lent my ears to you conversing, and now in the letters I have
seen the one I knew, and I have acknowledged much thanks to the most
honorable Phasganius, who brought me such a feast of words, and I have become
grateful concerning this benefaction. For what he announced I heard gladly;
and if what brought him to us also received an end according to his wish, let
this be thought the work of his zeal and care. And let your virtue write to us
often and procure pleasure.
32 TO THEODOTUS, BISHOP OF ANTIOCH.
There is then even from those who attempt to do injustice a very great
enjoyment of benefaction, and experience has become my teacher of this; for
having spent more time in the city of the Antiochenes, compelled because of
that injustice, I richly enjoyed the honey of your Piety, and I knew more
clearly of how many goods the company of your Piety is the sponsor. For the
consolation of your sight conquered the toil against the injustice, and the
pleasure of your sweetness stole away the pain. Unforgettable then is your
affection toward us ... 33 ..., he received authority and having been
proclaimed first of the prefects, but because he is a man full of wisdom,
knowing how to do good, honoring virtue, knowing how to rule, holding the
balances of justice unswerving, more exact than a scale about what is just,
eager to pity those ruled, using humanity as counselor, raising up cities
that lie fallen, bringing back to life their councils that are gasping and
known only by names, cheering by the power of authority those who possess and
have been compelled to contribute and have at last given up, and in a word
doing all things which serve the God who steers all and the gentlest king of
all; for he thinks with God, having chosen to be pious. For these things we
are glad, for these things we think life a feast. But you who are most
excellent of all not only in authority but also in eagerness to do good, give
us a first favor in this throne, which I lately announced. For I have already
written to your Greatness, while you were still dictating the laws for the
king, begging that the most reverend and most God-beloved Euthalius the
presbyter be released from the payment of the praetura, a man full of all
philosophy both in letters and in deed, and possessing little, scarcely
sufficient for himself and his household. And the most magnificent and most
glorious prefect of the city ordered him to be charged this tax, not knowing
that he is not a most splendid man, but a most elderly man. And if he were
still most splendid, he would have been released from such a tax, the law
that has honored the memoriales clearly declaring this. But I beg and again I
beg and I entreat. Honor


philosophy; free the priesthood from insult; grant us this immunity. And this
is easy for you if you wish; for affairs follow your nods. And you will wish,
I well know, being eager to grant both common and private favors.
34 TO MARANAS THE SCHOLASTIC.
I do not know what to call you, friendless or city-hating; for I do not know
the truth; but that you have come to us at none of the feasts prepares me to
suspect one of two things, and that you have preferred to friendship and
fatherland a field and yokes and mattocks, or those who wrangle about debts
and boundaries and run beside the balances of your vote. And I am greatly
vexed seeing the fatherland insulted by her offspring and I will not endure
to overlook it. In order then that you may flee such an indictment, come to
the city that bore you and make her more splendid by your presence; for it is
absurd that the field be brightened by your dwelling, and the city be made
small by your absence.
35 TO ROMULUS
Fathers also claim as their own the good repute of temperate children, and
masters make much of the service of well-disposed servants. And if such a law
is current among men, where envy often prevails, much more with God who is
good and whose nature is to do good to all without passion. Therefore now he
says to Abraham: I will bless those who bless you, and now to the holy
apostles: He who receives you receives me. Since then he thinks the honor of
his nurslings his own, be entreated, most admirable one, to honor his
Prophets, the holy Apostles, by your presence.
36 TO THEODOTUS THE GOVERNOR.
To little children the bogey is fearful, and to youths pedagogues and
teachers, but to those already counted among men the most fearful of all is
a judge and a tribunal and heralds and lictors and collections; and if want
also is added to these, the fear becomes double. Why I have gone through
these things I will teach your virtue. We expect, God nodding, to celebrate
the dedication of the apostolic and prophetic shrines on the fourteenth of
the month Gorpiaeus. And those who, as I said, are politicians among us fear
your presence. And they fear not the most gentle Theodotus, but the mask of
office which you wear; without which we would have paid much both to have you
present with us as we feast and to share the festival and to adorn the city
and to be chief of those who assemble. Be entreated then, O best of men, to
give Gorpiaeus as a holy month and to dedicate this to God and to the lovers
and beloved of God. For thus even being absent you will share the festival,
honoring the holy Apostles and Prophets with quiet and a stay of business,
and you will enjoy the blessing from them, and you will serve <the> God who
delights in the honor of the saints, and you will also grant a favor to us
who long and pray for peace and cheer poured out in that feast.
37 TO NEON THE GOVERNOR.
This is the definition of governorship; this is a just rule and one
profitable to the commons. For the care of those ruled not only benefits
these, but also helps those who have been entrusted with the rule as much as
possible. For those who care beyond measure for one of the two parts injure
both; but those who mix energy with gentleness, and by humanity blunt the
violence of the action, and honor the measure, according to the philosophic


definition, these plainly benefit both those who are collected from and those
who order the collecting. I know that your Splendor does this; for you steer
the authority with sagacity, and for this reason you rejoice in fatherly
gentleness, and using mild words you make the collections of the taxes better
than anyone harsh and breathing fire. For those who are asked, ashamed at
your mildness, forget their poverty, and strip themselves even of cheap rags.
Indeed even now in so great a scarcity of gold, the goods for sale being
thrown down, no one approaching either wet or dry fruits, having borrowed
gold pieces by fives and tens and twenties, and having stripped the soldiers
stationed among us, they sent the gold, as much as you ordered to be given.
So much can gentleness mixed with understanding; for that tragic saying is
plainly true, that: One wise counsel conquers many hands ... Continue then,
O best of men, steering in this way, bidding a long farewell to those who
provoke and preferring the forethought of the commons above all things
together. For thus you will both serve the God of all and you will keep safe
for the king the ci[ties] entrusted to you and you will harvest the good
repute from all.
38 TO ISOCASIUS THE [SOPHIS]T.
The noble and good Gerontius, who knows best how to carpenter woods, and has
learned from the art to set images of animals and trees of every kind into
the woods, and has a manner that most finely conquers the virtue of the art,
I have sent to your Magnificence, needing the conveyance of his art, but
preferring your service to our need. For having learned from the most
splendid Eurycianus that your Greatness needs the man for the decoration of
the house, I have sent him at once to your Wisdom, so that he may put the
work into the hands of the best of his fellow-craftsmen and return to us as
quickly as possible. Since then, having the man in our hands, we have
preferred your affairs, we promise to obtain the same favor; and we shall
obtain it, if, having set over your works those who ought, he returns to us
as quickly as possible.
39 TO ANTIOCHUS, FORMER PREFECT.
From many other places also it is possible to see your just judgment; but
what shows you most of all is the vote concerning the rulers to whom you have
entrusted to lead both the nations and the cities; for making a common care
of all the subjects, choosing the most unbribable and those stronger than
money and holding the balances of justice e[qual], and in a word the best,
you have set them over the cities as a kind of fathers and physicians and
pilots. We have then met many other noble and good men also who have obtained
by your vote to rule, but we have seen as especially admirable and most
worthy of respect the most splendid Neon. For we have also taken more
experience of him, since he has obtained to move the helms of our fatherland
too and through all the time of the rule he has prepared the ship, steering
wisely, to be borne with a fair wind. This man, now having ceased from the
rule, has himself been released from tumults and cares together, but he has
stripped those ruled of fatherly care; and he runs to your Greatness, having
acquired glory instead of money and shining in that admirable poverty worthy
of many praises. But send this man to us again with the helms. For may it not
be that a man who knows how to do good should be in want of occasions for
benefaction.
40 TO DOMITIAN THE QUAESTOR.


Through the affairs themselves we have learned that not nature but will
prevails in men in virtue and vice. And some honor incontinence, others
temperance, being steered not by nature but by choice. For though many accuse
the na[tion] of the Isaurians, that they love money and are gold-loving and
reach after more, we have found the most splendid Neon, born and reared among
them, but having abhorred the grasping life, or rather not only not enduring
to stretch out his hand unjustly, but not even choosing to gain justly, and
having spent his own things on the care of the cities, and having lent his
ear to every one who is wronged, but having shut the barriers of the court
against the unjust and sycophants. What then those deprived of such care
would suffer, it is possible for your Magnificence to learn from these
things.
41 TO ANDREW, BISHOP OF SAMOSATA.
You have painted wonderfully for us, O best of men, using the colors of
words, the most God-beloved Damian the presbyter; for we have seen him such
as you interpreted through the letters. And I took experience of the manner,
but the people enjoyed the word. For the illness hindered me, and I have only
heard from the hearers that he is such a man as your Piety indicated. But
having given us a taste, and now imitating the clouds that hang above, he
lets fall a few drops, and passes by those who thirst. And it pleased me that
he became a sponsor of letters to your Holiness; for even being weakly
disposed, I was eager through him to address your Piety and to stir you to
the prayer [on my behalf].
42 TO EUTHALIUS THE PRESBYTER.
Having had one consolation in th[is] cit[y], the company of your Godliness, I
was greatly grieved, not having enjoyed this in so long a time. And I ache
more that, having fallen into this unexpected loss, and being despondent, as
is likely, you do not even have the one who cheers the soul and drives away
the despondency with the charms of the all-holy Spirit. But, O admirable one,
become for yourself both counselor and teacher, and show in the contests the
philosophy which you have pursued from childhood, and let all learn that you
philosophize in deed rather than in word, thinking human things nothing, and
setting streams of wealth and utmost poverty as equal. For each of these, as
you know, is by nature such as to pass this way and that according to the
inflow of tidal straits, and now to be toward this one, now toward that. But
virtue, whose nursling you are, is a lasting good, and the one who possesses
it must guard it safely, and the one who does not have it must seek it
earnestly; and gold and the other goods of wealth, when present, one must
manage as one ought,
and when absent not long for. These things you continue to philosophize;
these things you have often also counseled to others. Let those who seek
learn then that they are a[ble] to take money, but they can never [pl]under
[vir]tue.
43 TO AGIANUS THE MONK.
Nothing in excess and measure is best, and all such things as have been said
by the wise of old, I hold to belong to every thing both noble and
profitable; for not only wishing to hinder high and overgrown thoughts did
those who took up the ordering of manners say these things, but also the
things too humble and lying somewhere below beyond measure


raising up and teaching due measure. You must then, master, being wise and
able to make others wise and being full of such words and having been taught
to love the neighbor as yourself, not harm the mind of the one loved by
encomia, nor raise him on high and lift him with words, and humble yourself
beyond the excess and choose this unpraised lack of measure, nor measure the
praise by longing, but prefer the truth to friendship, and hide the stains of
the friend (for this the lawgiver of friendship charges: Love, he says, will
cover a multitude of sins), and admire the apparent successes in due measure.
And saying these things, I do not teach your sagacity, which of all has been
most educated in such things, but I am beating back my praises; for the other
of these wearies [me]; [and] I have heard God [through the] prophet saying to
Israel: My people, those who bless you lead you astray, and they disturb the
path of your feet. Concerning these things I offer this entreaty to your
Godliness; and concerning the girl I teach your Godliness that I care for her
because of the orphanhood, yet I have not let myself down into the words
about betrothal, but I have yielded the authority to her mother. And I
entreat your Piety to make known to us both the name and the family of the
one who wishes to betroth (for the letters contained none of these things),
so that I may make these things known both to the mother and to those related
to the girl and may counsel what is needful.
44 TO ISOCASIUS THE SOPHIST.
The young men who study with you you not only train in the Hellenic tongue
and teach Attic eloquence, but you also think them worthy of the other
forethought, both caring for the seemliness of manners and preparing the life
to look toward virtue; and besides these you also make the other care of
them, freeing them from those who try to do injustice, introducing them to
those able to help, and in a word doing the things of fathers; for these
things are proper to those among you who put this rational wisdom into the
souls of men. Such benefactions our most well-born and most longed-for son
Philip has from your Great-mindedness. For when the contests about the
cerarchy stood in, you were among those [in] the contest and doing everything
whatever for the young man. And now too, as I have learned, your Highness has
contributed much zeal, so that he may not be of those led and carried by the
noble governors, but of those honored and thought worthy of honor. And may
many goods come to you, making such care of those deprived of a father. For
these things then we too acknowledge thanks to your Magnificence; but since
the rank for the present injures him (for it also deprives him of the first
places of the council) and all the intimates are displeased at this and
threaten no longer to care for the affairs, if this should happen, I entreat
your Wisdom to take up this form of benefaction; for it is a benefaction,
according to the aim of your Magnificence, even if to those who know the
affairs of the city it is thought a harm. For your Magnificence, doing this,
will show again the fatherly affection.
45 TO THEODOTUS, BISHOP OF ANTIOCH.
Nature has given to children to speak freely toward fathers in season, and to
household servants the law of slavery both to honor the mastership with
silence and to speak to those who possess them


with truth what is needful. This is a law also for child-wardens and
trainers and for all who have obtained to rule or who teach some art and
science. Trusting this <law>, and besides this the gentleness and disposition
of your sacred soul, with which your Godliness has from above thought us
worthy, I overstep the bounds, and with that freedom of speech of which I
spoke I teach my master the despondency that has befallen me unexpectedly.
For I truly ached and was greatly despondent, that no one announced to me the
visit of your Piety, I mean to that of the Ancient, not even the most
God-beloved Aetius the presbyter. For I indict him before your Holiness as
having been wronged in the greatest things. For I wanted both to learn
beforehand your lovable presence, and to run as if winged, and to pass
Gindarus, and to pay at least a very small portion of the honor I owed, and
to spend those few days together, and to bring every constraint of fatherly
affection, and to persuade you to hallow our city with your sacred feet. But
now I have been deprived of all these things. For when your Piety arrived I
did not know; and on the fourteenth, celebrating the festival of the
gloriously triumphant Martyrs at Meniggoi, I heard people spreading this.
And inquiring and examining the truth I found others saying that your
Godliness had departed, having spent only two days there; and not trusting
second reports, but sending to Maschala, and learning that both messages were
true, I was prevented from the road. And I ached harshly in my soul both that
I did not seek the ancestral prize, but was deprived of the spiritual
benefit, and that I have not been ranked in the portion of those who serve
your Holiness; for not having been included among those, I remained uninformed
of your visit, O father. These things as a son aching I have taught the
father and master most reverend to me for all things, both apologizing for

46 TO HELLADIUS THE CURATOR.
Report has made your Magnificence known to me; and I have not simply trusted
this, but having heard her speaking through all, and weaving her praises, and
going through the catalogue of successes. For there is nothing else to hear
about your Magnificence, than as much as it is likely the lover of virtue to
have. And one of those narrating these things is the most reverend deacon
Pappus; for he himself also urged us to write, saying that the mind is not
commensurate with your power, but agrees with a man wise and intelligent and
who has examined nature. Therefore I have dared <to write> the letter, and I
address your Greatness and I beseech the God of all both to guard the present
goods for you, and to add the greater.
47 TO EURYCIANUS THE TRIBUNE, CONSOLATORY.
I know the gloom of winter and I understand the violence of the surge, and
the present [li]fe has nothing lasting nor enduring; but the beauty of virtue
alone is unfading. And these things for the present I discuss commonly as
toward men; but it is time henceforth also to remind you of the divine
doctrines, and to lead you over from the suffering to those great hopes, and
to scatter the cloud of despondency, and to show by the word those things
which until the present are seen through faith. The power of death has been
dissolved, and that fearful death has become sleep, and we all who have
received the ray of the faith await resurrection. For this reason also our
Master Christ, the only-begotten Word of God, who exists before the ages, who
is coeternal with the


Begetter, the maker and adorner and steersman of all things, delivered to
death his own body, which he took from us on our behalf, not in order to
leave it dead, but having first raised that, through it he might sow in us
the hopes of the resurrection. And teaching this clearly the Lord himself
said: When I am lifted up from the earth, I will draw all to myself; and
again: There comes an hour when those in the tombs shall hear the Son of God,
and they shall come forth, those who have done the good things to a
resurrection of life, and those who have done the worthless things to a
resurrection of judgment. Confirming this hope to the holy Apostles, he
called the death of Lazarus sleep, saying: Lazarus our friend has fallen
asleep. Then, when they did not understand what was said and said: If he has
fallen asleep, he will be saved, he added: Lazarus has died. You do not
understand, he says, what I say; understand as I say; for I called death
sleep. And the blessed Paul also, handing down to us more clearly the account
of the economy, writing to the Corinthians said: But if Christ is preached,
that he has been raised from the dead, how do some among you say that there
is no resurrection of the dead? For if the dead are not raised, not even
Christ has been raised; and after a little: But now Christ has been raised
from the dead; he has become firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep. For
since through a man came death, through a man also resurrection of the dead.
And as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all shall be made alive. And to the
Thessalonians introducing he says thus. I do not want you to be ignorant,
brethren, concerning those who have fallen asleep. And he does not say of
those who have died, or who have come to an end; for the things having
received a change, he reasonably also exchanges the names. I do not want you
to be ignorant, brethren, concerning those who have fallen asleep, that you
may not grieve as also the rest who have no hope. For he does not cast out
grief, nor legislate passionlessness, nor command to become outside the
bounds of nature, but he measures the grief by the faith, all but saying: You
do not travel upon the same hopes, both you and the Greeks, and the Jews, and
those of the heretics who think the resurrection nonsense; nor do you have
the same starting-points for cheering the soul. For they think death a
dissolution of the living thing, and they suppose the end a complete
corruption; but you, seeing the things to come beforehand with the eyes of
faith, think the separation of the soul and the body a going abroad; you
believe it to be a transfer and a passage; a sleep longer than the customary.
These things all Apostles and prophets teach us. Into these hopes we were
baptized; for the saving baptism itself has a type of death and of
resurrection. Therefore the blessed Paul also cries: As many of us as were
baptized into Christ, were baptized into his death; and again: For if we have
become united in the likeness of his death, we shall also be of the
resurrection. Having such hopes then, I entreat, let us measure the
despondency by piety; let us show to all the philosophy which we were taught
by the faith; let us teach those still sick with unbelief of how many goods
the faith has become a sponsor for us even in the present life, preparing us
to bear all things nobly, and teaching that death is not death but a going
abroad. Marriage did not give you the little daughter, nor did marriage make
parents, but our maker and the lawgiver of marriage has made fathers; he does
not give us the children as paying a debt, but using munificence he gushes
the goods to men. Let us not then be displeased, that he has taken what he
gave; for he gave as he wished, and he took his own things, not ours; and
having taken he does not destroy, but he will give her back to us immortal,
and impassible, and unchangeable on the day of the resurrection. Let us
acknowledge thanks, that he has taken her blameless, free of sins, uninitiated
in vice,


untried in wickedness. Do we love the child? Let us rejoice with her in the
passage, that she has crossed the salty and bitter sea of life, and has put
in at the waveless harbors, and has become superior to this violent and harsh
surge, of which you have now tasted. Let us conquer by reasoning the stings
of nature. Let us take the trial as a time of martyrdom. Does nature command
to ache? Let reasoning acknowledge thanks, and follow his nods. Does the
memory of the girl sting? Let us set against it the memory of the one who
made her. Let us say: The Lord gave, the Lord has taken away; as it seemed
good to the Lord, so also it has come to be; may the name of the Lord be
blessed unto the ages. The one who uttered these things sent a chorus of ten
children at once to death, and he did not even send them forth one by one to
the tombs, nor having been sick for a time, so that the suffering, practiced
by time, might make the pain blunter, but he suddenly heard the end of all,
and an end new and paradoxical. For feasting and reclining on a couch they
were utterly destroyed, the house having collapsed upon them. What in life
has come to be harsher than that spectacle? What tragedy has received such
disasters? For consider how bitter and pitiable it is then to dig the house
become a mound, and to search the bodies of the children, and to find one
lying with the cup, and another having the hand stretched out to the table,
and of one the eyes dug out from seeing, and of another the legs broken, and
of another the hands cut off, of another the brain shattered. Seeing all
these things that noble father uttered nothing burdensome, but continued
hymning the maker, and acknowledging thanks upon all: The Lord, he says,
gave, the Lord has taken away; may the name of the Lord be blessed unto the
ages. The blessed Abraham the patriarch had one son, and this one in old age,
and after many promises, many years having passed; but when he ought at last
to see him a bridegroom, then the God of all ordered him to offer this one as
a priestly act and to bring a sacrifice. And he did not contradict, he was
not displeased at the request, but rejoicing he ran, and the length of the
road wore him, and he wanted to bring the sacrifice the same day. Indeed
having reached the place of the sacrifice, he at once both built the altar
and put together the wood; he bound the child, and stretched out the fatherly
right hand against the dearest. And let no one say that God there hindered
the slaughter, but let him examine the eagerness of Abraham. And someone
perhaps would say that God there ordered, and because of this Abraham obeyed.
But here too God has taken. For what if he did not say, Give me the
only-begotten daughter, whom you have loved? but we know that he himself has
taken; for in his hand is the soul of all the living. And the blessed David
also. You will take away their spirit and they will fail; you will send forth
your spirit and they will be created, and you will renew the face of the
earth. Let us then believe that we too have offered the child as a sacrifice
to God; for if we philosophize thus, we shall also be thought worthy of the
bosom of Abraham, and in the present life the Lord will give us another gift
instead of what he took. For the much-spoken-of Anna also, having borne Samuel
through prayer, then having brought this one to God, became mother of other
sons, according to the blessing of the priest. Bearing the suffering then
nobly, we shall obtain many goods; for we shall have God gracious, and
according to the present life we shall enjoy the forethought from him, and in
the future we shall receive those unspeakable recompenses, and we shall see
the daughter whom we sent forth wearing a crown, and in the present life we
shall harvest the good repute from all, and we shall be an archetype of
philosophy to those who wish to be benefited. These things


I write, since the time of the feast charges me to attend here. For I would
have run and by my own voice have brought you this consolation; but since the
need of the many has prevented me from doing this, we have sent the most
God-beloved presbyter Agapetus in our stead to your virtue.
48 TO AXIA THE DEACONESS, CONSOLATORY.
You both understand the mortality of human nature and have been taught the
hopes of the resurrection. And both are sufficient to blunt the despondency
over those who come to an end; and if someone also departs from life with
good repute, it is altogether fitting that the grief be quenched. For all
these reasons then I entreat you to bear nobly the end of the blessed
Susanna. And especially let the praises from all, both of those who dwell in
the country and of those who dwell in our city, cheer your Reverence. For all
sing her praised way of life; and one tells of her temperance, another of her
humanity, another of her care. And I am a witness of the things said by all;
or rather, I say more than all, by as much as I also have more than the
others, since I take care to learn. Acknowledge thanks then, O Christ-loving
one, to the Master Christ, that having perfected her in many forms of virtue,
he has transferred her to the life without pain, rejoicing and glad and
being harsh to those who weep, and think the separation of the daughter to be
an exercise of philosophy, and persuade yourself that she has gone abroad to
a longer land, living to God and awaiting the recompenses of virtue.
49 TO BASIL, BISHOP OF SELEUCIA OF ISAURIA.
Bearing fountains of words upon your tongue, most holy one, you said you
thirsted, and longed for our drops, doing the like as if someone should say
that the Egyptian river needs a small trickle. But to me even the brief
moisture brings much benefit; and your Godliness offers springs to others.
Yet I praise the noble greed; for it has as fruit the divine beatitude: For
blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness; because they shall
be filled. How much cheer I procured from the letters it is not easy to go
through. For first it showed me the marks of your love of God, and kindled
the torch of friendship; then the company of the one who brought it worked a
greater gladness for me. For the most God-revering fellow-presbyter Domitian
is able both to scatter every despondency and to put cheer in its place; for
he has a manner sweeter than honey, and he carries about in himself the
wealth of the divine oracles, not being adorned with the husk of the letters
alone, but also showing the pearl hidden in these. And having gladdened us
utterly, he grieved as much as he gladdened, leaving as quickly as possible.
And I address then through him your Holiness, and I entreat to enjoy the help
of the prayers.
50 TO AERIUS THE SOPHIST
If you did not know, O head dear to me, so-and-so and his manner and life,
you would reasonably have praised mercy and gone through praises of humanity;
but since you know who the man is, and that the inclination of his mind grows
toward the worse, and he does not wish to sing a palinode, learn from your
Plato of what this man is worthy. And if you say that those laws of his do
not prevail at the present, hear the Master of all legislating.


51 TO APELLES.
I have neither been ignorant of human nature, nor am I displeased at mercy,
of which I especially have need. I know also to grant to friends who ask,
since I too, needing this, wish to receive. But I wish to be asked and to ask
the things that do not work much harm. And if someone in ignorance should ask
something of this sort, I am compelled to strip the request, and to persuade
that I have heard as one hard to be moved. And the favor harms both the one
who asks and the one who gives. This I have thought needful to do now also
and to persuade your Magnificence; and to persuade, my conscience bearing
witness, that I have no human ill-will toward so-and-so; but it grieves me
that he has utterly renounced the divine laws and has become an instrument of
the opposing activity.
52 TO ISOCASIUS THE SOPHIST.
True then is the tragic saying that seeks the life without pain and cries
that it has found this with no one; for most men fall into unexpected
disasters. And this our most well-born son Theocles has also suffered. For
the father has made him master of one field; and this bears not myrtles and
flowers, according to that comic field, but the drunken outrages over the
taxes. And having inherited so much, he has been called to the most just
praetura, and being scarcely able to share in necessary food, he has been
compelled to be anxious about so great a burden of loss; for which reason he
is also eager to reach the imperial city. He needs then through us your
Magnificence, so as to arrive with your letters, which tragically tell his
poverty well and in the sophist's way. For entering the court with so great a
testimony he will persuade all who judge to bring out the vote of humanity
alone.
