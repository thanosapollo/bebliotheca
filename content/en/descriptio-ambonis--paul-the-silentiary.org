OF THE SAME AUTHOR: DESCRIPTION OF THE AMBO, SPOKEN SEPARATELY AFTER THE FIRST
GREAT RECITATION, IN THE PATRIARCHATE

You have, O emperor, the whole of your hope, having added what was lacking to
the all-beautiful temple. Therefore, best of men, fittingly I too now come
adding what was lacking to the book. For it would be a fair thing that words
should join the master in fashioning the temple of the Word. Well done, O
audience, well done for your zeal toward what is better; for I do not think
that you now give your mind to me, but that for the sake of the temple there
will again be a theatre and a festival. Well done, O audience; for already
three times, calling you together and raising you from your accustomed labors,
I have not yet seen one of you gloomy, nor setting any business as more
urgent. For there is nothing small, not even if it be small, among the things
of the greatest emperor and of the temple which has given us every grace and
pious festivals of long delight; which, having cast a holy cheerfulness into
all, has stirred every nightingale of speech, as when spring has appeared and
winter has gone, already then every branch nourishes the well-voiced melody of
varied echo. Nothing, then, of the temple is by nature small; for it is truly
a spectacle of all-bearing delight. Therefore I approach the venerable place
which the emperor, having lately wrought it, has consecrated as a most
beautiful station for the books of the mystic-guiding words.

God-fearing hymn-singers of Christ, at whose voice the utterance of the
undefiled Spirit came among men, announcing the human birth of Christ; and as
many as, after the unstained tomb, proclaiming to the nations of the earth the
signs of God risen, have brightened the whole circuit of the downcast world,
scattering the blind cloud of joyless mist; you who gladly endured the sword,
the life-destroying scourge, and the furnaces, and did not bend your necks to
mute idols, but set the foundation of your spirit firm in the martyr-honor of
the undefiled God; for whom the life-giving necessity of death was more
pleasing than the sun, by hopes unspeakable; whom Christ the Lord once crowned
when they had bathed in their own gore instead of a life-sustaining bath, and
with bloody streams cleansed the whole welt of the soul's covering: come, set
up your choruses and with holy songs weave a fellow-sounding strain with our
dances. For your place is sung, where a great unstained echo is raised by the
divine books.

There is, in the mid halls of the wide-pathed temple, a tower delicate to
behold and leaning a little more toward the east, set apart for the undefiled
use of the books, upright on its steps, approachable by twin paths, of which
one stretches toward night, the other toward dawn. For they are opposite to
one another, and both lead to one place, equal in circular circuit. For here a
single stone winds a space like a circle; yet it was not equal all round in
circular turning, but it draws together a little, until it lengthens the rim
of the stone. Toward west and dawn, running out from the circle of stone, a
neck is lengthened, set upon steps. And under silver metals the divine lord
has set fair walls as far as the girdle, like horns. For a silver plate does
not wind silver in a circling stone, unfolding an all-blessed circle; but in
the middle, spreading lasting circles of a wall that runs straight, a skilled
man opens on either side the passages of the stair. Nor does terror hold those
who descend upon the unfenced lips of the holy steps, but art has fitted
shining stone barriers; and they rose above the steps as much as to guide a
mortal hand. Grasping these, one lightens the toil as he climbs the upright
path. And those things set on either side of the slanting path grow together
with the mid steps and cease with them.

And indeed this stone is not idle; for they did not merely cut a wild peak of
a towering summit as the long-measured straightener of the path; but it is
wholly brightened with well-wrought works, and having a nature of shifting
form it is varied with gleam. Upon it they run through like eddies, in one
place equal to endless circles, in another they stretch out windings that have
wandered a little from the circles. And in one place there is a redness mixed
with pallor to see, in another a fair gleam like mortal fingernails. Elsewhere
a gleam, having started toward a white light, lingering gently, has imitated
the grace of boxwood or the lovely image of honeyed wax, which men, washing on
clean-flowing cliffs, often dry under the sun's rays; and another darts toward
silver-white, yet has not yet turned its whole color, still showing golden
remnants. Such also is ivory stained with long streaks, driving its
silver-poured color toward the fruit of years. And in one place it has a livid
grace; for divine nature has not simply left the livid color to wander, but
has mixed well-drawn devices upon the stone. And upon these a white,
many-turning flower-gleam runs around; for in one place it comes over a wide
space, dip-dyed at the edge with the livid saffron bloom, and in another it is
thinned and shines, and like a newborn moon it winds light about its slender
horns. And near is Hierapolis of the rocky cliff, which fitted its far-famed
name to the stone.

And that whole well-founded stone seat, where they raise the god-speaking
thoughts of wise books, art has fitted with eight cunning columns, of which
two are toward Boreas, two toward the South wind, and two toward Phaethon, two
toward the seats of night, so that it might be raised, and there might come to
be beneath the stone as it were another hall, where the interpreters of wisdom
prepare a gracious song of the bright Child. And for some it is a roof from
below, for others a floor from above. And the one you will perceive equal to
outstretched plains, unsloping about the sandals of mortals; but the one below
the stonecutter hollowed in grooves, and it rises above the heads of the holy
ones, arched upward with craftsman's adornment, like the bent back of a
strong-shelled tortoise, or like a hide of ox raised over a helmet, when the
pyrrhic dance tosses a man with well-whirled leaps. And the jagged brow of
that whole stone they girded all round with silver metals, where a
cunning-working man, carving with the point of iron trees of varied form and
fair flowers, set in the delicate leaves of many-branched clusters.

And for all alike, the steps and the floor and the columns themselves, a
skilled man, keeping all the foundations unshaken, raises beneath a base of
stone foundation, rising above the ground the height of a man's foot. And that
those foundations of the hall might be widened, they threw around the mid
belly on either side half-cut circles of stones, and in the cut-around space
they set columns standing apart, crowning a half-circle. And the whole belly
was widened under four most blessed columns here and there, toward South and
toward Boreas. And a stone, winding on either side under a barrier, shuts in
the cave like a house. These lovely columns the stonecutters raised from the
Phrygian land, from the halls of Mygdon's height, with strong picks; and one
seeing the flowers of the stone would say that white lilies were mixed with
rose-buds and the soft petals of the short-lived anemone, in one place enough
of rose-color and gently white in the metal, in another enough of shining
white and gently appearing as fire; and there mixed, thin fibers split through
the veins this way and that little by little; and poured into one another they
purple like the blood of the Laconian murex.

First, having set under a winding foundation, cunning, gleaming with bent
coils, they set stone pedestals fixed on high, cut from the well-stoned cliff
of the Bosporus. And they flash white, even if on scattered paths a dark-blue
vein is spread about the shining-white skin. Twice under four sides on each
pedestal the stonecutter polished grace, and a steel of carving bound a
circular neck upon them, so that a column might set an unshaken foot below,
having cast its track upon the circular pedestal. And splendor laughed over
the foundations of the whole temple, when the column was set upon the
well-polished pedestal, as when, the sun newly crossing as it rises, a white
cloud is varied with reddening rays. Thus they threw around a half-circle with
four columns standing about; and the other half, circling with four other
columns, they turned a lovely stone tunic around the well-wrought cave. And
looking at the three intervals among the four columns, these too he coped with
the metals of Hierapolis, winding a little-bent fair stone around; for it was
fitting that even the stone should nod toward the holy name, having crowned
the fair floor of the undefiled hall; and in the last space he set a
well-fitted door, gently rounded, from which a man comes, given leave, down to
the foundation of the cave with the holy books. And you will perceive all the
shapes of the cave alike, toward the South, the Garamantian, and toward the
Arimaspian wind, in columns and foundation and barrier. But for the doors the
skilled men did not set one place, but one they fixed westward, the other
eastward; the western toward Boreas, and the southern gate looks toward
Phaethon. And the barriers did not stand equal in measure to the fixed
columns, but they rise above the graceful foundation as much as to hide men
under the recesses of the cave. But the eight columns with deeply carved heads
stand out from the barrier, even if both set a firm base of equal stretch upon
one house-foundation. And they gleam round about with gold-inlaid heads,
flashing with wealth, equal to the gold-gleaming disk of the sun when it
shoots its arrows at the jutting cliffs.

And all the rising heads a circling rim of wooden adornment crowned from
above, so that it might yoke the columns bound in one whirl, even if each is
set apart from each; upon which you will find trees set with fire-clusters,
flashing in abundance the fire-flower of silver branches. Yet the shoot did
not run wandering wherever chance might have it, but it rises in order, equal
to a many-rimmed cone, heavy with lights. And from the wide-pathed circle,
always stealing away, it climbs sharp to the last point; and there you will
perceive a fair ending girdle, stained all round with sapphire dust and
crowned with golden leaves of ivy. And toward the house of Zephyr and toward
the wing of the burning Eurus they fixed on the rim, here and there, two
silver crosses, where a nail, wound at the tip, holds in company countless
lights of torches with its bindings, winding the round form of a well-bent
crook.

So much does the ambo, having paths on both sides, shine; for thus they named
this place, approachable by divine paths, where the people stretch a watching
eye, hearing the unstained rites of the god-fearing word. Nor did they set a
mere idle cutting of stones in the steps, but one will behold thin fibers
poured over the white; and they purple sweetly, equal to the sea-flowering
murex. And on the unworn stones the stonecutter roughened the unwearied ridge
of the man-receiving steps, holding an unturning support for the feet, lest
some descending path, snatching a slipping traveler from on high, dash him
unsteady upon the floor. Thus in order, with successive goings, one stone
stepping upon another yields, as much as one leaping up may plant a foot in
turn.

And as in the sea-swells an island rises, cunning with ears of grain and
grape-cluster and a blooming meadow and well-wooded cliffs; and travelers
sailing by count it blessed, shepherding the pains of sea-worn care; so in the
mid halls of the boundless temple the upright ambo appears, towered with
stones, cunning with a meadow of stones and the beauty of art. And indeed it
did not stand wholly set apart into the mid space, like the sea-girt haunts of
islands; but rather it is like some sea-beaten land, which an isthmus drives
as a jut through the grey swell in mid seas, and holding it bound from one
beginning has checked it from being seen as a true island; and running forward
upon the sea-streams it has fastened an isthmian cable to the shoreward hill.
Such to behold is this place; for, having sprung from the last eastern step, a
long channel goes through, until it plants its sole about a silver
double-door, striking the priestly barrier with a long foundation. And it is
fenced on both sides with walls. Yet they did not cast high-stretched slabs in
those barriers, but only so much as to mark the navel-girdle of a man standing
by. From there, turning back, a man of good tidings goes through, lifting the
golden book. And when the multitude is eager, according to the mystic honor of
the undefiled God, to press lips and palms about the holy book, countless
waves of moving peoples break around. And the place is stretched like a
double-striking isthmus, guiding toward the holy palace of the table a man
leaping down from a deep-cliffed lookout; and on either side they fenced the
whole path with Thessalian green-faced stone. And a great meadow under the
stones brings a lovely grace to the eyes. And about each Thessalian stone
there stands as it were another column of equal stretch, not at all like
circular cylinders; a man concerned with lines would say that the columns
follow the shape of a long cube not equal-sided. And here the stonecutters,
yoking a harmony of Molossian stone, wedged one stone into another in turn.
And from a Phrygian hill the stone-working craftsman cut these stones too. And
there, casting your eyes that shepherd cares, in one place you might see a
fair snake-like stone trailing around, and they swell sweetly with well-bent
goings; and beside one another stretched, a fire-red and a silver-white and
some middle color of both, a streak winding in turn, rolling a crooked coil in
a creeping course; and in another place you will perceive moon-like and
star-like seals of the stone by nature, with lines changing this way and that.
But also upon the outstretched lips of the barrier they fitted another long
stone, born of the same well-cliffed height, so that below, set in the
foundations of the base, it might be a firm footing of Thessalian stone, and
above held by another stone binding. But also a bond, shutting in the sides
themselves under four-cut columns, unturning and unshaken, fixes the bottom
upon the floor.

And as when someone many-colored, upon a Tyrian robe, casting gold-wound
threads circling all round, in one place winds a fitted adornment about the
whole hem, in another about the fair mouth of the robe, and in another has
crowned the passage of both hands; and splendor on every side holds the
well-meadowed spring-colored threads of the robe about it, and the gleam of
golden thread, flowering wealth upon wealth, raises beauty about beauty; so
the many-working man, having cast the gold-gleaming sparkle of the holy stone
upon the green metals, found a brighter flash to kindle in both. And at the
eastern end, beside the holy barriers of the table, they cut off the isthmus,
so that they might make a swifter passage of the path for travelers going by.

Such works my bright-giving scepter-holder raises for God the King. And upon
his many-crowned gifts he has also dedicated the gleam of his city-holding
calm above the much-hymned temple, so that by a god-receiving counsel he might
set a living honor for the adorner of the world, Christ the King of all. And
may you be gracious, gracious, all-shining reverence of the undefiled Trinity,
to the city of Rome and to the dwellers and to the lord and to the
lovely-to-behold temple.
