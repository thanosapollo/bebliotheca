* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

I do not doubt, brother, that after your wife was sent ahead in peace you have
turned to the composing of your mind, that you are thinking of the issue of
remaining single, and that you need counsel. Although in a case of this kind
each man ought to talk with his own faith and consult its strength, yet since
in this matter the necessity of the flesh stirs thought, a necessity that
almost always resists faith in the same conscience, faith needs counsel from
outside, like an advocate against the necessities of the flesh. That necessity
can very easily be fenced in, if the will of God is considered rather than his
indulgence. No one earns favor by using indulgence, but by obeying the will.
The will of God is our sanctification. For he wants his image also to become
his likeness, that we may be holy as he himself is holy. That good, I mean
sanctification, I divide into several kinds, that we may be found in some one
of them. The first kind is virginity from birth. The second is virginity from
the second birth, that is from the font, which either keeps a marriage pure by
compact, or perseveres in widowhood by choice. A third grade remains:
monogamy, when after one marriage has been cut short, sex is thereafter
renounced. The first virginity is a happiness: not to know at all that from
which you will later wish to be freed. The second is a virtue: to scorn what
you know well in its power. The remaining kind, marrying no more after a
marriage has been parted by death, is praise of modesty as well as of virtue.
For modesty is not to desire what has been taken away, and taken away by the
Lord God, without whose will neither a leaf falls from a tree nor a sparrow
worth a farthing falls to the earth.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

How modest, in the end, is that saying: The Lord gave, the Lord has taken
away; as it seemed good to the Lord, so it has been done. And so if we restore
marriages that have been taken away, we are without doubt straining against
the will of God, wanting to have again what he did not want us to have. If he
had wanted it, he would not have taken it away. Unless we interpret this too
as God's will, as if he wanted us again to have what he already did not want.
It is not the part of good and solid faith to refer all things to God's will
in this way, and for each man so to flatter himself by saying that nothing
happens without his nod, that we fail to understand that something is in
ourselves. Otherwise every offense will be excused if we contend that nothing
is done by us without God's will, and that definition will go on to the
destruction of the whole discipline, even of God himself, if either he
produces by his will things he does not want, or there is nothing that God
does not want. But as he forbids some things and even threatens them with
eternal punishment (for what he forbids he does not want, and by those things
he is offended), so on the contrary what he wants he commands, accepts, and
pays off with the wage of eternity. So when we have learned from his precepts
both what he does not want and what he wants, we still have a will and a
choice of electing the one, as it is written: Behold, I have set before you
good and evil; for you have tasted of the tree of knowledge. And therefore we
ought not to refer to the Lord's will what has been set out for our own
choice, as if he himself does not want, or does not want what is good, he who
does not want evil. So the will is ours when we want evil against the will of
God, who wants the good. Further, if you ask where that will comes from by
which we want something against God's will, I will say: From ourselves. And
not rashly. You must answer to your seed; for Adam, the first of both our race
and our offense, willed what he did wrong. The devil did not impose on him the
will to offend, but supplied material to the will. For the rest, the will of
God had come to obedience. In the same way you too, if you do not obey God,
who trained you by setting before you the precept of free power, will by the
liberty of the will willingly decline into what God does not want, and so you
think yourself overthrown by the devil. Even if he wants you to want what God
does not want, he still does not make you want it, because he did not then
force those first-formed to the will of offense, nor against their will, nor
ignorant of what God did not want. For he surely did not want it done, when he
appointed death for the deed. So the devil's work is one: to try whether you
will, which lies in you, that you will. But once you have willed, it follows
that he subjects you to himself, not having worked the will in you, but having
found the occasion of the will. Therefore, since willing alone is in us, and
in this our mind toward God is proved, whether we will the things that agree
with his will, I say that God's will must be thought through deeply and
firmly, what he wants even in secret.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

For what things are in the open we all know, and it must be seen in what way
they themselves are in the open. For although some things seem to be minded of
God's will, while they are remitted by him, not everything that is permitted
proceeds at once from the mere and whole will of him who permits. Whatever is
permitted is from indulgence. And although indulgence is not without will, yet
because it has some cause in the one to whom it is granted, it comes as if
from an unwilling will, having suffered a cause of itself that forces the
will. See of what sort a will is whose cause is another. A second kind must
likewise be considered: that of a pure will. God wants us to do certain things
pleasing to him, in which indulgence does not plead, but discipline rules. If
however he has set other things before these, things he wants more, is there
any doubt that we must pursue what he prefers, since what he wants less,
because he wants other things more, are to be held as if he did not want them?
For by showing what he wants more, he has erased the lesser will by the
greater. And the more he has set both before your knowledge, the more he has
defined that you must pursue what he has declared he prefers. So if he
declared it for this reason, that you should pursue what he prefers, without
doubt, unless you so act, you are minded against his will, by being minded
against his better will, and you offend more than you earn, by doing what he
wants indeed and refusing what he prefers. You offend in part; in part, if you
do not offend, you still do not earn. And is not the refusal to earn itself an
offense? Therefore if a second marriage is from that will of God which is
called indulgence, we will deny that it is a mere will whose cause is
indulgence; if it is from that will to which another, stronger will is
preferred, the pursuit of continence, we have learned that the lesser is
cancelled by the greater. I have sketched these things so that I may now run
through the apostle's words. First, however, I shall not be thought
irreligious if I notice what he himself professes: that he introduced all that
indulgence of marriage from his own, that is from human sense, not from a
divine rule. For when he laid it down concerning widows and the unmarried that
they should marry if they cannot contain, because it is better to marry than
to burn, he turned to the other kind and said: But to the married I give
charge, not I, but the Lord. Thus by the shift of his own person to the Lord
he showed that what he had said above he had spoken not in the Lord's person,
but in his own: It is better to marry than to burn. Although that saying
belongs to those whom the faith finds unmarried or widowed, yet because all
seize it as a license to marry, I want to examine what kind of good it shows,
a good that is better than a penalty, a good that cannot seem good except when
set beside the worst, so that marrying is good because burning is worse. A
thing is good if it keeps the name without comparison, I do not say with evil,
but even with another good, so that even if it is compared with another good
and shadowed by it, it still remains in the name of good. But if it is forced
to be called good by the condition of an evil, it is not so much a good as a
kind of lesser evil, which, darkened by a greater evil, is driven to the name
of good. Take away the condition of comparison, so that you do not say, It is
better to marry than to burn, and I ask whether you dare to say, It is better
to marry, without adding what that better thing is. Therefore what is not
better is certainly not good, because you have taken away and removed the
condition of comparison, which, while it makes that thing better, so forces it
to be held as good. It is better to marry than to burn is to be taken as it is
better to lack one eye than two: if you leave the comparison, it will not be
better to have one eye, because it is not even good. Let no one therefore
snatch a defense from this chapter, which properly looks to the unmarried and
the widows, for whom no union is yet counted. And yet I have shown that even
they must understand the condition of the permission.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

As for a second marriage, we know plainly that the apostle has pronounced: You
have been loosed from a wife; do not seek a wife. But if you marry, you will
not offend. Yet he introduced the order of this speech too from his own
counsel, not from a divine precept. There is a great difference between a
precept of God and the counsel of a man. I have no precept of the Lord, he
says, but I give counsel, as one who has obtained mercy from the Lord to be
faithful. In fact you will find neither in the Gospel nor in Paul's own
letters a permission of repeated marriage from a precept of God. From this it
is confirmed that one marriage is to be kept, because what is not found
permitted by the Lord is recognized as forbidden. Add that this very insertion
of human counsel, as if already feeling a second thought of its own excess, at
once reins itself in and calls itself back, when he adds, Yet such will have
distress of the flesh; when he says that he is sparing them; when he adds that
the time is shortened, so that even those who have marriages ought to act as
if they had none; when he sets the care of the married against the care of the
unmarried. For by these things, teaching why it is not expedient to marry, he
dissuades from what he had above indulged. And this of a first marriage: how
much more of a second? When he exhorts us to his own example, showing what he
wants us to be, that is continent, he equally declares what he does not want
us to be, that is incontinent. So he too, when he wants one thing, does not
permit the other either freely or in truth. If he had wanted it, he would not
have permitted it; he would have commanded it. But see again: he says a woman
whose husband has died can marry whom she will, only in the Lord. Yet she will
be happier, he says, if she remains so, according to my counsel. And I think
that I too have the Spirit of God. We see two counsels: the one by which he
earlier grants leave to marry, and the one by which he later teaches
continence from marrying. To which, you ask, shall we assent? Look and read.
When he grants leave, he alleges the counsel of a prudent man; when he enjoins
continence, he affirms the counsel of the Holy Spirit. Follow the warning that
divinity supports. Believers too have the Spirit of God, but not all believers
are apostles. So when he who had called himself a believer later added that he
had the Spirit of God, which no one would doubt even of a believer, he said it
in order to claim back for himself the height of an apostle. For apostles have
the Holy Spirit properly, who have him fully in the works of prophecy, in the
efficacy of powers, and in the proofs of tongues, not in part, as the rest do.
Thus he made the authority of the Holy Spirit attach to that kind which he
wanted us rather to obey, and it became no longer counsel of the divine
Spirit, but, given his majesty, a precept.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

The very origin of the human race supports the law of marrying once,
witnessing what God established in the beginning as a pattern for posterity to
study. For when he had fashioned man and had seen that a peer was needed for
him, he borrowed one of his ribs and formed one woman for him, though neither
the craftsman nor the material would have failed. There were more ribs in Adam
and unwearied hands in God, but not more wives with God. And so God's man Adam
and God's woman Eve, fulfilling one marriage between them, fixed a pattern for
men by the authority of origin and by God's first will. At last, he says, they
shall be two in one flesh, not three, nor four. Otherwise it is already not
one flesh, nor two into one flesh. They will be so, if the joining and growing
together in unity happens once. If it happens again or more often, it has
already ceased to be one, and they will not be two into one flesh, but plainly
one rib into several. But when the apostle interprets They shall be two in one
flesh of the Church and Christ, according to the spiritual marriage of the
Church and Christ (for Christ is one and his Church is one), we ought to
recognize that the law of one marriage has been doubled and pressed upon us,
both according to the foundation of the race and according to the sacrament of
Christ. From one marriage we are counted in both cases, carnally in Adam and
spiritually in Christ. Of the two births there is one rule of monogamy. In
each he degenerates who swerves from monogamy. The number of marriage began
from a cursed man. Lamech was the first married to two, and he made three into
one flesh.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

But the blessed patriarchs, you say, mixed unions not only with more wives but
even with concubines. Shall it therefore be lawful for us too to marry without
number? It will be lawful, if types still remain, sacraments of something
future, which your marriages would figure; or if even now there is room for
that voice: Increase and multiply; that is, if another voice has not yet come
upon it: The time is already shortened; it remains that even those who have
wives should act as if they had none. For by enjoining continence and checking
intercourse, the seedbed of the race, he has abolished that Increase and
multiply. As I judge, both pronouncement and arrangement are of one and the
same God, who then in the beginning sent forth the sowing of the race with the
reins of marriage loosed, until the world should be filled, until the matter
of the new discipline should grow. Now, however, at the ends of the times, he
has compressed what he had sent out and recalled what he had indulged, not
without reason for the extension at the beginning and the pruning at the last.
Beginnings are always given room. That is why a man plants a wood and lets it
grow, that he may cut it in its time. The wood was the old arrangement, which
is being reckoned from the new Gospel, in which the axe is laid to the roots.
So too Eye for eye and tooth for tooth has grown old since Let no one return
evil for evil grew young. I think that even in human constitutions and decrees
the later prevail over the earlier.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Why, from the old examples, should we not rather recognize those that share
with the later in discipline and pass the pattern of antiquity on to the new?
See: in the old law I notice that the license of marrying often has been cut.
It is provided in Leviticus: My priests shall not marry more. I can say that
even that is more which is not once. What is not one is a number. After one,
number begins. And everything that is once is one. But for Christ was
reserved, as in the rest, so in this too, the fullness of the law. Therefore
among us it is more fully and more carefully prescribed that those who are
chosen into the priestly order must be of one marriage. So much so that I
remember some twice-married men deposed from their place. But you will say:
Then it is lawful for the rest, whom he excepts. We shall be vain if we think
that what is not lawful for priests is lawful for the laity. Are not we laity
priests too? It is written: He has made us a kingdom also and priests to God
and his Father. The authority of the Church has set the difference between the
order and the people, and the honor sanctified by the session of the order. So
where there is no session of the ecclesiastical order, you offer and you
baptize and you are priest for yourself alone. But where there are three,
there is a church, even if they are laity. For each lives by his own faith,
and there is no respect of persons with God; since it is not hearers of the
law who are justified by the Lord, but doers, according to what the apostle
also says. Therefore if you have the right of a priest in yourself where it is
necessary, you ought also to have the discipline of a priest, where it may be
necessary to have the right of a priest. Do you, being twice married, baptize?
Do you, being twice married, offer? How much more is it a capital thing for a
twice-married layman to act for a priest, when the priest himself, if he
becomes twice married, is stripped of acting as priest? But necessity, you
say, is indulged. No necessity is excused that can fail to exist. Do not be
found twice married, and you do not fall into the necessity of administering
what is not lawful for a twice-married man. God wants us all so disposed that
we may be fit everywhere to undertake his sacraments. One God, one faith, one
discipline too. So much so that unless the laity also observe those things by
which presbyters are chosen, how will there be presbyters who are chosen from
the laity? Therefore we ought to contend first that a layman is commanded to
abstain from a second marriage, since no one can be a presbyter except a
layman who has been a husband once.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Let it now be lawful to marry again, if everything that is lawful is good. The
same apostle cries out: All things are lawful, but not all things are
profitable. Can what is not profitable, I ask you, be called good? If even
things that are not for salvation are lawful, then even things that are not
good are lawful. What then ought you rather to want: what is good because it
is lawful, or what is good because it is profitable? I judge there is a great
difference between license and salvation. Of the good it is not said IT IS
LAWFUL, because the good does not wait to be permitted, but to be taken up.
What is permitted is that about which it is in doubt whether it is good, which
can even not be permitted if it has no first cause of its own, because it is
on account of the danger of incontinence that it is permitted to marry a
second time, because unless the license of some thing not good were laid
under, there would be no means of proving who obeyed the divine will and who
obeyed his own power, which of us pursues the presence of what is useful and
which seizes the occasion of license. License is often a trial of discipline,
since discipline is proved through trial, and trial works through license. So
it comes about that all things are lawful, but not all things are expedient,
while he to whom permission is given is tried, and is judged while he is tried
in the permission. It was lawful for the apostles also to marry and to lead
wives about. It was lawful also to live from the Gospel. But he who did not
use this right as an occasion calls us to his own example, teaching that the
proof lies in that in which license has laid the ground for the trial of
abstinence.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

If we interpret his meaning to the bottom, a second marriage will have to be
called nothing other than a kind of fornication. For when he says that
husbands have this as their care, how they may please one another, not surely
of manners (for he would not mock a good care), and wants them to be
understood as anxious about dress and ornament and every pursuit of beauty for
working allurements, and since to please by form and dress is the genius of
carnal desire, which is also the cause of fornication, does a second marriage
not seem to you akin to fornication, since in it those things are found which
belong to fornication? The Lord himself says: He who has looked at a woman to
desire her has already committed fornication with her in his heart. But he who
has looked at her to marry her, has he done less or more? What if he has even
married her? which he would not do unless he had desired her for marrying and
had looked at her for desiring. Unless a wife can be married whom you have not
seen and have not desired. There is, to be sure, a great difference whether a
husband or an unmarried man desires another woman. Every woman is another even
to the unmarried man, so long as she is another's, and she does not become a
wife by any other means than that by which she also becomes an adulteress.
Laws seem to make the difference between marriage and fornication, by the
diversity of what is illicit, not by the condition of the thing itself.
Otherwise what thing is present to all men and women for marriage and for
fornication? The mixing of the flesh, of course, whose desire the Lord set
equal with fornication. Then, he says, are you already destroying first
marriages too, that is single ones? Not without reason, because they too
consist of that which is fornication. Therefore it is best for a man not to
touch a woman, and therefore the virgin's holiness is principal, because it
lacks kinship with fornication. And since these things can be advanced even of
first and single marriages for the cause of continence, how much more will
they prejudge the refusing of a second marriage? Be thankful if God has once
indulged you to marry. You will be thankful if you do not know that he has
indulged you a second time. For the rest, you abuse indulgence when you use it
without modesty. Modesty is understood from measure. It is not enough for you
to have fallen back from that highest grade of unstained virginity into a
second by marrying, but you still roll down into a third, and into a fourth,
and perhaps into more, after you were not continent in the second station,
because he who debated the calling of second marriages did not wish to forbid
more. Let us therefore marry daily, and marrying let us be overtaken by the
last day, like Sodom and Gomorrah, the day when that WOE over the pregnant and
the nursing will be fulfilled, that is over husbands and the incontinent; for
from marriages come wombs and breasts and infants. And when is the end of
marrying? I believe after the end of living.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

Let us renounce carnal things, that we may at last bear spiritual fruit. Seize
the occasion, if not the most longed for, yet a timely one: not to have one to
whom you would pay a debt, and by whom you would be paid. You have ceased to
be a debtor. Happy man! You have released a debtor: bear the loss. What if
what we have called a loss you will feel as gain? For by continence you will
traffic in a great substance of holiness; by sparing of the flesh you will
acquire the spirit. Let us think over our own conscience, how different a man
feels himself when he happens to cease from his woman. He is spiritually
minded. If he makes prayer to the Lord, he is near heaven. If he bends over
the scriptures, he is wholly there. If he sings a psalm, he is pleased with
himself. If he adjures a demon, he is confident in himself. Therefore the
apostle added a temporary purification for the sake of commending prayers,
that we might know that what profits for a time ought always to be practiced
by us, that it may always profit. Daily, at every moment, prayer is necessary
for men, and therefore continence too, since prayer is necessary. Prayer
proceeds from conscience. If the conscience blushes, prayer blushes. The
spirit leads prayer to God. If the spirit is guilty before itself of a
blushing conscience, how will it dare to lead prayer to the altar, when the
prayer blushes and the holy minister himself is suffused? For there is a
prophetic voice of the old testament: You shall be holy, because God is holy,
and again: With the holy you shall be sanctified, and with the innocent man
you shall be innocent, and with the elect elect. For we ought so to walk in
the Lord's discipline as is worthy, not according to the filthy desires of the
flesh. For so the apostle also says, that to be minded according to the flesh
is death, but to be minded according to the spirit is eternal life in Christ
Jesus our Lord. Again, through the holy prophetess Prisca it is so preached,
that the holy minister knows how to minister holiness. For purifying things
are in accord, she says, and they see visions, and turning the face downward
they even hear voices that are plain, as saving as they are hidden. If this
dulling, even when the thing of the flesh is practiced in a single marriage,
turns away the Holy Spirit, how much more when it is done in a second
marriage?

For the blush is double, because in a second marriage two wives stand about
the same husband, one in spirit, the other in flesh. You will not be able to
hate the former, for whom you keep an even more religious affection, as
already received with the Lord, for whose spirit you ask, for whom you render
yearly offerings. Will you then stand before the Lord with as many wives as
you name in prayer, and will you offer for two, and will you commend those two
through a priest ordained from monogamy or even consecrated from virginity,
surrounded by widows of one husband? And will your sacrifice go up with a free
brow, and among the other goods of a good mind will you ask for yourself and
for your wife chastity?

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:

I know with what pleas we color the insatiable desire of the flesh. We put
forward the need of helps: a house to be managed, a household to be ruled,
chests and keys to be kept, the wool-work to be allotted, food to be provided,
cares to be broken up. Of course only the houses of married men fare well. The
households of the unmarried have perished, the estates of eunuchs, the
fortunes of soldiers or of men who travel without wives. Are not we too
soldiers? and of a greater discipline, as of so great a commander? Are not we
too sojourners in this world? Why then are you so disposed, O Christian, that
you cannot do without a wife? Now a partner in the domestic burdens is also
necessary. Have some spiritual wife. Take from among the widows one fair in
faith, dowered with poverty, sealed with age. You will have made a good
marriage. Wives of this kind it is pleasing to God to have even in greater
number. But Christians think of posterity, for whom there is no tomorrow. Will
a servant of God desire heirs, who has disinherited himself from the world?
And shall a man therefore seek marriage again, if he has no children from the
former? Will he then have this as the first good, that he should want to live
longer, when the apostle himself is hastening to the Lord? Surely he will be
most unencumbered in persecutions, most constant in martyrdoms, most ready in
the sharing of goods, most temperate in acquisitions, and at last he will die
secure, having left children, perhaps to make offerings for him. Are such
things then done with an eye to the state as well, lest cities fail if
offspring are not raised, lest the rights of law, lest commerce, sink, lest
temples be deserted, lest there be none to shout, The lion for the Christians?
For these are the things they want to hear who seek sons. Let even these
suffice for the counsel of widowhood, especially among us: the burden of
children, whom men are compelled by laws to undertake, because no wise man
would ever have desired sons willingly. What then will you do if you fill a
new wife knowing what you do? Will you dissolve the conception with drugs? I
think it is no more lawful for us to harm one being born than one already
born. But perhaps at that time of a pregnant wife you will dare to ask from
God a remedy for so great a care, which, when it lay in you, you refused? Some
barren woman, I suppose, will be looked for, or one already of a colder age.
Quite prudent, and above all faithful. For we have believed no woman barren or
old who has given birth when God willed. Which is all the more likely to
happen if anyone, by the presumption of this foresight of his own, provokes
the rivalry of God. We know, in fact, one of the brethren who, for his
daughter's sake, caught a barren wife in a second marriage, and was made a
father again as much as a husband again.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:

To this exhortation of mine, most beloved brother, worldly examples also are
added, which have often been set before us even as a witness, when something
good and pleasing to God is recognized even by outsiders and honored with
testimony. In short, monogamy is in such high honor among the nations that
even for virgins marrying lawfully a woman of one husband is brought in as
brideswoman; and if it is for the sake of an omen, it is surely of a good
omen. Likewise, in certain solemnities and offices, the place of a woman of
one husband comes first. Certainly the Flaminica is only a woman of one
husband; which is also the law of the Flamen. For that the Pontifex Maximus
himself may not repeat marriage is surely a glory of monogamy. When Satan
affects the sacraments of God, it is a challenge to us, or rather a blush, if
we are slow to show God a continence which some render to the devil, now by
virginity, now by perpetual widowhood. We know the virgins of Vesta, and of
Juno at a town of Achaia, and of Apollo at Delphi, and of Minerva and Diana in
certain places. We know continent men too, and indeed the priests of that
famous Egyptian bull; and women of the African Ceres, for whom they even grow
old having of their own will put marriage away, thereafter shunning the touch
of males even to the kisses of their sons. The devil, of course, has found
after luxury even a chastity that destroys, that the Christian may be the more
guilty who has refused the chastity that preserves. Some worldly women too
will be a testimony to us, who have won fame by stubbornness in
one-husbandhood: some Dido, who, a fugitive on foreign soil, where she ought
of herself to have desired the marriage of a king, yet lest she should try a
second, preferred on the contrary to burn rather than to marry; or that
Lucretia, who, though she suffered another man once, by force and against her
will, washed the stained flesh with her own blood, lest she should live
already not a woman of one husband to herself. You may find more examples with
more care from our own, and those better than the others, because it is
greater to live in chastity than to die for it. It is easier to lay down your
life because you have lost a good, than by living to keep that for which you
would rather die. How many men, then, and how many women, in the
ecclesiastical orders are counted from continence, who have preferred to marry
God, who have restored the honor of their flesh, and who have already
dedicated themselves as sons of that age, killing in themselves the desire of
lust, and that whole thing which could not be admitted within paradise! From
which it is to be presumed that those who wish to be received within paradise
ought at last to cease from that thing from which paradise is untouched.
