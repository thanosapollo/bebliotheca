OF GREGORY, BISHOP OF NYSSA, ON THE SIXTH PSALM, CONCERNING THE
EIGHTH
Those who go from strength to strength according to the prophetic blessing,
and who set the good ascents in their own hearts, whenever they lay hold of
some good thought, are led by the hand through it toward a higher thought,
through which the soul's ascent to the height comes to pass. And so, always
stretching forward to the things that lie ahead, he will never cease from the
good ascent, always being led through the high thoughts toward the
understanding of the things that lie above. These things I have said to you,
brothers, attending with my mind to the sixth of the psalms and seeing the
necessary sequence of the order, how after her that inherits, the discourse
concerning the eighth was added for us. And you are certainly not ignorant of
the mystery of the eighth. For it is not likely that the mind of some should
be dragged down toward Jewish notions: those who bring down the grandeur of
the mystery of the eighth to the unseemly parts of our body say that the law
of circumcision and the purification after childbirth and such things are
indicated by the number =5.188= of the eighth. But we, having been taught by
the great Paul that the law is spiritual, even if this number is included in
the laws just mentioned, legislating both circumcision for the male and the
sacrifice of purification for the woman, neither reject the law nor receive it
in a lowly way, knowing that truly on the eighth the true circumcision takes
place, accomplished through the stone knife. And you understand, of course,
that by the rock that cuts away the unclean he means the rock itself which is
Christ, that is, the Word of truth, and that then the defiling flux of the
affairs of life ceases, when human life has been transmuted toward what is
more divine. But so that what is meant in such things may become clear to all,
I will set forth the discourse more clearly, as far as I am able. The time of
this life was completed, in the first fashioning of creation, through one week
of days. For the fashioning of the things that are began from the first, and
on the seventh the limit of creation came to its end together with it.
For there was day one, he says, in which the first things came to be; and so
on the second the second things, and in sequence all things up to the six. And
the seventh, becoming the limit of creation, marked off in itself the time
that stretches along with the construction of the world. As, then, neither has
another heaven come into being since then, nor has any other of the parts of
the world been added to those that came to be from the beginning, but creation
stood upon itself, remaining without need and undiminished in its own
measures: so neither has another time come into being besides the one that
appeared together with the preparation of the world, but the nature of time
was bounded by the week of days. And =5.189= for this reason, when we measure
out time by days, beginning from the one and closing the number at the
seventh, we step back again to the one, always measuring the whole interval of
time through the cycle of weeks, until, when the things that move have passed
away and the flowing motion of the world has at last ceased, as the apostle
says, there come the things that are no longer shaken, which change and
alteration no longer touch, that creation remaining always the same in the
same things toward the ages that follow, in which the true circumcision of
human nature takes place in the stripping away of bodily life, and the true
purification of the true uncleanness. And the uncleanness of a human being is
the sin that is born together with human nature (for "In sins my mother
conceived me"), which he who made the purification of our sins then cleanses
out completely, consuming out of the nature of the things that are everything
that is bloodlike and filthy and uncircumcised. And so we receive the law
concerning the eighth, which both purifies and circumcises, because when the
sevenfold time has ceased there will stand a day, an eighth after the seventh,
called eighth because it comes after the seventh, but no longer receiving upon
itself the succession of number. For it remains one forever, never divided by
nightly gloom. For another sun makes it, the one that shines forth the true
light; who, once he has dawned upon us, as the apostle says, is no longer
hidden in the west, but having embraced all things by his illuminating power
he produces for the worthy a light that is perpetual and without succession,
and making those who share in that light themselves other suns =5.190=, as the
Word says in the Gospel, that "Then the righteous will shine as the sun."
Since, then, in the preceding psalm he made the discourse concerning her that
inherits, and the inheritance is laid up in the eighth for the worthy, and in
this also the just judgment of God takes place, assigning to each what is
according to desert, the prophet did well to bring in together with the
mention of the eighth the discourse concerning repentance. For who, taking
remembrance of the fearful judgment of Christ, is not at once torn in his own
conscience and held by fear and agony, even if he happen to be conscious that
his life has been toward the better? Yet looking toward the exactness of the
judgment, in which even the finest of oversights is brought to examination, he
is altogether terrified by the expectation of the fearful things, not knowing
to what the end of the judgment will come for him. For this reason, having
taken as before his eyes the fearful punishments, that Gehenna and the dark
fire and the unending worm of conscience that always sucks the soul through
shame and renews the pains by the memory of things badly lived, he already
becomes a suppliant of God, begging not to be handed over to that wrath for
conviction, nor through that anger to have brought upon him the chastisement
for the things in which he has offended. For to those condemned to the bitter
chastisement of that fearful punishment, the discernment is thought to be a
work of wrath and of anger. And for this reason, as one already in the painful
things, he takes on the voices of those in pain, to whom what is brought upon
the ungodly for punishment seems to be wrath and anger. He says, then, that "I
do not =5.191= wait for the conviction of the hidden things to come to me from
that wrath through the fearful scourges, but I anticipate by confession the
necessity that comes from that anger." For what pain does in those who are
scourged, making the hidden things of lawlessness manifest against their will,
this choice does of itself, scourging and punishing itself through repentance,
and publishing the sin that was covered in the hidden things. Having said,
then, "Neither convict me in your wrath, nor chasten me in your anger," he
accordingly takes refuge in mercy, referring the cause of the evil not so much
to choice as to weakness of nature: "Heal me by mercy, me who have come to be
in evils; for from weakness I came to the passion." And what is the weakness?
"My bones were dislocated and were dissolved from their harmony with one
another." And by bones he means the sober reasonings that prop up the soul:
"Heal me, Lord, for my bones were troubled." And he interprets the riddle of
the saying by what he added to the things said, that "My soul was troubled
exceedingly."
Why then do you delay, he says, toward the healing, you, Lord? How long do you
not bring on the mercy? Do you not see the short-lived character of human
life? Anticipate by the turning of my soul the necessity of our life, lest
when death has taken hold every device toward healing be idle. For in death
there will no longer be the one who is able, through the memory of God, to
heal the disease that came to him from wickedness; because on earth confession
has power, but in Hades this is not. Then, as if someone had said, "How do you
call mercy upon the healing of the offenses? In what manner having propitiated
the divine?" he says thus, that "I have grown weary in my groaning, and I will
wash the bed from sin with the water of tears." Why these things? Because in
wrath, he says, my =5.192= eye was troubled, and for this reason I became
someone old and moldering, the wrath that had come into me from the enemies
having made the rotting in the soul. And if wrath alone produces so much fear
in the one who has offended through it, how much more is it likely that those
should come to despair of the saving hope who have known in their own life not
only the passions from wrath, but also all that desire works, and greed, and
conceit, and love of honor, and envy, and the rest of the swarm of human
evils? For this reason, turning the discourse toward the enemies of every
kind, "Depart from me," he says, "all you who work lawlessness." And by the
following saying he shows the good hope of the success that comes to us from
repentance. For at once he both brought to God the words on behalf of
repentance, and having come to a perception of God's goodwill toward him, he
publishes the grace and exults in the gift, saying, "The Lord has heard the
voice of my supplication; the Lord has received my prayer." So that the good
that came to him from repentance may remain with him forever, and so that life
may not again have need of a second repentance, he asks that his enemies be
turned away, being scourged through shame. For he who has been put to shame at
the attempt of the evil, using the shame as a tutor so as no longer to be
carried along with the same things, will keep himself from the trial of like
things in the time that follows. This, then, is the sequence of the good
ascent: the fourth psalm distinguished the immaterial good from the bodily and
more fleshly; the fifth prayed for the inheritance of =5.193= such a good; the
sixth pointed out the time of the inheritance, making mention of the eighth;
the eighth made manifest the fear of the judgment; the judgment advised us
sinners to anticipate the fearful things by repentance; then the repentance
that was brought to God according to reason announced as good news the gain
that comes to us from it, saying that "The Lord has heard the voice of the one
who turns to him with tears." When this has come to pass, so that the good may
remain for us unchangeable toward the time that follows, the prophet asks that
the vanishing of the enemy thoughts come about through shame. For there is no
other way for an enemy and lawless thought to be quenched, unless shame
produces its vanishing; for the shame at things badly lived becomes a chasm
fixed through the depth, walling off the human being's sin by itself. Let us
say, then, "Let all my enemies be ashamed and greatly confounded." And
enemies, clearly, are those of the household, who go out from the heart and
defile the human being; and when they have been quickly turned away with
shame, there will succeed us the hope of glory that does not end in shame, by
the grace of the Lord, to whom be the glory unto the ages. Amen.
