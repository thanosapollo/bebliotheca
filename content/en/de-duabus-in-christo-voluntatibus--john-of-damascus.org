* On the Two Wills in Christ
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of the blessed John, monk of Damascus, concerning the properties of the two
natures in the one Christ our Lord, and in passing also concerning two wills
and energies and one hypostasis.
1 Those who confess two natures and one hypostasis upon our Lord Jesus Christ
ought to confess together also the natural properties of the natures as double
and different, but those of the hypostasis as simple; for it is impossible for
a nature to stand apart from the natural properties according to it that
constitute it and distinguish it from the remaining natures, whose aggregate
will not be contemplated in another species. And again it is impossible that
the one and the same hypostasis differ from itself by its constitutive and
characterizing properties that also mark it off from the remaining hypostases
of the same species, the hypostatic properties, I mean, whose aggregate it is
impossible to contemplate upon another hypostasis. And those skilled in these
things are accustomed to call these accidents and extra-essential.
2 For nature is compared with nature and is said to differ from nature, that
is species from species, by the essential and natural differences. Likewise
also hypostasis is compared with a hypostasis of the same species and is said
to differ from the hypostases of the same species by the characterizing
properties of the hypostases; yet a hypostasis is not compared with a
hypostasis of another species as hypostasis. For the hypostatic properties of
Peter, for example, when compared toward this ox, mark off a nature rather and
not a hypostasis; for we do not say that this human differs from this horse
because the one is bald, and the other well-maned, nor because the one is
tall, and the other short, but because the one is rational, and the other
irrational, nor because the one is son of this one, and the other son of that
one.
And if we should also say that the one is son of Nestor, and the other son of
this horse, and the one wise, and the other unwise, natural differences are
assigned; for the hypostatic differences of humans toward hypostatic
differences of horses are reckoned natural. Therefore also upon our Lord Jesus
Christ the shape of the body and the character of the countenance and his
being son of the virgin toward his being shapeless son of God are not things
that mark off different hypostases, but different natures, and of one
hypostasis they mark it off from the hypostases consubstantial with it
according to its Godhead, of the Father I mean and of the Holy Spirit, and
from the hypostases consubstantial with it according to its humanity, of the
mother I mean and of the remaining humans in part; for when from two natures
one composite hypostasis comes to be, both these and those, both the natural
and hypostatic properties of the divine nature, for example, and of the human,
become constitutive of the same hypostasis. Whence Christ is God and human,
without beginning and begun, the same one hypostasis, visible and invisible,
created and uncreated, circumscribed and uncircumscribed, passible and
impassible, son of God and son of a virgin, that is of a human, without mother
from the Father, without father from the mother, before the ages and recent,
shapeless and in the form of a slave and in the fashion of a human, "fair in
beauty beyond the sons of humans." All these are constitutive and
characterizing of the hypostasis of Christ God, and he is named from all
together and from each one.
3 Wisely therefore the fashioner made a very great difference of natures, that
is of species, toward a showing of the wealth of his wisdom and power, so that
being wondered at he would be longed for more,


being longed for he would be understood, and being understood he would make
the intellective and rational animal godlike, for whose sake he also crafted
the multitudes of the species. "For his invisible things from the creation of
the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that have been
made," and "from the beauty of created things the generator is analogously
apprehended." And again he made of hypostases according to each species both
difference toward one another and conjunction, a natural conjunction (for all
the hypostases under the same species are united by the account of the
nature), and a hypostatic difference; for they are distinguished from one
another by certain characterizing properties, that is by accidents. For since
the monadic is without communion and unsoftened, but communion toward things
of the same species is sweet and mild (for every animal loves the like and is
delighted by the like), even this first, the only blessed and unoriginate and
indestructible nature is contemplated in three hypostases, Father and Son and
Holy Spirit. The enhypostatic Wisdom says at any rate: "I am the one in whom
he rejoiced," that is God and Father. For this reason according to each rank
of the angelic powers he created different hypostases, and likewise also of
the human nature, and not only so but also according to each species, so that
sharing with one another in the nature they might rejoice in one another and,
being joined by a natural relation, they might be disposed toward one another
in a friendly way. And the difference of the hypostases distinguishes the own
from the alien, so that each, recognizing the own according to kind and
possession, might take thought and might not trespass upon the alien as upon
the own, and so that the existence of each might be known. One who makes
inquiry concerning something must therefore know first what it is, and then
thus declare whether they are one or two. And I say this concerning the things
inquired upon our Lord Jesus Christ, natures I mean and hypostasis, wills and
energies and remaining properties. But in order that this may become clearer,
let us say first how nature differs from hypostasis, and then we shall know
plainly both the constitutive properties of the nature and those of the
hypostasis. And thus let us consider concerning the things in our Lord Jesus
Christ, whether they would be double or simple.
4,ξολ 1 Nature then according to the holy fathers is the common and
indefinite, that is the most specific species, as human, horse, ox, and
hypostasis is the particular, that which subsists by itself, a certain essence
with accidents, that has obtained by energy and in fact the existence by
itself, peculiarly and cut off from the remaining hypostases, that which
shares with the individuals of the same species by the account of the nature,
but by certain accidents, characterizing properties, has the difference toward
the individual of the same nature and of the same species. And to speak
simply: Nature is the common, as humanity (for Peter also is a human and Paul
and the remaining humans in part), and hypostasis is the particular, this one
and that one. Peter is a hypostasis and Paul another hypostasis, but Peter is
not a nature and Paul another nature, but Peter is a hypostasis having the
nature of humanity complete; wherefore he is also a complete human. And Paul
is another hypostasis having the same nature of humanity complete. And each
hypostasis of humans has the same nature complete, but not the same
hypostasis. Nor do all humans have one; for Peter is other and Paul other,
another hypostasis and another, but Peter is not another nature and Paul
another. For all humans are of one nature. And these things concerning nature
and hypostasis for the present. And let us say also what a natural property is
and what a hypostatic property is, and the difference of these. Nature then
according to the holy fathers is the common and indefinite, that is the most
specific species, as human, horse, ox, and hypostasis is the particular, that
which subsists by itself, as Peter, Paul, John. For nature is common,
gathering and


containing many; for each species is a nature. And first, even if it is
above-essence, the blessed nature of the Godhead; for it is one and common,
containing the three uncircumscribed hypostases, Father and Son and Holy
Spirit. Likewise also that of angels and of humans and each species of animals
or of plants or of lifeless bodies. And one must know that according to the
holy fathers nature and essence and form are the same. And the hypostasis is a
certain essence with accidents, that has obtained by energy and in fact the
existence by itself, peculiarly and cut off from the remaining hypostases,
that which shares with the individuals of the same species by the account of
the nature, but by certain accidents and characterizing properties of it has
the difference toward the individual of the same species and of the same
nature. For Peter and each human is essence (for he is a human), but with
accidents (for he is either tall or short or snub-nosed or hook-nosed; and
these are accidents), and each has had the existence by himself. And to speak
simply: Nature is the common of humans, the common of horses, as humanity (for
Peter also is a human and Paul and the rest, each is a human), and hypostasis
is the particular, this one and that one. Peter is a hypostasis and Paul
another hypostasis, but Peter is not another nature and Paul another nature.
For both Peter and Paul, each of them, is a complete human; but Peter is not
Paul nor Paul Peter. And these things concerning nature and hypostasis for the
present; and hypostasis and person and individual are the same. Let us say
then what a natural property is and what a hypostatic property is, and the
difference of these.
5,ξολ 1 A natural property then is that which constitutes the nature, without
which the nature whose property it is cannot stand, as the vital, the
rational, the volitive, the sensory, the walking, the breathing upon the
human, and such things. For the one who does not reason is not a human; for a
human did not come to be who does not reason, whether well or badly, and
likewise neither the one who does not sense nor walk nor breathe. A natural
property then is that which constitutes the nature and separates species from
species, that is nature from another nature, and is contemplated in each
hypostasis of the same species, as the rational. For it constitutes the nature
of the human as being taken in his definition (for a human is a rational
mortal animal), and the rational separates the human from all the irrationals.
For every human is rational; for the one who is not rational is not a human.
For it is impossible that a human not be a rational mortal animal. A natural
property then is that which constitutes the nature, without which the nature
whose property it is cannot stand, as upon the human the vital, the rational,
the sensory, the walking, the breathing, the volitive, the energetic, the
mortal and such things. For the one who does not live or does not reason is
not a human; for a human has not come to be who does not live or does not
reason, whether well or badly. And the one who does not will is not a complete
human; for a human did not come to be who does not will, whether well or
badly.
Likewise neither the one who does not sense nor breathe nor walk is a complete
human, but deficient. A natural property then is that which constitutes the
nature and separates species from species, that is nature from another nature,
and is contemplated in each hypostasis of the same species, as the rational
and the mortal. For they constitute the nature of the human as being taken in
his definition (for a human is a rational mortal animal), and they separate
the human, the rational from all the irrationals, and the mortal from the
immortals, from God and from angels. And every human is rational and


mortal; for the one who is not rational and mortal is not a human. For it is
impossible that a human not be a rational mortal animal.
6,ξολ 1 And a hypostatic property is that which separates hypostasis from
another hypostasis, as the snub-nosed, the white, the black, the bald and such
things; for not every human is snub-nosed, but the one is snub-nosed, and the
other hook-nosed, and the other well-nosed, nor is every human white, but the
one is white, and the other black, and the other wheat-colored, nor is every
human bald, but the one is bald, and the other long-haired, that is
thick-haired. And to speak simply upon humanity: As many things as all humans
have, given from the fashioner, are natural properties according to the holy
fathers and the true account, both those of the fashioning and of the
condemnation. For nature is a power given from a divine command to each
species, according to which it is naturally fitted to energize and to suffer.

natural (for the one deprived of one of these is not a complete human), but
the difference of these is hypostatic. Sight and seeing are natural, but the
different sight, that is the good and the bad, is hypostatic; the appetite for
food and the food itself are natural, but the appetite for different foods and
the different appetite, stretched and relaxed, is hypostatic; willing and to
will are natural, the different willing and to will differently are
hypostatic. And as many things as some of the hypostases of the same species
have, and some do not have, are hypostatic properties. And a hypostatic
property is that which separates hypostasis from another hypostasis, as the
snub-nosed, the black and such things; for not every human is snub-nosed, but
the one is snub-nosed, and the other hook-nosed, and the other well-nosed, nor
is every human white, but the one is white, and the other black, and the other
wheat-colored. And to speak simply: As many things as all humans have, given
from the fashioner, are natural properties of them, both those of the
fashioning and those of the condemnation. And sin is not natural; for it has
not been given from the fashioner. For nature is a power given from a command
of God to each species, according to which it is naturally fitted to energize
and to suffer. And as many things as some of the hypostases of the same
species have, and some do not have, are hypostatic properties. Neither then is
it possible for the aggregate of the properties of this species to be
contemplated in another species, nor is it possible for the aggregate of the
properties of this hypostasis to be contemplated in another hypostasis. For
the same natural property is contemplated in different species, as the
rational in angel and human and the mortal in human and in all the irrational
animals, and the same accident, that is hypostatic property, is contemplated
in different hypostases, as the white is contemplated in different hypostases
(for many humans are white), but neither are all the natural properties of the
human contemplated in another species, nor are all the hypostatic properties
of this human contemplated in another hypostasis. And that which is
contemplated in one species only and in all its hypostases is not called a
property, but a peculiar. So that the having eyes, noses and the remaining
members is natural to the human (for the one deprived of one of these is not a
complete human), but the difference of these is hypostatic, marking off
hypostasis from a hypostasis of the same species and of the same essence and
not from one of another species and of another essence; for as we have already
said, a hypostasis is not compared toward a hypostasis of another species as
hypostasis, but as nature toward nature. Sight and seeing are natural, but the
different sight, that is the good and the bad, is hypostatic; the appetite for
food and the food itself are natural, but the appetite for different foods and
the different appetite, the stretched and the relaxed, that is much and
little, is hypostatic; willing and energy and to will and to energize are
natural, the


different willing and energy and to will and to energize differently, well or
badly, are hypostatic. The according to nature toward obedience of the one who
created is therefore good, and the against nature according to pleasure and
own gnome contrary to the law of God is evil.
7,ξολ 1 One must therefore know that every hypostasis of humans has both the
being and the having been brought by the fashioner from non-beings into being,
that is the created, and the vital and energetic and sensory and intellective
and ratiocinative and appetitive, that is volitive, and the subsisting from
essence and accidents. These then are essential and natural, but the
peculiar-mannered and peculiarly-cut motion of these is a hypostatic
difference. For the sharing in these presents the sameness of the nature, but
the manner of existence and the cut-off and self-constituted hypostasis and
compaction of each and the peculiar-mannered and self-moved and the different
use of the natural things show the hypostases as cut off and make the humans
to be called many. So that upon Father and Son and Holy Spirit, since the
existence of each is different, there are three hypostases; but since each
does not will and energize in a peculiar manner nor peculiarly and cut off,
but unitarily, they are not three gods, but one God. These things having been
said thus, let us see whether there are two natures in Christ, and thus we
shall know whether he has the natural properties double; for it is impossible
for a nature to stand apart from its natural and constitutive properties. And
one must know that every hypostasis of humans has both the being and the
having been brought from the fashioner from non-beings into being, that is the
created, and the vital and energetic, both sensory and intellective and
rational and appetitive according to sense and reason, that is the volitive in
a self-determining way, and the subsisting from essence and accidents. All
these are essential and natural, but the peculiar-mannered and peculiarly-cut
motion of these is a hypostatic difference. For the sharing in these presents
the sameness of the nature, but the manner of existence introduces the
difference of the hypostases, and the cut-off existence of each and compaction
and the peculiar-mannered and self-moved and different use of the natural
things make the hypostases cut off and the humans to be called many. So that
upon Father and Son and Holy Spirit, since the existence of each is different,
there are three hypostases; but since the nature and its properties are
contemplated equally in each of the hypostases, they are of one essence and
nature; and since they are not cut off nor in a peculiar manner nor peculiarly
does each will and energize, but unitarily, the three are not three gods, but
they are and are called one God with one another and in one another. These
things having been said thus, let us see whether there are two natures in
Christ, and thus we shall know whether he has the natural properties double;
for it is impossible for a nature to stand apart from its natural and
constitutive properties. Thus then it must be said:
8,ξολ 1 Is Christ complete God and complete human even after the union of the
natures, complete in Godhead and complete in humanity? Yes, in every way. And
are Godhead and humanity one nature or two? Two, as truly; for another is the
nature of Godhead and another the nature of humanity. Therefore two natures in
Christ, and Christ in two natures, if indeed he is complete God and complete
human. And again: Do we confess Christ from two natures? In every way. Why
from two? Because he is from Godhead and humanity. For what does the saying
Godhead and humanity present? Two natures? In every way; for there is not one
nature of Godhead and humanity. Then: Do we confess Christ after the union in
Godhead and humanity? In every way. Therefore, if after the union we confess
him in Godhead and humanity, and Godhead and humanity are two natures and not
one, we confess him in two natures. And again: Does Christ have the nature of
the Father


even after the union complete and lacking nothing, unchanged and unconfused?
In every way. Does he also have the nature of the mother, that is the nature
of Adam, complete, lacking nothing, unchanged and unconfused? From every side.
Is the nature of God and Father and the nature of the holy virgin one or two?
Two in every way. Therefore two natures in Christ after the union. For before
the union there were not two natures in God the Word, but one, that of the
Father. It is impossible that the one and the same nature be created and
uncreated, begun and without beginning and passible and impassible. For if it
began, it is not without beginning, and if uncreated, it was not created.
So that Christ is not one nature, but one hypostasis having two natures, one
uncreated and one created, one without beginning and another begun. Is Christ
complete God and complete human even after the union of the natures according
to hypostasis? Yes, in every way. Complete in Godhead and complete in
humanity? Truly. And are Godhead and humanity one nature or two? Two, as
truly; for another is the nature of Godhead and another of humanity. And other
and other are not one, but two. Therefore two natures in Christ, and Christ in
two natures after the union according to hypostasis, if indeed he is complete
God and complete human after the union and complete in Godhead and complete in
humanity. And again: Do we confess Christ from two natures? In every way. Why
from two? Because he is from Godhead and humanity. For what? Does the saying
Godhead and humanity in every way present two natures? In every way; for there
is not one nature of Godhead and humanity. Then: Do we confess Christ after
the union in Godhead and humanity? In every way. Therefore, if after the union
we confess him in Godhead and humanity, and Godhead and humanity are two
natures and not one, it is necessary to say him in two natures after the union
just as also from two natures. And again: Does Christ have the nature of the
Father even after the union complete, lacking nothing, unchanged and
unconfused? In every way. And does he also have the nature of the mother, that
is of Adam, complete, lacking nothing, unchanged and unconfused? Without
doubt. Is the nature of God and Father and the nature of the holy virgin one
or two? Two in every way and not one. Therefore two natures in Christ after
the union. For before the union there were not two natures in God the Word,
but one, that of the Father. And again: Is the Godhead in Christ a passible
nature or impassible? Impassible in every way. And is the humanity in Christ
passible or impassible? Passible, clearly. A passible nature then and an
impassible are not one nature, but two, and two natures in Christ. Is the
Godhead of Christ the same as that of the Father and of the Spirit or not? The
same and not another. Is this a complete nature in Christ or a part of a
nature? Complete. Is his humanity a complete nature in him? Complete.
Therefore two complete natures in Christ. And if you should say one composite
nature of Christ, the Godhead and the humanity are parts of the composite
nature of Christ, and the Father and God and Adam will be parts of the one
nature of Christ and Christ will be a complete nature, but the Father a
partial and incomplete nature when compared toward Christ. And again: Why do
you not say two natures upon Christ? Because number introduces division.
Therefore also the saying three hypostases of the Godhead divides the Godhead.
But this is not so; for number makes plain the difference and the quantity of
the things numbered and only this, whether the things numbered are united, or
divided. For saying three hypostases of the Godhead we do not divide them from
one another (for they are united according to essence), but we signify the
difference of the hypostases and that they are not confused and smeared
together into one hypostasis. Likewise saying two natures of Christ we do not
divide them (for they are united according to hypostasis), but we signify the
difference of the natures and that they have not been turned and confused. For
you, saying Christ of one composite nature


from Godhead and humanity, say him neither consubstantial with the Father nor
with us; for neither is the Father of a composite nature nor are we composite
from Godhead and humanity.
But you also confess the Godhead passible; for if Christ is of one nature
composite from Godhead and humanity, and Christ suffered, clearly his one
composite nature, the God-manhood, suffered. But it is clear that you
Arianize; for as those, so also you say nature and hypostasis the same and you
say the Godhead of Christ passible and his nature composite, in order that you
may blaspheme him as of different substance from the Father. But may it not be
that we think thus; for we know a difference of nature and of hypostasis
according to the holy fathers. Wherefore we also confess the triadic of the
hypostases and the monadic of the nature upon the Godhead, and in our Lord
Jesus Christ according to Gregory the Theologian "inversely than upon the
Trinity" we proclaim the monadic of the hypostasis and the twofold and
different of the natures and we confess him one composite hypostasis,
consubstantial with the Father and impassible according to the nature of the
Godhead, consubstantial with us and passible according to the nature of the
humanity, the same. For it is impossible that the one and the same nature be
created and uncreated, begun and without beginning. For if it began, it is not
without beginning, and if it is uncreated, it was not created. So that Christ
is not one nature, but one hypostasis having two natures, one uncreated and
one created, one without beginning and one begun, one impassible and one
passible. And again: If the nature has been composed from different natures,
not even one of them is consubstantial. For each has its own definition only,
but the composite has another composite definition. And it is impossible that
the things that receive other and other definition be consubstantial. If then
Christ is one nature, how will he be consubstantial with the Father and with
the mother? For the one is God, and the other a human. And God and human are
not one nature; for there is not one definition of Godhead and of humanity.
For the one is creator, and the other a creature. And again: One and the same
nature cannot be consubstantial with things of different substance. And again:
A nature is not said consubstantial with a nature, nor is a hypostasis of one
nature consubstantial with hypostases of different substance. If then Christ
is one nature after the union, how will he be consubstantial with the Father
and with the mother who are of different substance? And that there is also one
hypostasis of Christ and not two, we shall know from this: Is Christ one son
or two? One in every way. And how two hypostases, if the son is one? If two
hypostases, other and other, and either there will be two Christs or the
Christ is other and God the Word other and no longer one son. And again: If
there are two hypostases of Christ, and he sits at the right of the Father
upon the cherubic throne and is worshipped by all creation with the Father and
the Spirit; therefore we worship a tetrad and not a triad. And again: If there
is one nature of Christ after the union, how is it named? Christ-hood, clearly
the God-manhood. By what nature? If one, clearly by his one nature. Therefore
his God-manhood, and the Godhead in Christ will be passible. And again: Did
Christ suffer? In every way. Did his Godhead suffer or his humanity, which
nature? The humanity. And did his divine nature remain impassible? Yes. Is the
nature of him that suffered and that which did not suffer the same, or other
and other? Other in every way and other. And if other and other, how are the
two one? But the two natures, or other and other, are not one nature, but one
hypostasis, and two natures, one impassible and one passible. And the account
will be well-running.
9 And we say Christ one composite hypostasis; for being one of the hypostases
of the Godhead he was incarnate by the good pleasure of the Father from the
Holy Spirit and Mary the ever-virgin and Theotokos and became a complete human
not according to change or confusion or


alteration, but according to assumption of flesh ensouled with a soul both
rational and intellective, remaining what he was, complete God, and becoming
what he was not, complete human, having assumed a nature, not a hypostasis, a
nature not anhypostatic, but one that hypostasized in him and had him as
hypostasis; for it did not come to be of this one or of that one, but of God
the Word. For every flesh and every soul is said of someone and is of someone
and has him as hypostasis; for the flesh and the soul of Peter have Peter as
hypostasis, and the soul of Peter does not have another hypostasis and his
flesh another. For the soul has another nature and the flesh another, the soul
bodiless and immaterial, the body material, but, since they are of one, they
have one hypostasis. Of whom then did the ensouled flesh from the virgin come
to be? If of God the Word, it has him as hypostasis. And if of another besides
him, it is not to be worshipped nor to be served; for we do not serve creation
nor another besides the Holy Trinity. But it did not come to be of another;
for not an angel, not an ambassador, but the Lord himself came and saved us.
And "the first Adam from earth, earthy; the second Adam, the Lord, from
heaven." It is called Trinity, because there are three hypostases of the
Godhead, Father and the only-begotten Son and the Holy Spirit. If then there
is another hypostasis of the only-begotten Son of God and another hypostasis
of the son of the virgin, either the only-begotten Son of God was cast out and
the one of the virgin was brought in instead, or the son of the virgin is not
worshipped, or four sit upon the throne and we worship a tetrad. But may it
not be that we worship a tetrad, but a holy and worshipful Trinity, one
Father, one hypostasis, one Son and Word of God incarnate, one hypostasis with
his flesh, one Holy Spirit, one hypostasis, a Trinity all-holy and glorified.
And again: That there is one hypostasis of Christ, we shall know from this.
The beginning of the existence of each infant and hypostasis is the seed cast
from a father in the womb of the woman, which the woman, conceiving, furnishes
her own bloods, and thus the infant is constituted. There is one hypostasis of
the seed and of the bloods from the mother that subsist in it into flesh, and
the conception is of the seed. And the woman conceives from the mixing of the
man the seed cast from him, and this itself is the hypostasis of the infant.
What then did the holy virgin conceive? Flesh? Then the flesh is not from her;
for a woman does not conceive from herself. What then did she conceive? The
Word of God? For the Holy Spirit mediated instead of conjunction and dwelt in
her, and the Son and Word of God was conceived and himself became flesh
ensouled with a rational and intellective soul, and he himself became as a
divine seed hypostasis to the flesh, one hypostasis of two natures, of Godhead
and of humanity. For being complete God he became complete human, and he is
one son of God and of a human, without mother from the Father as God, without
father from the mother as human, the same two natures, one hypostasis; for
Christ is one son and not two. And if one son, how two hypostases? For the
hypostasis makes other and other and marks off other from other; for if two
hypostases that have come to be twins from one father and one mother are not
one son, but two, how will the hypostasis begotten from a father of different
substance and begotten from a mother of different substance be one son? For
the Father and God and the virgin are not of the same essence. And if two
hypostases of humans that happen to be consubstantial will not be one son, how
will two hypostases of different substance be one son? It is necessary then
either to say Christ and God the Word two sons or one hypostasis and one son.
And how also do you say Christ of two natures, if the Christ is other and God
the Word other; for according to you God the Word will be of one nature and
one hypostasis and Christ of one nature and one hypostasis. And if this should
be granted, the scriptures that declare the Word who was in the beginning


and God to have become flesh will in every way be false, and that, just as one
God the Father, so also the Lord Jesus Christ one and the own and
only-begotten Son of God has been given by the Father for the world and has
suffered, and that the Christ who springs from the Israelites according to
flesh is God over all, blessed unto the ages, and power of God and wisdom of
God and heir of all and maker of the ages and radiance of the glory of the
Father and character of the hypostasis and great God, being in the form of
God, to have taken the form of a slave, and the things akin to these; for
these and such things are said concerning one and the same Christ and not
concerning other and other person or concerning other and other hypostasis,
that is other and other son and Christ. And how also would Christ himself our
God speak truly, saying himself older than Abraham and more ancient than the
foundation of the world and to have come down from heaven and to be by nature
light and life and resurrection and crying: "I and the Father are one" and "he
who has seen me has seen the Father" and the things near to these, through
which he is shown not a mere human and recently hypostasized, but God
incarnate and one Christ son of God in two natures, in Godhead I mean,
according to which he was known one toward the Father and God, and in
humanity, according to which he is of different substance toward the Father,
but consubstantial with us the earthly and in nothing altered from us
according to the nature, and not other and other, but one and the same
hypostasis, bearing in itself other and other nature without confusion and
change, the one uncreated and before the ages and fashioning of the creatures,
and the other created and recent. For the humanity has not been established
co-unoriginate and of the same species and consubstantial with the Godhead nor
has it come down from heaven, but, since one and the same was God the Word who
united the humanity to himself according to hypostasis, for this reason he
uttered concerning himself at one time high and God-befitting things, and at
another time low and human-befitting. Whence he also wished to be understood
both Lord of David and son, and he received as pious those who call him son of
God and those who name him son of David, the ones as signifying his invisible
Godhead and the generation before the ages from God and Father, and the others
as proclaiming the seen essence of his humanity and true nature. If all these
are set aside we shall be forced to say two only-begottens, one of God and
Father, and one of the virgin and mother. And the only-begotten will no longer
be called only-begotten, another having been brought in to him according to
adoption, and the one will be elder, and the other younger, the one natural
and genuine, and the other adopted and bastard. And then, if we should also
dare to introduce a fourth hypostasis and to worship together with the
three-hypostatic Godhead the one born from the virgin Mary, we shall be found
worshipping a strange god, both new and recent, which it is wholly forbidden
to do, and we shall be subjected in a Greek way to the charge of human-worship
and we shall be condemned with the curse brought upon the human-worshippers;
for "cursed," it is written, "is every human who has his hope upon a human."
But we neither impiously bring in another hypostasis to the three hypostases
of the Godhead that are alone worshipful and venerable, since we have not been
taught from the God-inspired oracles to say the one son and Christ two sons
and Christs, nor do we endure in a God-hating way to serve a mere human, but
we serve and worship one Christ composite from Godhead and humanity and
complete in both essences, from which and in which he was composed, who is
always worshipped together with the Father and the all-holy Spirit, honoring
with one worship his Godhead and humanity, as united according to hypostasis
inseparably and without any partition and having completed one person. But may
it not


be that we worship a tetrad, but a holy Trinity; one Father, one hypostasis,
one Son and Word of God incarnate, one hypostasis with his flesh, one Holy
Spirit, one hypostasis, a Trinity all-holy and glorified. For just as a sword
that has been fired is one even after the firing, so also, and beyond this,
Christ is one and one hypostasis even after the incarnation. And just as I
fear to touch the fired sword, not because of the nature of the iron, but
because of the nature of the fire united to it, so I worship the incarnate God
the Word and I worship together the flesh, not because of the nature of the
flesh, but because of God the Word united to it. I worship the king with the
purple and I honor the purple because of the king. And yet upon the king and
the garment the union is not according to hypostasis nor untearable nor
everlasting, but upon Christ our God the union according to hypostasis is
untearable and everlasting. Wherefore as one of the Holy Trinity I worship
together with the Father and the Holy Spirit Christ the incarnate Son of God
and God.
10 It has been shown then that there are two natures of Christ and one
hypostasis. If then he has two natures, it is necessary to confess in him the
natural properties of the two natures completely and lacking nothing, if
indeed we know him complete God and complete human.
11 He has from the divine and paternal nature the uncreated, the timeless, the
without beginning; for not formerly not being did he begin to be, but he
always was in a generated way the being God, the volitive, the energetic, the
almighty, the infinitely powerful, the fashioning, the all-working, the
infinite, the uncircumscribed, the incomprehensible, the unsearchable, the
immortal, the all-ruling, the holding-together, the good, the wise, the just,
the saving, the simple according to nature and uncomposite, the unchanged, the
unalterable, the all-seeing, the foreknowing of the things to come, the
containing of all things, that is the comprehending, the unparticipated, but
participable of the energy, not of nature; the self-determining and
all-authoritative, the benefiting, the imparting, the illuminating, the
invisible, the intangible, the above-God, the above-essence, and to speak
simply, all things, as many as the Father has, are of the Son except
unbegottenness. And a true witness of this is our Lord and God himself,
saying: "All things, as many as the Father has, are mine." And toward the
Father himself he says: "All my things are yours and your things mine."
12 And of his hypostasis it is peculiar to be a son begotten from the Father
and not an unbegotten father nor a begetter. For he has been begotten from the
Father impassibly, without pairing, that is without mixing, as light from
fire, or rather as complete light from complete light. From the Father then he
has the being and all things, as many as he has. Wherefore he also says: "My
Father is greater than I;" for I am from him, even if not after him, not he
from me. Wherefore he also honors the Father.
13 Having said then the properties of the divine nature of Christ, which he
had from the Father, let us say also the natural properties of his human
nature, which he had from the assumption from us. For if being complete God he
became for us according to us a complete human, it is clear that having all
the natural properties of the Godhead and according to this being complete God
he will also have all the natural properties of the humanity, that is all the
natural powers of the humanity, both the energetic and the passive, in order
that he may be a complete human; for if he did not assume some of the natural
things, he is not complete, but a deficient human.
14 And let it be predetermined that of every energy and use and act and
passion we have received the power naturally from the fashioner and these are
natural;


for it is impossible for any nature whatever to use an act or to energize an
energy or to suffer a passion, of which it did not receive the power according
to nature. For example the earth cannot sprout if it has not received
according to nature from the fashioner the sprouting power, nor can the water
live-sprout, that I may speak thus, the generation of the fishes and
contribute toward the sprouting of the fruits of the earth, if it has not
received such a power from the fashioner, nor can a plant grow, if it has not
received the growth-giving power, nor the winged fly if it has not received
the power of flying, nor a land-animal walk, nor a creeping thing creep, if
each has not received such a power. Thus also the human cannot see or think or
will in a self-determining way or energize, if he has not received the power
of each naturally from the fashioner.
15 The human then is a small world. For he has soul and body and has stood
midmost of mind and matter; for he is a bond of visible and invisible, that is
of sensory and intelligible creation. For the mind is also the eye and the
most pure of the soul. He therefore shares with the lifeless bodies according
to the blending and composition from the four elements, and with the plants
both according to these and according to the nutritive and growth-giving
power, and still also the seminal, and with the irrational animals also in
these, but also according to the vital and according to the irrational
appetite, that is anger and desire, and according to the imaginative and the
memorial and according to sensation and the movement according to impulse (and
movement according to impulse is the movement of the whole body) and the vocal
and the breathing and the transiting from place to place. And he is joined to
the bodiless and intellective powers through the rational and the rational
appetite of the mind, which is willing, the first motion of the mind; for
appetizing he is moved toward thinking and appetizing he reasons and
appetizing he judges. And this appetite is self-determining; for
self-determiningly appetizing he thinks and reasons and judges.
16 Peculiar to the body are cutting and flux and change according to quality,
that is cooling, heating, wetness and dryness, and the organization of the
members. And peculiar to a rational soul only itself by itself are life,
thinking and the rational appetite, which is properly willing. And before
these most specific of soul, separating it from the essence of the angels, is
the dwelling in a body and imparting to this of life and motion and the
leading the body in a self-determining way according to own appetite, that is
willing, and the irrational appetite, desire and anger and the movement
according to impulse, and naturally ruling the own body as own natural slave.
Wherefore also more than the angels the human is according to image; for the
angel does not have a natural slave nor is he naturally a master.
17 And common to soul and body, of which the soul imparts to the body, are the
vital, the imaginative, that is the apprehending or distinguishing of the
sensibles, the sensory itself, that is the five senses: sight, hearing, smell,
taste, touch, and still also the memorial and the movement according to
impulse and the nutritive and growth-giving and the irrational appetite, anger
I mean and desire, hunger and thirst and toil and saliva, pleasure and grief
and disgust, fear, hesitation and agony, anguish, disturbance, sweat and death
(for death is a separation of soul from the body) and all the economy of food
and growth.
18 All these are irrational passions and energies, energies and powers of the
nature (wherefore also all the natural things have been granted by God to the
nature), but passions, because every created creation is moved and energizes
passively; and only the divine nature is impassible, being moved impassibly
and energizing unmovedly. And still some of them seem rather to be energies,
and some passions; for it is more fitting to say life rather an energy, and
death and the cutting and the flux a passion. The cutting then and the flux
and the


change according to quality, that is whitening, blackening, cooling, heating,
disease and such things, are passions, and likewise also the organization of
the members, that is the fashioning; for the nature energizes organizing and
fashioning, and the body suffers being organized. And I call nature the law
and power given from the beginning by the fashioner to each species, according
to which it is moved or is at rest. It is moved toward the things for which it
has a power of energy, thinking, reasoning, willing, sensing, exchanging
places from places; for of these it has the power from the fashioner. And it
is at rest not being moved, not standing toward things for which it did not
receive a power of energy. And motions are generation and corruption and
alteration, growth, decrease and the local motion. And this is six-mannered,
forward, backward, right, left, up, down; and all the remaining things that
have been said are also motions; and the thinking and the rational appetite
and the deliberation and the seeking and consideration and counsel and
judgment and disposition and choice, gnome, and the impulse toward the use are
energies. And thinking seems rather to be an energy, and the appetite a
passion; for everything that appetizes suffers. And we call the rational
appetite the energy, because it is self-determining, having authority over the
irrational passions and judging and governing and bridling these, anger and
desire and sensation and the movement according to impulse. For these are
obedient and persuadable to reason, irrational passions; for they are
naturally fitted to be persuaded by reason and to be subjected and to be
moved, as reason may command, upon humans according to nature. And these also
are passions constitutive of the animal essence; for without these it is not
possible for the life to stand. And these were given to the human for service
by the fashioner, the desire toward God and his wills, and the anger against
the devil and sin and toward making the desire manly. The irrational appetite
then has been given toward constitution of the animal essence, and the
rational and self-determining, in order that it may bridle in a
self-determining way every natural motion and be subjected and subject to the
law of the one who created. Thinking and appetite have therefore been yoked
together in the human, in order that we, being rational, may not suffer the
things of the irrationals unwillingly. For the mind, appetizing in a
self-determining way and rationally and intellectively, thinks appetitively
and considers and seeks and counsels and distinguishes and is disposed toward
the thing judged and chooses and rushes toward the act; for by nature the
rational rules the irrational. If then as rational the human was created king
of all irrational creation, how will he not be self-determining of the
irrational passions in him? Being then appetitive and rational and
intellective he thinks and reasons appetitively and appetizes rationally and
intellectively in a self-determining way. And of the things common to soul and
body the vital is rather an energy and the imaginative and sensory and
memorial and the movement according to impulse are passive energies or
energetic passions, but anger and desire, hunger and thirst, toil, fear,
hesitation and agony, pleasure, grief, saliva and sweat and the nutritive and
growth-giving and upon all death are passions.
19 All these are natural and not to be blamed, granted by the fashioner to the
nature of humans. For he himself granted both the being and the being thus,
and there is no one of humans who has come to be without a share in these. And
the having these presents the wisdom and virtue of the fashioner, but to us it
is reckoned neither virtue nor vice; for they are good and have been granted
for good by the good, but the manner of the use of them constitutes virtue and
vice. For if the will is subjected to the law of God and according to his law
the mind governs the things obedient to it, all the psychic motions I mean,
and by excellence anger and desire (for these are obedient to reason), we have
worked virtue and we have fulfilled righteousness. For unto this we received
the authority of these and as


having received authority we also received a law and have come to be under a
law; for the irrationals, not having the ruling mind that is self-determining
of anger and desire, did not receive a law. Being therefore by nature slaves
of the fashioner and having the self-determining, that which is constitutive
of virtue (for what comes about by force is not virtue nor delightful), we
received a law, in order that we may know that we have a master, in order that
going about in darkness we may not fall into pits; "for your law is a lamp to
my feet and a light to my paths," said David the prophet; in order that our
longing and goodwill toward the master may be known, and in order that we may
share with God in the good, "in order that the good may be of the one who
chooses not less than of the one who furnished the seeds"; for he himself
granted us the power of good-working. And he made us self-determining, in
order that from him and from us the good might come to be; for God cooperates
toward the good with everyone who chooses the good; in order that having
guarded the according to nature we may obtain the things above nature, the
incorruption and the deification from the union toward the divine through the
likeness of the will toward it according to power; in order that using the
natural powers irrationally we may not be compared with the mindless beasts
and be likened to them. For if our willing is not subjected to the divine
will, but the self-determining mind uses anger and desire according to own
gnome and choice, it has worked vice; for vice is nothing other than sin and
missing-the-mark and disobedience of the master's law. So that the
self-determining is a first good befitting the rational nature, and the having
used the self-determining and having authority over and mastering the
irrational passions, anger I mean and desire, is virtue, but the betraying the
self-determining and being defeated by the irrational passions and living
irrationally and beastlike, this is vice, this is sin; in order that we may
not be unseemly doing the things of the irrationals, but may hold by reason
the impulses of the nature. For the way of life of the irrationals does not
befit the rational, but rather both to use the irrational passions rationally
and to rhythm these by reason and to lead them according to authority and to
charioteer them and to be led and to yield to the divine law and to use these,
just as the Lord ordered.
20 One must therefore know that the confusion of the names makes the error for
the heretics. Just as then for the monophysites, the acephaloi I mean, the
saying nature and hypostasis the same became a cause of saying one nature upon
Christ, in order that they may not divide Christ into two hypostases, and
still also for those of the same mind as Nestorius of saying two hypostases,
in order that they may not confuse the two natures of Christ into one, so also
for the monothelites the not knowing the difference of the natural and of the
hypostatic will, that is of the gnomic, became a cause of saying one will upon
Christ. In order then that we may escape the error from the homonymy, let us
thus unfold the account concerning the will.
21 Will is homonymous. For at one time it signifies the willing, that is the
volitive power, and at another time the willed itself, that which is subject
to the willing, that is the thing itself which someone wills. And willing is
other and the willed other, just as sight is other and the seen other; for
sight is the seeing power itself, according to which we see, that which
energizes in the eye, and seen is the thing seen itself, either a stone or
wood or something such. Thus also willing is the volitive power of the soul
itself, according to which we will, and willed is the thing itself which
someone wills. For example I will to sail, the to-sail is willed; I will to
marry, the to-marry is willed. And in every way the willed is either an
energy, that is an act, or a passion. Let us see then whether the willing is
natural or hypostatic.
22 Does then every human have the volitive power or not? It is clear that
every human has the being able to will and every human has the willing; for
what human


has come to be who willed nothing? But not every human wills the same, but the
one this, and the other that, nor does every human will in the same way, but
the one well, and the other badly, each according to his own gnome. So that
the willing, that is the volitive power and the simply to will itself, is
natural, but the willed, that is the to will something, is gnomic and
hypostatic.
23 And again: Will, being of the homonyms, as we said, at one time signifies
the willing, and at another time the willed. For one must know that willing is
other and the to will other and the to will something and how other and the
willed other and the volitive other and the one who wills other, just as sight
is other and the to see other and the to see something and how other and the
seen other and the seeing other and the one who sees other. For sight is the
seeing power of the soul itself that energizes in the eye, according to which
we see. And to see is to have used the seeing power and to energize and to
look; and no one sees who does not have sight, that is a power of seeing. And
to see something is to see heaven or earth or some other thing. How to see is
well or badly. And seen is that which is subject to the sight, that is the
body seen or color or shape. And seeing is that which has from nature the
seeing power, as the human has from nature the power of seeing, whether he
sleeps, or has been blinded; for the human is a seeing animal. And the one who
sees is the one who has used the sight, that is the seeing power, and who
energizes and looks. Thus willing is the volitive power itself naturally given
to the soul by the fashioner, that is the being able to will. And to will is
to have used according to energy the volitive power and to appetize; and no
one wills who does not have willing, that is a power of willing. And willed is
that which is subject to the willing, the thing itself which we will; for
example I will to sail, the to-sail is willed, I will to be temperate, the
to-be-temperate is willed. And unwilled is that which someone does not will,
just as unseen is that which someone does not see. And to will something is to
will to be united to God, to will to eat, to will to drink, to will to sail,
to will to be joined to a woman. And how to will is to have willed well, that
is to have willed well and according to a law of God to mix with one's own
wife or badly and contrary to the law of God with another's. And volitive is
that which has from nature the volitive power, as the human, the angel, God.
For each of them has from nature the volitive power; for each of the
hypostases of the Godhead wills and each hypostasis of angels wills and each
hypostasis of humans wills. And that which each hypostasis has is natural; for
natural is that which is contemplated in all the hypostases of the same
species and of the same essence. And the one who wills is the one who has used
the willing, that is the volitive power, and who appetizes, that is a
hypostasis.
24 Will then at one time signifies the willing, that is the volitive power,
and at another time the willed, that is the thing itself which we will; the
willing then is natural and the simply to will is natural. For every human has
the power of willing and every human wills, that is has used the willing; for
the human is a volitive animal. But the willed is not natural only, but also
gnomic and hypostatic. But not every human wills in the same way nor the same;
so that the how to will, well or badly, or the to will something, this or
that, is not natural, but gnomic and hypostatic both upon angels and humans.
For upon the holy and undivided Trinity there is one natural willing of the
three hypostases and one natural the good, and each hypostasis does not have
an own willed nor an own motion, but one motion and one willed of the three
hypostases; wherefore not three gods, but the three hypostases are one God,
one God with his


own powers, but not composite. For each of them is complete God and a complete
hypostasis; and a composition of nature from hypostasis does not come to be,
and this is wholly foreign and befitting God alone. But upon angels and humans
it is not thus; for the willing of the angels is one, the natural power of
willing given them by God. For each angel wills and each human wills, but
there is not one motion of all the angels nor indeed of all the humans, but
each hypostasis has an own motion and an own willed; for not similarly do all
the hypostases of the angels will nor the same, nor those of the humans, but
the one well, and the other badly, and the one this, and the other that, and
when this one is moved and willing the other is not co-moved and co-willing.
So that upon the angels and humans the willing is natural, and likewise also
the to will is natural. For every human is volitive and every human wills, but
not every human is volitive of the same thing nor wills the same. For upon the
Holy Trinity the three hypostases are in one another, and the Word and the
Spirit are powers of the Father, and for this reason one motion and in the
same way and the same willed. But the angels and the humans, each has his own
hypostasis cut off and peculiarly divided, even if they are of one nature; for
this reason the power of willing and the to will are one and natural, but the
how and the to will something are hypostatic and gnomic. And there is one
volitive nature of them, but many who will, willing differently and other and
other.
25 Willing then is an appetite both vital and rational; and the vital is in
every way also sensory. The irrationals then appetize sensorily, but not
rationally nor in a self-determining way; but the human, being a rational
animal, as animal appetizes vitally and sensorily, and as rational rationally
and in a self-determining way. Willing then is a natural appetite both
rational and vital, hanging upon the natural things only. And natural wills
are first the being subjected to the law of God (for by nature the human is a
slave of God and under his hand), and then also the things constitutive of the
nature, as hunger, thirst, sleep and such things; and hypostatic and gnomic
willing is an appetite according to pleasure and according to own gnome of the
hypostasis that has used it and not according to a law of God, and a manner of
use of the natural willing according to gnome of the hypostasis that has used
it. The cut-off of the hypostases then works the different gnome.
26 But they say that the human does not have a natural will. And they say
there are two wills only, the one the divine, the good, and the other the
wicked, the diabolic; and whenever the human has willed the good, he has had
the will of God, and when the wicked, that of the devil. But they do not know
that it is impossible for any nature whatever to use an act of which it did
not receive the power according to nature. For example the winged cannot fly
if it has not received according to nature the power of flying, and likewise
neither can the land-animal walk, if it has not received the walking power
according to nature. Thus also the human cannot think, if he has not received
thinking according to nature, nor speak, if he has not received the speaking
power according to nature, and likewise neither see nor will, if he has not
received the volitive power naturally. One must then first have by nature the
power of willing, which is willing, and thus will. Wherefore upon our Lord
Jesus Christ, since two natures, two natural willings, in order that he may be
complete God and complete human lacking nothing of the properties of the
Godhead nor indeed of those of the humanity, and two to will and to will
differently according to the natural difference, divinely and almightily (for
the divine will is almighty) and not almightily, but weakly and passively (for
the human will is passible and weak) and other and other natural willed. For
the wills of the humanity are not also willed of the Godhead; for by nature
volitive and


appetitive is the human nature of food and drink and sleep and such things,
but the Godhead is not appetitive of these. For it itself does not appetize
these, even if it wills that the human nature appetize these naturally and as
it ought and when.
27 According then to the natural difference two wills upon our Lord Jesus
Christ are not contrary. For neither is the natural will of the human contrary
to the divine will, nor the natural volitive power, nor the things naturally
subject to it, nor the natural use of the willing; for of all the natural
things the divine will is fashioner. And the things against nature only are
contrary to the divine will, which is the appetite according to own gnome and
of sinful pleasures, that is sin. This the Lord did not assume; "for he did
not do sin nor was deceit found in his mouth."
And since there is one hypostasis of Christ and Christ is one, one is the one
who wills according to both the natures, as God well-pleasing and as human
having become obedient, well-pleasing as God with Father and the Spirit to the
paternal and divine will, and as human having become obedient of his divine
and paternal will by our will. For not according to his divine will did he
become obedient to the Father; for the divine will is neither obedient nor
disobedient. "For these things are of those under the hand," as Gregory the
Theologian says, and of the form of the slave of the assumption from us. And
he became obedient naturally according to his human will; for obedience is a
self-determining and unforced subjection of a will to another will. He willed
then and wills as God the divine things and he willed and wills as human the
human things not by contrariety of gnome, but by property of the natures. For
he willed and wills the human things, as his divine willing was well-pleased
and when and what, his human willing being subjected in a self-determining way
to his divine willing.
28 "For willing and energy of God is," according to the blessed Irenaeus, "a
fashioning and provident cause of every time and place and age and of every
nature," "and human willing is a natural power appetitive of that which is
according to nature and holding-together of the things essentially present to
the nature; for the essence appetizes both the being and living and being
moved according to sensation and mind, longing for its own natural and full
beingness. For the nature is volitive of itself and of all the things
constitutive of it by the account of being, according to which it both is and
has come to be, hanging appetitively."
Wherefore others defining this natural will say: Natural will is a rational
and vital appetite, hanging upon the natural things only. "Willing then is,"
according to the blessed Clement, "an appetitive power of that which properly
