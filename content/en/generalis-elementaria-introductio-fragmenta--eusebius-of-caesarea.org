470 a proof of a man's character is the association with such-and-such. For
when he is worthless, of necessity he will be with the like; when again
temperate and wise, with those pursuing the same things as he. 471 In the way
the nature of the eyes has in itself the seeing power, but is not sufficient
by itself toward the grasping of the visible things, unless some other from
outside lighting and co-working with the use of the eyes and with the
contributing light toward the view of the sensibles, so also the intellectual
and rational nature has toward the holy word of God.
472 this nature has sown in the race of men to know the seeds both of virtue
and of vice, and has conceded to partake of one of these by reasoning, having
established him self-mastering and a judging leader and lord of himself; so
that as having in himself from nature the first-places of virtue and of vice
he would not be able, even if he should sometime fall into the very depth of
vice, not to honor virtue, the conscience stumbling, if he should choose the
worse. And a proof of these and a clear conviction is that no one dares to
become an accuser of his own vice but from the contraries as the power hides
and covers what he would secretly attempt beside the fitting. And the
disposition itself of each, not being disposed similarly toward those
benefiting and toward those attempting to harm him, becomes a self-taught
teacher to each of the knowledge concerning goods and evils, the law of
nature, forbidding some to do to others what one himself would not choose to
suffer from others. 473 It is possible, O child, to speak well, and to be
worthless; but acting well to be worthless is impossible.
