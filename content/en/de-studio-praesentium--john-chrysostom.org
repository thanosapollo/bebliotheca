HOMILY 5. A homily of the same, spoken in the church of the Holy Peace,
concerning the zeal of those present and the sloth of those left behind, and
concerning psalmody, and concerning that nothing in nature is a hindrance to
women for the courses of virtue.

=63.485=

αʹ. Sweetest is the reading of the Scriptures, sweeter than every meadow and
more pleasant than every paradise, and especially whenever knowledge is added
to the reading. For the meadow, and the beauty of flowers, and the hair of
trees, and the rose and ivy and myrtle delight the sight, and after a few days
have passed they wither; but the knowledge of the Scriptures walls the mind,
cleanses the conscience, uproots the inglorious passions, plants virtue, makes
the reasoning heavenly-minded, does not allow one to be baptized by the
unexpected circumstances of affairs, establishes one higher than the arrows of
the devil, transplants one to heaven itself, looses the soul from the bonds of
the body, makes the wings light, and every good thing one may mention it
introduces into the mind of the hearers. Wherefore also I do not cease
tormenting those who stay away, and calling blessed those who come, because
you gather a treasure that cannot be seized and wealth that costs nothing and
possessions that have no envy, and being filled with much gladness you depart
home. For nothing could make pleasure like a clean conscience; and a clean
conscience comes, even if it be burdened with ten thousand sins, by enjoying
continual hearing. For it does not add to the former trespasses, but it both
spends what exists, and makes the soul more sluggish to attempt the same
things again. For this reason I beseech your love to sing these things
continually to those who stay away, and quickly to lead them back to their
common mother, and to make them partners of the spiritual gains; for it is not
any diminution, but an increase, that this fellowship brings about. But, O the
tyranny of money! It either shepherds away the most of our brethren; for
nothing leads them away from here, but that difficult disease and that furnace
never quenched; this mistress, more barbarous than every barbarian, and harder
than a beast, and wilder than demons, taking her own captives, leads them
about now through the marketplace, laying upon them hard commands, and not
allowing
even a little breath from the destructive labors. But what will they do when
that fearful day arrives, the unpleaded vote, and the judge that cannot be
turned aside by entreaty? When the curtains of the heavens are drawn back, and
the host of angels descends with the Judge? When all things appear naked and
laid open? For neither cleverness of orators then, nor abundance of money, nor
anything else will be able to corrupt the just judgment. For when the judge is
unpleaded and knows all things clearly, and the sins themselves stand before
the eyes =63.486= as if inscribed in some picture; then neither king nor
private person appears, neither poor nor rich, neither wise nor ignorant, but
all these masks are taken away, and from the sight of the things done each one
is shown; one cannot see a diadem set around, nor one clothed in purple; one
cannot see one carried on carriages and having ten thousand rod-bearers
swelling in the marketplace; but all these things are thrust aside, and each
one is brought in naked, having his position from his deeds, or from his
betrayals, or from his salvation; for as each of these things happens to have
been done, so the vote is carried. Much good come to you, because with such
eagerness you hear these words; for the groaning of each one, and the striking
of the forehead, shows me the fruit of these seeds.

For these reasons I torment those who are left behind, because being able to
enjoy such care they are plunged into wounds and daily traumas, and do not
even know that they are sick; wherefore it is difficult to heal them. For who
will converse with them concerning these things? The wives who dwell with
them? But one care troubles those women, for gold trinkets and garments and
luxury and for the management of the house to trouble those dwelling in it.
But the servants? How, they who possess no freedom of speech, and having
themselves also one zeal, to fill up their service? But the judges? But they
have a word for nothing else but the care of civic affairs. But the kings and
those who bear diadems? But for them also all the labor and all the zeal is
concerning rule and dominion and money. But they themselves for themselves?
How, they who do not even have breath from the crowd of affairs, but spend
night and day on these things? Of what then would they not be more pitiable,
those receiving ten thousand wounds, and not meeting with the place where they
will be able to learn that even if they have wounds, it is possible to be
delivered of them and to obtain health? When then will these men care for
their own soul? For it is desirable that those continually meeting in the
church, and enjoying spiritual teaching, and obtaining such care, be able to
prevail over and bridle the evil desires, and to look toward the exercise of
virtue.

βʹ. But that we may not deprive you of your customary things by blaming them
the whole time, come then let us again set before you the usual table, and let
us narrate to you the gain of psalmody itself. For behold the psalm, having
entered among the different voices, mixed them, and prepared that one
harmonious song to be lifted up, and young men and old men, and rich and poor,
and women and men, and slaves and free, we all lifted up one certain melody.
For if a harpist, mixing the different strings with the exactness of his art,
makes the many one thing remaining many; what new thing is it that the power
of the psalm and of the spiritual song has wrought this same thing? For it
mixed not only us who were present, but =63.487= it mingled him who had fallen
asleep with the living; for that blessed prophet also sang with us, although
in royal courts it is not so, but he who wears the diadem sits, and all stand
in silence, and these being in the greatest offices. But here it is not so,
but the prophet utters his voice, and all answer, and all sound together; and
there is no slave to be seen here, nor a free man, nor rich and poor, nor
ruler and private person; but all this irregularity of life has been driven
out of doors, and one choir has been formed from all, and much equality of
privilege, and the earth imitates heaven. Such is the nobility of the church.
For one cannot say that the master sings with much freedom of speech, and the
slave is silenced; nor again the rich man moves his tongue, and the
poor man is condemned to voicelessness; nor again the man speaks boldly, and
the woman is silent and stands voiceless; but all enjoying the same equality
of honor, we offer the common sacrifice, the common offering; and this one has
nothing more than that one, nor that one than this one, but all are in the
same honor, and one voice from different tongues is sent up to the Maker of
the inhabited world. But the difference is not in slave and free, nor in rich
and poor, nor in woman and man, but in disposition, in zeal and in sloth, in
vice and in virtue. For thus I am able to call the poor man rich, and the rich
man poor, and the man a woman, and the woman a man, and the private person
wise, and the wise man a private person, not confusing the nature of things,
but introducing the best rule that corrects all things. And how, he says,
would the man become a woman, and how the woman a man? Not with the nature
being
changed, but with the choice. For whenever I see a man wearing gold, decking
himself out, arranging his hair, breathing perfumes, softened in the delicate
clothing and in his walk, attending to luxury, how could I call this man a
man, who has betrayed the nobility of his nature, and is being transformed
toward
the feminine? For if even a woman doing these things Paul does not deign to
number among the living, but exiles her from the choir of those who breathe,
and sets her with the dead, saying, She who lives in pleasure is dead while
she lives; how would the man be enrolled in the order of men doing these
things which a woman doing them has lost even life itself? For show me not the
man from the cloak, nor from the belt, nor from his swelling in the house and
being terrible and threatening, but from the philosophy in the mind, whenever
he masters the passions, whenever he prevails over the diseases of the soul,
whenever he rules the house of his interior, I mean the one according to the
soul, and is not dragged down by the absurd reasonings; this is especially a
man. But if he should be drunk and attend to revels, and spend and consume the
day in carousings and drunken bands, becoming softer than every wax by the
passion, how shall I call such a one a man, the captive, the slave, the one
bought for silver, the one yielding to all the passions, the one lying down,
the one being made soft, the one being wasted, the one being cast down by all,
the one not able even to stand in the battle line? Or do you not know that the
war and the battle array is not against men of like nature with us, but
against the unseen powers, against the phalanxes of the demons, according to
the blessed Paul: Our wrestling is not against blood and flesh, but against
the principalities, against the powers?

γʹ. For this is especially of a man. The one then who is playing the dandy,
but not playing the young man, the one decking himself out but not acting
manly, how would he stand in the battle line? Or how would he be called a man,
being disposed more softly than every woman? In the same manner then, as I
would not call this one a man, but softer than a woman, so also the woman who
acts manly, stouter than every man, if having snatched the spiritual weapons,
and having put on the breastplate of righteousness, and having placed on the
helmet of salvation, and having put forward the shield of faith, and having
girded the belt of truth, and taking the sword of the spirit, she stands in
the battle line shining from her weapons, becoming higher than the heavens by
her courage, breaking up the phalanxes of the demons, casting down the
tyranny of the devil, piercing the passions, making falls thicker than those
in the wars, not of men, but of absurd reasonings. For woman and man in the
bodily things are divided, and to her the loom and the spindle and the basket
and the keeping of the house and the chamber and the rearing of children are
assigned, and to the man the courts and the councils and the marketplace, and
wars and battles and battle arrays; but in the wrestlings of piety the stadium
is common, the wrestlings are common; likewise also women strip down, and are
not cast out of the contest, but are also arrayed and crowned and proclaimed
in the herald's cry, and have as prizes for valor, rewards and crowns, and
bright trophies are theirs, and continuous and successive victories. And that
you may not think these words to be a boast nor a clamor of words, but may
know clearly that not only are there women braver than men, but that they even
anchor, so to speak, toward the very dispassion of the angels as far as is
permitted, let the woman be led into the midst by nature the woman, but having
touched the heavens by the tension of her philosophy, the mother of the
Maccabees, having fastened on twice seven crowns, and let any soldier you
wish, most warlike, bravest, most magnanimous, be led into the midst by the
word, and you will see the woman so much higher, as much as the vaults of the
heavens stand apart from the earth. For the soldier and prize-fighter fears
one blow, and the quick death from it only; but she stood like a mountain
being mined, enduring for each child harder pains in her soul than the
tortures which they received in their sides; for she was both a mother, and a
widow, and driven to the last old age; know these things, as many of you as
became fathers, and as many women as were loosed from the pangs of children,
how much harder than any goad these things are. And nothing so sharp a weapon
brings so piercing a pain to the wounded as each of these pierced her soul.
For consider how great a thing it was for each of the children to see one body
divided by iron and fire, and long passages being made; how she saw, how she
heard, how she received the savor from those bodies, seeing ten thousand
deaths led against each of the children. But she stood like a rock unmoved,
not being shaken by the waves, but dissolving the waves into foam, like iron,
like adamant; rather, whatever I might say, I have not a term worthy of the
endurance of the woman. Shall we then say woman? Or man altogether, and not
even higher than the men themselves by much of the surplus? And what, when you
see another from the first age in a tender body passing sleepless nights,
foodless days, =63.489= casting down the desire of the belly, casting away the
love of money, having mortified the body, having crucified the flesh,
accounting nothing of the things present, walking lowly and touching even the
vaults of the heavens, trampling dominion, laughing at glory, spitting upon
ostentation, having clothed herself with sackcloth instead of fine linen, with
a hair-shirt around her, having spread ashes under her, using the floor for a
bed, and asking nothing more, shut up in a narrow room, keeping company with
prophets, handling a life that has the study of death, having been made dead
before death, being melted by hunger and sleeplessness and drought, and
dishonoring the appearances of this present life more than withering leaves;
this one will you call a woman, tell me? But will you not rather, having
removed her from the order of men, set her in the choir of the angels,
because in a female nature such philosophy is displayed?

δʹ. And who will contradict these things? There is no one. Hear what women
there were even among the apostles; hear Paul writing to the Romans and
saying: I commend to you Phoebe our sister, who is a servant of the church
which is at Cenchreae, who was a helper of many, and of myself also. What do
you say, O Paul? A woman became your helper, and you are not ashamed and do
not blush saying these things? I am not ashamed, he says, but I even glory in
it; and my Master was not ashamed to have a woman for a mother; she became my
helper, not because she was simply a woman, but because she was a philosopher
and flourishing in piety. And how did she become Paul's helper? For what did
Paul need, he who ran round the inhabited world, he who held the body as
nothing, he who trampled the necessities of nature, he who drove off demons,
he who healed diseases through garments, he whose voice and shadow the devil
trembled at, he who was honored as an angel by the faithful, he whom even the
beasts were ashamed to touch, he whom the sea was entreated, and having
received him night and day did not drown him, he the citizen of paradise, he
who was caught up to the third heaven, he who shared in the things unutterable
with God, he who labored more than all the apostles, the vessel of election,
the bridegroom's friend of the Church, the herald of the nations, he who ran
round earth and sea, and everywhere set up trophies of his own bravery, he who
wrestled continually with hunger and thirst, he who fought with nakedness and
cold, and says, Even to this hour we hunger and thirst and are naked; he who
was dead to the world, he who reckoned the world dead, he the citizen of the
heavens, the mad lover of Christ, hotter than fire, more tensile than iron,
stouter than adamant; does this man not blush saying concerning a woman, A
helper of many was she made, and of myself also? and he does not only say, but
also writes; and he does not only write simply, but writing to so great a
multitude of the Romans, burning and thinking great things from their outward
dignities, he sent these things; and he sent them not to those men only, but
he wished this to become manifest to the generations after. For if he had not
wished, he would not have fixed this in the epistle; but now, wishing the
thing to be handed over to everlasting remembrance, he both addresses the
woman, and rouses all to her reception, and calls her his own helper, and is
not ashamed nor blushes. So great is a philosophic soul even in a female
body. These things I say, that a woman may not make the pretext, saying that
because she is a woman she has not attained the full measure of piety. For
behold this one is a woman, and nature did not become a hindrance, but she was
counted worthy to become Paul's helper, and is proclaimed everywhere in the
inhabited world. And how did she become his helper? Perhaps she snatched him
out of dangers? She sat with him when bound, she consoled him in chains, she
brought in the comfort from necessary wants, she overturned those who plotted
against him, she gave herself over to death, so as to snatch away that holy
man being led off from the dangerous moments. Wherefore also he praises her
who was after her, saying thus: Greet Priscilla and Aquila. See here again the
woman shining; for if the team is one, yet the woman is placed before the
husband; for he did not say, Aquila and Priscilla, but, Priscilla and Aquila.
Who were these? Tentmakers by craft, standing at a workshop; but neither the
craft nor the poverty became a hindrance; but that blessed man, running
through the whole city, came down to this chamber. On what account and why?
Not because it had columns, nor because it had slabs and a floor inlaid with
mosaic stones, nor a golden roof, nor herds of slaves, nor the service of
eunuchs, but because of this, that above all these things the chamber was
clean; because a woman and a man gathering the labor from just things made the
house a church, not seizing, nor taking advantage, but being content with the
service of the body for the necessary sustenance.

Wherefore especially did Paul think this house suitable to lodge in. And that
you may learn that having approved the virtue of their soul he remained there,
hear the things that follow. Who =63.491= for my soul, he says, laid down
their own necks; to whom not I alone give thanks, but also all the churches
of the
nations. Do you see that neither a female nature, nor craft, nor poverty
hindered the course of virtue? Do you see a woman and a man hospitable, not
with a table only, but receiving the Apostle with blood; for what if they were
not slaughtered? Yet they filled up what was their own, and being alive
became martyrs, and often martyrs, always being arrayed to be slaughtered for
Paul. For he did not say, Who spent money, who opened the house, but, which
was greatest of all, he sets the murder, the slaughter in the midst, so as to
say that They chose to be cut off for me. Let them hear those who are now
rich, and hardly bestow an obol on the saints; those men shared even their
blood, and stripped off life itself, so as to preserve and serve that holy
man; but those present would not readily offer even the many-th part of their
own substance to those in need; but Priscilla and Aquila delivered both money
and bodies and life itself. Do you see how great is a woman philosophizing,
how great is a man even living in poverty and in a craft? These then let us
emulate, these let us imitate, and overlooking the things present, let us
give all for the things pleasing to God, so that we also may attain the good
things to come, =63.492= by the grace and philanthropy of our Lord Jesus
Christ, through whom, and with whom, to the Father with the Holy Spirit be the
glory, now and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
