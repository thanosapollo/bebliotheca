* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

You asked me, brother Fabius, only the other day whether one ought to flee in
persecution, because some rumor had been announced. On the spot I made some
reply against it, as place and time and the importunity of certain persons
allowed, and carried the matter off with me half treated, promising to return
to it now more fully with my pen, since your inquiry had commended it to me
and the condition of the times had already laid it upon me in its own name.
For the more frequently persecutions threaten, the more must we examine how
faith ought to meet them. The examination, however, belongs to you, who,
perchance, by not receiving the Paraclete, the guide into all truth, have
deservedly stood opposed to us in other questions also. So I have given your
inquiry an order as well, observing that one must first settle the standing
of persecution itself, whether it comes from God or from the devil, so that
the question of its outcome may the more easily be settled. For the
inspection of anything is clearer when its author is known.

It is enough, indeed, to lay down that nothing happens without the will of
God. But we shall not at once by that position give place to the other
disputations, lest we be drawn away from the point before us, should someone
answer: Then evil too is from God and sin from God; there is nothing now in
the devil, nothing even in ourselves. The question now is persecution. About
this I would say meanwhile that nothing happens without the will of God,
regarding it as above all worthy of God and, so to speak, necessary, namely
for the proving, or the rejecting, of his servants. For what is the outcome
of persecution, what other effect, but the proving and the rejecting of
faith, by which the Lord will indeed sift his own? This is the Lord's
judgment, persecution, by which one is judged either approved or rejected.
Judgment, moreover, belongs to God alone. This is that fan which even now
cleanses the Lord's threshing floor, the Church, that is, winnowing the
confused heap of the faithful and separating the grain of the martyrs and
the chaff of the deniers; and it is likewise that ladder which Jacob saw in
his dream, showing ascent to the things above for some, descent to the
things below for others. So also persecution can be understood as a contest.
From whom is the contest proclaimed, unless from him by whom the crown and
the prizes are set out? The edict of the law of this contest is in the
Apocalypse, with what rewards he invites to victory, especially those who
have properly conquered in persecution, having contended in truth not
against flesh and blood but against spiritual wickedness. So you will
acknowledge that the judging of the contest belongs to the same arbiter who
invites to the prize. The whole thing that is done in persecution is the
glory of God, proving and rejecting, imposing and deposing. But what belongs
to the glory of God will assuredly come to pass by his will. Yet when is God
believed more than when he is feared more, if not in the time of
persecution? The Church is in awe. Then faith is more anxious in expedition
and more disciplined in fastings and stations and prayers and humility, in
mutual diligence and love, in holiness and sobriety. For there is no leisure
except for fear and hope. So much so that from this very thing it is shown
us that it cannot be charged to the devil, that which makes the servants of
God better.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

If unrighteousness is not from God but from the devil, and persecution
consists of unrighteousness (for what is more unrighteous than that the
ministers of the true God, all followers of the truth, should be handled
after the manner of the most notorious criminals?), persecution therefore
seems to come from the devil, from whom comes the unrighteousness out of
which persecution stands; we ought to know, then, that neither is
persecution without the devil's unrighteousness, nor the proving of faith
without persecution; that the unrighteousness necessary for the proving of
faith gives not a patronage to persecution but a ministry; for the will of
God concerning the proving of faith, which is the reason of persecution,
goes before, and the devil's unrighteousness follows as the instrument of
persecution, which is the means of the proving. For elsewhere too, insofar
as unrighteousness is the rival of righteousness, so far is it the material
for the attestations of him whose rival it is, so that righteousness is
perfected in unrighteousness, as strength is perfected in weakness. For the
weak things of the world were chosen by God to confound the strong, and its
foolish things to confound wisdom. So unrighteousness too is applied, that
righteousness may be proved, confounding unrighteousness. Therefore, since
the ministry is not of arbitration but of service (the arbitration, namely,
is the Lord's in persecution for the proving of faith; the ministry, the
devil's unrighteousness, for the fashioning of persecution), we believe that
it comes to pass through the devil, if you will, but not from the devil.
Nothing will be permitted Satan against the servants of the living God
unless the Lord permit, either that he may himself destroy him by the faith
of the elect, victorious in the trial, or that he may make sport of the men
who have fallen away to him as his own. You have the example of Job, on whom
the devil could inflict no temptation unless he had received the power from
God, nor even upon his substance unless the Lord had said: Behold, all that
he has I give into your hand; only upon himself do not stretch out your
hand. In fact he did not stretch it out, until afterwards the Lord said to
him as he asked: Behold, I deliver him to you; only guard his life. So too
in the case of the apostles he asked the capacity of temptation, not having
it except by permission, since the Lord says to Peter in the gospel:
Behold, Satan has asked to sift you as wheat; but I have prayed for you that
your faith fail not; that is, that so much should not be permitted the devil
that faith should be endangered. Whence it is shown that both are with God,
the shaking of faith and its protection, since both are sought from him: the
shaking from the devil, the protection from the Son. And certainly, when the
Son of God has the protection of faith in his own power, which he asks of
the Father, from whom he receives all power in heaven and on earth, what is
it that the devil should have the shaking of faith in his hand? But in the
prescribed prayer, when we say to the Father, Lead us not into temptation
(and what greater temptation than persecution?), we profess that it happens
from him from whom we beg its pardon. For this is what follows: But deliver
us from the evil one; that is, do not lead us into temptation by permitting
us to the evil one. For then are we delivered from the hands of the devil,
when we are not delivered to him into temptation. Nor had the devil's legion
power even over the herd of swine unless it had obtained it from God; so far
is he from having it over the sheep of God. I can say that even the bristles
of the swine were then numbered with God, much more the hairs of the saints.
The devil seems to have a power of his own, if you will, over those who do
not belong to God, once for all assigned as a drop of the bucket, as dust of
the threshing floor, as spittle to the nations, deputed by God and for that
reason exposed to the devil as it were a vacant possession. But against the
household of God nothing is permitted him of his own power, because the
examples sealed in the Scriptures show when, that is from what causes. For
either on the ground of proving the right of temptation is granted him, the
man provoked or provoking, as in the cases above; or on the ground of
rejecting the sinner is delivered to him as to an executioner for
punishment, as Saul: And the Spirit of the Lord departed from Saul, and an
evil spirit from the Lord terrified and strangled him; or on the ground of
restraint, as the apostle reports that there was given to him a stake, an
angel of Satan, that he should be buffeted; nor is this kind permitted to
the devil against the saints, to humble them by harassment of the flesh,
except that at the same time the strength, that is, of endurance may be
perfected in weakness. For the apostle himself also delivered Phygellus and
Hermogenes to Satan, that they might be corrected, that they might not
blaspheme. You see that even from the servants of God the devil receives
power the more easily; so far is he from possessing it as his own.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Since, then, these examples fall rather in persecutions, seeing that then we
are proved or rejected, then humbled or corrected, they must needs be
permitted or commanded universally by him by whom they happen even in part;
by him, I mean, who says: I am he that makes peace and creates evil, that
is, war; for that is the contrary of peace. But what is war to our peace
rather than persecution? If the outcomes of persecution above all bring
either life or death, wound or healing, you have the author of the same: I
will smite and I will heal; I make alive and I put to death. I will burn
them, he says, as gold is burned, and I will prove them, he says, as silver
is proved. For when we are burned with the heat of persecution, then we are
proved in the tenor of our faith. These will be the fiery darts of the
devil, by which the burning and fusing of faith is administered, yet by the
will of God. Who can doubt this, I know not, unless plainly the frivolous
and frigid faith, which seizes those who assemble fearfully in the church.
For you say that we gather in disorder, and gather together, and throng in
numbers into the church, and we are inquired after by the nations, and we
fear lest the nations be disturbed. Do you not know that God is Lord of
all? And if it be as God wills, you will suffer persecution; but if he shall
not will it, the nations will be silent. You would surely believe, if
nevertheless you believe in that God without whose will not even a sparrow
of one as falls to the ground. But we, I suppose, stand above many sparrows.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Therefore if it is settled from whom persecution comes, we can now bring in
your inquiry and determine from this very discussion that flight in
persecution is not to be. For if persecution comes from God, in no way will
it be right to flee what comes from God; as a twofold reason defends:
because what comes from God ought neither to be shunned nor can it be
escaped. It ought not to be shunned, because it is good; for it must needs
be that everything which God has seen is good. And is it perhaps for this
reason that in Genesis it stands so, And God saw that it was good, not as
though he did not know it was good unless he saw it, but that by this
utterance he might portend that what God has seen is good? Many things
indeed happen from God and happen to someone's harm. Nay, it is good for
this reason, because it comes from God, as divine, as rational. For what is
divine, not rational, not good? What good, not divine? But if it seems so to
the sense of each, the sense of man does not prejudge the standing of
things, but the standing the sense. For each standing is a certain definite
thing and lays down the law to sense, to feel the standing as it is. If,
then, in its standing what comes from God is good (for nothing from God is
not good, because divine, because rational), but seems evil to sense, the
standing will be safe, the sense at fault. In its standing the best thing is
chastity and truth and righteousness, which displease the sense of many. Is
the standing therefore given over to sense? So also persecution is good in
its standing, because a divine and rational dispensation, but displeases the
sense of those whose harm it comes upon. You see that that evil too has its
rationale with God, when anyone is overturned from salvation in persecution,
as that good likewise falls out by reason, when anyone profits in salvation
from persecution; unless perhaps one perishes irrationally with the Lord or
is saved, he could not call persecution an evil, which even on the side of
evil is good, so long as it is administered by reason. So if persecution is
good in any way, because it stands in its nature, we rightly define that
what is good ought not to be avoided, since it were a fault to refuse what
is good; still more, what God has seen; and now, that it cannot be avoided,
because it comes from God, whose will cannot be escaped. Therefore those who
think flight ought to be, either impute an evil to God, if they flee
persecution as an evil (for no one shuns a good), or think themselves
stronger than God, who think they can escape if God should will such a thing
to happen.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

But what is mine, he says, I flee, lest I perish if I should deny; his it
is, if he will, to bring even me fleeing back into the midst. This answer me
first: are you certain you will deny if you do not flee, or uncertain? For
if certain, you have denied already; because by presuming you will deny you
have pledged that of which you have presumed, and in vain now do you flee,
lest you deny, who, if you are to deny, have denied already. But if you are
uncertain, why do you not, by the equal uncertainty of the fear between both
outcomes, presume that you are able also to confess and rather to be made
safe, so much the more as you do not flee, just as you presume you will
deny, in order that you may flee? Just now, then, either both are in us or
the whole is in God. If to confess or deny is in us, why do we not presume
the better thing, that is, that we shall confess? Unless if you wish to
confess, you do not wish to suffer; but not to wish to confess is to deny.
But if the whole is in God, why do we not leave the whole to his
arbitration, acknowledging his virtue and power, that he can both bring us
back into the midst as we flee, and shelter us as we do not flee; yes, and
even while we dwell among the people? What is it, that to flight you render
honor to God, who can bring even you fleeing forth into the midst, but to
witness bearing you do him dishonor, despairing of the power of protection
from him? Why do you not rather on this side say, of constancy and trust in
God: I do what is mine; I do not depart; God, if he will, will himself
protect me? This is rather ours: to stand under God's arbitration than to
flee under our own. The most blessed martyr Rutilius, when he had fled
persecution so often from place to place, and had even redeemed the peril,
as he thought, with money, after all the security he had provided for
himself, unexpectedly seized and offered to the prefect, broken by torments,
I believe as chastisement for his flight, then given to the fires,
attributed to the mercy of God the passion he had avoided. What else did the
Lord wish to show us by this document, but that flight is not to be, since
flight profits nothing, if God be unwilling?

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Nay, he says, he fulfilled the precept, fleeing from city to city. So a
certain man thought to argue, himself likewise a fugitive, and those who in
consequence refuse to understand the sense of that utterance of the Lord,
that they may use it as a cloak for their cowardice, though it had its own
persons, and times, and causes. When they begin, he says, to persecute you,
flee from city to city. We maintain that this belongs properly to the
persons of the apostles, and to their times and their causes, as the
subsequent senses will prove, which fit only the apostles: Into the way of
the nations go not, and into a city of the Samaritans enter not; but go
rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel. But to us the way of the
nations lies open, in which we also were found and up to the end we walk;
and no city is excepted, throughout the whole world we preach; nor is the
care of Israel laid upon us outside the order, except insofar as we ought to
preach to all nations. Even if we are apprehended, we are not led into their
councils, nor scourged in their synagogues, but shall be arraigned before
Roman powers and tribunals. So then the condition of the apostles required
the precept of flight, since first preaching was to be to the lost sheep of
the house of Israel. That therefore the preaching might be accomplished
among those among whom it ought first to be accomplished, that the sons
might take the bread before the dogs, therefore he then bade them flee for a
time, not to elude danger under the proper name of persecution (and yet he
was proclaiming that they would suffer persecutions and teaching that they
must be endured), but for the advance of the announcement, lest, being at
once crushed, the dissemination of the gospel too should perish.

For neither were they to slip away secretly into some city, but as about to
announce everywhere, and for this to undergo persecutions everywhere, until
they should fill out their teaching. Finally: You will not finish, he says,
the cities of Israel. So far was the precept of flight confined within the
boundaries of Judaea. But no defining of Judaea belongs to us, with
preaching to all flesh now poured out in the Holy Spirit. And so Paul and
the apostles themselves, mindful of the Lord's precept, testify it to
Israel, whom their teaching had already filled: To you it was necessary that
the word of God be first spoken; but since you reject it and judge
yourselves unworthy of eternal life, behold, we turn to the nations. And
thenceforth they too turned, as their predecessors had ordained, and went
into the way of the nations and entered into the cities of the Samaritans,
that indeed their sound might go out into all the earth, and their words to
the ends of the world. If, then, the exception of the way of the nations and
the entering into the cities of the Samaritans has ceased, why should not
the precept of flight, issued at the same time, have ceased? Finally, from
the time when, Israel being filled, the apostles passed over to the nations,
they neither fled from city to city nor doubted to suffer. And indeed Paul,
who had had himself let down through the wall to escape persecution, as at
that time belonging to the precept, this same man, now at the close of his
office and in the consummation of the precept, when the disciples were
greatly beseeching him not to expose himself at Jerusalem, about to suffer
there what Agabus had prophesied, did not subscribe to their solicitude, but
on the contrary: What do you, he says, weeping and troubling my heart? For I
am ready not only to suffer bonds, but even to die at Jerusalem for the name
of my Lord Jesus Christ. And so they all said: The will of the Lord be done.
What was the will of the Lord? Assuredly no longer to flee persecution.
Otherwise they could have proposed the former will of the Lord, by which he
had commanded to flee, who had rather he avoid persecution. Therefore since
even under the apostles themselves the precept of flight was temporal, as
also those of the other things prescribed, it cannot abide with us, which
ceased with our teachers, even if it had not been issued properly to them;
or if the Lord willed it to abide, the apostles sinned, who did not take
care to flee to the end.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Let us see now whether the rest of the Lord's edicts agree with a perpetual
precept of flight. First indeed, if persecution is from God, what is it,
that the same one bids it to be fled who also brings it in? If he wished it
escaped, he would better not have sent it, lest he seem to prevaricate his
own will by another will. For he either willed us to suffer persecution or
to flee. If to flee, how to suffer? If to suffer, how to flee? Now indeed
what inequality of utterances, commanding to flee and inviting to a passion
contrary to flight! Whoever shall confess me, I also will confess him before
my Father. How shall he confess, fleeing? How shall he flee, confessing?
Whoever shall have been ashamed of me, I also shall be ashamed of him before
my Father. If I avoid the passion, I am ashamed of the confession. Blessed
who shall have suffered persecution for the sake of my name. Unhappy, then,
who by fleeing, per the precept, shall not have suffered. Whoever shall have
endured to the end, he shall be saved. What then, bidding me flee, do you
wish me to endure to the end? So great a diversity of utterances, if it does
not comport with the divine gravity, makes clear by these very things that
the precept of flight had then its own reason, which we have shown.

But the infirmity, he says, of certain ones the Lord, foreseeing, in his
humanity nevertheless pointed out also the port of flight. For he was not
able even without flight, so base and unworthy and servile a protection, to
save in persecution those whom he knew to be weak! And yet he does not
cherish but ever declines the weak, first teaching that persecutors are not
to be fled, but rather not to be feared: Fear not them that kill the body
but cannot kill the soul; but rather fear him who can destroy both body and
soul in Gehenna. And thence what does he set before the fearful? Whoever
shall have made more of his soul than of me is not worthy of me; and who
does not take up his cross and follow me cannot be my disciple. Finally in
the Apocalypse he does not offer flight to the fearful, but among the rest
of the reprobate their portion in the lake of sulphur and fire, which is the
second death.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

He himself also fled violence at times, but on the same grounds on which he
had commanded the apostles to flee, until, that is, he should fulfill his
teaching; and when it was fulfilled, I do not say he stood, but he did not
even desire help from the Father of hosts of angels, having rebuked even
Peter's sword. He indeed professed his own soul anxious even unto death and
flesh weak, first that he might show you that in himself both substances
were human in their proper quality of anxiety of soul and weakness of flesh,
lest you interpret either flesh or soul of Christ as other, as certain have
now introduced; then that, their conditions being shown, you might know they
have no force of themselves without the spirit. And therefore he sets before
the willing spirit, that regarding the condition of each substance you may
understand that in you there is also the strength of spirit, as also the
weakness of flesh, and from this learn what you do and from what, and what
you subject to what: the weak, namely, to the strong; lest, as you now do,
you complain of the weakness of the flesh indeed, but of the firmness of the
spirit say nothing. He himself also asked of the Father that, if it were
possible, the cup of the passion might pass from him. Ask you also; but
standing as he stood, but asking only, but subjoining also the rest: Yet not
what I will, but what you. But fleeing, how will you ask this, yourself
granting to yourself the removal of the cup, and doing not what the Father
wills but what you will?

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

All things the apostles taught according to God, all things they turned over
of the gospel. Where do you show them renewing the precept of fleeing from
city to city? Because they could not have established such a thing, so
contrary to their own examples, as to command flight, who above all wrote to
the churches from bonds or islands, in which they were confined for
confession of the name, not for flight. Paul bids the weak be borne with,
who certainly were not fleeing. For how shall they be borne with, absent? By
patience? He says they are to be borne with, if anywhere they have stumbled
through the weakness of their faith, as the fainthearted are to be
consoled, but not sent into flight. But when he admonishes not to give place
to evil, he does not suggest a plan of flight, but teaches a tempering of
anger; and if he says the time is to be redeemed, because the days are evil,
he would have us gain the respite not by flight, but by the wisdom of
conversation. But he who bids us shine as children of light does not bid us
hide in flight as children of darkness. He commands us to stand immovable,
certainly not movable by flight; and girt, for flight, or for the encounter
of the gospel? He also displays arms, which would not be necessary for those
about to flee; among which also the shield, by which you can quench the
darts of the devil, resisting without doubt and receiving his whole force.

So also John teaches that souls are to be laid down for the brothers too,
much more for the Lord. This cannot be fulfilled by the fleeing. Finally,
mindful of his Apocalypse, in which he had heard the outcome of the fearful,
he admonishes from his own sense, and he too, that fear must be rejected:
Fear, he says, is not in love. But perfect love casts out fear, because fear
has torment, assuredly the fire of the lake; but he who fears is not perfect
in love, that is, of God. Moreover, who will flee persecution but he who
will fear? Who will fear but he who has not loved? But if you consult the
Spirit, what does he prove more than that utterance of the Spirit? For he
incites almost all to martyrdom, not to flight; that we may also make
mention of this: It is good, he says, for you to be publicized; for he who
is not publicized among men is publicized with the Lord. Be not ashamed;
righteousness brings you forth into the midst. Why are you ashamed, bearing
glory? Power is made, when you are looked upon by men. So also elsewhere: Do
not wish to go out in beds, nor in miscarriages and soft fevers, but in
martyrdoms, that he may be glorified who suffered for you.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

But, neglecting the divine exhortations, certain ones rather apply to
themselves that Greek little verse of worldly sentiment: He who fled will
fight again; so that perhaps he may flee again too. And when will he conquer
who, when he fled, was conquered? A good soldier he furnishes to Christ his
emperor who, armed so fully by the apostle, at the trumpet of persecution
heard, deserts the day of persecution. I too will answer something from the
world: Is dying so very wretched? Let him die in whatever way, either
conquered or conqueror. For even if he fell by denying, yet he fought with
the torments. I prefer one to be pitied than to be blushed at. Fairer is the
soldier transfixed by the spear in battle than safe in flight. Do you fear
man, Christian, whom the angels ought to fear, since you are to judge
angels? whom the daemons ought to fear, since even over daemons you have
received power? whom the whole world ought to fear, since by you too the
world is judged? You have put on Christ, since you have been baptized into
Christ, who flee the devil. You have besought Christ, who is in you, when
you made yourself a fugitive with the devil. But fleeing the Lord you
reproach to all fugitives the vanity of their counsel. A certain ardent
prophet also had fled the Lord; he had crossed from Joppa toward Tarsus, as
if he were crossing the sea from God also; but I find him, not to say in the
sea and on the land, but truly even in the womb of a beast, in which he
could neither die for three days nor even so escape God. How much better he
who, if he fears the enemy of God, does not flee him but rather despises
him, confident of the Lord's protection; or, if he stands in awe of God, so
much the more as he has stood before his eyes, saying: The Lord is able. All
things are his; where I shall be, I am in his hand; let him do what he will;
I do not depart; and if he shall will me to perish, let him himself destroy
me, while I keep myself for him. I would rather bring odium on him by
perishing at his own will than gall by escaping through my own.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

This every servant of God ought to feel and do, even one of lesser place,
that he may become of greater, if he shall have mounted some step in
endurance of persecution. But when the authors themselves, that is, the very
deacons and presbyters and bishops, flee, how shall a layman be able to
understand by what reason it was said: Flee from city to city? So when the
leaders flee, who of the common rank will stand to fix his step in the
battle line? Certainly indeed the good shepherd lays down his life for the
sheep, as Moses, to the Lord not yet revealed as Christ but already figured
in himself, says: If you destroy this people, he says, destroy me also
together with it. But Christ, confirming his figures, pronounces that the
bad shepherd is he who, seeing the wolf, flees and leaves the sheep to be
seized and scattered. Such a shepherd will be cast out of the villa; the
wages of his mission will be held back in compensation; indeed, even from
his former purse will be exacted restitution of the master's loss. For to
him who has, it shall be given; but from him who has not, even what he seems
to have shall be taken away. So Zechariah threatens: Awake, O sword, against
the shepherds, and pluck out the sheep, and I will turn my hand against the
shepherds. Against them Ezekiel and Jeremiah too plead with the same
threats, that they not only wickedly feed on the flocks, feeding rather
themselves, but also scatter the flock and give it over a prey to all the
beasts of the field, while there is no shepherd to them. And this never
happens more than when in persecution the church is deserted by the clergy.
If anyone also acknowledge the Spirit, he will hear him marking the
fugitives. Moreover, if it neither becomes nor is even lawful for those who
preside over the flock to flee when the wolves rush in (for he who pronounced
such a shepherd bad certainly condemned him; and everything condemned has
without doubt become unlawful), therefore the superintendents of the church
ought not flee in persecution. Otherwise, if the flock were to flee, the
shepherd of the flock would not have to stand, about to stand without cause
for the protection of a flock that would not desire it, by the license of
flight, of course.

What pertains, brother, to your problem, you have the answer and the
exhortation of our sentiment. Moreover he who asks whether persecution is to
be fled must now look forward to the following question also: whether, if it
is not to be fled, it is at least to be ransomed. Then further I will
suggest this to you too, defining that persecution, which is agreed not to
be fled, therefore is not to be ransomed either. The price differs; else, as
flight is a gratuitous redemption, so a money redemption is flight. Certainly
this too is the counsel of cowardice. What you fear, you redeem; therefore
you flee. On foot you stood; with money you ran. By this very thing, that
you stood, you fled by redemption. But that you should redeem a man with
money whom Christ redeemed with his blood, how unworthy of God and his
dispensations, who spared not his own Son for you, that he might be made a
curse for us, because cursed is he that hangs on the tree; who was led as a
sheep to the sacrifice, and as a lamb before the shearer so opened not his
mouth, but laid his back to the scourges and his cheeks to the palms, and
turned not his face from the spittings, and was numbered among the
unrighteous, and delivered to death, even the death of the cross. All this,
that he might win us from sins. The sun gave way on the day of our purchase.
Among the dead is our re manumission, and our stipulation is in the heavens.
The everlasting gates were lifted up, that the king of glory, the Lord of
hosts, might enter, who bought man from the earth, indeed from the dead,
into the heavens. Who is he now who withstands him, indeed despises his
merchandise, bought at so great a price, that is, with most precious blood,
and defiles it? Now then it is better to flee than to be made cheaper, if a
man will not cost himself as much as he cost the Lord. And the Lord indeed
redeemed him from the angels ruling the world, from the powers, from the
spiritual things of wickedness, from the darkness of this age, from eternal
judgment, from everlasting death. But you bargain for him with an informer
or a soldier or some paltry thief of a prefect under the tunic and in the
bosom, as they say, a furtive one, whom Christ bought throughout the whole
world; indeed he even manumitted him. Him, then, a free man, will you
appraise with a price and possess with a price, unless the same by which the
Lord was bought, that is, his own blood? Why then do you redeem Christ in a
man with money in whom Christ is? Not otherwise Simon too tried to do, when
he offered money to the apostles for the Spirit of Christ. Hear then this
man too, who, redeeming himself, redeemed the Spirit of Christ: Thy money be
with thee in perdition, because you thought the grace of God to be obtained
at a price. Yet who despises a denier? For what says that shaker down? Give
me money. Certainly that he may not hand him over, since he vends nothing
else than what he is about to give for your reward. When you give, certainly
you wished not to be handed over. But not handed over, were you not to be
exposed? Therefore while by not wishing to be handed over you do not wish to
be exposed, by not wishing you denied what you did not wish to be exposed
as. Nay, you say: while I did not wish to be exposed as what I am, I
confessed that I am what I did not wish to be exposed as, that is, a
Christian. Can he then vindicate you a martyr, showing Christ constantly?
Redeeming does not show. Before one, if you will, you confessed; therefore
before many also, by not wishing to confess, you denied. Salvation itself
will judge that the man fell away while escaping. He fell away, therefore,
who wished to have escaped. The refusal of martyrdom also is denial. A
Christian is saved by money, and for this has money, that he may not suffer,
while he will be rich toward God. But Christ was rich with blood for him.
Blessed, therefore, the poor, because theirs, he says, is the kingdom of the
heavens, who have only their soul in confiscation. If we cannot serve God
and mammon, can we be redeemed both by God and by mammon? For who will serve
mammon more than he whom mammons redeemed? Finally, by what example will you
use it in the redemption of surrender? The apostles, handled amid
persecutions, when did they free themselves with money? Money surely did not
fail them, from the prices of estates laid at their feet, certainly with
many rich believers, men and women, who furnished them even refreshments
from these. When did Onesiphorus, or Aquila, or Stephanas aid them in this
manner in persecution? Paul indeed, when Felix the governor had hoped that
he would receive money from the disciples for him, about which he also
treated with him in private, neither he himself for himself nor the
disciples for him paid. Those surely the disciples, who, weeping that he
equally persisted in tending toward Jerusalem and would not take precautions
against the persecutions predicted there, finally say: The will of the Lord
be done. What will is this? Assuredly that he suffer for the name of the
Lord, not that he be ransomed. For it behooves, as Christ laid down his soul
for us, so to be done for him by us, and not only for him, but indeed also
for the brothers for his sake. Which John, teaching, pronounced not to be
counted out for the brothers, but rather to be died. There is no difference,
if you ought not to redeem or buy off some Christian. And so the will of God
is thus. Behold the standing of kingdoms and empires, certainly disposed by
God, in whose hand is the heart of the king: so many remedies are daily
provided for augmenting the treasury, of tributes, imposts, contributions,
stipends; and never yet has anything of the kind been provided out of
Christians, for reducing head and sect under some ransom, though from a
multitude unknown to none an immense harvest could be reaped. Bought with
blood, paid out in blood, we owe no money for our head, because our head is
Christ. It does not befit Christ to cost money. How could martyrdoms take
place to the glory of the Lord, if we should compensate tribute for the
license of the sect? Therefore he who bargains for it with a price opposes
the divine dispensation. Since, therefore, Caesar has laid nothing upon us
in this manner as a tributary sect, nor indeed can ever lay anything of the
kind, with Antichrist already at hand and gaping for the blood, not the
money, of Christians, how can it be proposed to me that there is written,
Render the things that are Caesar's to Caesar? A soldier or informer or
enemy shakes me down, exacting nothing for Caesar, indeed doing the
contrary, when for a fee he dismisses a Christian, guilty by human laws.
Another is the denarius which I owe Caesar, which belongs to him, of which
the question was then, a tribute coin, owed by the tributary, that is, not
by the free. Or how shall I render the things that are God's to God, namely
the image and coin of his own inscribed with his name, that is, the
Christian man? But what do I owe God, as the denarius Caesar, except the
blood which his Son shed for me? But if I owe God indeed a man and my blood,
and now I am in that season that I demand back what I owe God, surely I
defraud God, acting that I may not pay what I owe. Well have I observed the
precept, rendering to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, but to God
denying the things that are God's.

But also to every one asking me I will give, in the cause of alms, not of a
shaking down. To him asking, he says. Moreover he who shakes down does not
ask. He who threatens, if he have not received, does not beg, but extorts.
He does not await alms who is not to be pitied, but feared. I will give,
therefore, in the name of pity, not of my cowardice, where he who receives
honors God and renders me a blessing, not where he rather believes he has
conferred a favor, and beholding his booty says: Of guilt. Shall I be angry
even at an enemy? But enmities have other titles too. Yet he did not say a
betrayer, or persecutor, or shaker down. For to him how much more shall I
heap coals upon the head, if I do not redeem myself? Accordingly, he says,
who takes away your tunic, grant also the cloak. That belongs to him who
sought to take away the thing, not my faith. I will grant the cloak also to
one not threatening surrender. If he has threatened, I shall demand back
even the tunic. All the Lord's utterances now have their own causes and
rules. Their terms look not to the infinite nor to all things. And indeed he
bids be given to every one asking; he himself does not give to those asking
a sign. Otherwise, if you think it is to be given to all who ask everywhere,
you seem to me to be about to give, I say not wine to a fevered man, but
even poison or a sword to one desiring death. But make to yourselves friends
of mammon: how this is to be understood let the parable set before teach
you. It was said to the Jewish people, who, having badly administered the
stewardship committed to them, ought to provide for themselves friends of
the mammon of men, which we were, rather than enemies, and relieve us from
the debts of sins by which we were held to God, if they should confer on us
that favor of the Lord, that, when grace began to fail from them, fleeing to
our faith they might be received into everlasting tabernacles. Although now
suppose another interpretation of the parable and of this saying, only know
that it is not likely that our extorters, reconciled in friendship through
mammon, will then receive us into everlasting tabernacles. But what will not
cowardice persuade? As if the Scripture permitted even to flee and bade even
to redeem. Finally it is too little if one or another is so ransomed. Whole
churches en masse have laid a tribute upon themselves. I know not whether it
is to be grieved or blushed at, when in the registers of the beneficiaries
and the spies, among hucksters and bath thieves and gamblers and pimps,
Christians also are contained as taxable. This form the apostles provided
more providently for the episcopate, that they might enjoy their kingdom
secure, under the pretext of providing! For such a peace, of course, Christ,
returning to the Father, commanded to be redeemed from the soldiers at the
Saturnalia!

But how shall we gather, you say; how shall we celebrate the Lord's
solemnities? Assuredly as the apostles also did, safe by faith, not by
money; which faith, if it can remove a mountain, much more a soldier. Let
wisdom be your caution, not a price. For neither will you at once be safe
from the people, if you have redeemed the military services. Therefore you
need both faith and wisdom for protection; without which you can lose even
your redemption; with which you will hardly need redemption. Finally, if you
cannot gather by day, you have the night, luminous against it with the light
of Christ. You cannot run through each. Let there be for you a church also
of three. It is better sometimes not to see your crowds than to sell them.
Keep for Christ his virgin bride; let no one make gain of her. These things,
brother, seem to you perhaps hard and intolerable, but recite that God said:
Who can hear, let him hear; that is, who cannot hear, let him depart. He
cannot be his who suffered, who fears to suffer. But he who does not fear to
suffer will be perfect in love, that is, of God. For perfect love casts out
fear. And therefore many are called, few chosen. It is not asked who is
ready to follow the broad way, but who the narrow. And therefore the
Paraclete is necessary, the guide of all truths, the exhorter of all
endurances. They who have received him knew neither to flee persecution nor
to redeem it, having him who will be for us, both to speak in the
interrogation and to aid in the passion.
