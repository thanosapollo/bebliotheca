If any freedom of speech belongs to me as well, men of Athens, I shall try to
say what I know about the present situation, having begged you not to raise an
uproar before you have heard everything. It is in truth a task to examine
purely what is to your advantage. For those who have counselled with these men
or with those have the favor of those for whom they chose to speak lying
beneath them

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

and still expect it from you who think the same things; while for those who
stand apart from this and are in the middle it has not befallen either to
speak so as to please those men or to please you in what you wish and expect.
So that it turns out far more profitable for those who have failed than for
the man who persuaded you of these things: the former have the support of the
friends for whose sake they contended, the latter have no gratitude at all
from you. Nevertheless, though the matter has such difficulty, I shall not
hold back. For it seems to me that of those who have spoken on behalf of
both, the speech on your behalf is the one still lacking. If, then, I appear
to be that man, I shall have gratitude from you on your behalf. For those who
spoke for the others keep, even if they are defeated by their opponents, the
favor of those for whom they chose to speak; but the man who has stood in the
middle has already deprived himself of favor from both sides, and it is
unclear what sort he will win from you. So it comes about that it is by no
means the same thing to have chosen the interests of the others and to have
you who think the same things well disposed, or to have stood up on your
behalf and yet to offend you, defeated and having failed in three ways, and
victorious and surviving to become equal to the defeated, the others having
instead of you those whom they chose and made their friends. Nevertheless it
seems to me shameful, and not the part of a citizen, and by no means equal,
that when speeches are made at Athens men are found speaking for the Thebans
and again for the Lacedaemonians, but for you they are wanting. And I praise
both orators, because, having found many and admirable things on each side,
they taught me what in common one must pronounce about them. For I think that
even if I knew nothing at all on my own account, by following what has been
said by these men I should be able to see what is strongest. But I find fault
with them again only so far as this: that, concealing everything and seeking
how they shall escape notice, speaking for favor, some for these and others
for those, they have failed to escape this one thing, that, seeking to escape
notice, they make it plain

:END:

by making it evident. For what need is there to speak? Am I come now to
gratify the Thebans and then to bid you help them, or again not wishing to
gratify the Lacedaemonians and then to command you to run risks on their
behalf? For if this posture is true of both, and one must believe them, as I
for one say one must, my speech has its place: to stand aloof from both. But
now, as though you were seeking whom you shall war against, when you owe it
rather to do what profits the city, some bid you make war with these and
others with those, as though you would not be running a risk of harm. This,
then, if it be laid in the middle: to counsel you to further your own
interests, holding neither side dear. Nothing is more profitable to the city,
nor anything whereby it will more surely preserve its men of age and be
saved, with ships and money and all the rest.

They say that you and your forefathers yourselves chose many dangers for the
sake of many men, and they speak the truth. But if you take such forethought
for the interests of others that you would not shrink even from making war on
their behalf, how shall you not take forethought for your own selves at least
so far as not to run risks out of season? For surely it is not the case that
the interests of others deserve to be secured even amid dangers, while our
own deserve it not even in quiet. Further, if anyone wishes to consider why
our fathers prospered, whether because of the very sailing and campaigning
and love of peril, or because of the posture of their deeds and their
pretexts being reasonable and necessary, he would not, I think, say it was
the former. For they would have assigned them the praise of pirates and
wreckers, if one were to admire this and hold it worthy of emulation, that
they always exposed themselves to dangers. But I think it was that they
defended the Greeks against the barbarians and nowhere let justice go for
softness: this made them glorious, and rightly. But the present war has no
such order that you share in it either of necessity, or for protection, or on
account of any such just reason. For this very thing is first not possible to
decide, on whose behalf one must give help. Why? For this one reason: that if
our entanglement in many

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

affairs together must be the cause of our not keeping quiet even now, this is
common to both sides. So that in those very things in which you would justly
stand with the one side, you will seem to err, in passing over the others.
Further, if we must remember the wrongs we suffered from both, we must add
nothing to either; but if we let the complaint go, surely not to these yes
and to those no, but we shall let it go alike against both. These things
being so, we shall have no one whom we shall choose, both having the same
claim of justice. And in general, how is it not absurd, that against those
whom it so falls out one must help, according to the argument of not bearing
grudges, for their sake one deliberately makes away with the others besides?
So that if help must be given, and this is impossible toward both, the
argument of blame comes into the middle, that of not giving it: that one
helped neither these, because those too had need, nor those, because it was
owed to these as well. So that because one must not bear grudges, for that
very reason one must not help.

It has befallen us what one would most have prayed for: the Thebans and the
Lacedaemonians have fallen upon one another and granted you an opportunity of
leisure and rest, while they themselves are embroiled in a war without end,
by the protection and providence of some god, as it seems to me, over our
city. For if we wished to take vengeance upon them, toils and dangers were
needed; if we desired quiet, they were left unpunished. Now, then, the matter
has been so generalled by the divine that, sitting at home, you have it in
your power to see them punished through one another. We must, then, use such
an opportunity with prudence and wariness, and not look to how the one of
them will be worsted by the other, but to how you may not be troubled by the
others. For it is absurd that they, each of the two, seeking what profits
themselves, not you, have sent ambassadors hither, and that you set the
concerns of those men before your own interests.

Consider, then, by Zeus, which speak better and more moderately: those who
bid you get up quarrels and defend the others and do this in the rank of
enemies, or the one

who counsels you neither to run risks, and to take vengeance on both, and to
offend neither. And yet, when you go out, it is not clear that what you will
meet with from those who fought beside you will be faithful; but while you
remain at home, apart from the security you will provide for yourselves, it
is plainly evident that both will owe you gratitude. For they will not make
complaint of those things in which each of them chanced to lack your fighting
alongside, for they know that to be impossible; but both will justly have
gratitude for those things in which, while they were faring ill, you were not
put to the test: for those who have resolved to trouble neither side know
that this alone is left them. How, then, is it not better, wiping out both
accounts, to lay up gratitude with both, than by adding yourself to the one
side to estrange neither, and yet use the one for enemies first and the other
after? That I suspect these things with reason, the speeches of a moment ago
have shown: about the Lacedaemonians, those who counsel with the Thebans;
about the Thebans, those who counsel with the Lacedaemonians. And it is clear
even without the speeches of these men that, having breathed together and
shared in each other's affairs throughout all the former time, as no single
people shares in the affairs of another, and then falling upon one another
with such eagerness and strength, and demanding to have you for friends whom
at that very time they were wronging, they will hereafter think nothing
straightforward or just toward you, above all when they have taken you up now
out of need, not out of goodwill. But again, when they know us for
reinforcements and are for all that come against one another, how, when we
take away the one side, are we not to think that the survivors will turn upon
us, knowing this well: that if they overcome us, no enemy will any longer be
left to themselves? But alas for justice, it is right to run the risk on its
behalf, even if one must fall into such thanklessness. For it is a dreadful
thing to look on while Lacedaemonians or Thebans have wrought for our city
goods so many and so great. I think that from the very multitude of the
benefits of each side I cannot now decide which of them has brought us more
and greater; and then again, when I call to mind how both of them

gladly and eagerly invaded our land every single year for close on thirty
years together, and how readily and lightly they fortified Decelea against us
and met us in Sicily and were on hand everywhere before they were so much as
called upon. For the destruction of the Plataeans, I suppose, each would
concede it to the other. Yes, by Zeus, but the Thebans fought well at Delium.
And what will anyone say worse of the Lacedaemonians at Amphipolis? As for
the other benefits, I am not able to recount them accurately, so many they
are. Such are the requitals these men have practiced toward the city. But the
Thebans joined the King long ago; and the Lacedaemonians later, while the
trophies they set up together with us were still standing. Do I not then
rightly stand in doubt which of the two has been the cause of greater goods
to the city? Yes, by Zeus. For the Lacedaemonians brought forward the vote
that would have saved our city: that is so. Then again, the Thebans, when the
Lacedaemonians called them, did not obey: that is so too. Must we then feel
gratitude to the Lacedaemonians because they did not destroy the city in the
same manner as Plataea, and to the Thebans because they refused to join in
the invasion? Or to the Lacedaemonians because they brought us to such a pass
that one might well be angry with them, and blame the Thebans far more
because they shared in all the former invasions? Or must we hold that we owe
gratitude to the one for saving us, and to the other for not joining even
once in the invasion?

And yet we ought no more to be bitterly disposed toward the Thebans for
bringing about the destruction than toward the Lacedaemonians for handing
over to them power to decree about us whatever they wished; nor again ought
we to be more angry with the Lacedaemonians who marched upon the Piraeus than
with the Thebans for the very fault for which they were left out. And if one
examines us accurately, we shall find it lies in this: that their vote did
not prevail. But why do I quibble so precisely? If anyone wishes to count
these things the greatest benefits, I do not disagree; but I will tell you
how I have judged. If we must look to what good each has done the city, I do
not think it right to rehearse the benefits of the Thebans and the wrongs of
the Lacedaemonians, nor again the reverse, but the good deeds of both alike.
And if we must examine their ingratitude, we must not display that of the one
side and conceal that of the other where they have done well, but, since both
sides have both things, choose one of two: either, judging both benefactors,
wrong neither; or, judging both ill disposed, help neither. And yet if each
must be repaid his worth, let it stand for the wrongs they have done us that
they go without the help, and for the good they have done let it stand for
each not to suffer harm: so shall you have considered fairly toward both. But
if you incline to one side or the other, you will deal justly with neither:
doing good to the one in the very things wherein he wronged us, and destroying
the other in the very things wherein he benefited us, when it was possible to
bank up justice and to deduct from the zeal for the one side just so much as
is needed for not overflowing in hatred of the other. But if I must say this
too: I think we have repaid the Thebans gratitude sufficient already for
their refusal to join the Lacedaemonians in the invasion and for receiving
Thrasybulus, for killing Lysander, and for the other deeds you know of; and
to the Lacedaemonians we ought now to repay instead of the vote the gratitude
of not destroying them with the Thebans at hand; and our alliance then was
strong enough in this, that we did not add ourselves to the Lacedaemonians,
and the goodwill of the Lacedaemonians then in this, that we are not now
likewise with the Thebans. And this too equalizes them: that it is reasonable
to help neither the Lacedaemonians, because they broke the treaties, nor the
Thebans, because they now have a strong claim upon us. Neither, then, is it
fair to join the one in the march nor to hinder the other, justly indignant.
And I will question those who spoke for each side separately. Men, you who
counselled for the Lacedaemonians, what is it you strive for? Is it your wish
that the Thebans not obtain the

Come then, again: men, you who have counselled
for the Thebans, is it your wish that nothing stand in the Thebans' way, and
that we not incline toward the Lacedaemonians? I vote with you. Then why do
we shout vainly and quarrel, when a reconciliation so common and so just
plainly lies before us? But is it not enough, this middle course of making
war on neither? If not for you, that is another argument. And indeed, if of
three things both sides are zealous on your behalf, one being that those they
have chosen should suffer no ill, another that their enemies should not
prosper, and a third that they make those they have chosen greater, and I
take away the third and hold to the others, how do I not share in your
opinion twice as much as by so much I stand apart? And I will add that in
this respect also I share your views, wherein you yourselves would claim to
excel: for how is it better to seek the city's interest in common than to
attach oneself now to some favor and now to some enmity? And then you do the
very things you rebuke in the others. You do not permit them to recite their
benefits, but you yourselves run through what you have chosen. You make your
defense against the charges, and then you accuse.

I, then, men of Athens, besides doing nothing contrary to myself, am of one
vote with these men; but they, each side, seem to speak at once for their own
party and at once for the others the opposite. And yet how must not I too,
differing from neither, in what at least I have shared their opinion, have a
share? For the advocacy cannot surely come round to bidding you help both.
How should it? But the advocate's place falls away entirely from falling upon
helping neither. For these men too, each of them, hinder themselves from
marching out with the others. Nothing, then, is my speech other than, as it
were, the reading of some counter-writer who has set down in order the speech
of each and made one out of two. But the cause of the King, of which you
remember mention was made just now, allows us to help neither side rather
than it urges us toward the others. What I mean by this you shall

know clearly. For it is not possible, surely, to help the Thebans on this
argument, for they have gone over to the Mede; nor the Lacedaemonians,
because they too emulate the wickedness of the Persian, and it is by no means
fitting that you be heirs of his wickedness and faithlessness. For surely you
would not profess to hate Hellas, so as to seek to make her weakest, as he
does; nor is it bearable, turning about, to appear so. But to leave those who
have delivered their speeches to be judged upon their own merits is without
reproach, and not even one of you would resent it. And then the King himself,
not coming down in person nor risking his body, but accomplishing the war out
of money, abounding in many resources, would have sent down a viceroy, having
no few of them; and this was the King's war: Pharnabazus, Pisuthnes,
Tissaphernes, falsehood, gold, and the like. But we, if we incline to one
side, shall suffer what he guarded against by means of these. For he looked
forward how he might never himself run a risk, and for that reason he worked
the matter from far off; but we, on the contrary, shall run the risk with our
very bodies and our country and, one may say, all our resources. For it is
not possible for us to make war through others, nor, sitting above the war,
to suffice to the contest, nor to know of events by hearsay, nor out of our
surplus, nor to share in what is happening with a share of luxury, taking our
wishes for our measure; but we must be present and toil along, and run
dangers no less than those who fell in from the beginning, not send a viceroy
whenever we wish. And even as to money: there is nowhere enough of it for us
that we could alienate it for so great a need; and even if we should furnish
it to one side in this fashion, one cannot say Susa and Ecbatana are not at
hand, nor must one hide that the sea, walking its seasonal road, is but a
journey of a day or two, for the injured to fall upon us and lead and carry
us away captive, so that from these things it comes to blows of necessity:
such are the fruits of misadventure.

And I would have bidden even myself come to the aid of the Lacedaemonians

by holding down the Thebans, had I not been persuaded that those men would
bring up in training as much again as these others have the advantage in
numbers and strength. For if the two had been established on an equality, I
for my part should have feared for the Thebans as the weaker; but since the
Thebans have gone on from doing well, I hold that the war has equal holds:
for it is possible for the Lacedaemonians also, setting out later, to join in
the pursuit. Then the war will hold together and make attrition: these,
puffed up by their successes, seeking to advance upon everything; and the
Lacedaemonians again, holding that they must be superior in virtue and
spirit, refusing to accept anything further at their hands. But if indeed one
must imitate the deed of some people, the fair deed of the Argives: who,
keeping quiet beside our war and that of the Lacedaemonians, throve in youth
and in equipment of country and in all things, until, laying hold of the
affairs, they let go the happiness they had, and their commons was then
dissolved, and again other evils followed. For wars, one may say, beget all
things: overthrow of polities, quarrels, losses such as no man could foresee
in their variety; in which, if any men have had experience, and we among
them, we should justly be on our guard. And I hear that our forefathers in
those times for a while withdrew from Salamis to the Megarians, and, if one
should say it, having lost all, made a decree to die. Where indeed it is
sometimes needful and profitable to yield up even one's own and let it go,
how is it worthy to inherit the evils of others, and to be bitter against
those very men who now call us for the invasions into Attica, whichever of
them it befell to make, and ourselves to cast ourselves idly into war and
tumult past all settling, and that knowing how easy it is to begin a war,
while nothing is so hard as to be quit of it? For most men choose dangers
while masters of themselves, but shun them once fallen to others; neither of
these courses is well minded. But with us for a long time the land has been
wretched, and for a long time now

the bodies are wasting away, and of the money, all that we once gathered
together to the acropolis, so great a mass has flowed away; and the city
needs a generation, which, as it climbs up again, we must not now mar; but
while these men hate and rage and fall upon one another and threaten, let us
sit as prize-givers of the war, keeping watch over the city, gathering for it
money and horses and triremes, furnishing the whole situation, like men
restoring a house that has gone to ruin. And if you are persuaded by me and
restrain yourselves, embassies will come a second time too, both from these
men and from those: they will come, know it well, when all things appear to
you thus ordered and established; and I think that not these men only, but
with the gods' favor many others besides, will come as envoys desiring our
friendship, if you endure, and are willing to be content with their
quarrelling for the building up of your supremacy. For no one, I say, fears
this: lest the two join together, and, giving each other up, turn both upon
us. Not as being gracious to us, whether of these or of those, do I say it,
but because there is no reason for it in the matter itself. In place of 'Why,
of course they will join'? Because, why of course, we marched out with
neither. Then by so much the more they are angry, by so much the more
impossible is it for them to come together. For when each side is indignant
that it was not through you that it was able to work its will upon its enemy,
how will it let him go? Or how will they stand together, when each of them
knows that the other is angry with him, because he had not you for allies
against him?

And apart from all this, there would be no leisure even now for such
thoughts: but the Thebans are given over to it, believing the Lacedaemonians
ready to destroy them, and they would not stand aside from these present
doings even for the King's whole empire; and the Lacedaemonians would most
gladly every man of them die, if they might but take satisfaction of the
Thebans: so does their anger possess them. And no wonder that, being worsted
against their deserts, they are sore

troubled with shame. And, to speak the head of the matter, their mutual
hatred and exasperation have overspread their enmity toward our city, and, as
when a cloud has passed over, they can see none of the things that concern
us. But if now we fear them lest we do them wrong, how much more shall we
with reason fear lest, by adding ourselves to one side, the other overcome
us? For at present there is no fear from the survivors, should they attempt
anything new. For cut down and worn out, as having won, as one may say, a
Cadmean victory, they will feel it, if they make their contest with us unhurt
and clear. But if we add ourselves to one side: this, if it chances that they
carry the day, they must become no less sharers in the evils, so that if
later they deal ungraciously, we shall be even with them; and that, if the
scales incline, the danger is plain. Now the victors among them conquer for
us; but then, through us, if it so fall out, the others will conquer. And
besides, our circumstances are not like theirs. For the Lacedaemonians,
dwelling at the farthest end of the Peloponnese, lie in great shelter from
the war and its affairs; the Thebans have been driven back behind Cithaeron;
but our city stands in the highway of the war, and for neither of the two
will there be any marching out against the other before falling upon ours,
and this again, being the ready one, to be laid waste. And in sum I do not
see what we should go to war about, unless we are to stand by neither of them
to the death, or sit upon the middle ground, sparing our opponents, it being
possible altogether to flee the appearance of wronging either these or those,
having said nothing in anger to the ambassadors, but being thus gently and
moderately disposed toward both; and if they called us out against others, to
obey readily, but when they call us against one another, not to consent to
go, not fleeing to do good to the others, but fleeing to wrong either. These
things, too, seem to me reasonable, looking at them from every side. But I
fear, men of Athens, lest you be better at setting right the affairs of
others than at ordering your own well.
