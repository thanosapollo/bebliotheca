* Letter to Marcellinus on the Interpretation of the Psalms
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of our father among the saints Athanasius, archbishop of Alexandria, to
Marcellinus, on the interpretation of the Psalms.

I admire you for your purpose in Christ, friend Marcellinus. For you also bear
the present trial well, though you have suffered much in it, and you do not
neglect the discipline. For when I inquired of the one who brought the letter
how you were faring even after the illness, I learned that you have your
leisure toward the whole of divine Scripture, and that you especially read
more frequently the book of the Psalms, and that you strive to grasp the
mind lying in each psalm. And for this reason, then, I approve you, since I
too have a great longing toward that very book, as also toward all Scripture.
And being so disposed, I met a certain diligent old man, and I want to write
to you also the things that man, holding the Psalter, related to me about it;
for it has a certain grace and persuasiveness together with a reasonable
narration.

For he spoke thus: All Scripture among us, child, both old and new, is
God-breathed and profitable for teaching, as it is written; but the book of
the Psalms has a certain persuasive observation for those who attend. For each
book serves and announces its own promise: for example, the Pentateuch the
generation of the world and the deeds of the patriarchs, and Israel's exodus
from Egypt, and the arrangement of the legislation; the Tritateuch the
allotment of the inheritance, and the deeds of the judges, and the genealogy
of David; the Kingdoms and the Chronicles the deeds of kings; and Esdras the
release from captivity and the return of the people, and the building of the
temple and of the city; and the Prophets prophecies concerning the sojourn of
the Savior, reminders concerning the commandments, and rebukes against those
who transgress, and prophecies for the nations.

But the book of the Psalms, like a garden bearing the things in it, sings
them, and again, singing its own things together with them, it shows them. For
it sings the things of Genesis in the eighteenth: "The heavens tell the glory
of God, and the firmament announces the work of his hands"; and in κγʹ: "The
earth is the Lord's and the fullness of it, the inhabited world and all who
dwell in it; he has founded it upon seas." And the things of Exodus and of
Numbers and of Deuteronomy it sings well in the seventy-seventh psalm, and in
the hundred and thirteenth psalm, saying: "When Israel went out from Egypt,
the house =27.13= of Jacob from a barbarous people, Judah became his
sanctuary, Israel his dominion." And it sings these also in the hundred and
fourth: "He sent Moses his servant, Aaron whom he chose; he set among them the
words of his signs and of his wonders in the land of Ham. He sent darkness,
and it grew dark, and they embittered his words. He turned their waters into
blood, and killed their fish. Their land teemed with frogs in the treasuries
of their kings. He spoke, and the dog-fly came, and gnats in all their
borders." And in short, this whole psalm and the hundred and fifth can be
found written about the same things.

And the things of the priesthood and of the tabernacle it proclaims in the
going-out of the tabernacle in the κηʹ psalm: "Bring to the Lord, sons of God,
bring to the Lord sons of rams, bring to the Lord glory and honor." And the
things of Joshua and of the Judges it indicates somehow in the hundred and
sixth, saying: "And they established cities of habitation, and they sowed
fields, and they planted vineyards." For under Joshua it has been given

to them the land of promise. And saying continually in the same psalm: "And
they cried to the Lord when they were afflicted, and he saved them from their
distresses," it signifies the book of the Judges; for then, when they cried,
he raised up judges according to the time, and saved the people from those who
afflicted them.

And the things of the Kingdoms it sings somehow in the nineteenth, saying:
"These in chariots and these in horses, but we will be magnified in the name
of the Lord our God. They were bound and fell, but we rose and were set
upright. Lord, save the king, and hear us in whatever day we call upon you."
And the things of Esdras it sings in the hundred and twenty-fifth of the
steps: "When the Lord turned the captivity of Sion, we became as those
comforted"; and again in the hundred and twenty-first: "I was glad at those
who said to me, Let us go into the house of the Lord. Our feet were standing
in your courts, Jerusalem. Jerusalem being built as a city whose sharing is
together; for there the tribes went up, the tribes of the Lord, a testimony
for Israel." And the things of the Prophets it signifies in almost every one.

Concerning the sojourn of the Savior, and that being God he will sojourn, it
says thus in the forty-ninth psalm: "The Lord will come manifestly, our God,
and he will not keep silence"; and in the hundred and seventeenth: "Blessed is
he who comes in the name of the Lord. We have blessed you from the house of
the Lord; God is the Lord, and he has appeared to us." And that this one is
the Word of the Father, it sings thus in the hundred and sixth: "He sent his
Word and healed them, and delivered them from their destructions." For the God
who comes is himself also the Word being sent. And knowing this Word to be the
Son of God, it sings a voice of the Father in the forty-fourth psalm: "My
heart has belched forth a good Word"; and again in the hundred =27.16= and
ninth: "From the womb before the Morning Star I begot you." For what else
would one call the offspring of the Father than his Word and his Wisdom?
Knowing this to be the one to whom the Father said, "Let there be light, and
firmament, and all things," this book too contains it, saying: "By the Word of
the Lord the heavens were made firm, and by the spirit of his mouth all their
power."

And that he is coming as Christ it did not fail to know; but especially
concerning this it says in the forty-fourth psalm: "Your throne, O God, is
unto the age of the age; a rod of uprightness is the rod of your kingdom. You
loved righteousness and hated injustice; therefore God, your God, has anointed
you with the oil of gladness beyond your fellows." And lest anyone suppose him
to come (to be) according to appearance, it signifies that he will become man,
and that this is the one through whom all things came to be, saying in the
eighty-sixth: "Mother Sion will say, A man, and a man was born in her, and the
Most High himself founded her." For this is equal to saying: "And the Word was
God; all things came to be through him, and the Word became flesh." Therefore
also, knowing the "from a Virgin," it did not keep silence, but at once gives
a certain indication in the forty-fourth psalm, saying: "Hear, daughter, and
see, and incline your ear, and forget your people and your father's house,
because the king has desired your beauty." For this again is like what is said
by Gabriel: "Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with you." For having called him
Christ, it at once also made plain the human generation from the Virgin,
saying: "Hear, daughter." See, Gabriel calls Mary by name, being a stranger to
her according to generation; but David, since she happens to be from his own
seed, fittingly addresses her as daughter.

And having said that he will become man, it accordingly also signifies him as
passible in the flesh. And seeing the plot coming about from the Jews, it
sings in the second psalm:

"Why did the nations rage, and the peoples meditate empty things? The kings of
the earth stood by, and the rulers were gathered together against the Lord and
against his Christ"; and in the twenty-first it says the quality of the death
from the person of the Savior: "You have brought me down into the dust of
death; for many dogs have surrounded me, a gathering of evildoers have
enclosed me. They dug my hands and my feet, they counted all my bones. They
themselves considered and looked upon me; they divided my garments among
themselves, and upon my clothing they cast a lot." And the digging of the
hands and the feet, what else does it signify by saying it than a cross?

Teaching all these things it adds that the Lord suffers these things not for
himself, but for us. And it says again from his person in πζʹ: "Your wrath has
been established upon me"; and in the sixty-eighth: "Then I paid what I did
not seize." For he was not =27.17= dying as being liable, but he suffered for
us, and he bore upon himself the wrath against us because of the
transgression, the one who says through Isaiah: "He took our weaknesses"; and
while we say in the hundred and thirty-seventh psalm: "The Lord will repay on
my behalf"; and the Spirit also saying in the seventy-first: "And he will save
the sons of the poor, and will humble the slanderer, because he delivered the
poor from the hand of a mighty one, and the needy who had no helper."

Therefore it also foretells his bodily ascent into the heavens, and says in
the twenty-third: "Lift up your gates, you rulers, and be lifted up, eternal
gates, and the king of glory will enter"; and in the fortieth: "God has gone
up with a shout, the Lord with the voice of a trumpet." It announces the
sitting, and says in the hundred and ninth: "The Lord said to my Lord, Sit at
my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool of your feet"; and in the
ninth it also cries the destruction that came about of the devil: "You sat
upon a throne, you who judge righteousness; you rebuked nations, and the
ungodly perished." And that he received all judgment from the Father it did
not hide, but it also foretells him coming as judge of all in οαʹ: "O God,
give your judgment to the king, and your righteousness to the king's son, to
judge your people in righteousness, and your poor in judgment"; and in μθʹ it
says: "He will call the heaven above and the earth, to judge his people. And
the heavens will announce his righteousness, because God is judge"; and in
παʹ: "God stood in the assembly of gods; and in the midst he will judge gods."
And indeed the calling of the nations can be learned from it in many places,
but especially from μςʹ: "All nations, clap your hands, shout to God with a
voice of gladness"; and in οαʹ: "Ethiopians will fall down before him, and his
enemies will lick dust; kings of Tharsis and the islands will bring gifts;
kings of the Arabians and Saba will bring gifts; and all the kings of the
earth will worship him, all nations will serve him."

These things are sung in the Psalms, and in each of the other books they are
announced beforehand. And again, not being ignorant, he said that in each book
of Scripture the same things concerning the Savior are especially signified;
and this is the announcement of all of them, and the same agreement of the
Spirit. For just as the things of the others can be found in this one, so also
the things of this one are often found in the others. For Moses writes a song,
and Isaiah sings, and Habakkuk prays with a song. And again in each book one
can see both prophecies and legislations and histories. For the same Spirit is
upon all; and according to the division of him that has come about toward
each, each serves and fulfills the =27.20= grace given to him, whether it is
prophecy, or legislation, or the memory of history, or the grace of the
psalms; but
since it is one and the same Spirit, of whom also all the divisions are, and
he is undivided by nature: for this very

reason the whole is in each, but according to the ministry the manifestations
and the divisions of the Spirit come about to each; and further, according to
the need that lies at hand, each often, the Spirit supplying, serves the word;
so that, as I said before, Moses legislating sometimes prophesies and sings,
and the prophets prophesying sometimes command: "Wash yourselves, become
clean. Wash wickedness from your heart, Jerusalem"; and sometimes they
narrate, Daniel the things concerning Susanna, and Isaiah the things
concerning Rabshakeh and Sennacherib.

So also the book of the Psalms, having the proper character of the songs,
sings with melody, in the voice according to breadth, the things that have
been said at length in the other books, as has been said before. It
legislates, then, sometimes itself also: "Cease from anger, and forsake
wrath"; and, "Turn from evil, and do good; seek peace, and pursue it." And it
also sometimes narrates according to the way of Israel, and prophesies
concerning the Savior, as was said earlier. Let such a common grace of the
Spirit, then, be among all, and let it be found coming about in each, and the
same among all, as the need may require and the Spirit may will. For more and
less do not differ in this need, as each unceasingly fulfills and completes
his own ministry.

But the book of the Psalms also has in this way again a certain grace of its
own and an exceptional observation; for besides the other things in which it
has relation and fellowship toward the other books, it further has this wonder
of its own, that it has the movements of each soul, and their changes and
corrections, written out and patterned in itself; so that one who wishes, as
one inexperienced, may take from it and understand, and so pattern himself: it
is written there. For in the other books one only hears the law commanding
what one must (not) do and what one must not practice; and one hears
prophecies, so as only to know the Savior who is coming; and one attends to
histories, from which one can know the deeds of the kings and of the saints.
But in the book of the Psalms, besides the hearer's learning these things, he
still also understands and is taught in it the movements of his own soul; and
further, toward what he suffers and in what he is held, he can again from this
have the image of the words; so that he does not only hear and pass by, but it
also teaches how, speaking and doing, one must heal the passion.

For there are also in the other books words that restrain, forbidding the base
things; but in this one it has also been patterned how one must abstain; for
example =27.21= the command is to repent; and to repent is to cease from sin;
but here both how to repent, and what one must say upon repentance, has been
patterned. And again Paul has said: "Tribulation works endurance in the soul,
and endurance proof, and proof hope, and hope does not put to shame." But in
the Psalms it has also been written and engraved how one must bear
tribulations, and what one who is afflicted must say, and what after the
tribulations, and how each is proved, and what the words of those who hope in
the Lord are. And again there is a commandment to give thanks in everything;
but the Psalms also teach what those who give thanks must say. Then, hearing
from others: "All who wish to live piously will be persecuted," from these we
are taught both how those who flee must speak, and what words those who are
persecuted and after the persecution have been delivered must offer to God.

We are commanded to bless the Lord and to confess to him; but in the Psalms we
are patterned both how we must praise the Lord, and what phrases we should say
as we confess fittingly. And in each case one would thus find the divine songs
laid down toward us and our movements and conditions. For this again is
paradoxical in the Psalms, that in the other books what the saints say, and
about whom they speak, these things also those who read, about whom those
things have been written, are announc

ing; and those who hear consider themselves other than those about whom the
word speaks, and as for the deeds announced, they come only as far as
marveling and zeal for them, unto imitation. But the one who takes this book
goes through the prophecies concerning the Savior, marveling and worshipping
in the usual way as in the other Scriptures, but the other psalms he reads as
being his own words; and the hearer also is pricked as if he himself were
speaking, and he is disposed together with the words of the songs, as being
his own.

And for the sake of clarity one must not hesitate, according to the blessed
Apostle, taking it up again, to say the same thing. There are very many words
of the patriarchs, and proper ones spoken by them; and Moses spoke, and God
answered; and Elijah and Elisha, sitting on Mount Carmel, called upon the
Lord, and always said: "The Lord lives, before whom I have stood today." And
the words of the other holy prophets are first those concerning the Savior;
then also very many have come about both to the nations and to Israel. And yet
no one would ever say the words of the patriarchs as his own, nor would anyone
dare to imitate and speak the proper phrases of Moses, nor those of Abraham
concerning the home-born and Ishmael and the things concerning the great
Isaac; even if the same need and necessity should overtake someone, no one
would dare to say them as his own. And if someone also suffers with those who
suffer, and at some time takes a longing for the better, he would never say as
=27.24= Moses: "Show yourself to me"; or again: "If you forgive them the sin,
forgive; but if you do not forgive, blot me out of your book which you have
written." But neither, taking the prophets' phrases as his own, would anyone
blame or praise with these those who have done things like those whom those
men blamed and praised; nor would anyone imitate saying as his own word: "The
Lord lives, before whom I have stood today." For even the one who meets with
the books is plainly speaking the words not as his own, but as of the saints
and of those indicated through them.

But the Psalms, the paradox, after the prophecies concerning the Savior and
the nations, the one who speaks the other things is speaking them as his own
phrases, and each sings them as written about himself, and he does not receive
and go through them as another speaking or signifying about another; but he is
disposed as himself speaking about himself; and whatever the things said are,
these he offers to God as having done them himself and speaking from himself.
For he will not be cautious about these as about the phrases of the
patriarchs, and of Moses, and of the other prophets; but especially as his own
and written about himself the one who sings these things has confidence in
speaking. For the Psalms contain both the one who has kept the commandment and
the one who has transgressed it, and the action of each. And every human being
must be held in these, and speak the words written about each, either as one
who has kept the commandment or as one who has transgressed it.

And it seems to me that these become for the one who sings like a mirror, so
that he too may understand in them the movements of his own soul, and so,
perceiving, may announce them. For the one who hears the reader also receives
the song as being said about himself; and either, convicted by conscience,
pricked, he will repent, or, hearing about the hope toward God and the help
that comes about toward those who believe, he rejoices as if such grace had
come about toward him, and he begins to give thanks to God. When, then,
someone sings the third, looking to his own tribulations, he thinks the
phrases in the psalm to be his own; and then the ιαʹ and the ιςʹ he is
announcing as for his own confidence and prayer; and the νʹ psalm as himself
speaking the proper phrases of his own repentance; and the νγʹ, and the νεʹ,
and the νςʹ, and the ρμαʹ, when someone sings them, he is not disposed as
another being persecuted, but as being the one who suffers, and he sings these
words to the Lord as his own. And in short each psalm has been spoken and
composed by the Spirit in such a way that in them, as was said before, the

movements of our soul are understood, and all of them as having been spoken
about us, and they are our own words, for remembrance of the movements in us
and correction of our way of life. For what those who sang have said, these
things can also be types and characters of us.

And this again is the grace of the Savior; for having become man for us, he
offered his own body on our behalf unto death, that he might set all free from
death; and wishing to show us his heavenly and =27.25= well-pleasing way of
life, he patterned it in himself, so that no longer anyone should easily be
deceived by the enemy, having as a pledge toward security the victory that
came about from him on our behalf against the devil. For this reason, then, he
not only taught, but also did what he taught, so that each might hear him
speaking, and seeing as in an image might take from him the example of doing,
hearing: "Learn from me, that I am meek and humble in heart." And one would
not find a more perfect teaching toward virtue than that which the Lord
patterned in himself. For whether forbearance, or love of humankind, or
goodness, or courage, or almsgiving, or righteousness, one will find all of
them having come about in him, so that nothing toward virtue is lacking for
the one who considers this human life. For Paul, knowing this, said: "Become
imitators of me, as I also of Christ."

The legislators among the Greeks have the grace only as far as speaking; but
the Lord, being truly Lord of the universe, and caring for those he made, not
only legislates, but has also given himself as a type, so that those who wish
may know the power of doing. For this reason, then, even before his sojourn
toward us he sounded this also in those who sing, so that just as in himself
he patterned and showed the earthly and the heavenly man, so also from the
Psalms the one who wishes may be able to learn thoroughly the movements and
dispositions of the souls, finding in them both the healing and the correction
of each movement.

For if one must also speak more persuasively, all divine Scripture is a
teacher of virtue and of true faith; but the book of the Psalms also has
somehow the image of the conduct of souls. For as the one who goes in before a
king is patterned both in bearing and in phrases, so that, saying things
contrary to these, he may not be cast out as uneducated; so the divine book
first, through the reading, reminds the one who runs toward virtue and wishes
to understand the Savior's way of life in the body of the movements of the
soul, and so then patterns and teaches those who meet with such words.

Such, then, being the arrangement of the Psalms, it is further possible for
those who meet with them to find in each, as I said before, the movements and
the condition of their own soul, and so the type and the teaching concerning
each; and what, speaking, one can please the Lord, and through what phrases
one can correct oneself, and give thanks to the Lord, so that the one who
speaks such things may not fall out into impiety. For we have to give account
to the Judge not only concerning works, but also concerning an idle phrase.

If, then, you should wish to bless someone, you have how one must, and upon
what, and what one must say, the αʹ and the λαʹ, and μʹ, and ριαʹ, and ριηʹ,
and ρκζʹ; and if you should wish to blame the Jews' plot against the Savior,
you have the second song. And if you are persecuted by your own, and have many
who have risen up against you, say the γʹ psalm. And if, being thus afflicted,
you called upon the Lord, and having been heard you wish to give thanks, sing
the fourth, and οδʹ, and ριδʹ. And if, still seeing those who do evil wanting
to lie in wait, you wish your prayer to be given ear, rising early =27.29= in
the morning sing the εʹ. And perceiving a threat of the Lord, if you see
yourself troubled because of this, you can say the ςʹ and the λζʹ. And if some
also counsel against you, as Ahithophel against David, and someone should
announce this to you, sing the ζʹ, and take heart in God who delivers you. And
seeing the grace of the Savior stretched out everywhere, and the race of human
beings saved, if you wish to address the Lord, sing the ηʹ. But again you wish
to sing a song of the winepress, giving thanks to the Lord, you have again the
same ηʹ and the πγʹ. And upon the enemy having been abolished and the creation
having been saved, not boasting in yourself, but knowing that the Son of God
accomplished this, say the θʹ psalm spoken unto him. And if someone should
wish to trouble you exceedingly, have your confidence in the Lord, and sing
the ιʹ. And when you see the pride of the many and wickedness abounding, so
that nothing holy has come about among human beings, take refuge yourself in
the Lord, and say the ιαʹ. And if the plot from the enemies lingers, do not
grow faint as if forgotten by God, but entreat the Lord singing the ιβʹ.
Whenever you hear some blaspheming against Providence, do not share in their
impiety, but meeting with God, say the ιγʹ and νβʹ. And further if you wish to
learn of what sort the citizen of the kingdom of the heavens is, sing the ιδʹ.
You have need of prayer because of those who have stood against you and
enclose your soul, sing the ιςʹ, and the πεʹ, and the πςʹ, and the ρμʹ. But
you wish to learn how Moses prayed, you have the πθʹ. But you have been saved
from your enemies, and delivered from those who persecute you, sing you also
the ιςʹ psalm. You marvel at the order of creation, and the grace of

Such, then, is also the character for benefit to human beings from the book of
the Psalms, it having psalms of its own, and in the remaining ones also
concerning the

bodily presence of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ more frequent prophecies,
as I said before. And why such words are sung with melody and a song, it is
necessary not to pass this by either. For some of the simple among us,
although they believe the phrases to be God-breathed, nevertheless suppose
that the psalms are sung with melody because of the good sound and for the
pleasure of the hearing. But it is not so; for Scripture did not seek the
pleasant and the persuasive; but this too has been patterned for the benefit
of the soul, for all reasons, but especially for these two: first, that it was
fitting that divine Scripture hymn God not only with continuity, but also with
the voice according to breadth. =27.40= According to continuity, then, have
been said such things as those of the law and the prophets, and all the things
narrated, together with the New Testament; and according to breadth are said
such things as the phrases of the psalms and odes and songs; for thus also it
will be preserved that human beings love God from all strength and power.

And second, that, just as a harmony composing the pipes produces one concord,
so, since various movements also appear in the soul, and there is in it the
reasoning, and the desiring, and the spirited, and from the movement of these
the activity of the members of the body also comes about, the Word wishes the
human being not to be discordant with himself, nor to be divided against
himself; so as to reason the best things, but to do the base things by the
spirited part, as Pilate saying: "I find no cause in him," but running with
the judgment of the Jews; or to desire the base things, but not to be able to
do them, as the elders against Susanna; or again not to commit adultery, but
to steal; or not to steal, but to murder; or not to murder, but to blaspheme.
So that no such turmoil may come about in us, the Word wishes the soul, having
the mind of Christ, as the Apostle said, to use this as a leader, and in this
to master the passionate things in it, and to rule the members of the body,
unto obeying the word; so that, as the plectrum in a harmony, so the human
being himself, having become a psaltery, and attending to the spirit wholly,
may also with all the members and the movements obey and serve the will of
God. And of such an untroubled and waveless condition of the reasonings, the
melodious reading of the Psalms is an image and a type. For just as we make
known and signify the thoughts of the soul through the words we utter, so the
Lord, wishing the melody from the words to be a symbol of the spiritual
harmony in the soul, has patterned that the odes be sung melodiously, and that
the psalms be read with a song. And this is the desire of the soul, that it be
well disposed, as it is written: "Is anyone cheerful among you? Let him sing."
Thus the turbulent and rough and disordered thing in it is made even; and that
which grieves is healed, as we sing: "Why are you very grieved, my soul, and
why do you trouble me?" And that which has slipped will come to knowledge,
saying: "But my feet were almost shaken"; and that which has feared will take
strength by the hope in saying: "The Lord is a helper to me, and I will not
fear what a human being will do to me."

Those, then, who do not read the divine songs in this manner do not sing with
understanding, but they please themselves, and they have blame, because
"Praise is not beautiful in the mouth of a sinner"; but those who sing
according to the manner said before, so that the melody of the phrases is
offered from the rhythm of the soul and the concord toward the spirit, such
men =27.41= sing with the tongue, and singing also with the mind, they greatly
benefit not only themselves, but also those who wish to hear them. The blessed
David, then, singing thus to Saul, himself was well-pleasing to God, and he
drove away the turmoil and the manic passion of Saul, and prepared his soul to
be calm. Thus the priests, singing, called the souls of the peoples toward an
untroubled state and toward concord with those who

dance in the heavens. The saying of the psalms with melody, then, is not a
zeal for good sound, but a token of the harmony of the reasonings in the soul.
And the melodious reading is a symbol of the well-rhythmed and unstormed
condition of the mind. For the praising of God on well-sounding cymbals and a
kithara and a ten-stringed psaltery was again a symbol and significant of the
members of the body being composed lawfully as strings, and the reasonings of
the soul becoming as cymbals, and further all these things being moved and
living by the sound and the nod of the spirit; so that, according to what is
written, the human being may live by the spirit, and put to death the
practices of the body. For thus also one who sings well rhythms his own soul,
and as from inequality leads it into equality; so that, standing in what is
according to nature, it may not be scared by anything, but may rather become
well-imagining, and take more longing of the goods to come. For being disposed
together with the melody of the phrases it forgets the passions, and rejoicing
it looks toward the mind that is in Christ, reasoning the best things.

It is necessary, then, child, that each of those who meet with this book meet
genuinely with all the things in it, which are God-breathed, and further take
from them, as from fruits of a garden, benefits, toward what he sees himself
having the need. For I think that in these words of the book the whole life of
human beings, and the dispositions of the soul, and the movements of the
reasonings, have been measured and contained; and that nothing beyond these is
found among human beings. For whether there is need of repentance or of
confession, or tribulation and trial have overtaken, or someone was
persecuted, and being plotted against was delivered, and if someone has also
become very grieved and is troubled, and suffers something such as has been
said in the things before, or also sees himself advancing, and the enemy being
abolished, or also wishes to praise and to give thanks, and to bless the Lord,
he has the teaching of these things in the divine psalms. For let him choose
the things said in them concerning each of these, and thus, saying them as
written about himself, and being disposed together with the things written,
let him offer them to the Lord.

Let no one, however, clothe them with persuasive phrases from outside, nor try
to recast the expressions or to change them at all; but let him thus artlessly
say and sing the things written, as has been said, so that the human beings
who served them, =27.44= recognizing their own, may also pray together with
us; or rather so that the Spirit who spoke in the saints, seeing the words
from himself that were sounded in those men, may also help us. For by as much
as the life of the saints is better than that of the others, by so much also
one would justly say that the phrases from them are better and stronger than
those composed by us. For in these they were well-pleasing to God, and saying
these things, as the Apostle said: "They overthrew kingdoms, they worked
righteousness, they obtained promises, they stopped mouths of lions, they
quenched power of fire, they fled mouths of a sword, they were empowered from
weakness, they became strong in war, they turned back camps of foreigners,
women received their dead from resurrection."

Therefore now also let each who says these things take heart, that through
them God will quickly hear those who ask; for if he is afflicted saying these
things, he will see great consolation in them; if he is tried and persecuted
singing thus, he will appear more proved, and he will be sheltered by the
Lord, who also sheltered the one who said these things; in these he will
overturn the devil, and he will scare away his demons. Saying these things, if
he has sinned, he will put himself to shame and will cease; and if he has not
sinned, he will see himself rejoicing, because he stretches toward the things
in front, and struggling he will have strength singing thus, and he will not
be shaken unto the age from the truth, but he will also refute those who
deceive and

those who attempt to lead astray; and of this not a human being is guarantor,
but the divine Scripture itself becomes so. For God commanded Moses to write
the great Ode, and to teach the people; and he orders the ruler who is
appointed to write the Deuteronomy, and to have this in his hands, and always
to meditate the phrases in it, as the words in them being sufficient both for
a reminder of virtue, and to bring help to those who genuinely meditate them.
Indeed, when the son of Nun entered the land, seeing the arrangements of the
enemies, and all the kings of the Amorites having come together into war, he
himself, instead of arms and swords, having read the Deuteronomy into the ears
of all, and having reminded them of the phrases of the law, and having armed
the people with these, prevailed over those who made war. And king Josiah, the
book having been found, and having been read into the hearings of all, no
longer feared the enemies. And if ever there was war for the people, the ark
having the tablets of the law went before all, and they were sufficient with
these instead of every array for help, unless there was among those who
carried it and among the peoples a sin and hypocrisy that had already
prevailed. For there is need of faith and a genuine disposition, so that the
law may work together toward the things according to prayer.

I at least, the old man said, have heard also from prudent men, that of old in
=27.45= Israel, only reading the Scriptures, they drove out demons, and they
exposed the plots that came about from them toward human beings. Whence he
also said that those who leave these things aside, but compose for themselves
persuasive phrases from outside, and in these name themselves exorcists, are
worthy of all condemnation. For they rather play, and they furnish themselves
to those beings to be mocked; such things as the Jews, the sons of Sceva, have
suffered, having attempted themselves also to exorcize in this manner. For
hearing these things from such men the demons play; but they fear the phrases
of the saints, or they cannot even bear them. For in the phrases of the
Scriptures is the Lord, whom not being able to bear they cried: "I beg you, do
not torment me before the time"; for they were burned even only seeing the
Lord present. Thus also Paul charged the unclean spirits; thus also the demons
were subjected to the disciples. And upon Elisha the prophet the hand of the
Lord came, and he prophesied concerning the waters to the three kings, when
the singer sang at his command.

Thus also now, if anyone cares for those who suffer, let him say these things,
and he rather benefits the one who suffers, and he will show his own faith
true and firm; so that God, seeing this also, may furnish the complete healing
to those who ask. For the holy one, knowing this also, said in the ριηʹ psalm:
"I will meditate in your statutes, I will not forget your words"; and again:
"Your statutes were songs to me in the place of my sojourning." For in these
they also gained salvation, saying: "Unless your law is my meditation, then I
would have perished in my humiliation." Whence also Paul secured his own
disciple in these things, saying: "Meditate these things, be in these things,
so that your advance may become manifest." Meditating these things yourself
also, and meeting with understanding; thus with the psalms, you will be able,
being led by the Spirit, to grasp the mind in each. And you also will
zealously seek such a life as the holy men who spoke these things, being
God-borne, had.
