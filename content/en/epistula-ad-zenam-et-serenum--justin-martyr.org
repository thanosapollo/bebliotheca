If not, even as toward an unlearned man. For to a foolish man who has asked
for wisdom, wisdom will be counted as wisdom; but to him who in his own fancy
has seemed to himself to be prudent, much dishonor will be charged besides.
And by the Spirit he is called foolish who through rusticity has been carried
aside in his perception, so that the foolish may be the rustic, as also the
simple. And Paul also writes: Who has known the mind of the Lord, that he
should instruct Him? And again: But we have the mind of Christ. For he who
through want of reason says or does what he ought not, this man, having seemed
to be prudent, will be counted a fool.

One must humble oneself not by word only but also by works, that we may not be
charlatans and fawners, but meek and not flatterers. And it still befits the
man who lives rightly both to keep quiet and to be free of meddling, not being
angry nor keeping silence for the sake of strife, but considering everything
that is needed, whether it can well be said, and then scarcely speaking. Hymns
and psalms and songs and praise one must utter; not, as one who is at war with
him and condemning him, to follow some men in riddles, to grieve the neighbor
through psalmody, so that he may not even seem to be at variance, and somehow
wound him as if by art, thinking that he makes his contentiousness unaccused.
One must also listen to him who advises, not only teach. One must likewise be
persuaded by him who wishes the neighbor to become one who agrees with him.
One must suppose others to be prudent, and must not assign understanding to
oneself alone. One must not run risk by the opinion of conceit about oneself.
Nor must one despise him who instructs, but either, because he does not think
rightly, teach the things concerning himself, or even if he speaks truth, be
persuaded by him and follow. For a contentious man doubles the wrong; and when
he ought to change, he has been eager, as the fable says, to win the Cadmean
victory; and the cause is, that he may be vainglorious as if he were not even
sinning, and may rest upon the opinion of his dangerous disposition. For they
are ridiculous who cry out every day because they believe they prevail in some
matter; into whom Strife, having thrown the apple, whenever they think they
are at peace, throws them into confusion, busying herself that they should
talk about the things in the world that are thought worth differing over. And
some are mimic-like and wretched, who say they are inexperienced in nothing,
but claim to know now carpentry, now shoemaking, and every pursuit put before
them in season, and who are hostile toward him who checks their nonsense. And
vainglorious also are those who, for the praise that comes from men, take care
of their neighbors.

Great also is the danger among those who call upon the day of the Lord; for
one must either believe, that he may flee the judgment, or also forgive the
neighbor, so that the forgiveness of sins may come to himself from God as
well; but they, by the derangement of anger, bind contracts against
themselves. One must still also guard against the boastfulness of some, who,
when they ought to correct themselves by being persuaded by those who advise,
say very sluggishly: I do not even defend myself to you; keep this suspicion
which you have about me. For what is it to me to speak about those who are
pleased with themselves, whenever someone wishes to admonish them? Who say,
very much in the world's way: Neither do you save me, nor have you power to
condemn. And I know many who, because of a bad conscience and the uncleanness
of their mind, rejoice at the sins of their neighbors. One must also condemn
those who lie in that they are not harsh, and those who practice speaking
mildly, but are betrayed by their color and their eyes and the unnoticed
movement of head or brows or the clapping of hands; for it is impossible that
a man should not become his own accuser. And every faithful man, whenever he
thinks he is being mocked by someone, according to the understanding of him
who is so disposed toward him, outwits that man's wickedness by knowing the
disposition of his unkindness. In short, everyone who judges himself will be
outside of every fault; but of those who cry out, no one is sound-minded. And
he who is now pale, then red, and is varied by the constitution of the body,
this man like chameleons wanders about in the heat and the desert. One must
still also observe the dispositions of some in their practice of the readings;
for as soon as a man reads, the manner of his thinking also becomes clear to
sound critics. And qualities of souls are shown also from the life; for
through the practice concerning their conduct the underlying realities even of
things not appearing are known. One must also decline the eristic man who by
art puts himself forward, by not matching his contention, and so be defeated;
for whom it is better, for the sake of not running risk in being eager to win,
not to practice according to right reason.

One must make conversations that are of good conscience. And walks should take
place for the little body, not for some business, stitching together violent
contracts against the neighbor. And it is not out of place that we, jesting,
should wipe away the peevishness of those who are over-intelligent. For
because of the necessity according to nature, for the sake of wiping
themselves, it is the custom of some to withdraw pretentiously to certain
appointed places and to compose the end of their consideration against certain
men in the latrines. And as the comic father says he bears with his son living
prodigally, and even if he smells the perfume, says he does not smell it,
trying to cover his sin with a stench, so I also advise those who have been
too harshly trained to show the more prudent men that they neither understand
what they do nor know what they say. But he who thinks himself prudent in his
own eyes knocks against admonitions and holds contrary opinions and becomes an
adversary of him who advises and studies in himself what is human and
unbelieving. For this reason let us present ourselves unassailable, not doing
only what seems good to ourselves but also what is of the many, that they may
be saved. And as for being angry, it is good if never; for such a form is of
madness; but if ever it also presses, one must use anger together toward the
healing of the neighbor. For he who occupies himself contrary to reason hates
first himself, then also the neighbor, disturbing himself and paining that
man. For it is written that Everyone who is angry with his brother without
cause shall be liable to the judgment. And as for the impulse, a man himself,
when he is by himself, is able to learn it. And already we know some who
through admonition toward their neighbors condemn themselves; for what a man
teaches the other, this let him do first himself, lest he unwittingly through
the neighbor make the sentence against himself.

One must therefore say to the well-disposed man toward himself, whenever he
applies his mind to the sins of certain men: Am I perhaps also such?

And wine should be drunk neither every day unto drunkenness, nor sought as
water is. For both are works of God; but water is necessary, while wine has
come to be for the help of the body. But he who first has his tongue bound,
and shines as with fire from the eyes, and trembles also in his steps and is
without sense, takes a deadly drug, not making the iron into a sickle or into
something of the things that work the earth, but fashioning it into a sword or
a spear and recasting the economy of God. For the wine-drinker, when he ought
in winter to drink because of the cold until he does not shiver, and in summer
for the healing of the inward parts, misusing it talks nonsense and, like dogs
or swine, boiling over with the flood of the remedy, behaves unseemly. And we
must not, as if living prodigally, misuse the works of God, dragging thirst
into drunkenness, but partake of drink for the sake of not thirsting; and as
many as gulp the wine as those in fever burning with heat drink, will all but
die at once through incontinence.

One must also decline daintiness, partaking of a few things and of the
necessary. For as a sick body needs many and countless drugs, so also the soul
that is unfortunate through itself wants to be filled almost even with hides.
And if someone asks the cause, he invents a bodily passion. To whom one must
say: Does not every practice strengthened by the dogma of thought have a
demonstration easy to defend, so that even as far as an adulterer and a
murderer and a runaway much defense is found, that what is done by them is
done well? And he who has spoken to him who asks the cause of the evil and
answers a dogma has uttered it very marvelously. Foods, then, one must use
unto not being hungry. But he who is not such wishes, as far as it is in him,
to walk about a hundred-handed and fifty-headed, seeking three-headed and
six-handed Geryons, having practiced to be gluttonous with many hands and more
mouths.

Wealth one must in no way praise, and poverty one must not revile. For the one
is strengthened from matter already convicted, and the end of matter is
corruption and worms and maggots and fire and falls and robbers and a tyrant;
but the other has been separated from the present age, and seeks only what is
true, distracted by no temporary thing. And these things I have set down for
you in writing, wishing to admonish the untroubled state, and hastening to fit
you toward what is perfect and virtuous. For he who is not such runs outside
the rule given to him and through disorder becomes disqualified from the
contest; and even if he very miserably obtains the help from the noble
athletes, having enjoyed food or drink or clothing, nevertheless he is no less
uncrowned.

And those who have driven into the beloved city of the Father will partake of
an everlasting prize, but for the others it will turn out, through the food
here, not to partake of the enjoyment according to truth.

Women one must honor, taking care lest we perhaps stumble. And one must
approach them soundly and not inquisitively, fearing

the danger of company with them, and knowing beforehand both their
changeableness and the conscience toward that for which they think they have
come to be. And before all one must guard against their comic practices in
secret, which, being done hiddenly, are condemned even by themselves, but take
place toward swallowing the neighbors. It happens, then, that some, stealing
themselves, because they do not dare to beautify their faces openly, do this
by art; for stooping into water or oil they judge themselves, whether they
might be able to war against temperance. But another manner also of such
plotting is practiced by them; for secretly touching the head of the locks,
stealing as it were the neighbors, they arrange the hair inquisitively, and
they touch the cheeks and the beard with the tips of the fingers. And they
also look about themselves while the head is unmoved. One must therefore
converse with such women more stoutly, cutting off their spreading branches;
for as soon as they are caught they nod down, and the fruit is eaten by the
foxes that den in the earth. For this reason, according to the prophet, one
must goad the ass and whip the horse, that the sluggish may be awakened, and
he who throws off the bit may be struck by admonitions because of being
unbridled. So that, according to Jeremiah, we may not fit ourselves as
stallions mad after females, nor neigh toward the wife of the neighbor.

And every woman is a sister, but wife of the neighbor has been said, that is,
of Jesus Christ our Savior (whom, after the love toward God and Father of all,
because He is his neighbor, one must honor and love), as Paul: I betrothed, he
says, you all to one husband, to present a pure virgin to Christ. And the
neighboring wife is also flesh in us, toward which one must not neigh; and he
also neighs who has not yet accomplished the desire, the appetite steaming up
in him, even if he does not take the end.

One must also attend to little children, because of such is the kingdom of the
heavens. And the sick one must not neglect, nor say, I have not learned to
serve. For he who puts forward delicacy or the unaccustomed, let him know that
he himself will suffer the same things; and whenever he suffers something of
this kind, let him blame the self-condemnation of his own mind, the same
things happening to him which he also inflicted. Nor, then, must the negligent
be neglected. For God is the one who judges. And he who wishes to be well
treated, let him treasure up through well-doing the benefaction toward himself
from the neighbor. And it is not right to be ashamed lest someone perhaps,
because of manual work, suppose us wretched and helpless, but one must only
guard lest someone slander those who claim to know God for wickedness and an
evil life.

In short, the sorrow of the world works death for those held by it, but the
sorrow according to God begets the mind of life. And before all let us know
that disease also is laid up for our little flesh, and loss and want, and He
who will grant favor is near. And for this reason one must practice beforehand
what will come about, that when it happens it may not furnish wonder and panic
to our hearts. For such a man will say to the passion that has come upon him:
I knew that you were going to come; I did not miss your expectation; I knew
beforehand your approach. And if a man is such, he will stand and will not
fall; but to be disturbed at the sudden, or to cry out, and often also to
wail, is of a very wretched mind.

One must therefore attend to virtue only, and approach those who recount the
things concerning it; but to those in whom the practice of the things said is
not present, having said farewell because of the ambush, let us seek those who
campaign with us. For he who practices saying, I am not a ruler and a judge,
but doing nothing good, this man, as if held by many tyrants, is unaware that
he is enslaved to sin and is running away from those who hasten to walk in the
way. Blessed, then, is he who is pure in heart; for by this man alone is it
possible for the divine to be seen. And the faithful man must be far separated
from envy and malice; and he must pray every day both to be found rich in word
and powerful in deed. For we are members of one another. For this reason let
us suffer with him who suffers, and rejoice with him who is glorified,
guarding ourselves from those who are not belonging to the legislation of
Christ. These things, brethren, advising you to do, I have also still urged
the rest
