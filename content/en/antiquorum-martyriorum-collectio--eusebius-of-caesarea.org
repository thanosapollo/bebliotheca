6. When the edict against the Christians was posted in Nicomedia under
Diocletian and Maximian, a man tore it down with both hands. =20.1529=
Having done this, he is at once seized, and after suffering a great deal,
as was to be expected for one arrested for so great a deed, he departed
this present life by martyrdom. Another at the same time, one of the
imperial servants, named Peter, proved in his confession to be in truth
more solid than a rock. For when he was ordered to sacrifice he stood
firm; he was raised aloft naked on a wooden frame, and his body was so
torn by scourges that the bones already showed, the flesh having failed.
And when even while suffering these things he remained unshaken, they
mixed vinegar with salt and poured it over the putrefying parts of his
body. And when he was still above even these pains, he is laid upon a
red-hot gridiron. And so, as the fire little by little consumed what
remained of his flesh, he gave up his soul victorious in the midst of
those very tortures. Dorotheus and Gorgonius, who also belonged to the
imperial household, together with many others, after contests of many
kinds exchanged this life by the noose and carried off the prizes of the
godly victory. At the same time Anthimus, who presided over the church of
Nicomedia, had his head cut off for his witness to Christ; and to him is
added a throng of martyrs almost beyond number. For a fire having been
kindled in the palace at Nicomedia in those very days, a report was spread
that the conflagration had been attempted by Christians, and by imperial
command they were piled together: some of the godly there were slaughtered
with swords, some were given over to the fire, others were thrown into the
depths of the sea; and being perfected in every way, they were not even
after death thought worthy of the customary burial. But their remains
being burned with fire, the dust was scattered into the air, or thrown
into the sea. And the same things were done against the Christians in
every province as well; but the persecution was at its height especially
in Phoenicia and Syria, in Egypt and the Thebaid, and in Africa and among
the nation of the Moors. Knowing it to be impracticable and altogether
impossible to list by name those who contended honorably in such
provinces, and to number their multitude and the various manners of their
perfection, I shall mention a few of the most notable. Lucian, a presbyter
from Antioch, excellent throughout his whole life and most distinguished
in letters, was brought to Nicomedia while the emperor was present and
condemned to inhabit a prison; and remaining there, starved for want of
necessities, he departed this life. This man, then, was from Antioch in
Coele Syria. Of the

of the martyrs in Phoenicia, Tyrannion, bishop of the church of Tyre, was
foremost, and Zenobius, a presbyter of the church of Sidon, and also
Silvanus, bishop of the churches around Emesa (this last, with others,
became food for wild beasts at Emesa itself). And both of those just named
glorified the Word of God unto death at Antioch: the bishop being given
over to the depths of the sea, and Zenobius, the best of physicians, dying
steadfastly under the tortures applied to his sides. Among the martyrs in
Palestine, Silvanus, bishop =20.1532= of Gaza, at the copper mines in
Phoenicia, together with others, one short of forty in number, had his
head cut off. And there also the Egyptians Peleus and Nilus, bishops, with
others endured death by fire. And Pamphilus too at that time, being a
presbyter of Caesarea in Palestine, was adorned with a divine martyrdom,
having contended splendidly. Among those who were perfected with
distinction at Alexandria and throughout all Egypt and the Thebaid, first
was Peter, bishop of Alexandria itself, and of the presbyters with him
Faustus, Dius, and Ammonius, perfect martyrs of Christ; also Phileas,
Hesychius, Pachymius, and Theodore, bishops of the churches in Egypt. At
the same time the soldiers seized a certain small town in Phrygia, because
all who dwelt in it confessed themselves Christians with one accord, and
setting fire to it they burned them together with women and infants, as
they called upon Christ. And another man who had attained Roman rank,
Adauctus by name, of a distinguished Italian family, counted worthy of the
first honor before the emperors, then distinguished himself by an
unshaken and unswerving confession of Christ, and was adorned with the
crown of martyrdom, having finished the contest nobly. And a certain holy
and wonderful woman, celebrated among all the people of Antioch for
wealth and birth and reputation, who had reared a pair of distinguished
virgin daughters, when much envy was being stirred up concerning them,
summoned them, who were living in hiding in a foreign land, back to
Antioch. But they were already enclosed within the nets of the soldiers.
Seeing herself and her daughters in a desperate plight, and reckoning the
horrors that come from men, and not enduring even to hear with the tips of
her ears the threat of fornication, and declaring that to be enslaved to
demons was worse than every death and every destruction, she proposed to
herself and to her daughters one deliverance from all these things: flight
to the Lord. And having agreed together in this purpose, and having
arranged their bodies decently, they came into the middle of the road and
threw themselves into a river that flowed beside it. These, then, did this
to themselves. But another pair of virgins at Antioch itself, in all
things godly and truly sisters, of distinguished family, of a splendid
life, young in years, fair in body, modest in soul, pious in manner,
admirable in zeal, the servants of the demons delivered to the depths of
the sea. Why should I now mention the rest by name, and number the
multitude of the men, and depict the manifold outrages of the wondrous
martyrs of Christ, some slain with axes, as happened to those in Arabia,
others having their legs broken, as befell those in Cappadocia; and some
hung head-downward from the feet on high, and with a slow fire kindled
beneath them, suffocated by the smoke that was sent up from the burning
matter, as was inflicted on those in Mesopotamia =20.1533=; and some
having noses and ears and hands cut off, and the remaining limbs and parts
of the body butchered, as was the case at Alexandria? What need is there
to rekindle the memory of those at Antioch, roasted on grids of fire not
unto death but for a long punishment, while others more quickly put their
right hand into the fire than they would touch the accursed sacrifice?
Some of these, fleeing the trial before they were caught and came into the
hands of their plotters, threw themselves down from high houses, counting
death a prize snatched from the wickedness of the impious. And others in
Pontus suffered things terrible to hear: their fingers were pierced with
sharp reeds from the tips of the nails of both hands, and others had
molten lead, boiling and fiery in its substance, poured down their backs,
and the most vital parts of the body were roasted; and others endured,
through the secret limbs and parts, shameful and pitiless sufferings not
even to be spoken in words. But the last of the calamities came when at
last the rulers in every province had given up at the excess of evils, and
weary of killing they turned to what they thought kind and humane: then it
was ordered that eyes be gouged out and that one of the two legs be
maimed. For these, then, were their humane measures, and of the
