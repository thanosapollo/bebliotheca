* Chapter 1

:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Some say Avidius Cassius came from the family of the Cassii, though
through his mother. Avidius Severus, a new man, begot him. That man had
commanded the ranks and afterward reached the highest offices.
Quadratus mentions him in his histories, and with weight, when he
asserts that he was a man of the first rank, necessary to the republic,
and very powerful with Marcus himself. For they say he already died by
a stroke of fate while Marcus was emperor.

This Cassius then, of the family, as we have said, of the Cassii who
had conspired in the senate-house against Gaius Julius, secretly hated
the principate and could not bear the imperial name. He used to say
that the name of empire was the heavier because it could not be taken
from the republic except by another emperor. Finally they say he tried
even in boyhood to wrest the principate from Pius as well, but through
his father, a holy and grave man, the passion for tyranny stayed
hidden. Yet the generals always held him suspect.

A letter of Verus himself, however, shows that he had laid a plot
against Verus, which I have inserted. From the letter of Verus:
/Avidius Cassius is greedy for empire, as it seems both to me and as it
already became known under my grandfather, your father. I would have
you order him watched. All our affairs displease him. He gathers no
small wealth. He laughs at our letters. He calls you a philosophizing
old woman, me a luxurious buffoon. See what must be done. I do not hate
the man, but see that you do not counsel poorly for yourself and your
children, when among men under arms you have such a one as the soldiers
gladly hear and gladly see./

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

But we shall briefly set out the man's nature and manners. For people
cannot know much more about those whose life no one dares even to set
in a fair light, because of those who crushed them. We shall add,
however, how he came to empire, how he was killed, and where he was
defeated. For I have proposed, Diocletian Augustus, to commit to
letters all who have held the name of emperors, whether with just cause
or unjust, that you might know all who have worn the purple as Augusti.

He was of such manners that he sometimes seemed harsh and rough, at
other times mild and gentle, often religious, at other times a scorner
of sacred things, greedy of wine and likewise abstinent, eager for food
and able to go without it, desirous of Venus and a lover of chastity.
Some even called him Catiline, and he himself rejoiced to be so named,
adding that he would be a Sergius if he had killed the writer of
dialogues. By this name he meant Antoninus, who so shone in philosophy
that, when he was about to go to the Marcomannic war, and all feared
that something fatal might come of it, they asked him, not in flattery
but in earnest, to set forth the precepts of philosophy. He did not
fear, but he discoursed for three days through a course of exhortation.
Avidius Cassius was besides tenacious of military discipline, and one
who wished to be called Marius.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

Since we have begun to speak of his severity, many tokens of his
cruelty rather than of his severity are extant. First, he crucified
soldiers who had taken anything from provincials by force, in those
very places in which they had sinned. He was also the first to invent
this kind of punishment: he would set up a huge pole of a hundred and
eighty feet and bind the condemned from the top all the way to the
bottom, and at the bottom set a fire, and, some being burned, kill
others by smoke, by torment, and even by fear. The same man used to
order men sunk ten at a time, chained, in a running river or in the
sea. The same cut off the hands of many deserters, and of others he cut
the legs and the hamstrings, saying that a living wretch who had done
crime was a greater example than one killed. When he was leading the
army, an auxiliary force, without his knowledge and at the instance of
their centurions, had killed three thousand Sarmatians who were acting
too carelessly on the banks of the Danube, and had returned to him with
huge booty. The centurions hoped for a reward because with a very small
band they had killed so great a number of the enemy while the tribunes
were sluggish and unaware. He ordered them seized and crucified and
given a slave's punishment, of which there was no precedent, saying
that it might have been an ambush, and that the reverence due to the
Roman empire would perish. And when a huge sedition had arisen in the
army, he came forward naked, covered only with a camp-kilt, and said,
/Strike,/
he said,
/me, if you dare, and add a crime to your ruined discipline./
Then, when all grew still, he came to be feared, because he himself did
not fear. This so added to Roman discipline, and so struck terror into
the barbarians, that they asked of Antoninus, though he was absent, a
peace of a hundred years, since they had seen that even those who had
won contrary to what was right were condemned by the Roman general's
judgment.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Aemilius Parthenianus, who has handed down to history those who aimed
at tyranny even from the ancients, records many grave things this man
did against the license of the soldiers. For he both beat with rods in
the forum and in the midst of the camp struck with the axe those who so
deserved, and cut off the hands of many, and besides bacon, hard
biscuit, and vinegar he forbade a soldier on campaign to carry
anything, and if he found any other luxury he punished it with no light
penalty. A letter of the deified Marcus to his prefect of this kind is
still extant concerning this man:
/I have given Avidius Cassius the Syrian legions, dissolving in luxury
and living in the manners of Daphne, which Caesonius Vectilianus wrote
that he had found all taking hot baths, and I think I have not erred,
if indeed you too know Cassius, a man of Cassian severity and
discipline. For soldiers cannot be ruled except by the old discipline.
You know the verse spoken by a good poet and repeated by all: 'The
Roman state stands by ancient manners and by men.' Do you only see to
it that supplies are present in abundance for the legions, which, if I
know Avidius well, I know will not be wasted./
The prefect to Marcus:
/You have counseled well, my lord, in that you have set Cassius over
the Syrian legions. For nothing is so expedient as a rather severe man
for soldiers of Greek manners. He will surely shake out all the hot
baths, all the flowers from the soldier's head, neck, and bosom. The
whole military commissariat is ready, and nothing is wanting under a
good general, for not much is either asked or spent./

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

The judgment they had formed of him did not prove false. For at once he
ordered it proclaimed at the standards and posted a notice on the
walls, that if anyone were found in uniform at Daphne, he should return
ungirt. He always inspected the soldiers' arms on the seventh day,
their clothes also and shoes and greaves. He removed all dainties from
the camp and ordered them to pass the winter under skins unless they
corrected their manners, and they would have done so, had they not
lived more honorably. The exercise of the seventh day was of all the
soldiers, so that they both shot arrows and drilled with weapons. For
he used to say that it was a wretched thing, when athletes, hunters,
and gladiators were exercised, that soldiers should not be exercised,
for whom the labor would be less if it were habitual.

Therefore, with discipline corrected, he conducted affairs most
excellently in Armenia and in Arabia and in Egypt, and all the eastern
peoples loved him, especially the people of Antioch, who even consented
to his rule, as Marius Maximus shows in the life of the deified Marcus.
For when the Bucoli soldiers were doing many grave things throughout
Egypt, this man beat them back, as likewise Marius Maximus relates in
that book which he published second on the life of Marcus Antoninus.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

This man called himself emperor in the East, as some say, because
Faustina wished it. She already distrusted Marcus's health and feared
that she alone could not protect her infant sons, and that someone
would arise who, having seized the imperial post, would do away with
the infants. Others, however, say that Cassius used this device with
the soldiers and provincials against their love of Marcus, that he
might get their consent, by saying that Marcus had met his last day.
For they even say he called him divine, that he might soften the
longing for him.

When he had gone forth in an imperial spirit, he at once made prefect
of the praetorian guard the man who had fitted the royal ornaments on
him. The army itself killed that man against Antoninus's will. It also
killed Maecianus, to whom Alexandria had been entrusted and who had
consented in hope of sharing with Cassius, against Antoninus's will and
without his knowledge.

Yet Antoninus was not gravely angry when he learned of the rebellion,
nor did he rage against his children or his kin. The senate named him
an enemy and proscribed his goods. Antoninus would not have these
gathered into the privy purse. Wherefore, the senate directing it, they
were brought into the public treasury. Rome was not free of terror when
some said that Avidius Cassius, while Antoninus was absent (who was
uniquely loved except by the pleasure-seekers), would come to Rome and
plunder the city like a tyrant, especially because of the senators who
had judged him an enemy with his goods proscribed. And Antoninus's love
shone forth most in this, that by the consent of all except the people
of Antioch Avidius was slain. Indeed he did not order him killed, but
suffered it, although it was clear among all that if it had been in his
power

He then enumerated all the princes who had been killed as having had
causes for which they deserved to be killed, and that no good man was
easily either conquered by a tyrant or killed, saying that Nero had
deserved it, that Caligula had been bound to it, that Otho and
Vitellius had not even wished to rule. Even concerning Galba he felt
the like, saying that in an emperor avarice is the bitterest evil.
Finally neither Augustus, nor Trajan, nor Hadrian, nor his own father
could have been overcome by rebels, although there had been many, and
they had been put down either against their will or without their
knowledge. Antoninus himself, however, asked of the senate that it not
deal harshly with those privy to the defection, at that very time when
he asked that no senator in his times be put to death, which won him
the greatest love. Finally, after very few centurions had been
punished, he ordered the banished recalled.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

He pardoned the people of Antioch,3 who 4 had consented to Avidius
Cassius, both these and other cities that had helped him, although at
first he had been gravely angry at the Antiochenes and had taken from
them their games and many other ornaments of the city, which he
afterward restored.
Antoninus Marcus granted the sons of Avidius Cassius a middle share of
their father's estate, so that he also honored his daughters with gold,
silver, and gems. For he gave both to Alexandria, Cassius's daughter,
and to his son-in-law Druncianus free leave to go wherever they wished,
and they lived not as a tyrant's pledges but as of senatorial rank in
the highest security, since he had even forbidden that the fortune of
their own house be thrown at them in a lawsuit, some who had been
insolent toward them being convicted of outrage, and these he commended
to his aunt's husband.

If anyone, however, desires to know this whole history, let him read
the second book of Marius Maximus on the life of Marcus, in which that
writer tells those things which Marcus alone did after Verus was
already dead. For then Cassius rebelled, as a letter sent to Faustina
proves, of which this is a copy:
/Verus had written me the truth about Avidius, that he desired to rule.
For I think you have heard what Verus's couriers were reporting of him.
Come therefore to the Alban villa, that we may treat of everything, the
gods willing, fearing nothing./
From this, however, it appears that Faustina did not know these things,
although Marius, wishing to defame her, says that Cassius had taken the
empire with her knowledge. For there is also extant a letter of hers to
her husband, in which she urges Marcus to punish him gravely. Copy of
Faustina's letter to Marcus:
/I myself will come to the Alban villa tomorrow, as you bid, presently.
Yet already I urge that, if you love your children, you pursue those
rebels most sharply. For the generals and soldiers have grown badly
used to this, who, unless they are crushed, will crush./
