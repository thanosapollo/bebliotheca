Now we love you still more, being so passionately devoted to Plato, whom I
would say I honor, in Homer's phrase, equal to my own head. For even if all
men are paltry beside him, still each man is most precious to himself, they
say. And yet what am I saying? I do not know if you believe me; but I will
speak the truth. If you had not said that he is close to us, you would not
have delighted me as you did by showing that you have already been seized by
him; so dear is the man to me, and beyond friends. Something of the same kind
happened to me also concerning Demosthenes, and I will tell it to you as well.
A man of the Roman senate, a Libyan by ancestry, Maximus by name, worthy of
whatever you please, and those skilled in these matters say he is among the
foremost orators of the Romans, this man was extraordinarily devoted to the
speeches of Demosthenes and, I know not how, was captured by these of ours as
well; and so, as one who both frequented the temple of Asclepius with me and
fell short in no love of what is fine, he was with me every day in the

speeches; so that I even went to some extra trouble toward him because of such
a thing. For I happened to have composed a certain speech as a third, Against
Leptines, from a beginning of this kind: I had Demosthenes' speech in my
hands, and when I came to the heads of argument which he was cutting off in
advance of what Leptines was going to say, I set myself apart from Demosthenes
and considered something of my own. And when I found two and three of the
useful points, then again I went on to something else. And as I tried, the
same thing kept happening. And so from the hem I finished the cloak, or if you
like, the lion from the claw. This speech, then, I attempted to show him, and
as I was about to show it I said beforehand that he would find things invented
against Leptines together with the Against Leptines. May I not do wrong, I
said. And he, by Zeus, nobly and as every man of letters would have loved and
admired, said, Do you know that I sit on Demosthenes' side? And I said, Then
you tend the same flock as I, so that if he should beat me, the man crowned is
no enemy, but the one for whom you and I both strive. And when he bade me show
the books in turn and the matter was in earnest on every side, In this way, I
said, I think the same as you about Demosthenes, and I hold the same as you,
that it is not easy to surpass that man. But if some one of the gods should
come to my aid as well, and many are the wonders at Asclepius's, you have not
shown, I said, the prizes, that I might contend more eagerly. For if I fail,
it is madness and to understand nothing about speeches; but if I should win, I
said, what will be left for me? And he was pleased to hear it, just as I was
pleased to hear from him that he stood with Demosthenes, and he gave leave to
show the speech. And how he was then disposed about what followed he himself
would know, and you too if you should ever meet the man and mention it. So, to
speak generally of the men of old, and of these in particular above

to me, it does not seem to me that a middle man would arise, nor that the one
would pass the other by at the other's side, for goodwill and for thinking the
same about the whole. Well then.

Come then, by Friendship's Zeus himself, since Plato too honors the truth
everywhere and takes conversations in speeches as a starting point of true
friendship, let us see more clearly what it was that offended you in the
speeches which some say we have composed against him. For I was trying
everywhere to show him voting with me. For if we have talked some nonsense,
there is no need of Stesichorus's palinode, but a sponge will heal it; or if
there is also something better in the speeches than that this prize should
befit them. And you, if it does not seem so to you, praise no more, but
forgive all the same, if you do not even think them worthy of honor,

but of this at least you would think them worthy. You said, as I hear, that
you were displeased because there was in the speeches mention of his journey
to Sicily, and that in these we seemed to be attacking Plato's life; whereas
this ought to have been somewhere apart, and the argument should have been
examined, and these are charges not only of a lover but of one who is sound
about speeches. But see that you do not accuse me when I am not to blame. For
I was not, I suppose, speaking ill of Plato, as if judging him in some court,
nor was I blaming the journeys abroad so that he might be thought worse, but
even in these I claimed they testified for me and for rhetoric. For it is
clear that if being wronged were worth some small thing, or taking justice
when wronged, no one would have stirred him to sail so great a sea and go to a
tyrant for Dion's affairs, nor, having returned from there, to join Dion in
everything, both when he wished to come home and to take justice for what he
had suffered. But as it is, he testifies in deed that it is not of a paltry
nature nor of ordinary power not to allow those who attempt to do wrong. And
that these things are true, there are those who have heard the speeches, I
suppose, and there are the speeches. And the rest I leave aside, with how much
reverence and gentleness we have discussed these very things, bringing in only
so much force as neither slandered him and made my argument stronger, but we
inserted, as it were, a parabasis, a place reserved for him. If you heard
this, remember it; if you did not hear it, do not prejudge. Come then, let me
furnish you the very words from the book, as those who contend in the courts
do. From the book. /I wish to make a small distinction, lest anyone think at
all that I am accusing Plato with these speeches, or speaking ill with a
pretext. For I myself make no charge, I suppose, if Plato thought he must go
abroad anywhere and in any way, nor do I think anyone else has sense if he
brings it forward, nor do I busy myself at all with anything outside what
belongs to the argument, [so that if this too must be said/

meant. And again, do not say I speak more bitterly than I should. For I am not
the cause of any of these things, but one had to accept the challenge. And
what was that? To dare to listen, as in a court, equal and common to both.

But it seems I was right in looking ahead about these things and saying
beforehand that unless someone hears the whole speech through, he will not
only not honor what is fitting, but will not even know anything of the matter.
For this reason I thought it right that whoever is going to see the whole
contest and cast a straight and just vote should listen to the end. For
neither, my excellent man, do the judges decide in this way and go away after
hearing some proem or narrative, nor again, leaving these aside, do they come
together for some part of the probabilities, or only so much as to hear one
testimony or the recapitulation of what has been said, but having heard from
beginning to end they thus cast their vote; and this I think is of use to them
as well. By the gods of the oaths, neither when you hold the Olympia do you
preside over the contest in this way, having heard all of one of the
competitors, and of another only so much as the entrance, or of some a bit in
the middle, or of those at the end. Why then do I say this? Because having met
with some small part of the speech you judged the whole from that, and you did
not hear the proem nor the whole statement of the case, which one might think
no less worthy than the whole contest, to speak of it as a part. For the whole
character would be more here. For in those very parts you would have known
that we were coming in more to praise the man than to pull him down. We
rendered him all reverence and honor, so that if he himself were going to
speak against himself, he would not, I think, have spared himself more.

And I think if even a son of his were going to speak against him, he would not
have made a greater plea, nor would he have adorned him more conspicuously
before the Greeks. And you, left out of these, are like the man who judges the
story before hearing both sides. And yet another man, having a continuous
argument, would have forgotten even himself, let alone his opponent, and
showing the matter at hand would have sought nothing more. But I, as if
fearing that he might be thought inferior in these things, spend the whole
speech striving for his part; and though the proof is so continuous and dense,
let it be allowed to say, the figure of the speeches toward the man is no less
admirable, because I did not lay the foundation in one way and bring on the
rest in another, but the regard for the man follows the argument everywhere,
and we show that while contradicting what is necessary we adorn him from
abundance. And yet I would have wished him, having come here, to speak against
me thus, but perhaps he would have cared less for such a thing.

Consider then also the things at the end of the speeches, how they follow the
proem: on the one hand, discussing the true orators, And Plato, we say, where
in the chorus will he take his stand? We are ready to rank him in the fairest
place, in words of some such kind, for I do not remember the more exact
wording; on the other hand, having mentioned certain other little men, and
then toward Plato, the father and teacher of the orators, thus I think we have
spoken what follows, and so much at least there is, not of one exalting his
life and his speeches as far as possible, and all this I examine closely and
go through for the sake of the truth, says Demosthenes, since as for Pindar's
saying, many sing it: /one must do everything to obscure the
enemy./ And by enemy he means, I suppose, the opponent. And almost in the
ball-fights and in the gymnastic contests themselves, and I will add the
horse-races too, we see those who contend for victory not following the wishes
of their opponents,

all the Muses, if we added this only, the all, who would have sufficed for
Plato to such a degree? Do not think this is a word otherwise towed along, as
in Homer there is /and all nine Muses,/ but if one may speak about one's own,
the addition is of unspeakable amount. By Zeus, someone might say. But is it
only in these things that he is more officious than we, and in the rest the
man is full of caution? Does he not attach a hiccup to Aristophanes, nothing
to the argument, or rather a thing altogether graceless and out of tune with
the speeches about love, and he is so far from saying what is necessary or the
truth that he has falsified the whole gathering, I suppose, and fabricates the
dead as living. And you may know it from the things said themselves. For the
Mantineans, whom he mentions in the speech, saying they were split up in the
time of the Arcadians under archon so-and-so, but Socrates had died so much
and so much before, and Aristophanes hiccups for him and says these things in
Socrates' presence. Then he is allowed to fabricate some things that fit
nowhere, even if they are clearly outside the argument, and shall someone bear
a grudge against us if we brought into the open something of what was done by
him?
