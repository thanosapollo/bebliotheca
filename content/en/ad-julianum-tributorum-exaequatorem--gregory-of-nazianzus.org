ORATION XIX.
On the discourses, and to Julian the equalizer.
A. What is the tyranny by which from love we are always tyrannized? What is my
wisdom and knowledge, on account of which at every festival we are warred
upon? For I find none, going through and directing myself from every side.
Except this one thing at least I am conscious of in myself, =35.1045= and
perhaps it is not paltry, even if some name it simplicity. I wished at every
time to be dead to the life, and to live the life hidden in Christ, and to
become some great merchant, having bought the precious pearl of all that I
have, and having given in exchange the things that flow and are swept away for
the things that stand and are heavenly; which indeed is the greatest and most
secure of businesses for those who have sense; but if not, yet at least to
endure this, to yield the thrones to those who wish them, and myself through
life to be a child and a disciple, until I wash off the salty with the
drinkable of words. Let this then be one thing of my philosophy, or of my
simplicity, and the first; and second, which is also the greatest.
B. Since by word I was not able to hold back the word of the many, and the
impulse and eagerness now holding all, toward teaching and speaking the things
of the Spirit without the Spirit; I came upon another road, as I persuade
myself, both better and less laborious, to educate all toward quiet by the
archetype of silence; if they think something great concerning us, being
ashamed of the superiority; but if small and as much as is worthy, measuring
themselves with the equal in honor. This is the reason of my silence; this is
the mystery of our endurance.
C. But what am I to suffer? One from this side, another from that, drag us
apart and tear us, and they judge concerning my labors, as if exacting the
word inexorably as some other debt, and they love me more than I myself; and
all are wiser than I, knowing more than I the season of word or of silence;
and they say they will not let go, striking us with reproaches as iron a
fire-stone, until from a small spark they kindle the torch of words. And
already some of them even promise better things, and they set forth great
prizes for the words; first that they will do good to themselves, having given
to God and to us to bear fruit for the word; then also all these here, through
the registration, my lot, if indeed the paternal is mine, my flock, whom I
would greatly wrong, not being eager to benefit in every way. =35.1048= And
the fairest thing is that, what those who have labored much ought to have
accomplished by words, this they contribute on behalf of the words themselves.
O the fair rivalry, in which they contend to defeat me! O the praiseworthy
contribution! You see how great is the work of my silence; it has made the
words themselves more longed-for to you. You see the fruit of our lack of
glory. Would that there might be even of the words so great a benefit as of
the silence.
D. Therefore since these things seem good, and you have defeated the
undefeated, and you have set up a trophy against my philosophy, come, let me
utter something toward you, better than the silence. I will utter therefore,
not something soft and postponed, nor of the harmony sweetest to the many (for
I would not well repay my lovers, conversing thus), but even very manly and
intense, and by which perhaps you would become better, having been transferred
from the flesh to the spirit, and having been raised sufficiently in
understanding. Sons of men, how long will you be heavy-hearted, (for I will
make a prelude toward you from the most loud-voiced David)? Why do you love
vanity, and seek falsehood, taking the life here, and the luxury, and the
little glory, and the humble power, and the lying prosperity, as something
great? Which are not of those who have them more than of those who hoped, nor
of these more than of those who did not even expect; like dust under a
whirlwind being tossed and thrown from some to others at other times, or like
smoke flowing away, and as a dream playing, and as a shadow not being held;
neither when absent hard to hope for to those who have not acquired them, nor
when present faithful to those who have them.
E. Will we not look up to heaven above? Will we not sober up? Will we not take
the rheum from the eyes? Will we not know what is the true wealth, and what is
the real splendor, and where the dignity that does not fall through? What is
the boundless blessedness, and where the unshaken good, neither being
transferred nor being plotted against? Will we not acquire these things with
many sweats and labors, if it so happens? No; if one must luxury from here,
will we not luxury in the hopes? Will we not know the holy martyrs, both all
the others who have occupied the whole inhabited world, as some common bonds,
and those of the present festival? For what did they eagerly undergo wounds,
and bonds, and tortures, and threat of fire, and edge of swords, and =35.1049=
wildness of beasts, and darkness, and famine, and pits, and seizures of goods,
and losses of limbs, and deaths at last, and all things, as if competing in
bodies not their own? That they might become what, and what they might
inherit, is clear to all, even if we do not say. Will we not, with the same
hope, under the same umpire and president of the games, array ourselves
against the same tyrant, the bitter persecutor of souls both then and now, the
invisible enemy and adversary? Will we not be manly similarly as in this
common theater of the world, even if not at the peak of danger that has also a
swift deliverance, yet at least in the contests and wrestlings of each day,
that we may be deemed worthy of the same crowns, or as near as possible?
F. I for my part exhort every one, both man and woman, and old man and young,
and townsman and rustic, private person and ruler, rich and poor (for the same
contest calls all), to strip for this eagerly, and not to be soft, nor to
delay, nor to let go the season, which it is impossible to hit upon again. For
the present is of work, but the future of recompense. Rise, let us go from
here, you heard the Savior saying, not more leading the then disciples away
from the place of Judaea, but also removing all those later from here, and
drawing them to himself being lifted up, as the promise. Let us follow a good
Master; let us flee the worldly desires, let us flee the deceiving world and
world-ruler, let us become purely of the one who made us, let us honor the
image, let us reverence the calling, let us change the life. Why do we make
ourselves humble, having become high? Why do we remain in the things seen?
G. Let each, whatever he is able, bear fruit to God at every time, and in
every form both of lives and of circumstances, according to the measure of the
power present to him, according to the gift given to him; that by all measures
of virtue we may fill all the dwellings from there, having reaped so much as
we sowed, or rather having stored in the divine winepresses so much as we
farmed. Let one contribute, one money, =35.1052= another having nothing; one
being eager, another receiving the one who is eager; one a praiseworthy deed,
another a well-aimed contemplation; one a timely word, another a reasonable
silence; one an unfailing teaching and a life not opposing, another an
obedient and well-disposed hearing; one a pure virginity and cutting off the
world entirely, another an honorable marriage and not wholly separating from
God; one an unvaunting fast, another an enjoyment not unchaste; another the
undistracted in prayers and spiritual hymns, another the in patronage of those
in need; all tears, all purification, all ascent and the stretching forward to
the things before.
H. Fair also is simplicity a fruit-bearing, and laughter being made temperate,
and anger being chastised, and an eye being trained, and a mind not being
allowed to wander. Nothing of the things contributed to God is so small, even
if it be least, even if falling far short of the worth, which he does not
altogether receive and accept, even if he knows to weigh mercy with the just
judgment. He receives also Paul's planting, as of Paul, and Apollos' watering,
and the two mites of the widow, and the publican's humility, and Manasseh's
confession. Moses was pitching the tabernacle below, the antitype of the
heavenly things, and all contributed what was proclaimed to them. And some
even of their own accord, some gold, some silver, some precious stones for the
ephod; and some fine-spun linen, some spun scarlet, some purple, some rams'
skins dyed red; and some even goats' hair for the work of the tabernacle, the
paltriest; and some whatever each man or each woman happened to have; but all
brought, and no one was without contribution, not even of the poorest. Thus
also we to the precious tabernacle of God of this Church, which the Lord
pitched, and not man, which is built together with various beauties of virtue,
one a small thing, another a greater, but all similarly let us contribute
toward a perfect work, toward a dwelling-place of Christ, toward a holy
temple, being put together and fitted together by the architecture of the
Spirit. And we will altogether contribute nothing so much as we have received,
even if we contribute all. Since even the being is for us from God, and the
knowing God, and the very having what we may contribute. And the fairest and
most philanthropic thing is that not =35.1053= by the worth of what is given,
but by the power and the disposition of the one bearing fruit, God measures
the contribution.
I. Do not then wait to become good, but already become; nor because you fall
short of the worth, fall short of the whole. But contribute the one, and be
eager for the other, and on behalf of this ask that pardon be given to the
weakness. You shall not appear, he says, empty before me. Let no one be empty,
nor fruitless, nor any soul barren and unproductive. Let each from the things
present bear fruit to God, and from his own; the one sinning the change, the
one running well the vigor, the young man the self-control, the gray hair the
prudence, the rich man the distribution, the poor man the thanksgiving, the
one in authority the misfortune, the exactor the gentleness. You priests, put
on righteousness, or, to speak more truly, let us put it on; let us not
scatter the sheep of the pasture, and destroy them, for whom the good shepherd
laid down his life, who knows his own, and is known by his own, and calls them
by name, and leads them in, and leads them out both from unbelief to faith,
and from this life to the rest from there. Let us fear, lest the judgment
begin from us, according to the threat; lest we receive from the hand of the
Lord the sins double, ourselves not entering, and preventing those who are
able to enter.
J. You sheep, do not shepherd the shepherds, nor raise yourselves above your
own bounds; for it is enough for you, if you are shepherded well. Do not judge
the judges, nor legislate for the legislators; for God is not of instability
and disorder, but of peace and order. Let there then be no head, who is
scarcely a hand, or a foot, or some other of the more paltry members of the
body; but each in the rank in which he was called, brethren, in this let him
remain, even if he be worthy of the better; in that he is content with the
present, being more well-reputed than in seeking what he did not receive. Let
no one, when it is possible to follow without danger, desire to rule with
danger; nor let the law of subordination be dissolved, which holds together
both the earthly things and the heavenly; nor let us make the many-rule into
no-rule. You who are about words, do not trust the words excessively, nor be
wise in things superfluous and beyond the Word, nor desire to defeat all
things badly, but some things even endure to be defeated well. Give to the
Word the word; make education a weapon of righteousness, not of death.
=35.1056=
K. Be content with your own wages, you soldiers, and demand nothing beyond
what has been appointed. These things John commands you together with us, the
great herald of the truth, the forerunning voice of the Word. What does he
mean by wages? Clearly the imperial ration, and the gifts belonging by law to
the ranks. But the surplus, of whom? I for my part hesitate to say the
blasphemous thing; but you, I know, understand, even if I spare. Render the
things of Caesar to Caesar, and the things of God to God; to the one the tax,
to the other the fear, you who are named of the state; and when I say fear, I
forbid greed. What then shall we gain, perhaps you would say, something great?
The greatest of all; and if you wish, through me as mediator, the good hopes,
and the first places of the city above, not of this small and least among
cities, of which not even the ruling, that I may say moderately honoring it as
having nourished me, is very august and exceedingly splendid. Let us wish to
become first there; let us lay claim to the splendor from there; in the bosom
of Abraham, instead of the compassion from here, let us rest; let us judge a
just judgment; let us deliver the poor and needy; let us have mercy on widow
and orphan; let us buy off those being killed, or to say the most moderate
thing, let us not ourselves kill; let us not overlook the one who needs us,
even as far as the crumbs from the table; and let us not pass by the one
ulcerated and thrown at our gates; let us not live in luxury, while others
suffer; let us not abhor the fellow-slave; let us not, friends and brothers,
become of the portion of the rich man, nor be pained in the flame, nor be
separated by the gulf from the holy, nor need Lazarus the poor man, to cool
with the tip of a finger our tongue burning, and not even obtain so much. Let
us become good, compassionate, sympathetic; let us imitate the goodness of the
Master, who makes the sun rise on good and wicked, and nourishes all alike
with the rain. Let us not accept to be rich through the poverty of others; let
us not come so far from the divine equality; nor let us mix our wealth with
others' tears, by which as by rust and moth it will be consumed, or, to say
the thing of Scripture, it will be vomited. But are we more insatiable than
what is due? It is possible also to overreach well. =35.1057= Let us give
something small from here, that from there we may be rich.
L. These then already are the commands common toward all, not only those in
the state; for common also are the medicines of a common sickness. But you
register us justly, O registrar of our taxes; register, not my words
carefully, of which the gain is nothing, or small, or unto grace and pleasure
of hearing, but my people holily and humanely; having reverenced, if nothing
else, the season itself, because even the Savior is born in a registration.
For there went out a decree, he says, from Caesar Augustus, that all the
inhabited world should be registered; and it was being registered. And Joseph
also went up to Bethlehem with Mary who was betrothed to him as wife to be
registered, because he was of the house and lineage of David. And then the
Savior is born, the wonder, the maker and master of all, in a paltry and small
lodging. Let us fear the mystery; let us reverence the economy, and let us
ourselves contribute something to the season. Now angels rejoice, now
shepherds are flashed around, now a star from the East runs toward the
greatest and unapproachable light; now magi fall down, and bring gifts, and
know the king of all, and well by the star they infer the heavenly one; and
now Herod rages, and murders children, and through the liberator he destroys
those who ought to obtain the liberty. But let us at least be with those who
worship, and let us offer to the one who became poor for us even as far as a
body, not frankincense, nor gold, nor myrrh; the one as to God, the other as
to a king, the other as to one who tasted death for us; but mystical gifts,
and beyond what appears; at least the taking nothing, nor yielding anything to
wealth against poverty, nor wronging the creature through the creature.
M. With Christ you write, with Christ you weigh, with the Head you assay, with
the Word you reckon. Christ is just now born for you, and he is God, and
becomes man, and associates with men. What does the word show? It seems to me,
for the admonition of those entrusted with such things, that God always enters
together with the greatest of the administrations. And =35.1060= that he may
shame the registrars, then he associates with flesh and men; but that he may
comfort us of the slavery, and legislate the good-will (for this too must not
be overlooked), he himself also pays the didrachm, and not for himself only,
but also for Peter the most honored of the disciples; since he also became man
for us, and took on the form of a slave, and was led to death for our
lawlessness. These things the Savior, even being able by the will alone, as
God, to save, since he also established all things by a command; but he
brought in for us something greater and more shaming, the sympathy and the
equal honor. But what of us the disciples of Christ the meek and
philanthropic, and who has ministered so much for us? Will we not imitate the
compassion of the Master? Will we not be good to the fellow-slaves, that we
ourselves also may obtain such a Lord measuring as we measure? Will we not
acquire our own souls through gentleness? It is enough for the free to be
slaves, and that the difference be so much, that of the same clay, one rules,
the other is ruled; and one assigns taxes, the other is registered; and for
some it is possible to wrong and to do badly, but for others to beg and
struggle so as not to suffer; and these things to be about the one image, and
the one dignity, the heirs of the same life, for whom Christ died alike. These
things are enough for the free; and let not the yoke be heavy, nor the penalty
of our first sin.
N. Would that evil might perish, and the first sowing of it, and the Evil one
who sowed the tares upon us sleeping, that the beginning of the bad might be
the neglecting of the good, just as also of darkness the withdrawal of the
light. These things the tree, and the bitter tasting, and the envious serpent,
and the disobedience that condemned to live in sweat of the face. From this I
am naked and unseemly, and I knew the nakedness, and I put on the coat of
skin, and I fell from paradise, and I was turned back to the earth, from which
I was taken, having this one thing instead of luxury, to know my own evils;
and I was condemned to unceasing grief instead of the small pleasure, and war
toward the one who was badly befriended, and who pulled me under through the
tasting. These are the wages of evil for me; from this the =35.1061= being
born with toil, and living, and being dissolved. This is mother of need, and
this of greed, and this of wars, and wars are fathers of taxes, and these of
the heaviest of the condemnation. But let us at least not stretch the fine,
lying under the same sentence, nor become bad to others. God demands of us
philanthropy toward one another, even if we are being punished by him.
O. There will be also some other enrollment, and another registrar; if you
hear of some book of the living, and a book of those not being saved. There we
will all be enrolled, or rather we have already been written according to the
worth of the things lived here, each. Wealth does not have the more there, nor
poverty the less, nor favor, nor enmity, nor any other of all the things by
which here the just is stolen. We have all been written by the finger of God,
and the book will be opened to us in the day of revelation. Small and great is
there, and servant together with master, I will utter the thing of Solomon,
and king with the one ruled, and registrar with the one registered, and the
splendor is near. And the ill-omened I will keep silent; except such as we
write, such we will also be written. Make this enrollment more useful for
yourself, having appeared to us good and philanthropic.
P. What do you say to these things? What do you write, O best of friends and
of those of the same age and sharer with us of teachers and of lessons, even
if now God has ranked us in the better portion, to teach you these things who
are in authority (for I hesitate to say the heavier)? O of a pious fatherland
and race, O holy plant of holy parents, O root of more holy children, how ever
do you receive my words? Do we persuade at all, or will it be necessary to
charm at greater length? This one must not conjecture, but know well, that my
words have you from more. For even if we had to trust in no other thing, yet
in the words themselves, which always lead you easily toward the good things,
either beginning or following; for this is that by which the wise differ from
the many. And I will still add a little to what has been said; you have given
a wage to my words, whichever you have given; but they give you and bring
forward poor men, a whole choir of priests, a choir of philosophers, held down
by no bond below, possessing only the bodies, and not even these entirely;
having nothing for Caesar, all for God, hymns, prayers, vigils, tears,
=35.1064= a possession not held, to die to the world, to live to Christ, to
empty the flesh, to draw the soul from the body. Sparing these, or even purely
giving back to God the servants of God and initiates, and watchers of the
heavenly things, the first-fruits of our race, the support, the crowns of the
faith, the precious pearls, the stones of the temple, of which the foundation
and cornerstone is Christ, I mean of the fair fullness of the Church; and you
would counsel most fairly both for these, and for yourself, and for all of us.
And I pray that this wealth from us may become yours rather than treasures of
much gold and silver, now existing, and after a little not going to be.
Q. This is the fruit-bearing of my words to you, even if less than the hope,
yet at least according to our power. But you, may you give me in return the
greater of my contribution, the obedience; that both in the other things and
you may less need my words, considering one another unto a provocation of love
and of good works; that having obtained a good and philanthropic writing from
there, and dancing about the king of all, which is the only work of those
written there, we may minister to God the praise, the one of Father, and of
Son, and of Holy Spirit, both beholding and lifting up the glory and the
splendor of the Godhead, because to him is the glory, and the honor, and the
worship unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
