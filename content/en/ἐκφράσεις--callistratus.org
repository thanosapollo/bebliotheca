** On a Satyr.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

1. There was a cave near Egyptian Thebes, likened to a shepherd's pipe,
winding of itself in coils in a circle about the depths of the earth. For it
did not open in a straight line and split into straight-running galleries, but
running round the curve at the mountain's foot it stretched underground coils,
falling out into a wandering hard to find. In it was set a figure of a Satyr,
fashioned of stone. He stood on a base, arranging his pose for the dance and
lifting the sole of his right foot behind him; he also held a flute and was
the first to rise toward the sound. For the ear received no melody of one
playing, nor was the flute vocal, yet the passion of flute-players had been
brought into the stone through the art. You would have seen even the veins
rising as if filling from some breath, and the Satyr drawing the breath from
his chest for the sounding of the flute, and the image wanting to be at work,
and the stone falling into a struggle. For it persuaded one that there was in
it a native power of breath, and a showing of panting that found from within,
out of things impossible, a way. The body had no share of softness, but the
hardness of the limbs stole away the bloom, roughening the form into the
symmetry of manly joints. For a fair girl's flesh is soft and yielding, and
her limbs delicate; but a Satyr's look is dry, as of a mountain spirit leaping
for Dionysus. Ivy crowned him, not because art had plucked the fruit from a
meadow, but the stone, poured from hardness into tendrils, ran about the hair,
creeping from the brow to meet at the tendons of the neck. Pan stood beside
him, delighted at the flute-playing and embracing Echo, as if, I think,
fearing that the flute, having stirred some musical note, might persuade the
Nymph to answer the Satyr. Seeing this image we believed also that the
Ethiopian stone of Memnon had become vocal, which, when Day approached,
brightened at her presence, but when she departed, struck with grief, groaned
in mourning, and alone of stones, governed by the presence of pleasure and
pain, departed from its own deafness, overcoming insensibility into the power
of voice.

** On the Statue of a Bacchante.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

2. Not only are the arts of poets and prose-writers inspired upon their
tongues when a divine possession falls from the gods, but the hands of
craftsmen also, seized by the contributions of more divine spirits, prophesy
works possessed and full of madness. For Scopas, as if moved by some
inspiration, let the divine possession go into the making of the statue. Why
should I not recount to you from the beginning the enthusiasm of the art?
There was a statue of a bacchante made of Parian stone, changing toward the
real bacchante. For the stone, remaining in its own order, seemed to overstep
the law among stones; what appeared was truly an image, yet the art led the
imitation into true being. You would have seen that, though solid, it was
softened into the likeness of the female, a fierce brightness setting the
womanly aright; and though without power of movement it knew how to revel, and
as the god entered it echoed within. Seeing the face we stood in
speechlessness; so a showing of sense attended even when sense was not
present, and a bacchante's maddening possession was indicated though
possession did not strike, and as many things as a soul stung by madness
bears, so many proofs of passion shone, blended by the art in an unspeakable
way. The hair was let go to be driven by the west wind and was parted into the
bloom of hair, which most of all put reason beside itself, because even to the
fineness of hair the stone, being stone, yielded, and obeyed the imitations of
locks, and though stripped of vital habit it had the vital. You would have
said that the art had even brought in the beginnings of growth, so both what
was seen was unbelievable and the unbelievable was seen. Moreover it showed
hands at work; for she did not shake the bacchic thyrsus, but carried some
victim as if crying euoe, a sign of a more bitter madness (it was a figure of
a she-goat, livid in color, for the stone put on even the dead as well), and,
though the matter was one, it divided the imitation into death and life,
setting up the one as breathing and as it were reaching toward Cithaeron, the
other slain by the bacchic sting and wasting the prime of the senses. So
Scopas, in fashioning even the lifeless generations of images, was a craftsman
of truth, and in the bodies he molded the wonders of the soul; while
Demosthenes, who shapes statues in words, all but showed a perceptible form of
words, blending with the offspring of mind and thought the drugs of his art.
And you will know at once that the statue set out for viewing is not even
deprived of motion from within, but both rules at once and in the character
preserves its own begetter.

** On the Statue of Eros.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h1
:END:

3. The speeches wish to prophesy other sacred things of art. For it is not
lawful for me not to call the offspring of art sacred. It was Eros, a work of
Praxiteles, Eros himself, a blooming boy and young, having wings and a bow.
Bronze formed him, and as if forming Eros, a tyrant god and great, it too was
mastered; for it did not endure to be bronze in all things, but as much as it
was, it became Eros. You would have seen the bronze growing delicate and
wantoning beyond measure into plumpness, and, to speak briefly, art fulfilling
for itself what was needed, being sufficient. He was supple though without
share of softness, and having a color in concert with bronze he was seen
blooming; deprived of the works of movement he was ready to show movement, for
he was set in a standing pose, yet he deceived as if he also ruled the motion
through the air. He exulted into a laugh, flashing something fiery and gentle
from the eyes, and one could see the bronze obeying the passion and receiving
easily the imitation of laughter. He was posed bending the right wrist toward
the crown of the head, with the other hand raising the bow and inclining the
balance of his stance to the left; for he raised the displacement of the left
flank, breaking the compactness toward the bronze's ease. Locks shaded his
head, blooming and curly, gleaming with a youthful flower. And the bronze was
marvelous of its kind: to one looking, a redness shone, rising from the tips
of the curls; to one touching, the hair rose a little, softening toward the
sense. When I had seen the art it occurred to me to believe that Daedalus also
trained a moving chorus and gave senses to gold, since Praxiteles put into the
image of Eros all but thoughts as well, and devised that it cut the air with a
wing.

** On the Statue of an Indian.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

4. By a spring an Indian stood, set as an offering to the Nymphs. The Indian
was stone growing black and going over to the color of his race from nature.
He had a thriving and curly mane, not shining with unmixed black, but from the
tips rivaling the bloom of a Tyrian shell. For as if faring well and being
moistened by the neighboring Nymphs, the hair, rising from the roots darker,
grew purple toward the tips. The eyes, however, did not agree with the stone;
for about the pupils of the eyes a whiteness ran, at that part of the rock
changing over to the whiter, where also the color of the Indian's nature grows
white. Drunkenness put him beside himself, and the stone's color did not
declare the drunkenness, for it had no device to redden the cheeks, the black
covering the drunkenness; but from the pose it accused the passion, for he
stood frantic and reveling, unable to plant his feet, but trembling and
sinking at the ham. The stone under the passion seemed like one struck, and as
if gasping it showed the shaking from the drink. The image of the Indian had
nothing delicate, nor was it worked into grace of color, but was articulated
only into the joining of the limbs. He was uncovered and naked, as Indian
bodies are accustomed to make themselves manly against the flaming of the
heat.

** On the Statue of Narcissus.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1
:END:

5. There was a grove, and in it a most beautiful spring of very pure and
translucent water, and on it stood Narcissus made of stone. He was a boy, or
rather a youth, of the same age as the Erotes, flashing beauty as of lightning
from the body itself. The pose was of this kind: he flashed with gilded hair,
the hair winding into a circle at the brow and poured down the back at the
neck; he looked not purely proud, nor purely cheerful, for grief also had been
planted in the eyes by the art, so that the image might imitate along with
Narcissus his fortune also. He was dressed like the Erotes, to whom also the
prime of his bloom had been likened. The adorning form was of this kind: a
white-flowering peplos, of the same color as the body of the stone, running
round in a circle, pinned at the right shoulder and coming down over the knee,
stopping there, freeing only the hand from the brooch. So tender was it and so
made toward the imitation of a peplos that even the color of the body shone
through, the whiteness in the wrapping allowing the gleam in the limbs to go
out. He stood using the spring as a mirror and pouring into it the form of his
face, and it, receiving the marks from him, accomplished the same
image-making, so that the natures seemed to vie with one another. For the
stone as a whole was changing toward that real boy, and the spring contended
against the devices of art in the stone, in a bodiless form working the
likeness of the bodily model and putting around the shadow coming down from
the image a kind of flesh, the nature of the water. So living and breathing
was the form upon the waters that one would have thought it was Narcissus
himself, who they say came to a spring and, when his form was seen to him upon
the waters, died among the Nymphs, having fallen in love so as to mingle with
the image, and now appears in meadows, blooming in spring seasons. You would
have seen how the stone, being one, fitted both the color and the making of
the eyes, and kept a history of character, and showed senses, and indicated
passions, and followed the power of hair, loosening into the bend of the hair.
What cannot be said in speech: stone relaxed into wetness and offering a body
contrary to its substance; for having obtained a more solid nature it sent out
a sense of delicacy, spreading into a somewhat rare bulk of body. He also held
a syrinx, with which he used to make first-offerings to the pastoral gods and
made the solitude sound with the songs, if ever he longed to converse with
musical instruments. Having admired this Narcissus, young men, I have brought
him also to you, molding him into the Muses' court. And the speech has it as
the image also had it.

** On the Statue of Opportunity at Sicyon.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

6. I wish to set before you in speech also the work of Lysippus, which the
craftsman, having fashioned as the most beautiful of statues, set out for the
Sicyonians to see. Opportunity had been formed into a statue of bronze, art
competing with nature. Opportunity was a boy in his prime, from head to feet
setting upright the flower of youth. In look he was fair, shaking the first
down and leaving the hair free for the west wind to toss wherever it wished,
and he had a blooming color, showing the flowers by the radiance of the body.
He was for the most part like Dionysus; for the brows shone with graces, and
his cheeks, reddening into a youthful flower, were made fair, casting a tender
blush upon the eyes; he stood on a sphere, having set himself on the tips of
the soles, winged in both feet. The hair had not grown in the customary way,
but the hair creeping down over the brows shook the curl on the cheeks, while
the hinder parts of Opportunity were free of locks, showing only the growth of
hair from birth. We, then, struck with speechlessness at the sight, stood
looking at the bronze contriving the works of nature and stepping out of its
own order; for being bronze it grew red, and being hard in nature it was
spread softly, yielding to the art toward whatever it wished, and though
lacking vital sense it persuaded one that it had sense dwelling in it, and
truly it was planted, having set the sole firm, yet standing it showed that it
had power of impulse, and it deceived your eye as if it also ruled the motion
forward, and as having received from the craftsman even the cutting of the
aerial portion with the wings, if it wished. Such was our wonder; but one of
those wise about the arts, who know how to track the wonders of craftsmen with
a more technical sense, also brought reasoning to the work, explaining the
power of opportunity preserved in the art: the winging of the soles hinted at
swiftness, and how, unwinding much of time, he is carried riding on the
seasons; the blooming beauty, that everything timely is fair and opportunity
alone is a craftsman of beauty, while everything that has lost its bloom is
outside the nature of opportunity; and the hair on the forehead, that when he
is approaching it is easy to take hold of him, but when he has passed, the
prime of affairs goes out with him, and it is not possible, having neglected
him, to catch Opportunity.

** On the Statue of Orpheus.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1
:END:

7. On Helicon (the place is a shady precinct of the Muses), beside the streams
of the river Olmeius and the violet-colored spring of Pegasus, a statue of
Orpheus, the son of Calliope, stood among the Muses, most beautiful to see;
for the bronze together with the art brought forth the beauty, marking also by
the splendor of the body the musical in the soul. A Persian tiara spotted with
gold adorned him, rising high from the crown; a tunic drawn from the shoulders
to the feet was bound at the chest with a golden strap; the hair was so
blooming and marking the vital and the breathing that it deceived the sense,
as if it also is shaken and stirred toward the breaths of the west wind. For
the hair of the neck was poured down the back, while that which from above,
split in two, came upon the brows showed the casts of the eyes clear; his
sandal was blossomed with most yellow gold, and a peplos free down the back
came to the ankle; he held the lyre, and it had attached as many notes as the
Muses; for the bronze both played the part of strings and, changing toward the
imitation of each, was led obediently, all but becoming vocal toward the very
sound of the notes. Under his feet the base was not a heaven formed, nor
Pleiades cutting the aether, nor the turnings of the Bear that have no share
in Ocean's baths, but there was every kind of bird beside itself at the song,
and all the mountain beasts, and whatever feeds in the recesses of the sea,
and a horse was charmed, held by the song instead of a bit, and an ox, leaving
the pastures, listened to the lyre-playing, and the unyielding nature of lions
was lulled toward the harmony. You would have seen the bronze also forming
rivers flowing from springs toward the songs, and a wave of the sea rising in
love of the song, and rocks struck with a sense of music, and every timely
growth from its haunts hastening toward the Orphic muse; and there was nothing
that sounded, nor anything raising the lyre's harmony, yet the art in the
animals declared the passions of the love of music, and made the pleasures
appear in the bronze, and unspeakably brought out the charms that bloom upon
the sense of the animals.

** On the Statue of Dionysus.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h1
:END:

8. Daedalus, if one must believe the wonder about Crete, was able to contrive
moving works and to force gold toward human sense; but the hands of Praxiteles
altogether made the works living. There was a grove, and Dionysus stood
imitating the form of a youth, so tender that the bronze was reshaped toward
flesh, so supple and relaxed in body that he seemed born of another matter,
not of bronze; who, being bronze, grew red, and though having no share of life
wished to show the form of life; and if you touched him he rose a little
toward the prime, and truly the bronze was compact, yet softened by the art
into flesh it escaped the sense of the hand. He was blooming, full of
delicacy, flowing with longing, such as Euripides, having formed him in the
Bacchae, brought to light; ivy crowned him, running round in a circle. The
bronze was as ivy, bent into tendrils and drawing up the coils of the curls
poured from the brow; and he was full of laughter, which was beyond all
wonder, that the matter should give off proofs of pleasure and the bronze
should act the showing of passions. A fawnskin covered him, not such as
Dionysus is accustomed to fasten on, but the bronze changed into the imitation
of the hide; he stood resting the left hand on the thyrsus, and the thyrsus
deceived the sense and, though made of bronze, was thought to flash something
green and thriving, changing toward that matter. The eye was translucent with
fire, mad to see; for the bronze showed the bacchic too, and seemed to be
possessed, as if, I think, Praxiteles had been able to mix in even the bacchic
sting.

** On the Statue of Memnon.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

9. I wish to recount to you also the wonder of Memnon, for the art is truly
paradoxical and greater than a human hand. There was an image of Memnon son of
Tithonus in Ethiopia, made of stone; yet being stone it did not remain in its
own bounds, nor did it endure the silence of nature, but even being stone it
had power of voice. For now it addressed Day as she rose, marking with the
voice its joy and brightening at the mother's presence; now, as she declined
toward night, it groaned something piteous and painful, grieving at the
absence. Nor was the stone at a loss for tears, but it had these too serving
the will; and the Memnonian image seemed to me to differ only in the human
body, yet to be guided, led by some soul and a like purpose. At any rate it
had the things that pain mixed in, and again a sense of pleasure took it,
struck by both passions. Nature brought forth the generation of stones
voiceless and deaf, neither willing to be governed by pain nor knowing how to
be pleased, but unwounded by all fortunes; yet to that stone of Memnon art
also gave pleasure and mixed grief into rock, and this alone we know as the
art that put thoughts and voice into the stone. For Daedalus went so far as to
play the youth as far as movement, and his art had power to put the materials
beside themselves and to move them into dance, but it was impossible and
wholly without a way to work the products as also sharing in voice; yet the
hands of the Ethiopians, finding ways of the impossible, overcame the
voicelessness of the stone. Echo also, the story goes, answered that Memnon
whenever he uttered, and when he groaned mournfully sent back a mournful song,
and when he fared well gave back the sound in imitation. That work also lulled
Day's griefs and did not let her seek the child, as if Ethiopian art set
against her the Memnon who had vanished by fate.

** On the Statue of Asclepius.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h1
:END:

10. Then we are persuaded that the Argoan ship became vocal, the one fashioned
by the hands of Athena, which also obtained the fortune among the stars; yet
we shall not believe a statue, into which Asclepius sends his powers, bringing
in the providential mind, that it turns the power of the indwelling toward
communion with himself? Nay, we will grant that the divine comes down into
human bodies, where it might even be defiled by passions, but we shall not
believe if it has grown beside that in which there is no offspring of vice? To
me, then, what is seen does not seem to be a type, but a molding of the truth.
See, for the art is not without character, but having imaged the god it goes
out into him. Being matter it sends up a godlike thought, and being a work of
a hand it does what is not allowed to craftings, bringing forth unspeakably
proofs of soul. The face, when you look at it, enslaves the sense; for it is
not shaped toward an added beauty, but stirring an all-holy and gracious eye
it flashes beneath an unspeakable depth of dignity mixed with reverence. Coils
of locks flowing with graces, some thriving toward the back, are poured free,
others coming over the brow toward the eyebrows wind about the eyes. And as if
watered from a vital cause they too wind together into the bend of the curls,
the matter not obeying the law of the art but thinking that it shapes a god
and must rule. While things that come to be are accustomed to perish, the form
of the statue, inasmuch as it bears in itself the substance of health,
thrives, acquiring an undying prime. We have made you first-offerings also of
young words, O Paean, offspring of memory; for you command it, I think; and we
are eager to sing the strain for you, if you should grant health.

** On the Statue of a Youth.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h1
:END:

11. Have you seen the youth on the acropolis, whom Praxiteles set up, or must
I set the matter of the art before you? He was a tender boy and young, the art
softening the bronze toward the soft and the youthful; he was full of luxury
and longing and showed the flower of youth, and all things were to be seen
changing toward the will of the art. For he was tender though having a
substance at war with tenderness, and he was led toward the supple though
deprived of wetness, and the bronze as a whole stepped out of its own nature,
shifting the bounds into the true type. Though without share of breath it put
on the breathing; for of things which matter did not receive and did not have
inborn, of these the art procured the power. He shared his cheeks with a
blush, which was even paradoxical, a redness born of bronze, and it was a
flower of boyish age shining out; the hair had coils coming upon the brows.
He, crowning the hair with the strap and pushing the hairs from the brows with
the diadem, kept the forehead bare of locks. And as we examined the art also
part by part and the cunning works in it, we stood struck with speechlessness;
for the bronze showed the flesh well-nourished and glistening, and was adapted
toward the movement of the hair, now winding together with the plaits of curly
locks, now spreading with the hair when it wished to be poured stretched down
the back, and when the figure wished to be bent, yielding also toward the
bend, and when it wished to stretch the limbs, shifting toward the taut. The
eye was full of longing, mixed with reverence, full of Aphrodisian grace; for
the bronze knew how to rival the lovable and obeyed the image when it wished
to exult. Though this ephebe was unmoved, he would have seemed to you to share
in movement and to be making ready for the dance.

** On the Statue of a Centaur.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12-h1
:END:

12. Going into a sanctuary, something august and great, which transferred into
itself the most beautiful likeness, I see in the propylaea of the temple a
centaur set up, not like a man according to the Homeric image, but like a
wooded mountain ridge. The centaur was a man down to the flank, ending into a
four-legged horse's base; for nature, having cut the horse and the man by
halves, fitted them into one body, separating some of the limbs and crafting
others in concert with one another. For as much of the human as is carried
down from the waist to the extreme of the foot, it took away; and as much of
the horse's body as comes down to the navel, cutting it, it joined to the
human type, so that the horse longs for the head and the neck tendons and as
much as, coming down to the back, is broadened, and the man seeks the support
from the navel to the base. Such being the body, you would have seen also
spirit rising to the surface of the work, and the body made savage, and the
beast-like blooming on the face, and the rock playing the hair most
beautifully, and all things hastening toward the true type.

** On the Statue of Medea.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h1
:END:

13. I saw also the much-talked-of Medea in the bounds of the Macedonians. It
was stone declaring the form of the soul, the art having molded into it the
things that complete the soul; for the showing accused reasoning, and spirit
rose up, and the image passed toward a disposition of grief, and, to speak
briefly, what was seen was an interpretation of the drama about her. For the
reasoning declared the woman's counsels over the deed, and the spirit, writing
nature aside by the rush of anger, raised her toward the work, introducing the
impulse toward the blame; and the grief marked the pity for the children,
drawing the stone weakly from the spirit toward maternal understanding. For
the image was not unyielding, nor beast-like, but was divided into a showing
of spirit and of distress, serving the counsels of the woman's nature. For it
was likely that after the bile, being purified of the spirit, she should turn
toward pity, and that the soul, coming into thought of the evil, should feel
compassion. These passions along with the body the image imitated, and one
could see the stone now bearing the spirit in the eyes, now looking sullen and
softening into gloom, as if the maker had filled the starting-point of the
imitation straight from Euripides' play-making, in which she both deliberates,
stirring together even a sane understanding, and her character grows savage
into spirit, overstepping the bounds of love of offspring fixed by nature
toward the children, and after the lawless slaughter she takes up the
children's words. She had also a sword-bearing hand ready to serve the spirit
as she hastened toward the pollution, and neglected hair marking the dryness,
and a mourning dress following the soul.

** On the Painting of Athamas.
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14-h1
:END:

14. There was a painting on the Scythian shores, not (only) for display, but
also worked not without the Muses toward a contest of the beauties of
painting. Athamas had been formed on it, stung by madnesses. He was to be seen
naked, reddening the hair with blood, the hair blown by the wind, the eye
frantic, full of astonishment; and he was armed not only by madnesses toward
daring, nor was he made savage only by the soul-destroying terrors from the
Erinyes, but iron also was held out from the hand, like one rushing out. For
the image was truly unmoved, yet it seemed not to keep the standing still, but
by the appearance of movement it put the spectators beside themselves, looking
something pale and dead from fear; and Ino was there, terrified, trembling,
and she had also embraced an infant child and brought the nipple to his lips,
dripping the nourishing springs for the nursling. The image was pressing on
toward the headland of Sciron and the sea at the mountain's foot, and the surf
was hollowed toward receiving, accustomed to wave, and something of Zephyrus
held the body, lulling the sea with a clear breath; for the wax played upon
the sense, as knowing how both to craft breath and to shoot up sea breezes and
to lead the imitation into the works of nature. Sea-dolphins also leaped
beside, cutting the surf in the painting, and the wax seemed to be breathed
through and to grow moist toward the imitation of the sea, changing toward the
substance itself. At the edges of the panel an Amphitrite rose from the
depths, looking something wild and dreadful and flashing a sea-green gleam
from the eyes; Nereids stood about her, and they were tender and blooming to
look upon and dripping Aphrodisian longing from the eyes, and winding the
dance over the tops of the sea waves they struck wonder. About them Ocean
deep-eddying was poured, the river's movement having all but been taught to
swell in waves as well.
