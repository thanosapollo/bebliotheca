Aristides to the council and the people of Cotiaeum, greeting.

It was reasonable that even from all of Greece men should come to you to share
the grief at such a disaster and to honor a man so far first among the Greeks;
I, then, sending this and ranking myself among those who count what has
happened as their own, do not think I shall seem to be officious. For of all
the names that are held among men as fair and honorable, that give pleasure to
children and are reverend to elders, the man had left none of these wanting
for me. But having been reared under him and educated, and having shared with
zeal in all that fortune afterwards granted, I could call him foster-father,
teacher, father, companion, everything. And the greatest thing between us was
that we could think equally of one another: I taking pride in him as my
teacher, and he

counting what concerned us as a part of his own glory. So long, then, as it
was possible to send to him, I did this, and we had a not uncultured
intercourse about speeches through letters. But since it is no longer possible
to converse with him, and he did not even receive with those hands dearest to
me what I happened to send just now, it remained to send to you, taking the
hearth of the city in his place. For I thought thus to honor Alexander with a
double honor, both remembering of him what is fitting, and at the same time
making you my own through him; and to do for you twice what would be pleasing,
both trusting you publicly and remembering a man of whom you make the greatest
account; and I think not you only, but all who in any way at all belong among
the Greeks. It happens that in memory I reach all his excellences, but in
speech I am at a loss to go through them. For they all come upon me in a mass,
which it is impossible to tell one by one, and to begin even to try does not
seem to me to have the right moment; but if I should speak from a part, I fear
I may be better silent. For he first of all changed the thing that happens
about praises. For he was so far from being distinguished through the
commonness of his race, as one taking refuge in a people or a city, that,
though your race is of course the most ancient of races, and your city in
turn, as they say, in the common race of the Phrygians, he established his own
distinction for the city and for the whole race, and for all of you it is no
small boast toward the Greeks to be his fellow-citizens. He is said to have
used the best teachers, but he appears to have surpassed them as much as all
children. And having in the times of his life touched all the most famous, of
the most ancient he became a pupil, and of all those after them, of some a
teacher, of others a fellow-worker. And what sort of men he produced others
will say; but those with whom he was educated together

none of them was greater than he. And indeed the others, reaching after great
things, pass over the small; but he, beginning from the slightest, went
through even to the most complete, just as they say it is also lawful to be
initiated first in the small rites. Others searched out the traces and the
beginnings to a great length, or rather even wore out their own life in these
things, but of that for whose sake it is worth finding these out, some did not
see it at all, and others, it seems, did not reach it; but he searched it out
as some road from beginning to end, nowhere omitting anything of the things
worthy of study, of whatever sort. And so indeed he became for the Greeks in
place of a common treasury; for whatever one needed of the things that belong
to culture, it was possible to take it from him, as drawing from springs. And
the greatest of his qualities, and most worthy to mark, which I myself once
said to him in conversation: for having gone through all the studies, as I
say, and there being nothing in which he was not more exact than those who sat
over each, he neither inscribed upon himself the most imposing of the names,
but remained upon the old one, and he did not deprive the others of their
schools, but was plainly cooperating with each and always increasing them
according to his power. And so the others become greater through their arts,
but he even made the art itself greater by the part from himself; for he
raised it from many sides, so that it has its form from him. For it is enough
for the others to surpass those of their own time, but he appears to have held
first place of the whole art simply. For of the others assigned here to
culture, some were thought to have fared well in judgment, but became nothing
in voice; others reached as far as speaking, but had not consorted much with
knowledge; others again practiced much learning, but by this very thing were
blinded toward the better and let go the best: all these one man gathered and
possessed. And indeed that one so gifted in voice should not withdraw into
writings and such a form, but should choose the service that belonged to the
ancient Greeks: how is this not

:PROPERTIES:

:END:

like his ungrudging and unsparing conduct toward those who came to him?
Therefore he at once filled them with the studies, and for as many as he saw
in need of a livelihood he procured introductions and posts for them; so that
this one man appears to have educated and distributed the most, and to have
become exactly for the Greeks in place of a founder.

And one may compare what belongs to him also to the mother-cities. For he
settled some here, some there upon the earth, for the benefit both of those
men themselves and of those who used them. And he alone also convicted Hesiod
by deed of lying when he wrote that /even bard envies bard/. For he was as a
common father of those of the same art, and all had more hopes in him than
each in himself. And he alone both did not put private men to shame and among
the gifted was admired into the first rank. And he alone prevailed both in the
number and in the exactness of witnesses. And it was a point of pride both for
those concerned with speeches to have been with him, and for those otherwise
famous and brilliant, in place of some other of the things that make for
preeminence, to appear as using him. So that he was welcome to all, and
whenever he was not in public, he would be either among the powerful, or in
the very house of the emperor. And indeed even his sojourns with the emperors
he made thus, using them as a kind of road: for having held first place
through all the Greek world and having given countless proof of himself, thus
he arrived both at the emperor's courts and at his hearing. And from this, one
taking him over from another held him in place of another ornament of the
imperial things. Having come, then, to such authority and power that he held
even the place of foster-father, not only of teacher, of the children, the
rest of the temperance and seemliness he showed is no slight work to narrate.
And he did the same as also concerning his old schools. For in the schools he
is said never to have been the cause of anything harsh to anyone of all, but
always of some good both to the pupils themselves and to their attendants. For
both to be set free and

to obtain other honors: Alexander became the cause to the most of all, most of
all, I think, by furnishing to each such boys that even those who followed and
shared in the entrance to him were well thought of by those who had set them
over them, and then himself openly asking such favors instead of those
customary to the others.

And similar things also in the imperial affairs: for he never grieved anyone,
but lived doing some good to kinsmen and friends and fatherland and cities.
And having done countless kindnesses to countless men he never asked a wage
for the kindness, yet of his art he was not ashamed to take. For it seemed to
him to be profitable for the young, for the sake of the studies, to dare to
pay out, and this whoever was able; since those unable he did not trouble, but
we know him even adding from his own. Nor then, while he benefited so much
from his art and his associations, was he worsted by others in deeds and civic
life.
You would know this most of all, and I learn these things from some of you,
that he all but raised up the whole city for you from new. So his affair was
not only some speech; and what is worth more than the buildings, the
temperance and the justice, leading all that he thought fit both to say and to
do, and nowhere going beyond need, but that the distinctions he procured for
you were ones that had sense. Nor indeed even in this was he good for free,
but the greatest of the ornaments for you are a sign of his greatness of soul.
And I think that even if he had never spent anything upon you, from the rest
of what he said and did on your behalf he would reasonably be counted a
benefactor. And if again he had neither said nor done anything else, but had
brought in so much from his own house, he would reasonably be well thought of
among the first. And indeed if he had become useful to you publicly neither
from money nor from the rest, that he was established in this rank among

the Greeks would reasonably hold distinction for you; for his glory has become
common to the city. For it is a great thing both for a city and for a people
to furnish the one who is first. And you enjoyed his fortune even more than
others. For to none of all was the fatherland so named. But just as the others
are known from their father or from other affairs, so he was heard at once
with the city. Since even in the books which he corrected this has been left
as a token. For upon Alexander there was a superscript, the fatherland. So
that as often as anyone remembers him, so often you are well thought of. And
your city has been established in the form of a mother-city for ancient
Greece. In return for which you justly both adorn the tomb and honor the man
with all things as founder and settler, and vote what belongs to those who
first founded your city. These things then, when I heard them, somewhat
consoled me as over the disaster, and through these most of all I was led to
write, admiring the resolve. For it is shameful if the Amphipolitans thought
fit to sacrifice to Brasidas as to a hero and founder, because he made them
revolt from the Athenians, but you, the man who joined you together to all the
Greeks, and who never became the cause of anything harsh to anyone, but
continued benefiting all in word and deed, beginning from you of the
fatherland, this man you were not going to honor in the portion of a founder,
common at least to the Greeks, as I say.

And indeed if to furnish Homer to the Smyrnaeans and Archilochus to the
Parians and Hesiod to the Boeotians and Simonides to the Ceans and Stesichorus
to the Himeraeans and Pindar to the Thebans and Sappho and Alcaeus to the
Mytileneans, and some others to others, brings distinction, for Athens I let
go for now, surely it is also reasonable for you to think greatly upon the man
who adorned and displayed all these. For I think if some one of the gods had
given them a rising again while this man was still living, they would all

having gathered their fellow-artists into the same place, bid them learn from
this man what one ought to think and say about them. And I for my part used to
laugh at those who set Plato in front and philosophize about him only; but he,
in my judgment, was more pleasing to Plato than any other. And he so far
surpassed the others in the poets, the prose-writers, all the flowers that the
seasons, they say, bring forth. And indeed this at least is likely to escape
neither you nor those later, that even what he left having written is fair and
better than all of the same kind. Yet they are some small images of his
culture and judgment. He so far prevailed in his associations that to me at
least the saying of Plato himself used to come, that the things in writings
seem to be play compared to the very conversations of wise men; so that let no
one ever think from these that he will see all of Alexander; and yet what
belongs to the Homeric writing is sufficient, and an ornament to this in many
places. And it seems to me that Aesop's affair too was something clever and
wise, but to compare it to Alexander's culture is child's play; since by
however small a part Aesop's wise things were of Alexander's, by so much the
earth brought forth for you the second better than the first. And this too is
wonderful. For to him alone the divine appears to have measured out all things
evenly: a body most beautiful and strongest and healthiest and most stately.
Not, at least in my judgment, was anyone so in his prime and fair as he in the
recess of old age. And a soul most gentle and having a share by nature in
every fair thing; culture to excess, and glory according to worth, and honors
from private men and from emperors alike according to the rank of each; and
the lot of his house always advancing, and labors with ease, and the longest
bounds of life, and, so to speak, every form of prayer come together, and
justice seen together with fortune.

And yet to take all the same things seemed until this to be of the impossible;
but he, or at least of the most of them, appears as one man who obtained them.
And one might know, examining one thing only of his, that concerning his
association with the emperors, and setting beside it that of Aristotle with
Philip and Alexander. How that of the former was not altogether without blame
for the Greeks, but he seemed to be with the rivals and the enemies of the
common race; but this man's had good repute together with the power. For he
was not with the ill-disposed and the enemies of the Greeks, but with
benefactors; nor was he with them for the common harm of those of the same
race, but besides opposing nothing, also always benefiting, if anything was
possible, the one of the Greeks who came into need, and publicly established
for all in place of a common proxenos. And again Plato's with Dionysius,
Dionysius being of course the man who was later well seen in Corinth.

But I let this go. But what I wished to say about the association, that it is
possible to call it noble, but not fortunate; yet for him both were present,
both always to wish the best and to obtain it. And so it is not easy to find
to which race the man is more honorable, whether to that of the Romans or to
the Greek, so much worth he furnished himself to the rulers. And among what
men is it not likely that there is account of him both of old and now? Who
dwell so upon the ends? Who are so without sense of the fair? Who will not
weep when the report comes? For even if the death was in season, yet it was
not in season for the Greeks at least to be deprived of such a man. Surely now
all poetry has lost its bloom, and all writing has been contracted, and all
things have failed, not having the interpreter and champion, and the art is a
widow. And of necessity the small has been contracted in a small way for the
many. And what Aristophanes says about Aeschylus, that there is darkness when
he has died, this it is worth saying also about this man now

in the account of culture. O fairest form. O eldest of the Greeks. O admired
through life. O longed for by those who met you, and longed for by the others
to meet, and blessed even of the end of which we hear, who, not consumed in
diseases nor struck by pains, but having spent the day in the accustomed
things, as if called by the divine, let go your soul upon the book, and ended
your life according to your name, just as those who have finished some other
thing through exactness to the end, having gone through the whole journey of
life and having done your own things to the last of your power. And if the
words of Pindar and of Plato and of all the workshop about Alexander are true,
and there are some sojourns of those in Hades, surely now it is likely that
choruses of poets stand about him, beginning from Homer stretching out the
right hand, and of speech-makers and writers and of all, each calling him to
himself and bidding him encamp with him, binding him with fillets, crowning
him, and not indeed with judgment and dispute toward another, but from the
first cry; and I think that not even for many years will a head come down
sufficient to compete against this man, but that his throne is perpetual as
best herald of the Greeks and leader. And let none of you take it ill, if in
the midst of writing to you I uttered these things, since the cause even of
the letter itself is the wish both to hear and to speak about him. Nor indeed
is it a charge even to be stung over the man, not even if he is gone being of
such an age; for most of all it is not the newest things one should reasonably
welcome, but the most serious. Then too the loss is so much greater as the
windfall is greater and rarer, to see a man in this of old age faring thus in
all things, and through all preserving the condition of health, memory,
understanding, cheerfulness,

of the rest. For much more, while he still lived, it was reasonable to be in
agony lest he ever depart, and to look upon him, while it was possible, as
some other beloved thing, rather than, when he has died, to reckon the age.
But there is no fear that any of you will censure these things, whom I hear
both doing and saying everything. And I shall turn toward you again with the
speech, and the rest will be brief. For I say that you justly honor him in all
the other things, and that in remembering his household you do most rightly
and what belongs to the whole resolve, counting his wife as some sacred thing
and, if it is lawful to say, the allotment of some good spirit, and counting
his kinsmen as the fairest memorials of him, and leading the older children
through all reverence, and rearing the youngest for now most eagerly and being
guardians publicly.

And a suffering not the slightest has befallen him: for his father, having
educated the most of the Greeks, and having reached even to educating the
children of those whom he educated, did not reach his own son, but the son of
the common teacher of the Greeks needs another teacher. And you, both this
disaster and all the hardship about what has happened, restore, standing forth
as in the portion of fathers, and, to speak in brief, counting the hearth of
Alexander as your public one; and it is necessary for me to urge these things,
which indeed I also said at the beginning of the letter, or whatever you wish
to call the book, that in many ways his things belonged to me; since even of
the friendship with which I loved him I used to gather fitting fruits; and
these were that they loved one who loved no less, and he showed it apart from
the rest also by what he did when I was ill in Rome, becoming nothing that was
not of the things toward salvation for me at least, and of being saved into my
own country, after

the gods, he himself became most responsible. And indeed the later things,
what would one say? And his entreaty also pained me, for countless upon
countless times both himself present and later through letters he asked me to
dedicate my writings among the other books for you, promising them the first
rank. But I, still needing to look them over, was not able to consent. Thus
Alexander neither copied our books nor knew the most of them. But indeed the
vote which he always cast about our speeches, I do not know if it was better
than envy, but he cast one greater than excess; all which both makes me
remember him and count what has happened as no slight disaster, and besides
not seem to be officious in now speaking to you. And I would have wished also
to have my body better, both for the rest of the reasons and so that you might
have some use of me too; since I take those belonging to him to belong equally
to myself.
