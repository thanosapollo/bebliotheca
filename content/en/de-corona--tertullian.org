* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Very lately this took place. The largess of our most excellent emperors was
being paid out in camp, and the soldiers were coming forward wreathed in
laurel. One man there, more a soldier of God, more steadfast than the rest of
his brothers who had presumed they could serve two masters, shone out alone,
his head free, the wreath idle in his hand, already known as a Christian even
by that discipline.

Then they began one by one to point him out and mock him from a distance, and
to gnash at him up close. The murmur was carried at once to the tribune, and
the man had already left the ranks. At once the tribune said, Why so different
a dress? He denied that it was lawful for him as it was for the rest. Pressed
for his reasons, he answered, I am a Christian. O soldier, glorious in God!
Then came the votes, the case was held over, and the accused was sent to the
prefects.

There and then he laid down the heaviest cloaks, taking it as an omen of being
unburdened. He loosed from his feet the most troublesome scout's boot,
beginning to stand upon holy ground. He gave back the sword, which is not
needed even for the Lord's defense. The laurel too dropped from his hand. And
now, reddened with the hope of his own blood, shod with the preparation of the
gospel, girt with the sharper word of God, wholly armed from the apostle and
better crowned with the white of martyrdom, he waits in prison for the
donative of Christ.

Then judgments began to be passed upon him, I do not know whether of
Christians, for those of the heathen are no different, as of a man abrupt and
headlong and eager to die, who, questioned about his dress, made trouble for
the Name: he alone, of course, the brave one among so many soldier brothers,
he alone a Christian. It remains, plainly, that they should even plan to
refuse martyrdoms, who have already spat out the prophecies of the same Holy
Spirit.

So they mutter that a peace so good and so long is being put at risk for them.
Nor do I doubt that some are leaving the Scriptures behind, packing their
bundles, girding themselves for flight from city to city. For they care for no
other memory of the gospel. I know their shepherds too: lions in peace, deer
in the fight. But of the questions that belong to confessions we shall teach
elsewhere.

For now, since they also put this against us, Where, though, are we forbidden
to be crowned? I will take up this more local substance of the present case,
so that those who ask out of the anxiety of ignorance may be instructed, and
those who contend in defense of the offense may be refuted, above all the
laurel-crowned Christians themselves, for whom this alone is the question, as
if a fault that admits of questioning could be held either none at all, or at
least uncertain. That it is neither none nor uncertain I shall show from this,
for the present.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

I say that not one of the faithful has ever known a crown on his head except
at a time of trial of this kind. All observe it so, from catechumens through
to confessors and martyrs, or, as the case may be, deniers. You may see for
yourself where the authority of the custom comes from, which is now the chief
question. But when it is asked why a thing is observed, it is meanwhile plain
that it is observed. Therefore a fault committed against an observance that
already claims its own name, and is warranted enough by the patronage of
consent, cannot be held either none or uncertain.

By all means let the reason be sought, but with the observance kept safe, not
for its destruction but rather for its building up, so that you may observe it
the more once you are sure of the reason as well. What sort of thing is it,
though, that a man should call an observance into question just when he has
fallen from it, and should ask where he got the observance from just when he
has left it off? Even if he wants to seem to raise the question in order to
show that he has not offended by dropping the observance, it is still plain
that he offended earlier by taking the observance on himself. For if he has
not offended today by taking the crown, he offended once by refusing it.

And so this treatise will not be for those to whom the question does not
belong, but for those who, in their eagerness to learn, bring not a quarrel
but a consultation. For this is not always under question, and I praise the
faith that believed the thing must be observed before it learned why. And it
is easy at once to demand where it is written that we should not be crowned.
But where is it written that we should be crowned? Those who demand the
patronage of Scripture on the other side prejudge that the patronage of
Scripture ought to be present on their own side as well. For if it will be
said that crowning is lawful because Scripture does not forbid it, it will
equally be turned back that crowning is not lawful because Scripture does not
command it. What will discipline do? Will it receive both, as if neither were
forbidden, or reject both, as if neither were commanded? But what is not
forbidden is freely permitted? No: what is not freely permitted is forbidden.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

And how long shall we draw the saw back and forth along this line, when we
have an observance grown old, which by coming first has made a standing rule?
If no Scripture has marked this out, custom has certainly strengthened it, and
custom has without doubt flowed from tradition. For how can a thing be put
into use if it has not first been handed down? Even when tradition is pleaded,
you say, a written authority must still be demanded. Then let us ask whether a
tradition, unless it is written, ought not to be received. We shall certainly
deny that it should be received, if no examples of other observances prejudge
the case, observances which we claim with no instrument of any Scripture, on
the title of tradition alone and then on the patronage of custom.

To begin, then, from baptism: about to enter the water, there and then, and
also a little earlier in the church under the bishop's hand, we declare that
we renounce the devil and his pomp and his angels. Then we are dipped three
times, answering something more than the Lord marked out in the gospel. After
that, being taken up, we taste beforehand the harmony of milk and honey, and
from that day we keep from the daily bath through the whole week. The
sacrament of the Eucharist, commanded by the Lord to all and at the time of a
meal, we take even in assemblies before dawn, and from no other hand than that
of those who preside. We make offerings for the dead, for their birthdays, on
the yearly day. On the Lord's day we count it a crime to fast, or to worship
from the knees. We enjoy the same freedom from Easter Day through to
Pentecost. We suffer it anxiously if any of the cup, or even of our own bread,
is struck down to the earth. At every advance and every move, at every going
in and going out, at dressing and at putting on shoes, at baths, at tables, at
lamps, at beds, at seats, wherever our way of life occupies us, we wear the
forehead with the sign.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

If you demand a law of the Scriptures for these and other such disciplines,
you will read none. Tradition will be held out to you as the author, custom as
the confirmer, and faith as the observer. The reason that will patronize
tradition and custom and faith you will either see for yourself, or you will
learn from someone who has seen it. Meanwhile you will believe that there is
some reason to which obedience is due.

I add one more example, since it will be fitting to teach from the old things
as well. Among the Jews it is so solemn a thing for their women to veil the
head that they are known by it. I ask for a law. I set the apostle aside for
now. If Rebecca, when she saw her bridegroom far off, seized a veil, a private
shame could not make a law, or, if it made one for her own case, let virgins
alone be covered, and that only when they come to be married, and not before
they have known their bridegrooms. If Susanna too, unveiled at the trial,
furnishes an argument for veiling, I can say: here too the veil was a matter
of choice. She had come as an accused woman, blushing at her infamy, hiding
her beauty with good reason, or because she already feared to please. For the
rest, I would not think that she who pleased walked veiled in her husband's
park. Grant now that she was always veiled. In her case too I ask for a law of
the dress, or in any other. If I find a law nowhere, it follows that tradition
gave this custom to habit, a custom that would one day have the apostle's
authority from the interpretation of reason.

By these examples, then, it will have been announced that even an unwritten
tradition can be defended in an observance, confirmed by custom, a fit witness
of a tradition already proved from the perseverance of the observance. Custom,
even in civil affairs, is taken for law when law is wanting, and it makes no
difference whether it stands by writing or by reason, since reason commends
even a law. Further, if law stands by reason, then everything that has stood
by reason, from whomsoever it has been brought forth, will already be law. Or
do you not think it lawful for every believer to conceive and to establish,
provided only that it agrees with God, serves discipline, and profits
salvation, when the Lord says, Why do you not also of yourselves judge what is
just? And not of judgment only, but of every sentence on things to be
examined, the apostle also says, If you are ignorant of anything, God will
reveal it to you, himself accustomed to supply counsel when he did not have a
command of the Lord, and to speak from himself, having the Spirit of God as
the guide of all truth. And so his counsel has already obtained the standing
of a divine command, from the patronage of divine reason.

Demand this reason now, with respect for tradition kept safe, by whatever
hander-on it is counted, and do not look to the author but to the authority,
and first of all to the authority of the custom itself, which is to be
cultivated for this very reason, that it may not fail to be the interpreter of
reason, so that, if God has given this too, you may then learn not whether the
custom is to be observed by you, but why.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

The reason of Christian observances is made greater when nature also defends
them, which is the first discipline of all. And therefore this first of all
prescribes that a crown does not belong on the head. I think, though, that the
God of nature is our God, who formed man and, for the fruits of things to be
sought, judged, and obtained, set certain senses in him, in a way the proper
organs of the members: he dug hearing in the ears, kindled sight in the eyes,
shut taste in the mouth, aired smell in the nostrils, weighed touch in the
hands. Through these ministries of the outer man, serving the inner man, the
fruits of the divine gifts are led down to the soul from the senses.

What, then, is the fruit from flowers? For the proper substance of crowns,
certainly the chief one, is the flowers of the field. Either scent, you say,
or color, or both together. What will the senses of color and of scent be?
Sight, I suppose, and smell. What members have been allotted these senses? The
eyes, unless I am mistaken, and the nostrils. Use flowers, then, by sight and
by smell, of which senses they are the fruit; use them through the eyes and
the nostrils, which are the members of those senses. The substance has been
handed to you by God; the fashion comes from the world. And yet not even an
extraordinary fashion drowns out ordinary use. Let flowers, set in and bound,
on thread and on rush, be to you what they are when free and loose: a thing of
looking and of breathing. Count a crown, if you like, a bundle of flowers
caught in a row, so that you may carry more at once and use them all together.
Then put them in your bosom, if they are so clean, and scatter them on the
bed, if they are so soft, and trust them to the cup, if they are so harmless.
Enjoy them in as many ways as you also sense them.

For the rest, what savor of a flower is there on the head, what sense of a
crown, except that of a bond only, by which neither color is seen, nor scent
drawn, nor softness recommended? It is as much against nature to chase a
flower with the head as food with the ear, or sound with the nostril. And
everything that is against nature deserves, among all people, the brand of a
monster; among us, though, it deserves also the charge of sacrilege against
God, the Lord and author of nature.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

You will ask, then, for the law of God? You have that common one in the public
of the world, on the natural tables to which the apostle too is used to
appeal, as when, on the veiling of the woman, he says, Does not nature herself
teach you? as when, to the Romans, saying that the nations by nature do the
things of the law, he suggests both a natural law and a legal nature. And in
the earlier letter as well, affirming that males and females had changed among
themselves the natural use of creation into one that is not natural, from the
repayment of error in turn as a punishment, he is certainly patron of the
natural things. We first know God himself according to nature, calling him, of
course, the God of gods, presuming him good, and invoking him as judge.

You ask whether nature ought to go before us in the enjoying of his creation?
Yes, lest we be seized by that force with which the rival of God corrupted the
whole creation, made over to man for certain uses, together with man himself:
whence the apostle also says that it has unwillingly fallen under vanity,
overthrown first by vain uses, then by shameful and unjust and impious ones.
So too, around the pleasures of the spectacles, creation has been defamed by
those who by nature do indeed sense that all the things from which the
spectacles are furnished are God's, but by knowledge fail to understand this
as well, that all of them have been changed by the devil. And on this matter
too, for the sake of our lovers of play, we have given satisfaction in the
Greek style as well.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

Let these crowned men, then, first acknowledge the authority of nature under
the name of common wisdom, by which men live, and the pledge of their own
religion, by which they worship the God of nature from close at hand, and
then, as it were out of abundance, look at the remaining reasons as well,
which forbid crowns to our head in particular, and indeed forbid all of them.
For we are pressed to turn from the communion of the natural discipline to the
property of the Christian, the whole of which must now be defended through the
remaining kinds of crowns as well, which seem to be provided for other uses
and built of other substances; so that, because they are not made of flowers,
whose use nature has marked, like this very military laurel, they may not be
believed to escape the interdiction of the sect because they have slipped past
the prescription of nature.

I see, therefore, that the matter must be handled both more curiously and more
fully, from the origins of the thing on to its progress and its passing away.
Secular letters are necessary for this. Secular things must be proved from
their own instruments. Small as the marks I have touched are, I think they
suffice. If there was ever a Pandora, whom Hesiod records as the first of
women, this head was first crowned by the Graces, when she was receiving gifts
from all, whence Pandora. But for us Moses, a prophetic and not a poetic
shepherd, describes the first woman, Eve, far more easily with her shameful
parts bound in leaves than with her temples bound in flowers. There is no
Pandora, then.

But the origin of the crown must be blushed at even on the score of its
falsehood; and now also on the score of its own truths. For it is certain that
there were certain authors of the thing, or at any rate illuminators.
Pherecydes reports that Saturn was crowned before all others; Diodorus that
Jupiter, after the Titans were conquered, was honored with this gift by the
rest. The same writer gives fillets also to Priapus, and to Ariadne a garland
of gold and Indian gems, the work of Vulcan, and after the gift of Liber,
afterward a constellation. Callimachus introduced the vine for Juno. So too at
Argos her image is shown wreathed with a vine shoot, treading with its feet on
a lion's skin laid beneath, insulted by the stepmother over the spoils of both
her stepsons. Hercules now carries the poplar on his head, now the wild olive,
now parsley. You have the tragedy of Cerberus; you have Pindar, and
Callimachus besides, who records that Apollo too, when he had killed the
Delphic dragon, put on the laurel as a suppliant. For suppliants among the
ancients were crowned.

Liber, the same as Osiris among the Egyptians, Harpocration argues to be
crowned with ivy by design, because it is the nature of ivy to defend the
brain against drowsiness. But the common crowd also acknowledges Liber in
another way as the founder of the crown, plainly the laurel, in which he
triumphed from the Indies, when it calls the days kept in his honor the Great
Crown. If you also turn the leaves of Leo the Egyptian, you learn that Isis
first carried ears of corn found out upon her head, a thing more for the
belly. To those who seek more, Claudius Saturninus, a commentator most
excellent in this material too, will exhibit everything. For he has there a
book on crowns, setting out their origins and their causes and their kinds and
their solemnities in such a way that you find no grace of a flower, no
gladness of a leaf, no turf or vine shoot that has not been consecrated to the
head of someone. From this we are taught enough how foreign we ought to judge
the institution of the crowned head, brought in by those and afterward
dispensed in honor of those in succession whom the world believed to be gods.

If, then, the devil works the lie of divinity, a liar from the beginning, even
in this kind, without doubt he himself had also provided those in whom the lie
of divinity was to be carried on. What, then, is to be held among the people
of the true God, a thing introduced by the devil's candidates and dedicated to
them from the beginning, and which already then was being initiated into the
worship of idols, by idols and amid idols still living? Not as if the idol
were anything; but because the things done to idols by others pertain to
demons. Further, if the things others do to idols pertain to demons, how much
more what the idols themselves did to themselves while they lived? They, of
course, procured demons for themselves, through those in whom they had feasted
before what they procured.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Hold meanwhile this conclusion, while I shake out the onset of the question.
For I already hear many other things being said, things brought in by those
whom the world believed to be gods, and yet found today in our own uses, and
in the holy things of the ancients, and in the affairs of God, and in Christ
himself; found, of course, in no other way, once a man has died, than through
these common instruments of human use. Let it be so, plainly, and I will not
yet dispute the older claim in the origins. Mercury first set out letters:
necessary, I will confess, both for the commerce of things and for our studies
toward God. And if the same man plucked the strings into sound, I will not
deny that this gift of his has also served the saints and ministered to God,
hearing David. Aesculapius first explored medicines: Isaiah too remembers that
he gave something medicinal to sick Hezekiah; and Paul knows that a little
wine profits the stomach. But Minerva also first built a ship: I shall look at
Jonah sailing and the apostles. More than that, Christ too is clothed; Paul
will even have a cloak.

If you name for the furniture of each and the vessels of each some author from
the gods of the world, I must also acknowledge Christ reclining on a couch,
and pouring the basin for the disciples' feet, and pouring in water from a
jug, and girt about with a linen towel, in the proper dress of Osiris. To a
question of this kind I everywhere answer thus: admitting, indeed, the
communion of utensils, but appealing it to the distinction between things
rational and things irrational, because the passivity of matter deceives,
overshadowing the corruption of the creation, by which it is subject to
vanity. For we say that only those things have fitted both our uses and the
higher uses and the affairs of God and Christ himself which procure mere
utilities and sure helps and honest solaces for the needs of human life, so
that they are believed to be given by the very inspiration of God, the earlier
overseer and furnisher and delighter, if you will, of his man; but that the
things which have passed beyond this order do not fit our uses, especially
those which, for that very reason, plainly cannot be recognized either in any
saint, or in the affairs of God, or in the ways of life of Christ.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

Who, finally, is found crowned, whether patriarch, or prophet, or levite, or
priest, or ruler; or after these, apostle, or evangelist, or bishop? I think
not even the very temple of God, nor the ark of the testament, nor the
tabernacle of testimony, nor the altar, nor the candlestick, to which surely
it would have been fitting to be crowned both at the first solemnity of
dedication and at the second rejoicing of restoration, if it had been worthy
of God. Yet if these were figures of us (for we are both temples of God and
altars and lights and vessels), this too they portended figuratively: that men
of God ought not to be crowned. The truth will have to answer to the image.

If perhaps you set against us Christ himself crowned, to this you shall hear
briefly for the present: so crown also you yourself; it is allowed. Yet even
that crown of impious insult the people did not share in. It was a device of
the soldiers of the Romans, from the use of a secular matter, which the people
of God never received, either in the name of public gladness or of inborn
luxury: returning from the captivity of Babylon more readily with timbrels and
pipes and psalteries than with crowns, and after meat and drink rising to play
without crowns. For neither would the description of gladness nor the notation
of luxury have been silent about the adornment or the disadornment of a crown.
So much so that Isaiah, too, who says, Because they drink wine with timbrels
and psalteries and pipes, would have said also, with crowns, if ever this use
had been among the affairs of God.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

So, when you therefore set forth that the devices of the gods of the world are
found even among the things of God, in order that among these you may claim
for common use also the crown of the head, you yourself already prescribe that
what is not found among the things of God is not to be held in the communion
of use among us. For what is so unworthy of God as what is worthy of an idol?
And what is so worthy of an idol as what is worthy of the dead as well? For it
is also thus that the dead are crowned, since they themselves at once become
idols, both in fashion and in the worship of consecration, which among us is a
second idolatry. Therefore those who lack sense, theirs it will be, in
proportion to that thing of which they lack sense, just as if they wished to
abuse it, if they did not lack sense. There is truly no difference of abusing
when the truth of using ceases: with the nature of sensing gone, by which a
man wills, how shall he abuse, when he has not that by which he uses? To us,
however, to abuse is not allowed by the apostle, teaching rather not to use;
except that neither do those abuse who sense nothing, but the whole is vacant,
and the work itself is dead, as far as idols go; alive plainly, as far as
demons go, to whom superstition pertains.

The idols of the nations, says David, are silver and gold: eyes they have, and
see not; nostrils, and smell not; hands, and they shall not handle. For in
these it is to enjoy flowers. But if such the edict pronounces those who shall
fabricate idols, such already are those who use a thing according to the
adornment of idols. All things are clean to the clean; so also unclean, all
things to the unclean. But nothing is more unclean than idols. Clean, however,
is the substance, as the things of God, and by this its condition common to
use. But the administration of the very use also counts. For I too slaughter
for myself a cock, no less than Socrates did for Aesculapius, and if the scent
of some place offends me, I burn something of Arabia; but not with the same
rite, nor the same fashion, nor the same apparatus with which it is done among
idols. For if by a bare word the creation is polluted, as the apostle teaches,
But if anyone say, This is offered to idols, touch it not; much more is it
defiled when you have leapt into the fashion and rite and apparatus of things
offered to idols. So also the crown is made an idol-offering. For with this
rite and fashion and apparatus it is sacrificed to its authors the idols,
whose proper use it is by far the most; so that what is not found among the
things of God cannot be admitted into communion.

Therefore the apostle cries out, Flee idolatry! Certainly the whole of it, and
all of it. Consider the wood, and how many thorns lie hidden in it. Nothing is
to be given to an idol; so neither is anything to be taken from an idol. If to
recline in an idol temple is foreign to the faith, what is it to be seen in
the dress of an idol? What communion has Christ with Belial? And therefore
flee. For he commands a long divorce from idolatry, to be carried on in
nothing close at hand. Even an earthly dragon sucks in men from afar with its
breath. John goes higher: Little children, he says, keep yourselves from
idols! Not now from idolatry as from an office, but from idols, that is, from
the very image of them. For it is unworthy that you, the image of the living
God, should become the image of an idol and a dead man.

Up to this point we claim for idols the property of this dress, both from the
account of its origin and from the use of superstition, and further from this,
that, while it is not referred to the things of God, it is more and more
deputed to those with whose antiquities and solemnities and offices it agrees.
Finally, the very doors, and the very victims and altars, the very ministers
and priests of them are crowned. You have the crowns of all the priestly
colleges in Claudius. But we have also interwoven that distinction of
differing rational and irrational things, meeting those who defend communion
in all things on the occasion of the examples of certain ones. To this part,
then, it remains to inspect the very crown-causes themselves, so that, while
we show them foreign, indeed even contrary to discipline, we may prove that
none of them is upheld by the patronage of reason, whereby a dress of this
kind too could be claimed for communion, just as by certain ones whose
examples are set against us.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

For indeed, to take up the very cause of the military crown, I think one must
first inquire whether soldiering in any way suits Christians. Otherwise, what
kind of thing is it to reconsider accidents, when the fault lies with the
things that went before? Do we believe it lawful for a human oath to be
superinduced upon a divine one, and to answer to another lord after Christ,
and to abjure father and mother and every kinsman, whom the law has commanded
to be honored and loved after God, whom the gospel too, counting only Christ
of more worth, has also so honored? Will it be lawful to live by the sword,
when the Lord pronounces that he who took the sword will perish by the sword?
And will the son of peace serve in battle, to whom it does not even suit to go
to law? And will he administer bonds and prison and torments and punishments,
who is not the avenger of his own injuries? Will he now keep watches either
for others more than for Christ, or on the Lord's day, when not even for
Christ? And will he stand guard for the temples he has renounced? And will he
dine there, where it does not please the apostle? And those whom by day he
drove out with exorcisms, will he defend by night, leaning and resting upon
the shaft by which the side of Christ was pierced?

And will he carry a standard that rivals Christ? And ask a sign from a
commander, who has already received one from God? And when dead, will he be
disturbed by the trumpeter's trumpet, who waits to be raised by the trumpet of
the angel? And will the Christian be burned by the discipline of the camp, to
whom it was not permitted to burn, to whom Christ granted the deserts of fire?
How many other unlawful things can be seen there to be interpreted in the
transgression of the camp's walls! To carry the very name from the camp of
light into the camp of darkness is a transgression. Plainly, if there are any
whom soldiering overtook and a later faith found, there is another condition,
as of those whom John admitted to the bath, as of the most faithful
centurions, whom Christ approves, and whom Peter catechizes; provided,
however, that, the faith having been received and the seal put on, either the
post must be deserted at once, as has been done by many, or it must be hedged
against in every way, lest anything be committed against God which is not
permitted even outside soldiering; or lastly, suffered for God, which even the
pagan faith also agreed to. For soldiering promises neither impunity for
faults nor exemption from martyrdoms. Nowhere is the Christian another man.
There is one gospel and the same Jesus, who will deny every denier and confess
every confessor, and will make safe the soul lost for his name, but will
destroy the life gained against his name on the contrary.

With him, the pagan faithful is as much a soldier as the soldier faithful is a
pagan. The state of faith does not admit necessities. There is no necessity of
offending, for those to whom there is one necessity of not offending. For even
to sacrifice and to deny outright a man is pressed by the necessity of
torments or punishments; yet to that necessity discipline does not yield,
because greater is the necessity of fearing to deny and of undergoing
martyrdom than of escaping the passion and filling the office. Moreover, a
plea of this kind subverts the whole substance of the oath, so that it loosens
the clasp even for voluntary faults. For will could also be contended to be
necessity, having, of course, that by which it may be compelled. This barrier
I would have raised also against the remaining causes of official crowns, to
which that coaxing drawing of necessity is most familiar, when therefore
either offices must be fled that we do not fall into faults, or martyrdoms
must be endured that we break the offices. Of the first kind of the question,
that soldiering itself is unlawful, I will make no more, so that the second
may be given back; lest, if I have expelled soldiering with every force, I
have then appealed in vain about the military crown. Suppose, then, that
soldiering is lawful up to the cause of the crown.

But now first I will speak of the crown itself. That laurel is sacred to
Apollo or to Liber, to the former as the god of missiles, to the latter as the
god of triumphs. So Claudius teaches, when he says also that soldiers are wont
to be wreathed with myrtle. For the myrtle is Venus's, the mother of the sons
of Aeneas, and even the paramour of Mars, through Ilia and the Romuli Roman.
But I do not believe Venus in this respect Roman together with Mars, wherein
there is the pain of a paramour. Since also the olive crowns the militia, it
is idolatry toward Minerva, equally the goddess of arms, but crowned also from
this tree when peace was struck with Neptune. In these will be the
superstition of the military garland, everywhere defiled and defiling all
things. Which things are now defiled also by their causes.

Behold the annual naming of vows: what does it seem? First in the
headquarters, second in the capitols. Take, after the places, the words too:
Then to thee, Jupiter, we vow a bull with horns adorned with gold, to be
future. What is the voice of this sentence? Certainly of denial. Even if the
Christian there is silent with his mouth, crowned in head he answers. The same
laurel is announced in the dispensing of the donative. Plainly not a
gratuitous idolatry, vending Christ for some pieces of gold, as Judas for the
silver. Will this be, You cannot serve God and mammon: to hand the hand to
mammon and depart from God? Will this be, Render to Caesar the things that are
Caesar's and to God the things that are God's: not to render the man to God
and to take away the denarius from Caesar?

The laurel of triumph is built of leaves, or of corpses? It is adorned with
fillets, or with tombs? It is anointed with unguents, or with the tears of
wives and mothers? Perhaps of certain ones even of Christians; for even among
the barbarians is Christ. Whoever has carried on his head the cause of this,
has he not fought even himself? There is also another soldiery, of the royal
households. For they are called courtiers too, munificent, and those of the
solemnities of the Caesars. But then also you are in like manner soldier and
servant of another, and if of two, of God and of Caesar, certainly then not of
Caesar, when you owe yourself to God, even in the commoner things, I believe,
the better.

The public orders also crown with laurels for public causes, but the
magistrates moreover with golden ones, as at Athens, as at Rome. There are
carried up to them also Etruscan crowns. This is the name of crowns which,
distinguished with gems and leaves of golden oak from Jupiter, for drawing out
the sacred cars, they take with palm-bearing togas. There are also provincial
golden ones, seeking heads greater by the number of images. But your order and
your magistrates and the very name of the curia is the church of Christ. You
are enrolled of him in the books of life. There is the blood of the Lord for
your purple, and the nail of his side in the cross itself; there the axes,
laid now to the trunk of the tree; there the rods from the root of Jesse. Let
the public horses look to it with their crowns. Your Lord, when he willed to
enter Jerusalem according to the Scripture, had not even a private ass. Some
trust in chariots and some in horses, but we will call upon the name of the
Lord our God. From the very dwelling in that Babylon in the Apocalypse of John
we are moved away, much less from the platform.

The common crowd too is crowned, now from the exultation of the prosperities
of princes, now from the propriety of municipal solemnities. For all public
gladness is a coaxing luxury. But you are a stranger of this world, a citizen
of the city above, Jerusalem; our citizenship, it says, is in the heavens. You
have your own accounts, your own fasti: you owe nothing to the joys of the
world, nay, you owe the contrary. For the world will rejoice, but you will
mourn. And, I suppose, he calls blessed those reading, not those crowned.
Weddings also crown the bridegrooms; and therefore we do not marry the
heathen, lest they draw us on even to idolatry, from which among them the
weddings begin. You have the law from the patriarchs indeed, you have the
apostle commanding to marry in the Lord. Secular liberty also crowns. But you
have been redeemed already by Christ, and that greatly. How will the world
manumit another's slave? Although liberty seems, yet servitude will seem: all
things in the world are imaginary and nothing true.

For even then you were free of man, redeemed by Christ, and now you are
Christ's slave, although manumitted by man. If you should think the liberty of
the world true, that you might also seal it with a crown, you have returned
into the servitude of man, which you think liberty; you have lost the liberty
of Christ, which you thought servitude. Shall the agonistic causes also be
disputed, which their very titles at once condemn, the sacred and the funeral
ones, of course? For this remains, that Olympian Jupiter, and Nemean Hercules,
and poor little Archemorus, and unhappy Antinous be crowned in the Christian,
that he himself may become the spectacle which it is disgusting to behold. We
have enumerated, I suppose, all the causes, and none of them is with us: all
foreign, profane, unlawful, once for all abjured in the attestation of the
oath. For these were the pomps of the devil and of his angels: the offices of
the world, honors, solemnities, popularities, false vows, human servitudes,
vain praises, foul glories; and in all these, idolatry, even in the mere
account of crowns, with which all these things are bound about.

Claudius will indeed preface that even the heaven, in the songs of Homer, is
crowned with stars, certainly by God, certainly for man: therefore also man
himself is to be crowned by God. But from the world there are crowned also
brothels and latrines and bakehouses and the prison and the gladiatorial
school and the very amphitheaters and the very spoliariums and the very
undertakers' rooms. How sacred and holy, how honest and clean that dress is,
judge not from one poetical heaven, but from the commerce of the whole world.
But the Christian, you say, will not defile even his own door with laurels, if
he knows how many gods the devil has even affixed to doorposts: Janus from the
door, Limentinus from the threshold, Forculus and Carna from doors and hinges,
and even among the Greeks Apollo Thyraeus and the daemons Antelii.

So far is it that he should offer to his own head the gift of idolatry, nay, I
would already say of Christ, since the head of the man is Christ: as free as
Christ himself, liable not even to a veil, much less to a bond. Moreover, what
is liable to a veil, the head of the woman, by this very fact already
occupied, does not lie vacant also to a bond. She has the burden of her
humility. If with uncovered head she ought not to be seen because of the
angels, much more crowned; perhaps then she will have scandalized those
crowned. For what is a crown on the head of a woman but a bawd of beauty, a
badge of the highest wantonness, the last denial of shame, the confection of
enticement? Therefore neither will the woman be adorned more laboriously from
the prospect of the apostle, that she be not crowned by the artifice of her
hair.

He, however, who is both the head of the man and the face of the woman, the
man of the church, Christ Jesus: what garland, I beg you, came upon him for
either sex? Of thorns, I suppose, and briars, in the figure of the faults
which the earth of the flesh brought forth for us; but the virtue of the cross
took away, dulling every sting of death in the endurance of the Lord's head;
certainly beyond the figure: the insult is at hand, and the disfigurement and
the foulness, and the savageness entwined with these, which then both defiled
and mangled the temples of the Lord. So that you now with laurel and myrtle
and olive and some more splendid leaf, and, what is more in use, with
hundredfold roses too, picked from the garden of Midas, and with lilies of
both kinds and all violets, should be crowned, perhaps even with gems and
gold; so that you emulate also that crown of Christ which afterward befell
him? But he tasted the honeycomb after the gall, nor was the King of glory
saluted by the heavenly ones before the King of the Jews was proscribed to the
cross, lowered first by the Father somewhat short of the angels, and so
crowned with glory and honor.

If for these things you owe him your head, repay him, if you can, such a one
as he offered his own for yours; or neither be you crowned with flowers, if
you cannot with thorns, because you cannot with flowers.

Keep to God his own thing unstained. He will crown it, if he will. Nay, he
wills it, finally, he even invites: To him that overcometh, he says, I will
give a crown of life. Be you also faithful unto death, and contend you also
the good contest, of which crown the apostle also justly confides one laid up
for himself. The angel of victory also receives a crown, coming forth on a
white horse that he might overcome; and another is adorned with the compass of
the rainbow in the heavenly meadow. The elders also sit crowned, and with the
same gold the very Son of Man flashes above the cloud. If such are the images
in the vision, what shall the truths be in the reality? Look on those, adore
those.

Why condemn the head to the neckerchief or the neckband, when it is destined
for the diadem? For Christ Jesus has made us kings also to God and his Father.
What have you to do with a flower that is to die? You have the flower from the
rod of Jesse, over whom the whole grace of the divine Spirit rested, a flower
incorrupt, unfading, everlasting; which also the good soldier, by choosing,
advanced in the heavenly ordination.

Blush you, his fellow soldiers, to be judged hereafter not by him, but by some
soldier of Mithras. For he, when he is initiated in the cave, in the camp
truly of darkness, when the crown has been offered to him, a sword having been
put between, as it were a mime of martyrdom, then fitted to his own head, is
warned to strike it from his head with his opposing hand, and to transfer it,
if you will, to his shoulder, saying that Mithras is his crown. And from then
on he is never crowned, and he has this as a sign for the proving of himself,
wherever he may be tried concerning the oath; and at once he is believed a
soldier of Mithras if he has thrown down the crown, if he has said that it is
in his own god. Let us acknowledge the wiles of the devil, affecting certain
things also of the divine, for this reason, that he may confound and judge us
in the faith of his own.
