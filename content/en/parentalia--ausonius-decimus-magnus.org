** Preface in prose
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

I know that my little verses tend to be read with distaste: they have earned
as much. Yet subject matter often recommends some, and now and then the mere
wording of a title may catch a reader, so that, won over by the festive
heading, he is willing to put up with the clumsiness as well. This little work
is neither charming in its matter nor pleasant in its name. It has a sad
piety, in which I commemorate the deaths of those dear to me with a grieving
affection. The title of the booklet is Parentalia. This is a day of ancient
name, instituted as far back as Numa for the offerings due to kinsfolk: and
the reverence of the living holds nothing more sacred than to remember the
lost with honor.

** A second preface, marked in verse
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

The names of those I love, now laid away with due burial, wept first with
tears, I will now recall in measures:

bare, without ornament, and lacking the polish of speech. Funeral honor is
enough for the rites of the dead, a dirge dutiful enough in its burial
laments, so that you may not pass the yearly gifts in silence which Numa
dedicates as solemn rites to kindred shades, as the rank of death or of
kinship asks. This is enough for tombs, enough for those who lack earth: to
call souls by voice has the force of a funeral. Laid ashes rejoice to hear
their names spoken: monuments command this also with writing on their faces.
Even he who lacked the urn of a sad tomb will be almost buried once his name
is spoken thrice. But you, whoever you are, reader, who deign to recall the
fates of my people in sad elegies, may you run through the unshaken seasons of
your life and weep no deaths beyond what is just.

** JULIUS AUSONIUS, FATHER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h1
:END:

First among these is father Ausonius, whom to set first, though a son may
hesitate, order commands: God's care, in that he lived out the honor of a calm
old age and completed eleven times two Olympiads. He saw all that he wished
turn out well: whatever he hoped for came to him as he wished, not because the
Fates were too indulgent, but because that man's prayers were so moderate.

His age matched him with the Seven Sages, whose teaching he cultivated in his
character, so that he might live the art of the wise rather than speak it,
though he was no stranger to an eloquent mind, gifted also to stretch out the
lives of men by the art of healing and to lengthen the delays of fate. Hence
this reverence remains for a life fully lived, that our age has given him this
title: as Ausonius had no one to follow, so he has none now who imitates him.

** AEMILIA AEONIA, MOTHER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

Next you, mother Aeonia, of mixed blood, from a Tarbellian mother and an
Aeduan father. To you fell every virtue of an obedient wife, the fame of
chastity and of a wool-working hand, loyalty in marriage, care in governing
children, and a gracious gravity and a cheerful seriousness. Embracing forever
the peaceful shades of your husband, once you warmed his bed in life; now,
dead, warm his tomb.

** AEMILIUS MAGNUS ARBORIUS, UNCLE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1
:END:

Piety has been observed by me, father and mother named first, but Arborius
shrinks from being called third. To mention him first would be a sin to me,
with my father second; again, not to set him first is almost a sin. A mean
must be used, and he must be named next before the rest, though he will be
second to my father.

You, brother of my mother and of one mind with my father, and to me what
father and mother were, who from my nursing, boyhood, youth, and manhood
adorned me with arts that I am glad to have learned: the learned gown of
Palladian Toulouse preferred you to itself, Narbonese Gaul set you first; you
adorned its tribunal with Latin speech, and the forums of the Iberians and
those of the Nine Peoples. From here, with growing fame, you were sought
as far as Europe, and Constantinople flourished with you as its rhetor. You,
through a thousand measures, through a thousand oracles of speech, learned,
eloquent, swift, and sure of memory: you, after I was given over in my first
years and pleased you, said that I as a son was enough for you. You declared
that I was a bright honor to you and to my fathers, and you dictated words the
Fates should mark as mine. Therefore farewell, uncle, allotted a seat in
Elysium: I pour these songs for you from your own Muses.

* Section 4
:PROPERTIES:

** CAECILIUS ARGICIUS ARBORIUS, GRANDFATHER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

Do not desert your pious office, dutiful page: after these let us commemorate
my mother's father, Arborius, a name drawn from an Aeduan line, embracing many
houses in its nobility, wherever the province of Lyon is, wherever the
powerful Aedui, and wherever Vienna lies under the Alpine ridge.

But too envious a distress settled on that proud stock and its wealth; for
grandfather and father were proscribed when Victorinus held the throne as
leader, and the empire fell back upon the Tetrici. Then, a fugitive in the
lands where the Adour bursts forth and the rage of the Tarbellian ocean roars,
fearing the shafts of Fortune that had long been raging, the lot of poor
Aemilia entangled him. Soon a thin store of money, sought with much effort,
gave a weary man comfort, but not riches. You knew the measures of the sky and
the stars that share the secrets of fate, pursuing the study in secret. The
pattern of my life too was not unknown to you, which you had stored on marked
tablets, never published; but my mother's care unpicked it, which the anxious
care of a timid grandfather had covered. When you had led your life through
nine times ten years, having felt the shafts of the goddess Fortune that must
be feared, you wept a son lost through three decades, wounded: by that death
you were robbed of light. Yet you used to say that long consolations warmed
you, that a special honor awaited my destiny; and now, mixed in the councils
of earlier souls, you have at last the known fates of your grandson. You feel
that I, quaestor, that I, prefect and likewise consul, commemorate you with an
honorary gift.

** AEMILIA CORINTHIA MAURA, GRANDMOTHER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7-h1
:END:

Now name Aemilia, your grandmother, pious care of a grandson, who was wife to
the Arborius just named. A playful name was given her, because once, dark of
skin, she was called Maura among her age-mates. But she was not dark in mind,
which would have been brighter than a swan and whiter than untrodden snow. Not
ready to pardon shameful pleasures, she held herself and her own to the
plumb-line. She, when I was snatched from the cradle and from my mother's
breast, trained me with a gentle touch under stern commands. Grant quiet ashes
to my grandmother, you peaceful shades forever, if I speak pious words.

** AEMILIA HILARIA, MATERNAL AUNT, CONSECRATED VIRGIN
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8-h1
:END:

And you, maternal aunt by degree of kinship, but in a mother's place to be
commemorated by a son's pious affection, Aemilia, who in the cradle gained the
name Hilaria, because you were cheerful and gracious after the likeness of a
boy: you frankly presented a true young man, trying the physician's arts in
the manner of men. Hatred of the female sex was always yours, and from that
grew the love of dedicated virginity, which was cultivated through nine times
seven years, and the end of life was itself the end of chastity.

These last rites I render as a son, because you cherished me with warnings and
love as a mother would.

** CL. CONTEMTUS AND JULIUS CALLIPPIO, PATERNAL UNCLES
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9-h1
:END:

And remember my paternal uncles, elegy, in song, Contemtus, whom the Rutupine
land covers; great wealth of various fortune, sought out, perished, safe under
no heir's name; for he was snatched in happy and still flourishing years
across the sea, and died unknown to his brothers. Julius drew out his fate
into a long old age, afflicted by countless losses, who, gracious, bland, and
easy at a rich table, had us as heirs in name alone. Both pious, alike in
face, mixing jest and earnest, they did not have an equal fortune of life.
Though you lie apart in tombs and in honor, take this common gift: farewell.

** ATTUSIUS LUCANUS TALISIUS, FATHER-IN-LAW
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10-h1
:END:

Whoever would celebrate the nobles and the ancient senate and the bright
lineages of Bordeaux from its rising, let him remember you and your house,
Lucanus Talisius, who adorned your old forefathers with your character. Fair
in the honor of your face, gracious with a calm breast, though greater still
by an eloquent mind: through hunting and the tillage of the country and
elegance of living you spent your whole life, despising public affairs:
wishing to be known among the first, refusing to be first, living apart by
your own choice. You wished me as a son-in-law in my blooming youth: this only
was given, to wish, not also to have. The gods approve the vows, and the Fates
fulfill what favors holy minds, what good men have willed. And now under the
honor of a lasting tomb you feel how reverent I remain toward your daughter
and toward you. For I, a son-in-law still unwed, now pay these pious gifts:
for I shall never cease to be both unwed and a son-in-law.

* Section 9
:PROPERTIES:

** ATTUSIA LUCANA SABINA, WIFE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11-h1
:END:

Thus far our dirge has sung, in pious measures, those who were dear and also
wept with a just funeral. Now grief and torment and a wound that cannot be
handled: the death of a wife torn from me must be recalled. Noble from her
ancestors and bright by senatorial origin, Sabina was brighter still by good
character. I mourned you, a young man cheated in my first years, and through
nine Olympiads I weep you, unwed. Nor may I lull to sleep a grief overgrown
with age; for it always grows raw for me, almost fresh. Others admit the
comforts of a painful time: for me the long day makes these wounds heavier.

I twist the gray hairs cheated by a celibate life, and the more I am alone,
the more I live in sorrow. The wound is fed because the mute house is silent
and the bed is cold, because I share neither evils nor goods with anyone. I
grieve if another has a good wife; I grieve, on the other hand, if a bad one:
you are always present to me as the pattern. You come to me as a cross from
either side: if she is bad, because you were unlike; if good, because she is
like. I do not lament empty wealth and hollow joys, but that you were snatched
from me, a young man, while I was a young husband. Cheerful, chaste, serious,
renowned in birth and renowned in beauty, both the grief and the glory of
Ausonius her husband, you who, about to complete four times seven Decembers,
left two children, our pledges. They, by God's favor, as your vows were,
flourish, heaped with the goods you wished, and I pray they may thrive, and
that at last, with both surviving, my ashes may announce this to yours.

** PASTOR, GRANDSON BY A SON
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13-h1
:END:

You too, untimely boy, broke off the ripe griefs of a grandfather with a
bitter mourning, Pastor, dear grandson, whose sure hope you would have been,
third offspring of a western father, a name that chance had given (because a
pipe first sounded a pastoral tune at your birth), understood too late as a
proof of a short life: because breath flees from the reeds when they are
blown. You die, struck by the weight of a tile thrown down, which a workman's
hand cast from a roof. It was not a workman's hand: that hand was the sure
hand of bloody fate, about to furnish its victim. Alas, what vows, what
joys you break for me, Pastor! That tile, thrown, aimed at my head. O you who
were more worthy to groan at the fates of my old age and to complain, sad, at
my funeral!

** JULIA DRYADIA, SISTER
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h14-h1
:END:

If there was any virtue that a wise woman would wish to be her own, sister
Dryadia did not lack it, and she had many besides that the stronger sex and
the nobility of men would wish to have: skilled enough to tend her life with
cultivation and to guard her fame, skilled in good manners, teaching her own,
to whom truth was dearer than life, and whose one care was to know God and to
love her brother before others.

She lost her husband while still young, but shunning light ways she matched
austere old women in character and drew out a cheerful life through six
decades, and died in the house and under the roof where her father died.

Muse, grant Avitianus, my own brother,

** VAL. LATINUS EUROMIUS, SON-IN-LAW
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h16-h1
:END:

O glory of a bright house, o bitter death to me, Euromius, son-in-law chosen
from a band of young men, you die, snatched from me in the flower of first
youth, scarcely well known as father of a rejoicing son. You, sprung from a
stock of nobles, and outstripping even those from whom your race of bright
nobility came, handsome of face, good in mind, eloquent, and thriving in every
dexterity, and outstanding in loyalty.

The prefect's seat, the Illyrian shore knew this with you as governor, and the
treasury itself as client. Yet no praise was taken from you by the shortness
of life: mature in nature, you die untimely in death.

** POMPONIUS MAXIMUS, KINSMAN
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h17-h1
:END:

And you, a brother not by blood but in a brother's place, Maximus, my dirge
shall sing, now dead: for you, joined as husband to my sister, leave her a
widow, robbed of the fruit of your years. Not your house alone felt this: the
senate of Bordeaux felt the bitter fall, wounded, ah, thriving when you were
foremost, slipping back when you failed, and falling, at your death, upon
Valentinus. Alas, why, Maximus, snatched from us, do you not enjoy a son who
was the fruit and flower of grandsons? But you do enjoy him, if a divine
portion inhabits the shades and it is given to know the joys that will one day
be. Here even a longer fruit of the blessed is held to be this: to have
anticipated long what you now share.

** VERIA LICERIA, WIFE OF ARBORIUS, SISTER'S SON
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h18-h1

You too, whether daughter-in-law to me in name, or in a daughter's place,
Veria, take the song of last honor.

If her honesty, if her beauty and fame and the loyalty of an obedient wife and
a wool-working hand were now to be praised, from far off and from the deepest
shades the voice of her great-grandfather Eusebius would have to be summoned.
Since he, long since stored away after a finished life, has transferred his
part into my words, receive the funeral laments, mourned granddaughter, wife
of my Arborius, to be commemorated, who, leaving small comforts for a huge
grief, abandon your children, and so torment us the more. But that you may not
lack the care of a beloved husband, you now tend a monument joined to your
marriage-chamber. Here, where the first wedding was, there is the sad seat of
the tomb: you can be called bride more than buried.

** FL. SANCTUS, HUSBAND OF PUDENTILLA, SISTER OF MY SABINA
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h20-h1
:END:

You who cultivate jests and gladness, who condemn sadness, and fear no one,
and live so as not to be feared, who catch no one with snares and provoke no
one with a lawsuit, but live a just life, both merciful and wise: honor with a
pious mouth and with good words the peaceful shades and the last mild rites of
Sanctus, who carried out his military service diligently, with no storm, in
whose governorship the Rutupine land rejoiced, whose calm old age of eighty
years changed with no worse day. Therefore pray, favoring, that as his times
of life were, so Sanctus may spend his ease among the shades.

** NAMIA PUDENTILLA, KINSWOMAN
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h21-h1
:END:

And you, address Pudentilla with last words, you who have care of fame and of
honesty. She was noble, frugal, upright, cheerful, chaste, comely; she held
the marriage of Sanctus without break. Keeping inviolate the praises of a
chaste life, she ruled her own wealth while her husband lived at ease: not on
that account reproaching her husband, or with clouded brow, because a woman
alone managed the whole house.

Alas, too young, but glad with son and husband still living, she went,
enduring her last fate. Once of one mind with our Sabina, and her full sister,
and called sister by me with an unoffending name. Even now a pious care draws
back her peaceful shades and honors Pudentilla with the tribute of one who
speaks.

** SEVERUS CENSOR JULIANUS, FELLOW FATHER-IN-LAW
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h24-h1
:END:

Cease, o ancient Calpurnian names, to think Frugi a glory proper to your house
alone, and let not Cato the Censor always alone, nor Aristides alone the Just,
take pleasure in these titles. For whoever was wise and who cultivated truth
and faith, let him take himself as your companion, Censor. You were grave and
gracious, mild with justice, trained to join temperance to austerity. You,
though I was not adopted by you nor joined by blood, wished to ally our houses
as fellow fathers-in-law. Surely some likeness of your character in us offered
an image of our nature; or already the turning of Fortune was spinning itself
so that the Fates gave such good vows their weight. If you feel anything among
the shades, this warms your mind: that what you had wished to happen, God also
willed.

** PAULINUS AND DRYADIA, CHILDREN OF PAULINUS AND MEGENTIRA, MY NIECE
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h25-h1
:END:

You who bore your father's name and face, Paulinus, who were the likeness of a
lost father; because of whom the grief of a wretched mother had begun to
recede, when your form offered your father, and restored his character, and to
his character added that which father Paulinus lacked, eloquence: you are
snatched in happy and growing years and trouble a mother's heart still raw.
For we weep also a sister snatched from the seat of her marriage-chamber,
Dryadia, alas, by a death not ripe. We weep, I first, who am the mother's
uncle, and I love you as the offspring of children. She died among a mother's
hands and a father's kisses; you, far off in the Spanish region, how, a tender
and new flower, you are plucked in first age, not yet a youth girt with purple
cheeks! The mother, now dead, had then brought forth four births, but has
already given doubled deaths to the tombs. Let this be enough, father
Paulinus; a division has been made: the rest of the offspring is owed to the
mother.

** PAULINUS, SISTER'S SON-IN-LAW
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h26-h1
:END:

Whoever loves a glad nature and even character, and who cultivates faith with
holy piety, let him, honoring with me the shades of Paulinus, sprinkle the
yearly cakes with a friend's tears, Paulinus, my equal in age, who gained my
sister's daughter in marriage: thus almost a son-in-law to me. Your mother was

Having earned the desks of a prefect, then set over the Libyan accounts, you
take rich rewards. For Spanish Tarraco offered you the tribunal of a
corrector, aiming to be a client to you. You, honoring your mother-in-law as a
mother, could not be held a mere kinsman, since you enjoyed a son's place. You
lived faithful among friends in concord, having completed eighteen Olympiads.

** AEMILIA MELANIA, SISTER

Aemilia also, a sister scarcely known to me, receive the complaints which a
sad elegy owes to your ash. An equal infancy joined our cradles, though you

** POMPONIA URBICA, FELLOW MOTHER-IN-LAW, WIFE OF JULIANUS THE CENSOR
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h32-h1
:END:

A woman of bright birth, and of the old manners as well, Urbica, ennobled by
the Censor's marriage-bed; strong in inborn virtues and increased also by
those which her husband taught, which father and mother taught: those which
Tanaquil had, which Pythagorean Theano had, and she who died without example
at her husband's death. And if fate had granted you to make such an exchange,
the Censor would still be living in this time of ours; but you, not long
tortured under a widowed age, at once followed your husband with the end you
wished. Now we bring you yearly due rites with sad laments, I, Ausonius,
fellow father-in-law, with son-in-law and children.
