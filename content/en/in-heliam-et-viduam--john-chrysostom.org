Elijah did, but foretold that it was about to come. What then did the king who
was reigning in those times? He put on sackcloth, it says; for the calamity
had humbled him; yet even so humbled, when he heard a certain woman wailing
over =51.344= things that had happened because of the famine, he was then
carried into such wrath as to cry out at once and say, "So may God do to me,
and more also, if the head of Elisha the son of Shaphat shall stand upon him
today." Did you see the king's wrath? Learn the woman's philosophy; for she,
finding not the one who had foretold a famine, but the one who had brought it
on, and being near the city, was not indignant, did not grow savage, did not
call others to punishment, but even obeyed with much gentleness.

8. And you know that whenever we are occupied about some need, we often do not
even look with pleasure on our friends, but we are even impatient toward them;
and when so great an affliction is present, even the light itself seems to
trouble us; and this again one may learn from a Jewish example. When then
Moses came announcing to the Jews countless good things, deliverance from
tyranny, freedom, and return to the ancient fatherland: "Seeing him," it says,
"they did not listen to him from faintness of soul, and from the hard labors."
Those men, seeing the one who brought such good tidings, turned away; but she,
seeing the prophet come, not so as to loose the famine, but so as even to
become a burden to her as well, suffered nothing of this kind. And they were
so impatient because of the toil of the works; but she, not of toil, but of
famine pressing heavily (and great is the difference between toil and famine),
not only did not turn away from the man, but even emptied all her poverty, so
as to receive the one who had brought the famine upon them. And she went, it
says, to fetch water, and the prophet cried out and said, "Fetch me also
bread, and I will eat." What then did the woman do? Not even here does she
grow impatient, but what does she say? "As the Lord your God lives, if I have
a cake, but only so much as a handful of flour." Why does she swear? The
prophet asked for bread, and she did not have bread. She feared then lest,
while she was baking, roasting, and preparing, and then delaying, the prophet,
not waiting out the delay, might leap away, and the quarry of hospitality
might flee. For this reason she forestalled him with an oath, saying not, "I
have no flour," but, "There is no cake, yet there is flour." And she does not
assure him by the oath only, but also by the very demonstration of the facts.
"For behold," she says, "I am gathering two little sticks, and I will go in,
and I will make them for my children, and we shall eat, and we shall die." Let
those who build splendid houses, and buy costly fields, and lead herds of
servants about the marketplace, hear; or rather let both the well-supplied and
the poor all hear; for after this widow there will no longer be any defense
for anyone.

So many were the hindrances for her, yet she cut through all those things and
passed beyond them. Hear then. She was a foreigner: this is one hindrance. A
Sidonian: a second hindrance. For it is not the same simply to be a foreigner,
and to be a Sidonian of the most wicked city; for as an extreme =51.345=
example of wickedness, Christ brought that city forward in the Gospels. She
was then both a foreigner, and a Sidonian, and a woman, the weak sex and in
every way needing help. Widowhood was added as well, a fourth hindrance; a
fifth, greater than all those, the care of raising children. Let the widows
who are also rearing children hear, that this pretext was not enough for not
doing almsgiving, nor for not receiving strangers; and only a handful of flour
was left, and

after that death was expected. For you, even if you empty all your money, even
if you strip yourself of your substance, can go to the doors of others and
find consolation; but then it was not even possible to beg, so had the famine
silted up all the harbors. Yet none of these things hindered her. Let me speak
also of a seventh hindrance, the very man who was about to be a guest to the
woman. For he was neither a kinsman, nor an acquaintance, but a stranger and
an alien, and by the very account of piety separated from her. And not only a
stranger and an alien, but he himself was the one who had brought on the
famine. 9. Yet none of these things turned the woman aside, but she was giving
food to the mouth that had consumed all her food, and she was feeding the
author of the famine with the remnants of the famine. "Because of you," she
says, "all my substance has come down to this handful; yet I do not spare even
this handful because of you, but I will give over both myself and the children
to death, in order that you," she says, "the author of the straitness, may not
even have a small sense of the straitness." What other excess of hospitality
could one invent? It is not possible to find any. She saw a stranger, and at
once forgot nature, and ignored the birth-pangs, and looking toward the choir
of her children, she was not broken.

And I know that I have often heard many saying that So-and-so, seeing a poor
man, having stripped off the little tunic which alone he had on, clothed the
naked man, and himself, having borrowed a garment from another, went away
thus; and this seemed to be a great and wonderful thing. And indeed it is
truly great; but this widow's deed is far greater than this. For that man,
having stripped himself and clothed the naked, was able to receive a garment
from another; but she, putting forth the handful of flour, was not able to
receive another handful; nor was the danger for her only as far as nakedness,
but after that death was expected both for her and for the children. When
therefore neither poverty, nor the care of raising children, nor a harsh
famine, nor such beggary, nor expected death, hinders, what defense shall we
who are well-supplied have? what shall the poor have? "As the Lord your God
lives, if I have a cake, but only so much as a handful of flour in the jar,
and a little oil in the cruse; and behold I am gathering two little sticks,
and I will go in, and I will make them for my children, and we shall eat, and
we shall die." Let each man write this pitiable, or rather let each write this
blessed voice, worthy of the heavens, on the walls of his own house, in the
chamber in which we sleep, in the room in which we take breakfast.

This at home, this =51.346= in the marketplace, this in the gatherings of
friends, this going to a lawcourt, this entering, this going out, let each man
rehearse the voice; and I would strongly insist that not even if someone were
of stone, and of iron, and adamant, will he endure to send away a poor man who
has come with empty hands, if he inscribes this voice, if he has the widow
before his eyes. But perhaps someone will say this: "Bring me a prophet too,
and I will receive him with the same goodwill." Promise this, and I bring you
the prophet. And why do I speak of the prophet? I bring you the Master of the
prophet himself, our common God and Lord, Christ. For he himself says, "You
saw me hungry, and you fed me." And if some disbelieve the voice, and neglect
courtesy, they will then learn this through chastisement and punishment. For
just as those who have neglected Christ himself, so they will go away to
unbearable chastisement. So then those who feed the poor, as having served
Christ himself, are led into the kingdom of the heavens. 10. Perhaps what has
been said is more than needful. Would that it were possible to spend all the
days on the words concerning almsgiving. But if these things seem to you to be
enough, come, let us recapitulate them all. I said why the prophet was sent to
the widow, that you may not count poverty blessed, that you may not marvel at
wealth, that you may not think the well-supplied man enviable, that you may
not think the one living in beggary wretched and pitiable, that you may learn
the Jewish wickedness. For it is God's custom, when he is about to punish,
also to make his defense to us through the things themselves; that afterward,
seeing the common Savior of all driven out by those, but received by the
nations, you may not marvel and be at a loss, having learned from of old their
ingratitude, and that it is their custom to drive out their benefactors; that
you may not think the prophet's prayer, and the prolongation of the
punishment, to be of cruelty, but of divine zeal and care; that you may learn
that even in the greatest achievements our nature still needs chastening; that
when you are called to the same zeal as the prophet, you may not think the
imitation impossible. I spoke of the widow, how, being in so great a strait,
with the famine constraining her, she did not even cast a bitter word at the
prophet, though it was likely; and I showed this too from the Jewish tempers;
but she suffered nothing of that kind, but received him with all courtesy, and
emptied all her poverty for his honor, and though she was a Sidonian and a
foreigner, she had neither heard the prophets philosophizing about almsgiving,
nor Christ saying, "You saw me hungry, and you fed me." What pardon then shall
we have, if after such exhortations, if after the promise of such prizes, if
after the kingdom of the heavens, we should not come up to the same measure of
courtesy as the widow? For she was both a Sidonian, and a foreigner, and a
widow woman, and she was caring for many children, and she saw a dangerous
famine and expected death, and she was about to receive an unknown man and the
one who had brought on the famine, and not even so did she spare the handful;
but we =51.347= who have had a share in prophecy, and who enjoy divine
teachings, and who have many things to philosophize about the things to come,
and who do not even see a famine pressing, and who possess far more than the
woman, what defense shall we be able to put forward, sparing our goods, and
not sparing our own salvation? In order then that we may escape =51.348= those
harsh punishments, let us show all compassion toward the poor, that we too may
be counted worthy of the good things to come, by the grace and love for
mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, with whom be glory to the Father, together
with the Holy Spirit, unto the ages of ages. Amen.
