CATECHETICAL DISCOURSE. Prologue. The discourse of catechesis is necessary for
those who preside over the mystery of piety, so that the church may be
multiplied by the addition of those being saved, when the faithful word
according to teaching is brought to the hearing of the unbelievers. Yet the
same manner of teaching will not fit all who come to the word, but it is
proper to adapt the catechesis also according to the differences of the
religions, looking toward the same aim of the word, but not using the
constructions in the same way in each case. For the one who judaizes is
preoccupied with other assumptions, and the one who lives with Hellenism with
others, and the Anomoean and the Manichaean and those according to Marcion and
Valentinus and Basilides and the rest of the catalogue of those wandering
according to the heresies, each preoccupied with their own assumptions, make
necessary the fight toward their suspicions; for according to the form of the
disease the manner of the therapy also must be adapted. You will not heal with
the same things the polytheism of the Greek and the unbelief of the Jew
concerning the only-begotten God, nor from the same things will you overturn
for those led astray according to the heresies the deceived myth-makings about
the doctrines; for not through the same things by which one would set
Sabellius right will one also benefit the Anomoean, nor does the fight toward
the Manichaean also profit the Jew, but one must, as has been said, look
toward the preconceptions of men and make the discourse according to the error
lying in each, putting forward certain reasonable beginnings and premises in
each discussion, so that through the things confessed on both sides the truth
may be unveiled according to sequence. Therefore when the discussion is toward
one of those who hellenize, it would be well to make this the beginning of the
discourse. Whether he has supposed the divine to be, or whether he is carried
with the dogma of the atheists? If then he should say it is not, he will be
led from the things technically and wisely administered according to the world
to confess through these that there is some power shown forth in them and
lying above the whole; but if he should not doubt that it is, yet be carried
in his suspicions into a multitude of gods, let us use such a sequence toward
him. Whether he holds the divine to be perfect or deficient? And when he, as
is likely, bears witness of perfection to the divine nature, let us demand of
him the perfect through all the things contemplated in deity, so that the
divine may not be viewed as mixed from opposites, from deficient and perfect.
But whether according to power, or according to the conception of the good, or
according to the wise and the incorruptible and the everlasting and whatever
other thought fitting to God happens to be attached to the contemplation, he
will reasonably agree according to the sequence of this that perfection is
viewed about the divine nature in everything. And this being granted it would
no longer be hard to bring round the scattering of the mind into a multitude
of gods toward the confession of one deity. For if he should grant that the
perfect in everything is confessed about the subject, and should say that
there are many perfect things characterized through the same things, there is
every necessity, in the case of things distinguished by no variation but
viewed in the same things, either to show the peculiar or, if the thought
grasps nothing peculiarly in those in which the distinguishing is not, not to
suspect the distinction. For if he should find the difference neither by the
more and the less, because the account of perfection does not admit
diminution, nor that according to the worse and


the more honorable; for that of which the appellation of the worse is not
absent would no longer have a supposition of deity; nor according to the
ancient and the recent; for what is not always is outside the supposition
about the divine; but one and the same is the account of deity, no peculiarity
being reasonably found in anything, there is every necessity that the
wandering imagination about the multitude of gods be squeezed together toward
the confession of one deity. For if the good and the just, the wise and the
powerful, should be said in the same way, and incorruptibility and
everlastingness and every pious thought should be confessed in the same
manner, every difference according to every account being taken away, the
multitude of gods is of necessity taken away together from the dogma, the
identity through all things bringing the faith round to the one.

1 But since the account of piety also knows how to see a certain distinction
of hypostases in the unity of the nature, so that by the fight toward the
Greeks the discourse may not be carried down for us toward Judaism, it is
proper again by a certain technical distinction to set right the error about
this also. For not even by those outside our dogma has the divine been
supposed to be without reason; and this, being confessed by them, will
sufficiently articulate our account. For he who confesses that God is not
without reason will altogether agree that the one who is not without reason
has a Word. But indeed the human word also is said homonymously. Therefore if
he should say that he suspects the Word of God also according to likeness of
the things among us, he will in this way be transferred toward the higher
supposition. For there is every necessity to believe the Word to be
corresponding to the nature, as also all the other things. For some power and
life and wisdom also is seen about the human; but no one from the homonymy
would suspect such a life or power or wisdom also in the case of God, but
toward the measure of our nature the impressions of such names also are
brought down together. For since our nature is corruptible and weak, for this
reason the life is short-lived, the power without subsistence, the word
without compactness. But in the case of the nature that lies above, everything
said about it is raised together with the greatness of what is contemplated.
Therefore even if Word of God is said, he will not be thought to have his
hypostasis also in the impulse of the one who speaks, passing over into the
non-existent according to likeness of ours; but as our nature, being
perishable, has also a perishable word, so the incorruptible and ever-standing
nature has an everlasting and subsisting Word. If then this should be
confessed according to sequence, that the Word of God subsists everlastingly,
there is every necessity to confess that the hypostasis of the Word is in
life. For it is not pious to think that the Word subsists without soul after
the likeness of stones. But if he subsists, being some intelligent and
bodiless thing, he lives altogether; but if he is separated from living, he is
not altogether in hypostasis either. But indeed it has been shown to be
impious that the Word of God is without hypostasis. Therefore it has been
shown together according to sequence that this Word is viewed in life. And the
nature of the Word being believed, as is likely, to be simple and showing in
itself no doubleness and composition, no longer would anyone view the Word in
life according to participation of life; for such a supposition would not be
outside composition, to say that the one is in the other; but there is every
necessity, simplicity being confessed, to think the Word to be life itself,
not a participation of life. If then the Word who is life lives, he also has
altogether a power of choice; for none of the living is without choice. And it
is pious to reckon according to sequence that this choice is also powerful.
For if one should not confess the powerful, he will altogether construct the
powerless. But indeed the powerless is far from the supposition about the
divine. For none of the things that are unfitting is viewed about the divine
nature, and there is every necessity to confess the power of the Word to be as
great as the purpose also is, so that some mixture of opposites and a
running-together may not be viewed about the simple, powerlessness and power
being viewed in the same purpose, if he should


be able in some respect, but be in a powerless state toward something; and
that the choice of the Word, being able in all things, has its inclination
toward none of the evils; for the impulse toward vice is alien to the divine
nature; but that whatever is good, this it also wills, and willing it is
altogether also able, and being able it is not without activity, but brings
every purpose of good into activity. And the world is good, and all the things
in it viewed wisely and technically. Are all things then works of the Word who
lives and subsists, because he is Word of God, and who chooses, because he
lives; and who is able in whatever he may choose, and who chooses the good and
the wise altogether, and whatever belongs to the better signification? Since
then the world is confessed to be some good, and it has been shown through
what has been said that the world is a work of the Word, of the one who both
chooses the good and is able, and this Word is other besides the one whose
Word he is; for this also is in a certain way among the things said
relatively, since the father of the Word must altogether be understood
together with the Word; for there would not be a word, if he were not word of
someone; if then the mind of the hearers distinguishes by the relative of the
signification both the Word himself and the one from whom he is, our mystery
would no longer be in danger of being carried together, while fighting with
the Hellenic assumptions, with those who honor the things of the Jews; but it
will equally escape the absurdity of both, confessing both the living Word of
God, both active and productive, which the Jew does not accept, and that he
does not differ according to nature, both the Word himself and the one from
whom he is. For as in our case we say the word is from the mind, being neither
wholly the same as the mind, nor altogether other; for by being from that it
is some other and is not that; but by bringing the mind itself into the
manifest it would no longer be suspected as some other besides that, but being
one according to nature it is other in the subject; so also the Word of God,
by subsisting by himself, is divided toward that one from whom he has the
hypostasis; but by showing in himself these things which are observed about
God, he is the same according to nature as that one who is found through the
same marks; for whether goodness, or power, or wisdom, or being everlasting,
or being incapable of vice and death and corruption, or being perfect in
everything, or whatever such thing at all one should make a sign of the grasp
of the Father, through the same signs he will find also the Word who subsists
from him.

2 And as we came to know the Word from the things among us by way of ascent
upon the nature that lies above, according to the same manner we shall also be
led to the thought about the Spirit, viewing in the nature among us certain
shadows and imitations of the unspeakable power. But in our case the spirit is
the drawing of air, of an alien thing being of necessity drawn in and poured
forth toward the constitution of the body, which at the time of the utterance
of the word becomes voice, making manifest in itself the power of the word.
But in the case of the divine nature it has been thought pious that there is a
Spirit of God, just as it was also granted that there is a Word of God,
because the Word of God must not be more deficient than our word, if, while
this is viewed with spirit, that one should be believed to be without spirit.
Yet it is not fitting to God to think that some alien thing according to
likeness of our spirit flows upon God from outside and that the Spirit comes
to be in him; but as, having heard Word of God, we did not think the Word some
thing without hypostasis, nor coming to be from learning, nor appearing
through voice, nor being dissolved after being brought forth, nor suffering
any other such thing as the passions that are viewed about our word, but
subsisting essentially, both choosing and active and all-powerful; so also,
having learned Spirit of God, the one who accompanies the Word and makes
manifest his activity, we do not think a breath of panting; for the greatness
of the divine power would be brought down toward lowliness, if according to
likeness of ours the


Spirit in him also should be suspected; but a substantial power, itself viewed
upon itself in a peculiar hypostasis, neither able to be separated from God in
whom it is, or from the Word of God whom it accompanies; nor being poured out
toward the non-existent, but being according to hypostasis after the likeness
of the Word of God, choosing, self-moved, active, always choosing the good and
having the power concurrent with the will toward every purpose.

3 So that the one who accurately looks through the depths of the mystery
receives in the soul according to the secret some moderate comprehension of
the teaching according to the knowledge of God, yet is not able to make clear
by word this inexpressible depth of the mystery; how the same is both
numerable and escapes the numbering, and is seen dividedly and is grasped in a
monad, and is distinguished by hypostasis and is not bounded off by the
subject. For the Spirit is some other by hypostasis, and the Word is another,
and that one again is other, whose both the Word is and the Spirit; but when
you have thought the distinguished in these, again the unity of the nature
does not admit the partition, so that neither the might of the monarchy is
split, being cut into different deities, nor the account agrees with the
Jewish dogma, but the truth goes through the middle of the two assumptions,
both taking down each of the heresies and receiving from each what is useful.
For the dogma of the Jew is taken down by both the acceptance of the Word and
the faith of the Spirit; and the polytheist error of those who hellenize is
made to vanish, the unity according to nature striking out the plural
imagination. And again from the Jewish assumption let the unity of the nature
remain; and from Hellenism the distinction according to the hypostases alone,
the impious suspicion having been healed from each side correspondingly; for
the number of the Trinity is as it were a therapy of those who wander about
the one, and the account of unity of those who have been scattered into a
multitude.

4 But if the Jew should speak against these things, the discourse toward him
will no longer become equally difficult for us. For from the teachings native
to him will be the manifestation of the truth. For that there is a Word of God
and a Spirit of God, powers subsisting essentially, both productive of the
things that have come to be and encompassing of the things that are, is shown
more clearly from the God-inspired scriptures. And it is enough, having
mentioned one testimony, to leave to the more ambitious the finding of the
more. By the Word of the Lord, he says, the heavens were made firm, and by the
Spirit of his mouth all their power. By what word and by what spirit? For
neither is the Word a saying, nor the Spirit a pant. For the divine also would
be made human according to likeness of our nature, if they should dogmatize
that the maker of the whole uses such a word and such a spirit. And what power
so great from sayings and panting, as to suffice toward the constitution of
heavens and of the powers in them? For if like to

our saying both the Word of God is, and the Spirit to the spirit, the power
from like things is altogether like; and as much strength as ours has, so much
also the Word of God has. But indeed the sayings among us and the spirit that
goes through together with the sayings are both without activity and without
hypostasis. Those who bring the divine down toward the likeness of the word
among us will altogether construct those also as without effect and without
hypostasis. But if, as David says, the heavens were made firm by the Word of
the Lord and their powers had their constitution in the Spirit of God, then
the mystery of the truth stands together, instructing us to say Word in
essence and Spirit in hypostasis.

5 But that there is a Word of God and a Spirit, perhaps the Greek through the
common thoughts and the Jew through the scriptural ones will not speak
against; but the economy according to man of the Word of God each of them will
equally reject as both unconvincing and unfitting to be said about God.
Therefore from another beginning we shall also lead those who speak against
toward the faith about


this. Do they believe that all things have come to be by Word and wisdom from
the one who constituted the whole, or do they also stand hard to persuade
toward this assumption? But if they should not grant that Word and wisdom lead
the constitution of the things that are, they will set unreason and lack of
art over the beginning of the whole. But if this is both absurd and impious,
it is altogether confessed that they will confess that Word and wisdom govern
the things that are. But indeed in what has gone before it has been shown that
the Word of God is not this very thing, a saying, or a habit of some knowledge
or wisdom, but some power subsisting according to essence, both choosing every
good and having in strength everything according to choice; and the world
being good, that the power that both chooses and produces the goods is the
cause. But if the hypostasis of the whole world hangs from the power of the
Word, as the sequence has shown, there is every necessity not to think some
other cause of the constitution of the parts of the world either, but the Word
himself, through whom all things had the passage into coming to be. And
whether one should wish to name this Word, or wisdom, or power, or God, or
some other of the high and honorable things, we shall not differ. For whatever
saying or name is found indicative of the subject, one is the thing signified
through the voices, the everlasting power of God, the productive of the things
that are, the inventive of the things that are not, the holding-together of
the things that have come to be, the foreseeing of the things that will be.
This God Word then, the wisdom, the power, has been shown according to
sequence to be maker of the human nature, not led by some necessity toward the
construction of man, but by excess of love having created the coming-to-be of
such a living being. For it was necessary that neither the light be unseen,
nor the glory without witness, nor the goodness without enjoyment, nor all the
other things, as many as are observed about the divine nature, lie idle, there
not being the one who both participates and enjoys. If then man comes into
coming-to-be upon these things, upon this, that he become a participant of the
divine goods, he is of necessity constructed such as to be fitted toward the
participation of the goods. For just as the eye through the brightness lying
in it by nature comes into communion of the light, drawing the kindred through
the inborn power, so it was necessary that something kindred toward the divine
be mixed into the human nature, so that through the corresponding it might
have the desire toward its own. For even in the nature of the irrational
things, as many as have obtained the watery and the aerial life, each has been
constructed correspondingly to the form of the life, so that through the
qualitative formation of the body the air is proper and of the same tribe to
the one, and the water to the other. So then also man, having come to be upon
the enjoyment of the divine goods, needed to have something kindred in the
nature toward what is participated. For this reason he was also adorned with
life and word and wisdom and all the goods fitting to God, so that through
each of these he might have the desire toward his own. Since then one of the
goods about the divine nature is also everlastingness, it was altogether
necessary that the construction of our nature be not disinherited of this
either, but have in itself the immortal, so that through the power lying in it
it might both recognize what lies above and be in desire of the divine
everlastingness. These things the account of the cosmogony indicated by a
comprehensive voice through one saying, saying that man has come to be
according to the image of God; for in the likeness according to the image is
the numbering of all the things that characterize the divine, and as many
things as Moses goes through more historically about these, setting doctrines
before us in the form of a narrative, hold to the same teaching. For that
paradise and the peculiarity of the fruits, whose eating gives not a filling
of the belly, but knowledge and everlastingness of life to those who taste,
all these things sing together with what has been contemplated beforehand
about man, that our nature was at the beginning both good and in goods. But
perhaps the one who looks toward the present things speaks against what has
been said and thinks to refute the account as not being true, by man being
seen not in those things now,


but in almost all the contrary ones. For where is the godlike of the soul? And
where the impassibility of the body? Where the everlasting of the life?
Short-lived, passionate, perishable, fitted toward every form of sufferings
both according to body and soul, saying these and such things and running down
the nature, he will think to overturn the account given about man. But so that
the account may nowhere be turned aside from the sequence, we shall also take
up these things in few words. That the human life is now in absurdities is not
a sufficient refutation that man never came to be in goods; for since man is a
work of God, of the one who through goodness brought this living being into
coming-to-be, no one would reasonably suspect that he, of whom the cause of
constitution is goodness, came to be in evils from the one who made him; but
there is another cause both of these things being about us now and of being
emptied of the more honorable. And the beginning again also toward this
account for us is not outside the agreement of those who speak against. For he
who made man upon participation of his own goods and constructed in the nature
the starting-points of all the beautiful things, so that through each the
appetite might be carried correspondingly toward the like, would not have
deprived him of the most beautiful and most honorable of the goods, I mean the
grace according to being without a master and self-governing. For if some
necessity stood over the human life, the image would have been falsified in
that part, having been made alien by the unlike toward the archetype; for how
would the nature that is yoked under certain necessities and is enslaved be
named image of the nature that reigns? Therefore that which has been likened
through all things toward the divine needed altogether to have in the nature
the self-ruling and without a master, so that the participation of the goods
might be a prize of virtue. From where then, you will say, did the one honored
through all things with the most beautiful things exchange the worse for the
goods? Clear also is the account about this. No coming-to-be of evil had its
beginning from the divine will; for vice would have been outside blame,
writing God as its own maker and father; but the evil is somehow inborn from
within, being constituted with the choice then, whenever there comes to be a
withdrawal of the soul from the beautiful. For just as seeing is an activity
of nature, and maiming is a deprivation of the natural activity, so also
virtue stands opposite toward vice. For it is not possible to think a
coming-to-be of vice otherwise than as absence of virtue. For as when the
light has been taken away the gloom has followed, and when it is present it is
not, so, as long as the good is present in the nature, vice is some
non-existent thing by itself; and the withdrawal of the better becomes a
coming-to-be of the opposite. Since then this is the peculiarity of
self-governance, to choose according to authority what is according to one's
wish, God is not for you the cause of the present evils, having constructed
the nature for you both without a master and unfettered, but the ill-counsel
that chose the worse instead of the better.

6 And perhaps you seek also the cause of the missing according to the counsel;
for to this the sequence carries the account. Therefore again some beginning
will be found for us according to the reasonable, which will also make clear
this inquiry. We have received such an account from the fathers; and the
account is not a mythical narrative, but bringing the trustworthy from our
nature itself. A double comprehension is in the things that are, the
contemplation being divided into the intelligible and the sensible. And
nothing besides these would be grasped in the nature of the things that are
being carried outside this division. And these have been divided toward one
another by much the middle, so that neither the sensible is in the marks of
the intelligibles, nor that one in the sensibles, but each is characterized
from the opposites. For the intelligible nature is some bodiless thing and
untouchable and formless; but the sensible, according to the name itself, is
within the comprehension through the sense-organs. But as in the sensible
world itself, there being much opposition of the elements toward one another,
a certain harmony has been thought of through the


opposites, fitted by the wisdom that stands over the whole, and thus there
comes to be a symphony of the whole creation toward itself, the natural
opposition nowhere dissolving the sequence of the breathing-together;
according to the same manner there also comes to be according to divine wisdom
some mixture and blending of the sensible toward the intelligible, so that all
things may participate the beautiful equally and nothing of the things that
are may be without a share of the nature of the better. For this reason the
place corresponding to the intelligible nature is the fine and easily moved
essence, having in the supercosmic lot much kinship toward the intelligible by
the peculiar of the nature, but by a better providence there comes to be some
co-blending of the intelligible toward the sensible creation, so that nothing
of the creation may be rejected, as the apostle says, nor disinherited of the
divine communion. For this sake the mixture according to man from both
intelligible and sensible is shown forth by the divine nature, as the account
of the cosmogony teaches; for God, he says, taking dust from the earth formed
man and through his own inbreathing planted the life in the formed thing, so
that the earthly might be raised together with the divine and one grace
according to equal honor might go through all the creation, the nature below
being mixed together toward the supercosmic. Since then the intelligible
creation having pre-subsisted, and a certain activity toward the constitution
of the whole having been assigned to each of the angelic powers by the
authority that stands over all things, there was also a certain power ordered
both to hold together and to rule the place around the earth, having been
empowered toward this very thing by the power that administers the whole; then
the earthly formed thing was constructed, an image of the power above; and
this living being is man; and in him was the godlike beauty of the
intelligible nature, mixed together by some unspeakable power; the one who
obtained the economy around the earth makes it a terrible thing and not
bearable, if from the nature under his hand some essence should be shown forth
likened toward the exceeding dignity. And how the one created upon no evil by
the one who constituted the whole in goodness flowed down upon the passion of
envy, to go through with accuracy is not of the present treatise, but it would
be possible even through a little to set the account beside those more easily
persuaded. For the opposition of virtue and vice is not viewed as of two
certain things appearing according to hypostasis; but as the not-being is
divided against the being and it is not possible to say according to
hypostasis that the not-being is opposed toward the being, but we say that
non-existence is divided against existence, according to the same manner vice
also stands opposite to the account of virtue, not being some thing by itself,
but being thought by the absence of the better; and as we say that maiming is
divided against seeing, maiming not being by itself in the nature, but a
deprivation of a habit that was there before, so we say that vice also is
viewed in the deprivation of the good, as some shadow happening upon the
withdrawal of the ray. Since then the uncreated nature is incapable of the
movement according to turning and change and alteration, but everything that
has come to subsist through creation has a kinship toward alteration, because
the hypostasis of creation itself also began from alteration, the not-being
having been transferred into being by divine power; and the power mentioned
was also created, choosing what seemed good by a self-governing movement;
since he shut his eye toward the good and the ungrudging, as the one in the
sun who puts his sight under the eyelids sees darkness, so that one also, by
the very not willing to think the good, thought the opposite to the good. And
this is envy. And it is confessed that the beginning of every thing is cause
of the things that happen after it according to sequence, as to health the
being in good condition, the working, the living according to pleasure; and to
disease the being weak, the being without activity, the having life in
unpleasantness. So also all the other things follow their own beginnings
according to sequence. As then impassibility becomes a beginning and
underlying of the life according to virtue, so the


inclination toward vice that came to be through envy became a way of all the
evils shown forth after it. For when once the one who by the turning-away from
goodness generated envy in himself had the inclination toward the evil, as a
stone broken off from a mountain-ridge is driven together by its own weight
toward the steep, so that one also, having been torn from the connaturality
toward the good and having grown heavy toward vice, was carried off, being
pushed together of himself as by some weight toward the last bound of
wickedness, and having made the thinking power, which he had from the one who
created him for cooperation of the participation of the better, a co-worker
toward the finding of the things thought according to vice, he goes around the
man resourcefully through deceit, having persuaded him to become of himself
both murderer and self-slayer. For when man, having been empowered through the
divine blessing, was high in dignity; for he was ordered to reign over the
earth and all the things upon it; and beautiful in form; for he had become an
image of the archetypal beauty; and impassible in nature; for he was an
imitation of the impassible; and full of boldness, luxuriating in the divine
manifestation itself face to face; and these things were kindlings of the
passion according to envy for the opposing one; and he was not able to work
what was according to his mind by some strength and force of power; for the
power of the blessing of God was stronger than his force; for this reason he
contrives to make him stand away from the power that was strengthening him, so
that he might become easy to catch for him toward the plot. And as in the case
of a lamp, when the fire has laid hold of the wick, if someone unable to
quench the flame by blowing should mix water with the oil, by this device he
will dim the flame, so the opposing one, having mixed vice into the choice of
man through deceit, made a certain quenching and dimming of the blessing,
which failing, of necessity the opposite comes in instead. And death stands
opposite to life, and weakness to power, and the curse to the blessing, and
shame to the boldness, and to all the goods the things thought according to
the opposite. For this reason the human is now in the present evils, that
beginning having furnished the starting-points of such an end.

7 And let no one ask whether God, having foreknown the human misfortune that
would happen to him from the ill-counsel, came into creating man, for whom
perhaps not to come to be was more profitable than to be in evils. For these
things those who have been dragged aside through deceit to the Manichaean
dogmas put forward for the constitution of their own error, as through this
showing the creator of the human nature to be wicked. For if God is ignorant
of none of the things that are, and man is in evils, the account of the
goodness of God would no longer be preserved, if he brought man toward life
who was going to live in evils. For if the activity according to the good is
altogether of a good nature, this painful and perishable life, he says, would
no longer be brought up to the creating of the good, but one must think
another cause of such a life, whose nature has an inclination toward
wickedness. For all these things and such things seem to those who have
received in depth, as some fast dye, the heretical deceit to have a certain
strength through the surface persuasiveness; but to those more clear-seeing of
the truth they are clearly observed as being rotten and having a ready showing
of the deceit. And it seems to me to be well to set the apostle in these
things as advocate of the accusation against them. For in the account toward
the Corinthians he divides both the fleshly and the spiritual states of the
souls, showing, I think, through the things said, that it is not proper to
judge the beautiful or the evil through sense, but having stood the mind away
from the things appearing according to the body, to distinguish the nature
itself upon itself of the beautiful and of the opposite. For the spiritual, he
says, judges all things. This I think to have been the cause of the
myth-making of these dogmas having come to be in those who bring such things
forward, that


defining the good toward the pleasant of bodily enjoyment, because the nature
of the body, being composite and flowing toward dissolution, is of necessity
subject to passions and infirmities, and some painful sensation somehow
follows such sufferings, they think the making of man to be a work of a wicked
god. As if their thought had looked toward the higher, and having settled the
mind away from the disposition about pleasures they had looked without passion
upon the nature of the things that are, they would not have thought any other
evil to be besides wickedness. And every wickedness is characterized in the
deprivation of the good, not being by itself, nor viewed according to
hypostasis; for no evil lies outside choice upon itself, but by the good not
being it is so named. And the not-being does not subsist, and of what does not
subsist the creator of the things that subsist is not creator. Therefore God
is outside the cause of the evils, being the maker of the things that are, not
of the things that are not; the one who created the seeing, not the maiming;
the one who showed forth virtue, not the deprivation of it; the one who set
the prize of the goods as a prize of choice for those who live according to
virtue, not having yoked the human nature by some violent necessity toward
what seemed good to himself, drawing it unwillingly toward the beautiful as
some soulless vessel. But if, when the light from a clear sky is shining round
purely, someone should willingly put the seeing under the eyelids, the sun is
outside the cause of the one not seeing.

8 But the one who looks toward the dissolution of the body is altogether
indignant, and makes it a hard thing that our life is dissolved by death, and
says this to be the last of the evils, that our life is quenched by deadness.
Therefore let him inspect through this gloomy thing the excess of the divine
benefaction; for perhaps rather through these things he would be led to marvel
at the grace of the care of God about man. Living is choiceworthy to those who
share the life because of the enjoyment of the things according to their wish.
As if someone should live through in pains, by much for such a one not being
is judged more honorable than being painfully. Therefore let us examine
whether the supplier of life looks toward some other thing, and not how we may
live in the most beautiful things. For when by the self-governing movement we
drew upon ourselves the communion of the evil, having mixed the evil into the
nature through a certain pleasure as some poison seasoned with honey, and for
this reason having fallen from the blessedness thought according to the
impassible, we were transformed toward vice, for this sake man as some earthen
vessel is again resolved into earth, so that, the filth now enclosed in him
having been separated, he may be formed again into the shape from the
beginning through the resurrection. And such a dogma Moses sets out for us
more historically and through riddles. Yet even the riddles have the teaching
clear. For when, he says, the first humans came to be in the things forbidden
and were stripped of that blessedness, the Lord puts coats of skin upon the
first-formed; it does not seem to me that he carries the thought of the
account toward such skins; for of what animals slaughtered and flayed is the
covering thought for them? But, since every skin separated from the living
being is dead, I think altogether that the power toward being made dead, which
was exceptional of the irrational nature, the one who heals our vice put upon
men after these things by providence, not as remaining forever; for the coat
is of the things put upon us from outside, furnishing its own use to the body
for a time, not grown together with the nature. Therefore from the nature of
the irrational things deadness was economically put around the nature created
into immortality, covering what is outside it, not what is inside, taking hold
of the sensible part of man, but not attaching to the divine image itself. And
the sensible is dissolved, not made to vanish. For vanishing is the
passing-over into the not-being; but dissolution is the diffusion again into
the elements of the world, from which it had the constitution. And what has
come to be in these has not perished, even if


it escapes the grasp of our sense. And the cause of the dissolution is clear
through the example spoken to us. For since sense has a kinship toward the
thick and earthly, and the intelligent nature is better and higher than the
movements according to sense, for this reason, the judgment about the
beautiful having been missed in the testing of the senses, and the missing of
the beautiful having worked the hypostasis of the opposite habit, the part of
us that was made useless by the acceptance of the opposite is dissolved. And
the account of the example is such. Let it be granted that some vessel has
been constituted from clay, and that this has become full from some plot of
melted lead, and that the lead poured in has set and remains unpourable, and
that the one who possesses it claims the vessel, and having the knowledge of
pottery he crushes the potsherd with the lead; then thus again according to
the former shape he forms the vessel again toward his own use, having become
empty of the matter mixed in. So then also the former of our vessel, vice
having been mixed into the sensing part, I mean that according to the body,
having dissolved the matter that received the evil, having formed it again
unmixed of the opposite through the resurrection, will re-element the vessel
toward the beauty from the beginning. And since there comes to be a certain
binding-together and communion of the sufferings according to sin both for the
soul and for the body, and there is a certain analogy of the bodily death
toward the psychic death; for as in flesh we name being separated from the
sensible life death, so also in the case of the soul we name the separation
from the true life death; since then there is one communion of the evil, as
has been said before, viewed both in soul and body; for through both the
wicked proceeds into activity; for this reason the death of the dissolution
from the putting-on of the dead skins does not touch the soul. For how would
what is not composed be dissolved? And since there is need that the stains
grown in from sins be taken out of that also through a certain therapy, for
this sake in the present life the drug of virtue was added toward healing of
such wounds. But if it should remain unhealed, the therapy has been stored in
the life after these things. But as there are certain differences of the
sufferings according to the body, of which some more easily, and some more
hardly, admit the therapy, upon which both cuttings and cauteries and bitter
drinkings of drugs are taken toward the taking-away of the passion that has
settled in the body, such a thing also the judgment after these things
announces toward therapy of the infirmities of the soul, which to the more
slack is a threat and a setting-right of gloomy things, so that by fear of the
repayment of the painful things we may be made temperate toward the flight of
vice; but to the more understanding it is believed to be a healing and therapy
from God who brings his own formed thing back toward the grace from the
beginning. For as those who scrape off by cutting or burning the nails and the
warts that have come upon the body against nature do not bring a painless
healing to the one being benefited, yet they do not bring the cutting upon
harm of the one who endures, so also as many earthy remainders as are hardened
upon our souls that have been made flesh through the communion of the
passions, in the time of the judgment are both cut and scraped off by that
unspeakable wisdom and power of the one who, as the gospel says, heals the
evil. For those who are healthy, he says, have no need of a physician, but
those who are in a bad state. And because much connaturality of the soul
toward the evil has come to be, as the cutting of a wart stings the surface;
for what has grown against nature in the nature attaches to the subject
through a certain sympathy, and there comes to be some unreasonable
co-blending of the alien toward what is ours, so that the sense is pained and
bitten being separated from what is against nature; so also, the soul being
made thin and melted in the rebukes for the sin, as somewhere the prophecy
says, because of the kinship toward the evil that has come to be in depth
there follow of necessity certain unspeakable


and inexpressible pains, whose narration has the unspeakable equally with the
nature of the goods hoped for. For neither these nor those are brought under
the power of words or the guess of thought. Therefore one looking off toward
the end of the wisdom of the one who administers the whole would no longer
reasonably name the creator of men cause of evils from smallness of soul,
either saying that he is ignorant of what will be, or that knowing and having
made he is not outside the impulse toward the wicked. For he both knew what
would be and did not hinder the impulse toward what comes to be; for that the
human would be turned aside from the good, the one who holds all things round
in the knowing power and sees the next equally with the past did not not know.
But as he saw the turning-aside, so he also thought again his calling-back
toward the good. What then was better, not to bring our nature into
coming-to-be at all, since he foresaw that the one who would come to be would
miss the beautiful, or having brought him and him having become sick, to call
him back again through repentance toward the grace from the beginning? And to
name God maker of evils because of the bodily pains, which of necessity happen
upon the flowing of the nature, or not even to think him creator of man at
all, so that he may not be suspected as cause also of the things that pain us,
this is of the last smallness of soul of those who distinguish the beautiful
and the evil by sense, who do not know that that alone is good to the nature
which sense does not touch, and that alone is evil the alienation of the true
good. And to judge the beautiful and the not beautiful by toils and pleasures
is peculiar of the irrational nature, upon which the comprehension of the
truly beautiful has no place because they do not participate mind and thought.
But that man is a work of God, having come to be both beautiful and upon most
beautiful things, is clear not only from what has been said, but also from
countless others, whose multitude we shall pass by because of the lack of
measure. And having named God maker of man we have not forgotten the things
distinguished by us in the prologue toward the Greeks, in which it was shown
that the Word of God, being some essential and hypostatic thing, is himself
both God and Word, having enclosed every productive power, or rather being
power itself and having the impulse toward every good and working whatever he
may will by having the power concurrent with the will, whose both will and
work is the life of the things that are, from whom also man was brought into
living, having been adorned in a godlike way with all the most beautiful
things. And since only that is unalterable according to nature which does not
have the coming-to-be through creation, but as many things as have come to
subsist from the uncreated nature from the not-being, having begun at once
from turning of being, always proceed through alteration, if they should act
according to nature, the alteration coming to be for them toward the better
forever, but if they should be turned aside from the straight, the movement
toward the opposite receiving them; since then among these was also man, for
whom the turnable of the nature slipped toward the opposite, and once the
withdrawal from the goods according to sequence bringing in instead every form
of evils, so that by the turning-away from life death was brought in instead,
and by the deprivation of the light the darkness came on, and by the absence
of virtue vice was brought in instead, and to every form of the goods the
catalogue of the opposites was numbered against, the one who by ill-counsel
had fallen into these and such things; for it was not even possible for the
one who had turned away from prudence to be in prudence and for the one who
had withdrawn from wisdom to counsel something wise; through whom did he need
to be called back again toward the grace from the beginning? To whom did the
setting-upright of the fallen belong, the calling-back of the lost, the
leading-by-the-hand of the one who had wandered? To whom else than altogether
to the Lord of the nature? For to the one who from the beginning had given the
life alone was it possible and fitting at once also to call back what had been
lost. Which we hear from the mystery of the truth, learning that God made man
at the beginning and has saved him who had fallen through.


9 But up to these things perhaps the one who looks toward the sequence will
agree with the account, because none of the things said seems to be outside
the thought fitting to God; but toward the things next he will not be in the
same way, through which especially the mystery of the truth is made firm;
human coming-to-be and the growth from infant toward perfection, and eating
and drinking, and toil, and sleep, and grief, and tear, and slander and a
law-court, and a cross, and death, and the placing in a tomb; for these
things, being taken together with the mystery, somehow blunt the faith of the
more small-souled, so that they do not even receive together the next of the
things said because of the things said before. For they do not admit the
fitting-to-God of the resurrection from the dead because of the unfitting
about the death. But I think one must first, having stood the reasoning a
little away from the fleshly thickness, think the beautiful itself upon itself
and what is not such, by what marks each of these is grasped. For I think none
of those who have reasoned will speak against, that one thing alone of all
according to nature is shameful, the passion according to vice, and what is
outside vice is alien to every shame; and that in which no shameful thing has
been mixed is altogether grasped in the portion of the beautiful, and the
truly beautiful is unmixed of the opposite. And everything that is viewed in
the place of the beautiful is fitting to God. Either then let them show birth
to be vice, the rearing, the growth, the advance of the nature toward the
perfect, the trial of death, the return from death; or if they agree that the
things said are outside vice, they will of necessity confess that what is
alien to vice is nothing shameful. And the thing freed of every shamefulness
and vice being altogether shown forth as beautiful, how are they not pitiable
for unreason who dogmatize that the beautiful is not fitting upon God?

10 But the human nature, he says, is small and easily circumscribed, and the
deity is infinite, and how would the infinite be enclosed by the atom? And who
says this, that by the circumscription of the flesh as by some vessel the
infinity of the deity was enclosed? For not even in the case of our life is
the intelligent nature shut within the bounds of the flesh. But the bulk of
the body is circumscribed by its own parts, and the soul by the movements of
thought is spread out according to authority over all the creation, both going
up as far as heavens, and boarding the abysses, and in the breadth of the
inhabited world

going about, and slipping in toward the under-earth through busyness, and
often even comes into thought of the heavenly wonders, being in no way weighed
down by the trailing of the body. And if a soul of man, mixed together with
the body according to the necessity of the nature, comes to be everywhere
according to authority, what necessity is there to say that the deity is
fenced in by the nature of the flesh and not, through the examples that have
room for us, to take some fitting guess about the divine economy? For as the
fire upon the torch is seen having laid hold of the underlying matter, and the
account distinguishes both the fire upon the matter and the matter that
kindles the fire, but in work it is not possible, having cut these from one
another, to show the flame upon itself unyoked from the matter, but the
both-together become one, so also in this case; and let no one take together
the destructive of the fire with the example, but having received only as much
as is seemly in the image, let him refuse what is unfitting; in the same
manner then, as we see both the flame hung from the underlying and not shut up
in the matter, what hinders, having thought some union and approach of divine
nature toward the human, from preserving the thought fitting to God even in
the approach, believing the divine to be outside every circumscription, even
if it is in a man?

11 And if you seek how deity is mixed down toward the human, it is time for
you before this to seek what the connaturality of the soul toward the flesh
is. And if the manner of your own soul is unknown, according to which it is
united to the body, do not think that that also must altogether come within
your grasp; but as here we have also believed the


soul to be some other besides the body from the flesh, having been left alone
of the soul, becoming both dead and without activity, and we do not come to
know the manner of the union, so there also we confess that the divine nature
differs toward the more magnificent from the mortal and perishable, but we do
not have room to see together the manner of the blending of the divine toward
the man. But that God has come to be in nature of man we do not doubt through
the wonders that are related, but the how, as greater than according to an
approach of reasonings, we decline to search through. For not even, believing
all the bodily and intelligible creation to have come to subsist from the
bodiless and uncreated nature, do we examine together with the faith about
these things the from-where or the how. But accepting that it has come to be,
we leave the manner of the constitution of the whole unbusied, as being
altogether unspeakable and uninterpretable.

12 And let the one who seeks the proofs of God having been manifested in flesh
look toward the activities. For not even of God being at all would anyone have
another proof, except the witness through the activities themselves. As then,
looking off into the whole, and inspecting the administrations according to
the world and the benefactions worked from God according to our life, we grasp
that some power lies above, productive of the things that come to be and
preservative of the things that are, so also in the case of the God manifested
to us through flesh we have made the wonders according to the activities a
sufficient proof of the manifestation of the deity, having thought all things
in the works that have been related, through which the divine nature is
characterized. Of God is the making men live, of God the preserving the things
that are through providence, of God the granting food and drink to those who
have obtained the life through flesh, of God the benefiting the one in need,
of God the bringing the nature that has been turned aside from weakness back
again through health toward itself, of God the standing over all the creation
in like manner, earth, sea, air, and the places above the air, of God the
having the power sufficient toward all things and before all things the being
better than death and corruption. If then the history about him was deficient
of some of these and such things, reasonably those outside the faith would
strike out our mystery; but if through the things by which God is thought, all
things are observed in the narrations about him, what is the thing that
hinders the faith?

13 But, he says, both birth and death is peculiar of the fleshly nature. I say
so too. But the before the birth and the after the death escapes the
commonness of our nature. For looking toward each of the bounds of the human
life, we know both from where we begin and into what we end. For man, having
begun to be from passion, is completed together with passion. But there
neither did the birth begin from passion, nor did the death end into passion;
for neither did pleasure lead the birth, nor did corruption receive the death.
You disbelieve the wonder? I rejoice at your unbelief; for you altogether
confess, through the things by which you hold what is said to be above faith,
that the wonders are above the nature. Let this very thing then be for you a
proof of the deity of the one who appeared, that the preaching does not go
forward through the things according to nature. For if the narrations about
Christ were within the bounds of the nature, where is the divine? But if the
account oversteps the nature, in the things in which you disbelieve, in these
is the proof that the one preached is God. For a man is born from pairing and
after death comes to be in corruption. If the preaching held these things, you
would not altogether have thought him to be God who is witnessed in the
peculiarities of our nature. But since you hear that he has come to be, and
that he has gone out from the commonness of our nature both by the manner of
the coming-to-be and by the incapability of the alteration into corruption, it
would be well according to sequence to use the unbelief upon the other, toward
not thinking him a man, one of those shown in the nature. For there is every
necessity that the one who does not believe such a one to be a man be led into
the faith about him being God. For the one who related him to have been born


also narrated together the having been born from a virgin. If then through the
things said it is trustworthy that he has been born, through these same things
altogether the having been born thus is not unconvincing either. For the one
who said the birth also added the from virginity; and the one who mentioned
the death also bore witness of the resurrection to the death. If then from the
things you hear you grant both to have died and to have been born, from the
same things you will altogether also grant that both his birth and the death
are outside passion. But indeed these things are greater than the nature.
Therefore that one also is not altogether within the nature who is shown to
have come to be in the things above the nature.

14 What then is the cause, he says, of the divine having come down toward this
lowliness, so that the faith is ambiguous, if God, the uncontainable and
incomprehensible and unutterable thing, what is above every glory and every
greatness, is mixed down with the defilement of the human nature, so that even
his high activities are made cheap together by the admixture toward the low.

15 We are not at a loss also toward this of an answer fitting to God. Do you
seek the cause of God coming to be in men? If you take away the benefactions
that come to be from God from the life, from what things you will come to know
the divine you would not be able to say. For from the things by which we are
well treated, from these we come to know the benefactor; for looking toward
the things that come to be, through these we reckon the nature of the one who
works. If then love of man is a peculiar mark of the divine nature, you have
the account you sought, you have the cause of the presence of God in men. For
our nature having grown weak needed the one who heals, the man in the fall
needed the one who sets upright, the one who had missed the life needed the
one who makes live, the one who had slipped away from the participation of the
good needed the one who brings back toward the good, the one shut in the
darkness needed the presence of the light, the captive sought the ransomer,
the prisoner the fellow-combatant, the one held by the yoke of slavery the
liberator. Are these things small and unworthy to put God to shame toward
coming down for inspection of the human nature, humanity being so pitiably and
wretchedly disposed? But it was possible, he says, both for man to be
benefited and for God to remain through in impassibility. For the one who
constituted the whole by the will and made the not-being subsist in the
impulse of the will alone, why did he not also, having pulled man away from
the opposing power through some authentic and divine authority, bring him
toward the state from the beginning, if this was dear to him; but he goes
round long circuits, putting on nature of body, and going through into the
life through birth, and going through every age in sequence, then tasting
death, and thus through the resurrection of his own body accomplishing the
aim, as if it were not possible for him, remaining upon the height of the
divine glory, to save man through a command, and to let such circuits be?

Therefore there is necessity that the truth also stand against such
oppositions from us, so that the faith may be hindered through none of those
who seek the account of the mystery examinatively. First then, which has
already been examined moderately also in what has gone before, let us inspect
what stands opposite to virtue according to the opposite. As darkness to light
and death to life, so vice to virtue, it is clear, and nothing other besides
this. For as, many things being viewed in the creation, nothing else has the
opposition toward the light or the life, not stone, not wood, not water, not
man, not any other of the things that are, except properly the things thought
according to the opposite, such as darkness and death; so also in the case of
virtue one would not say some creation to be thought according to the opposite
to it, except the thought according to vice. Therefore if our account were
advocating that the divine has come to be in vice, the one speaking against
would have a time to run down our faith, as dogmatizing things both unfitting
and unseemly about the divine nature; for it was not lawful to say that wisdom
itself and goodness and


incorruptibility, and whatever high thought and name there is, has fallen over
toward the opposite. If then God is the true virtue, and some nature is not
divided against virtue, but vice, and God does not come to be in vice, but in
nature of man, and only the passion according to vice is unfitting and
shameful, in which God has neither come to be, nor has nature to come to be,
why are they ashamed at the confession that God has touched human nature, no
opposition toward the account of virtue being viewed in the construction of
man? For neither the rational, nor the thinking, nor the receptive of
knowledge, nor any other such thing, which is peculiar of the human essence,
has been opposed to the account of virtue.

16 But this very turning of our body, he says, is a passion. And the one who
has come to be in this comes to be in passion; and the divine is impassible.
Therefore the assumption about God is alien, if they determine that the one
impassible according to nature come into communion of passion. But toward
these things again we shall use the same account, that passion is said the one
thing properly, and the other from misuse. That then which touches choice and
turns from virtue toward vice is truly passion, but as much as is viewed
going-through in the nature proceeding according to its own sequence, this
would more properly be named a work rather than a passion, such as the birth,
the growth, the remaining of the subject through the inflowing and outflowing
of the nourishment, the running-together of the elements about the body, the
dissolution again of what has been put together and the passing-over toward
the kindred. Of what then does our mystery say the divine has touched? Of the
passion said properly, which is vice, or of the movement according to nature?
For if the account were insisting that the divine has come to be in the things
forbidden, one ought to flee the absurdity of the dogma, as going through
nothing sound about the divine nature; but if it says that he has touched our
nature, whose both the first coming-to-be and hypostasis had the beginning
from him, where does the preaching miss the thought fitting to God, no
passionate disposition in the assumptions about God entering together with the
faith? For we do not even say that the physician comes to be in passion, when
he heals the one who comes to be in passion; but even if he attaches to the
infirmity, the healer remains through outside passion. If the coming-to-be
itself by itself is not a passion, one would not name the life a passion
either, but the passion according to pleasure leads the human coming-to-be,
and the impulse of those living toward vice, this is an infirmity of our
nature; but indeed the mystery says that he is clean of both; if then the
coming-to-be has been made alien of pleasure, and the life of vice, what
passion remains, of which the mystery of piety says that God has had a share?
And if one should name the unyoking of the body and the soul a passion, much
earlier would he be just to name the running-together of both thus. For if the
separation of the things joined is a passion, the joining of the things
standing apart would also be a passion; for there is a certain movement both
in the putting-together of the things standing apart and in the distinguishing
of the things interwoven or united. What then the last movement is named, this
it is proper that the preceding also be called. But if the first movement,
which we name coming-to-be, is not a passion, neither would the second
movement, which we name death, be said according to sequence to be a passion,
according to which the running-together of the body and the soul is
distinguished. And we say that God has come to be in each movement of our
nature, through which both the soul runs together toward the body, and the
body is distinguished from the soul; and having been mixed down toward each of
these, I mean toward both the sensible and the intelligent of the human
compound, through that unspeakable and inexpressible co-blending he
administered this, that of the things once united, I mean soul and body, the
union should also remain through forever. For our nature having been moved
through its own sequence even in him toward distinction of the body and the
soul, he joined again the things distinguished, as by some glue, by the


divine power I mean, fitting together what had been split toward the
unbreakable union. And this is the resurrection, the return of the things
yoked together after the dissolution into an undissolved union, growing
together with one another, so that the first grace about the human may be
called back, and we may come back again upon the everlasting life, the vice
mixed into the nature having flowed out through our dissolution, as happens
upon the wet, when the vessel around it has been crushed, being scattered and
made to vanish, there being nothing that covers round. And as the beginning of
death, having come to be in one, went through together with all the human
nature, according to the same manner also the beginning of the resurrection
through one stretches upon all the humanity. For the one who joined again the
soul taken up by himself to his own body through the power of himself that was
mixed into each of these at the first constitution thus by some more generic
account mixed the intelligent essence together with the sensible, the
beginning succeeding according to sequence upon the end. For in the man taken
up by him, the soul having come back again after the dissolution toward the
body, as from some beginning the union of what was distinguished passes over
equally by the power into all the human nature. And this is the mystery of the
economy of God about the death and of the resurrection from the dead, not to
hinder the soul from being dissolved from the body by death according to the
necessary sequence of the nature, but to bring them back again into one
another through the resurrection, so that he himself may become a boundary of
both, of both death and life, having stopped in himself the nature being
divided by death, and himself having become a beginning of the union of the
things divided.

17 But someone will say that the opposition brought against us has not yet
been dissolved, but rather that what is brought forward to us by the
unbelievers is made stronger from the things said. For if there is so great a

18 And yet perhaps it is superfluous, having believed that God has come to

19 But nevertheless, since neither to those who hellenize nor to those who

20 Therefore it is confessed by all that one must believe the divine not only
to be powerful, but also just and good and wise and everything that carries

not to wish the one of the things fitting to God to appear in the things that
have come to be, and the other not to be present; for wholly none of these

for the unjust or unwise or powerless good is not; nor is the power separated

the economy according to man is left behind of any

21 What then is the justice? We remember altogether of the things
distinguished according to sequence in the first of the account, that man was
constructed an imitation of the divine nature, preserving the likeness toward
the divine both by the remaining of the goods and by the self-governing of the
choice, being of a turnable nature of necessity; for it was not possible that
the one who had the beginning of being from alteration not be turnable
altogether; for the passage from the not-being into being is some alteration,
non-existence being transferred into essence according to divine power, and
otherwise also the turning being of necessity viewed in man, since man was an
imitation of the divine nature; and what imitates, if it should not happen to
be in some otherness, would altogether be the same as that to which it has
been likened. In this then the otherness of the one who has come to be
according to image toward the archetype being, in this, that the one is
unturnable by nature, and the other is not so, but came to subsist through
alteration according to the account given, and being altered does not
altogether remain in being; and alteration is some movement into another from
that in which it is, going forward forever; and two are the forms of such
movement; the one always coming to be toward the good, in which the advance
has no standing, because no bound of what is gone through is grasped; and the
other toward the opposite, whose hypostasis is in the not subsisting; for the
opposition of the good, as has been said in the things before, has some such
mind according to the distinction, as we say that the being is divided against
the not-being and existence against non-existence; since then according to the
turnable and alterable impulse and movement it is not possible for the nature
to remain unmoved upon itself, but the choice altogether goes upon something,
the desire toward the beautiful drawing it by nature into movement; and the
beautiful is one thing truly according to nature, and the other is not such,
but flowered with some imagination of beautiful; and the criterion of these is
the mind, seated within us from inside, in which it is risked either to hit
the really beautiful, or having been turned aside from it through some deceit
according to the appearing to slip away upon the opposite, such a thing as the
myth from outside says the dog suffered, having looked in the water toward the

then the mind, having been cheated of the desire toward the really good, was

vice the


imagination of the beautiful had not been molded round; man then having come

22 What then in these things is the just? Not to use some tyrannical
authenticity against the one holding us, nor by the excess of the power,

justification to the one who enslaved the man through pleasure. For as those

them is it possible to cry out the freedom, even if those who deserted toward
this misfortune are of good father; but if someone caring for the one sold

23 What then was it likely that the one who rules would rather choose to take?
It is possible through sequence to take some guess of his desire, if the
things clear should become for us proofs of the things sought. He then who
according to the account given beforehand at the beginning of the writing, by

But indeed in the things related from the age, he had not known in anyone such

bare, coming to be from him in a saying alone and an impulse of the will, and

the fear against the demons, and the authority of the passions according to

saw in that one more of the

24 But it is likely that the one attending to the sequence of the things said

been made essence toward what seemed good. But the descent toward the low is

sequence of the mystery upon headings let us make the apology complete toward

fitting assumptions and not the one thing be thought highly upon it, and the
other of the

25 And that the deity came to be in our nature would reasonably bring no
strangeness to those who do not think the things that are overmuch
small-souledly. For who is so infant in soul as, looking into the whole, not
to believe the divine to be in all, both putting it on and encompassing and
sitting in? For the things that are hang from the being, and it is not
possible for something to be that does not have the being in the being. If
then all things are in him and that is in all, why are they ashamed at the
economy of the mystery that teaches God to have come to be in man, who even
now is believed not to be outside the man? For even if the manner of the
presence of God in us is not this same as that, yet the being in us both now
and then has been confessed equally. Now then he is mixed in us as holding the
nature together in being; but then he was mixed toward what is ours, so that
what is ours by the admixture toward the divine may become divine, having been
taken out of the death and having come to be outside the tyranny of the
opposing one; for that return of his from the death becomes a beginning for
the mortal race of the return into the immortal life.

26 But perhaps someone in the examination of the justice and of the wisdom

coming to be within the one who rules is a deceit in a certain way and a
fallacy, since it is peculiar of those who deceive to turn the hopes of those
plotted against toward another and to work another besides what was hoped. But
the one who looks toward the truth will agree that this also of all things
most is of both the justice and the wisdom. For it is of a just one to
distribute to each according to worth, and of a wise one neither to turn aside
the just, nor to separate the good aim of the love of man from the judgment
according to the just, but to join both to one another resourcefully, by the
justice giving back according to worth, and by the goodness not standing out
of the aim of the love of man. Let us inspect then whether these two are not
viewed in the things that have come to be. For the giving-back according to
worth, through which the deceiver is deceived in return, shows the just, and
the aim of what comes to be becomes a witness of the goodness of the one who
works. For it is peculiar of justice to distribute to each those things of
which someone laid down beforehand the beginnings and the causes, as the earth
according to the kinds of the seeds that have been thrown down also gives back
the fruits; and of wisdom, in the manner of the giving-back of the like
things, not to fall out of the better. For as both the one who plots and the
one who


heals the one plotted against mix the drug similarly with the food; but the
one the poison, and the other the antidote of the poison, and the manner of
the therapy did not damage the aim of the benefaction; for even if from both a

the one, and we are hard toward the other; so also here according to the

down through his own choice; for he also is deceived by the putting-forward of
the man, the one who deceived the man beforehand by the bait of the pleasure;
but the aim of the things that come to be has the variation upon the better.
For the one worked the deceit upon corruption of the nature, and the just at
once and good and wise used the device of the deceit upon salvation of the one
corrupted, not only benefiting the lost through these things, but also the one
himself who worked the destruction against us. For from the death approaching
the life, and the darkness the light, and the corruption the incorruptibility,
there comes to be a vanishing of the worse and a passing-over into the
not-being, and a benefit of the one being cleaned from these. For just as, a
more dishonored matter having been mixed down with the gold, the healers of
the gold, having consumed by the spending through the fire the alien and
rejectable, bring the more honorable matter back again toward the brightness
according to nature; yet the distinction does not come to be without toil, the
fire in time making the bastard vanish by the consuming power, yet it is a
therapy of the gold that that which lies upon it for ruin of the beauty be
melted out; according to the same manner, death and corruption and darkness
and whatever offspring of vice having grown round the finder of the evil, the
approach of the divine power, having worked like fire a vanishing of what is
against nature, benefits the nature by the cleaning, even if the distinction
is toilsome. Therefore not even by the opposing one himself would it be
doubted that what has come to be is not just and saving, if he should come
into sensation of the benefaction. For now as those who are cut and burned
upon therapy are hard toward those who heal, being stung by the pain of the
cutting, but if the being healthy should come on through these and the pain of
the burning should pass, they will know thanks to those who worked the therapy

of those punished in the cleaning and of those who did not even at the

27 And it is altogether sequential that the one being mixed up toward our
nature receive the co-blending toward us through all the peculiarities of it.
For as those who wash out the dirt of the garments do not leave some of the

so, the human life having been polluted by the sin both in beginning and in
ending and in all the things through the middle, it was necessary that the
washing-out power come to be through all, and not heal the one thing by the
cleanser, and overlook the other unhealed. For this sake, our life being taken
hold of from each side by two bounds, I mean that according to the beginning
and the end, the correcting power of the nature is found at each bound, both
having touched the beginning and having stretched


itself as far as the end and having taken hold of all the things through the
middle of these. And there being one passage into the life for all men, from
where did the one coming in toward us need to be housed into the life? From
heaven, perhaps says the one who spits upon as both shameful and inglorious

some disease of vice endemic in the supercosmic life. And the one being mixed
down with the man was making the co-blending with the aim of the benefit.
Where then the evil was not, nor was the human life being lived, how does one
seek from there the man to have been woven round for God, or rather not a man,
but some image and likeness of a man? And what would have become of the
correction of our nature, if, the earthly living being having grown sick, some
other of the heavenly things received the divine admixture? For it is not
possible for the one who is sick to be healed, if the part that is in pain has
not received the healing peculiarly. If then the one who is sick was upon
earth, and the divine power did not touch the one who is sick, looking toward
what is fitting to itself, the occupation of the divine power about the things
that have no communion with us at all was useless to the man. For the
unfitting upon the deity is equal, if it is even lawful at all to think some
other unfitting besides vice. Yet for the one who judges the divine greatness
small-souledly in this, in the not receiving the communion of the
peculiarities of our nature, it in no way more consoles the inglorious that
the divine was shaped together with a heavenly body or with an earthly. For
from the highest and unapproachable according to the height of the nature all
the creation has stood away equally upon the down, and the whole has gone
under it with equal honor. For what is wholly unapproachable is not
approachable to some one, and unapproachable to another, but has stood above
all the things that are equally. Neither then is the earth farther from the
dignity, nor the heaven nearer, nor do the things dwelling in each of the
elements differ at all from one another in this part, so that the ones touch
the unapproachable nature, and the others are separated, or thus we would
suspect the power that rules the whole not to go through all things equally,
but to be more in some, more deficient in others, and by the difference toward
the less and the more and the rather and the less the divine will appear
sequential as composite, not agreeing with itself toward itself, if it should
be suspected to be far from us by the account of the nature, and neighboring
to some other and becoming easy to take from the near. But the true account

are displeased, not knowing that all the construction of the body has equal
