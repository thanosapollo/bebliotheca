Persuading also their enemies to do good; and their women are pure and
virgins, and they do not expose what is in their wombs; and their men keep
themselves from every unlawful union and from impurity, or rather the women
also keep themselves likewise; for they hold fast to the great hope that is to
come. But also if they have slaves or maidservants or children, they persuade
them to become Christians, that they may have faithful ones; and when they
become such, they call them brothers, without distinction. Foreign gods they
do not worship. They are gentle and equitable and modest and truthful, and
they love one another; they do not overlook a widow, and they rescue the
orphan; he who has supplies to him who has not without grudging; strangers,
when they see them, they bring in under their own roof and rejoice over them
as over a true brother; for they call themselves brothers not according to the
flesh but according to the soul. And when they see a poor man at his end,
contributing abundantly each according to his ability, they bury him; and if
they hear that any have been condemned or imprisoned for the name of the
Christ, contributing together they send to those who have need, if it is
possible, and they deliver them. And if any one is a slave or poor, they fast
two or three days, and what they were going to set before themselves they send
to those, supposing that they rejoice even as they have been called to
rejoicing. And the commandments of their God they keep securely, living holily
and justly; and as the Lord their God commanded them, they give thanks to him
every morning and at every hour, in all food and drink and the other good
things. But if one of them dies who is devout, they rejoice and give thanks
and pray concerning him, and they send him forth as one journeying abroad. And
when a child is born to them, they give thanks to God; but if an infant
departs, they give great thanks that it departed without sin. But if one dies
having sins, they weep over him as over one departing to punishment. These
things then, O king, are their doctrines; having need they ask them of God,
and so they cross over this world until the consummation of the ages, because
God subjected all things to them as slaves. Thankful then are they to him, and
through them the whole governance came to be and the creation. Truly then they
have found it.

1.1 I, O king, came into this world by the providence of God. And having
beheld the heaven and the earth and the sea, and the sun and the moon and the
rest, I marveled at their ordered arrangement.

1.2 And beholding the world and all the things that are in it, that it moves
by necessity, I understood that the one moving and governing it is God. For
everything that moves is stronger than what is moved, and that which governs
is stronger than what is governed.

1.3 Him therefore I declare to be God, the one who composed all things and
governs them, without beginning and eternal, immortal and in need of nothing,
superior to all passions and defects, both anger and forgetfulness and
ignorance and the rest.

1.4 Through him all things consist. He has no need of sacrifices and libations
or of any of the things that appear, but all things have need of him.

2.1 These things having been thus said concerning God, as far as I was able to
speak concerning him, let us come also to the race of men, that we may see
which of them partake of the truth and which of error.

2.2 For it is manifest to us, O king, that there are three races of men in
this world. These are the worshipers of the gods among you who are so called,
and Jews, and Christians. But again those who worship the many gods divide
themselves into three races, Chaldeans and Greeks and Egyptians; for these
have become leaders and teachers to the other nations of the worship and
adoration of the many-named gods.

3.1 Let us see then which of these partake of the truth and which of error.

3.2 For the Chaldeans, not knowing God, went astray after the elements and
began to worship the creation rather than their Creator; and making certain
images of these, they named them the impressions of the heaven and the earth
and the sea, of the sun and the moon too, and of the other elements or
luminaries; and shutting them up in temples they worship them, calling them
gods, and they guard them securely, lest they be stolen by robbers; and they
did not understand that everything that guards is greater than what is
guarded, and the maker greater than what is made. For if their gods are
powerless concerning their own salvation, how will they grant salvation to
others? Great error then did the Chaldeans go astray in, worshiping lifeless
and useless images.

3.3 And wonder comes upon me, O king, how their so-called philosophers did not
at all understand that the elements themselves are corruptible. But if the
elements are corruptible and subject by necessity, how are they gods? But if
the elements are not gods, how are the images, which came into being for their
honor, gods?

4.1 Let us come then, O king, to the elements themselves, that we may
demonstrate concerning them that they are not gods, but corruptible and
changeable, brought in from non-being by the command of him who is truly God,
who is incorruptible and unchangeable and invisible; but he himself sees all
things and alters and changes them as he wills. What then shall I say
concerning the elements?

4.2 But those who suppose the heaven to be a god are in error. For we see it
turning and moving by necessity and composed of many parts; therefore also it
is called a cosmos. Now a cosmos is the workmanship of some craftsman; but
that which has been fashioned has a beginning and an end. And the heaven moves
by necessity together with its luminaries; for the stars, borne along in order
and interval from sign to sign, some setting and some rising, and making their
courses at seasons so as to accomplish summers and winters, as they were
appointed by God, do not transgress their own terms, according to the
unavoidable necessity of nature, together with the heavenly cosmos. Whence it
is manifest that the heaven is not a god but a work of God.

4.3 But those who suppose the earth to be a goddess went astray. For we see it
insulted and ruled over by men and dug and kneaded and becoming useless. For
if it is baked, it becomes dead; for from a potsherd nothing grows. And again
if it is salted beyond measure, both it and its fruits perish. And it is
trodden underfoot by men and by the other animals, it is defiled with the
blood of the slain, it is pierced through, it is filled, it becomes a tomb of
corpses.

4.4 These things being so, it is not possible for the earth to be a goddess,
but a work of God for the use of men.

5.1 But those who suppose water to be a god went astray. For it too came to be
for the use of men and is ruled over by them, and it is defiled and corrupted
and altered, being boiled and changing colors and being frozen by the cold and
polluted with bloods.

5.2 And it is led to the washing of all unclean things. Therefore it is
impossible for water to be a god, but a work of God.

5.3 But those who suppose fire to be a god are in error. For it too came into
being for the use of men and is ruled over by them, being carried about from
place to place for the boiling and baking of all kinds of meats, and again of
dead bodies also. And it is corrupted in many ways by men, being extinguished.
Therefore it is not possible for fire to be a god, but a work of God.

5.4 But those who suppose the breath of the winds to be a goddess are in
error. For it is manifest that it serves another and was fashioned by God for
the sake of men, for the conveying of ships and the harvests of the grains and
for their other needs. And it increases and ceases at the command of God.

5.5 Therefore it has not been believed that the breath of the winds is a
goddess, but a work of God.

6.1 But those who suppose the sun to be a god are in error. For we see it
moving by necessity and turning and passing from sign to sign, setting and
rising, to warm the plants and the shoots for the use of men, and moreover
having distributions with the other stars.

6.2 And being much less than the heaven, and failing of its light, and having
no self-mastery. Therefore it has not been believed that the sun is a god, but
a work of God.

6.3 But those who suppose the moon to be a goddess are in error. For we see it
moving by necessity and turning and passing from sign to sign, setting and
rising, for the use of men, and being less than the sun, waxing and waning and
having eclipses. Therefore it has not been believed that the moon is a
goddess, but a work of God.

7.1 But those who suppose man to be a god are in error. For we see him moving
and being begotten by necessity, and being nourished and growing old, even
when he does not wish it.

7.2 And at one time he rejoices, at another he grieves, needing foods and
drink and clothing.

7.3 And he is wrathful and envious and lustful and repentant, and has many
defects. And he is destroyed in many ways by the elements and by animals, and
by the death that lies upon him. Therefore it is not possible for man to be a
god, but a work of God.

7.4 Great error then did the Chaldeans go astray in, after their lusts. For
they worship corruptible elements and the lifeless images, and they do not
perceive it, making gods of these.

8.1 Let us come then to the Greeks, that we may see what they think concerning
God.

8.2 The Greeks then, wise as they say they are, were made more foolish than
the Chaldeans, introducing many gods to have been begotten, some male and some
female, slaves of all kinds of passions and makers of all sorts of lawless
deeds. Those whom they themselves set forth as adulterers and murderers,
wrathful and envious and vengeful, father-slayers and brother-slayers, thieves
and robbers, lame and maimed and sorcerers and madmen, and some of these dead,
and some struck by lightning and beaten and lamented and having served men and
become fugitives and transformed into animals through evil and shameful
unions.

8.3 Whence the Greeks, O king, introduced laughable and foolish and impious
words, addressing as gods those who are not such, according to their evil
lusts, so that having them as patrons of wickedness they may commit adultery
and seize and murder and do all terrible things.

8.4 For if their gods did such things, how shall not the men also who give
heed to them do the same?

8.5 From these pursuits of error then it happened that men have many wars and
slaughters and bitter captivities.

9.1 But also if we should wish to pursue the discourse concerning each one of
their gods, you will see much absurdity.

9.2 Thus there is introduced to them before all the god called Cronus, and to
him they sacrifice their own children.

9.3 And he had many children by Rhea, and going mad he ate his own children.

9.4 And they say that Zeus cut off his genitals and cast them into the sea,
whence Aphrodite is fabled to have been born. Having bound therefore his own
father, Zeus cast him into Tartarus.

9.5 You see the error and licentiousness which they introduce concerning their
god. Is it possible then for a god to be bound and mutilated? O madness! Who
of those having reason would say these things?

9.6 Second is introduced Zeus, whom they say reigned as king over their gods
and was transformed into animals, that he might commit adultery with mortal
women.

9.7 For they introduce him transformed into a bull toward Europa, and into
gold toward Danae, and into a swan toward Leda, and into a satyr toward
Antiope, and into lightning toward Semele; then to have begotten from these
many children, Dionysus and Zethus and Amphion, and Heracles, and Apollo and
Artemis, and Perseus, Castor and Helen and Polydeuces, and Minos, and
Rhadamanthys, and Sarpedon, and the nine daughters whom they addressed as
Muses. Then thus they introduce the things concerning Ganymede.

9.8 It happened then, O king, that men imitated all these things and became
adulterers and sodomites, and workers of other terrible deeds, in imitation of
their god.

9.9 How then is it possible for an adulterer or a sodomite or a father-slayer
to be a god?

10.1 With this one they also introduce a certain Hephaestus to be a god, and
this one lame and holding a hammer and tongs and forging for the sake of food.

10.2 Is he then needy? Which it is not possible for a god to be, lame and in
need of men.

10.3 Then they introduce Hermes to be a god, lustful and covetous and a
magician and maimed and an interpreter of words.

10.4 Which it is not possible for a god to be, such a one.

10.5 But Asclepius they introduce to be a god, being a physician and preparing
medicines and the composition of plasters for the sake of food. For he was
needy; and later to have been struck by lightning by Zeus on account of
Tyndareus the Lacedaemonian's son, and to have died.

10.6 But if Asclepius, being a god and struck by lightning, was not able to
help himself, how will he help others?

10.7 But Ares is introduced to be a god, a warrior and lusting after beasts
and after certain other things; and later, committing adultery with Aphrodite,
to have been bound by the infant Eros and by Hephaestus. How then is the
lustful and warrior and prisoner and adulterer a god?

10.8 But Dionysus they introduce to be a god, leading nocturnal festivals and
a teacher of drunkenness and dragging away the wives of their neighbors and
raging and fleeing; and later to have been slain by the Titans. If then
Dionysus, when slain, was not able to help himself, but was also raging and a
fugitive, how would he be a god?

10.9 But Heracles they introduce to have become drunk and to have gone mad and
to have slain his own children, then to have been consumed by fire and so to
have died. How would a drunkard and a child-slayer and one being burned be a
god? Or how to help others, who was not able to help himself?

11.1 But Apollo they introduce to be a god, envious, and moreover holding bow
and quiver, and at times also lyre and pipe, and divining to men for the sake
of hire. Is he then needy? Which it is not possible for a god to be, needy and
envious and a lyre-player.

11.2 But Artemis they introduce to be his sister, being a huntress, and to
have a bow with quiver, and this one to roam about the mountains alone with
the dogs, that she may hunt a deer or a boar. How then shall such a woman be a
goddess, and a huntress and roaming about with the dogs?

11.3 But they say that Aphrodite also is a goddess, being an adulteress. For
at one time she had Ares as adulterer, at another Anchises, at another Adonis,
for whose death she laments, seeking her lover; whom they say even to descend
into Hades, that she might ransom Adonis from Persephone. Do you see, O king,
a greater folly than this? To introduce as a goddess her who commits adultery
and laments and weeps?

11.4 But Adonis they introduce to be a god, being a hunter, and this one to
have died violently, struck by a wild boar, and not being able to help his own
misery. How then will the adulterer and hunter and one who died a violent
death take thought for men?

11.5 All these things and many such and much more shameful and wicked did the
Greeks, O king, introduce, having fabricated them concerning their gods, which
it is not lawful either to speak or even to bear in remembrance at all. Whence
men, taking occasion from their gods, practiced every lawlessness and
licentiousness and impiety, defiling both earth and air with their terrible
deeds.

12.1 But the Egyptians, being more foolish and more senseless than these, went
astray worse than all the nations; for they were not satisfied with the cult-
objects of the Chaldeans and Greeks, but introduced moreover dumb animals to
be gods, both land animals and water animals, and the plants and the shoots,
and they were defiled in all madness and licentiousness, worse than all the
nations on the earth.

12.2 For from the beginning they worshiped Isis, who had as brother and
husband Osiris, who was slain by his brother Typhon. And because of this Isis
flees with Horus her son to Byblos of Syria, seeking Osiris, bitterly
lamenting, until Horus grew up and killed Typhon.

12.3–5 Neither then was Isis able to help her own brother and husband; nor was
Osiris, being slain by Typhon, able to lay hold of himself; nor did Typhon the
brother-slayer, perishing by Horus and Isis, have the means to deliver himself
from death.

12.6 And being made known in such misfortunes, they themselves were supposed
to be gods by the senseless Egyptians. Who, not being satisfied even in such
things or in the other cult-objects of the nations, introduced even the dumb
animals to be gods.

12.7 For some of them worshiped a sheep, and some a goat, and others a calf
and the pig, and others the raven and the hawk and the vulture and the eagle,
and others the crocodile, and some the cat and the dog and the wolf and the
ape and the dragon and the asp, and others the onion and the garlic and
thorns, and the rest of the creatures.

12.8 And the wretched ones do not perceive concerning all these that they have
no strength. For seeing their gods being eaten by other men and burned and
slaughtered and rotting, they did not understand concerning them that they are
not gods.

12,9-13.1 Great error then did the Egyptians and the Chaldeans and the Greeks
go astray in, introducing such gods and making images of them and deifying the
deaf and senseless idols.

12,9-13.2 And I wonder how, seeing their gods being sawn and hewn and
mutilated by the craftsmen, and wearing out with time and dissolving and
melting, they did not think concerning them that they are not gods. For those
who have no strength concerning their own salvation, how will they take
thought for men?

12,9-13.3 But their poets and philosophers, of the Chaldeans and Greeks and
Egyptians, wishing to dignify the gods among them with their poems and
writings, more greatly uncovered their shame and set it forth naked to all.
For if the body of man, being many-membered, does not cast off any of its own
limbs, but has toward all the members an indissoluble union, it is in accord
with itself; how then in the nature of God shall there be such battle and
discord? For if there was one nature of the gods, a god ought not to pursue a
god, nor slay nor mistreat him.

12,9-13.4 But if the gods were pursued by gods and slain and seized and struck
by lightning, there is no longer one nature, but divided judgments, all
wicked; so that none of them is a god. Manifest then it is, O king, that all
the theology concerning the gods is error.

12,9-13.5 And how did the wise and learned of the Greeks not understand that,
having established laws, they are condemned by their own laws? For if the laws
are just, their gods are certainly unjust, having done lawless deeds, mutual
slayings and sorceries and adulteries and thefts and sodomies; but if they did
these things well, then the laws are unjust, having been established against
the gods. But now the laws are good and just, praising the noble and
forbidding the evil; but the deeds of their gods are lawless. Lawless then are
their gods and all worthy of death, and impious those who introduce such gods.
For if the histories concerning them are mythical, they are nothing but words;
but if naturalistic, no longer are those who did and suffered these things
gods; but if allegorical, they are myths and nothing else.

12,9-13.6 It has been demonstrated then, O king, that these many-god cult-
objects are works of error and perdition. For one ought not to name as gods
things visible and not seeing; but one ought to worship the invisible God, who
made all things.

14.1 Let us come then, O king, also to the Jews, that we may see what they too
think concerning God. For these, being descendants of Abraham and Isaac and
Jacob, sojourned into Egypt. And from there God led them out ...

14.2 For they worship even now God the one All-ruler, but not according to
knowledge; for they deny the Christ, the Son of God, and they are like the
nations, even if they seem in some way to draw near to the truth, from which
they removed themselves. These things concerning the Jews.

15.1 But the Christians trace their lineage from the Lord Jesus Christ. And he
is confessed the Son of the most high God, coming down from heaven in the Holy
Spirit for the salvation of men, and born of a holy virgin without seed and
incorruptibly, took flesh and appeared among men, that he might recall them
from the error of the many gods.

15.2 And having accomplished his wondrous dispensation, he tasted of death
through the cross by willing counsel according to a great dispensation; and
after three days he came to life again and ascended into the heavens. The fame
of his coming you are permitted to know, O king, from the gospel holy writing
called so among them, if you should come upon it. And he had twelve disciples,
who after his ascent into the heavens went out into the provinces of the world
and taught his greatness; as one of them went about our countries proclaiming
the doctrine of the truth. Whence those who yet serve the righteousness of
their proclamation are called Christians.

15.3 And these are they who beyond all the nations of the earth have found the
truth; for they know God the maker and craftsman of all things in the only-
begotten Son and in the Holy Spirit, and another God besides this one they do
not worship. They have the commandments of the Lord Jesus Christ himself
engraved in their hearts, and they keep them, awaiting the resurrection of the
dead and the life of the age to come.

15.4 They do not commit adultery, they do not commit fornication, they do not
bear false witness, they do not covet the things of others, they honor father
and mother, and they love their neighbors, they judge justly.

15.5 Whatever they do not wish to happen to themselves they do not do to
another; they beseech those who wrong them and make them their friends; they
strive to benefit their enemies; they are gentle and equitable.

15.6 From every unlawful union and from all impurity they keep themselves,

15.7 they do not overlook a widow, they do not grieve an orphan; he who has
supplies abundantly to him who has not; if they see a stranger they bring him
under their roof and rejoice over him as over a true brother; for they do not
call themselves brothers according to the flesh but according to the spirit.

15.8 They are ready to give up their souls for Christ; for they keep his
commandments securely, living holily and justly, as the Lord their God
commanded them, giving thanks to him at every hour, in all food and drink and
in the other good things.

16-17.1 Truly then this is the way of the truth, which leads those walking in
it to the eternal kingdom promised by Christ in the life to come. And that you
may know, O king, that I do not say these things of myself, having looked into
the writings of the Christians you will find that I say nothing outside the
truth. Well then did your son understand, and justly was he taught to worship
the living God and to be saved unto the age that is about to come. For great
and wonderful are the things said and done by the Christians; for they speak
not the words of men, but the things of God. But the other nations are in
error and lead themselves astray; for walking in darkness they dash against
themselves as drunken men. Thus far my discourse to you, O king, the discourse
dictated to me in my mind by the truth. Therefore let your foolish
philosophers cease from vain-talking against the Lord. For it profits you to
worship God the Creator, and that you be shown heirs of the things that perish
not.
