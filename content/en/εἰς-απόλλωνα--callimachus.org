#+BEGIN_QUOTE
To Apollo edited by Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff
#+END_QUOTE

How the laurel shoot of Apollo has shaken, how the whole hall too; far, far
away, whoever is sinful. And surely now Phoebus is striking the doors with his
fair foot; do you not see? The Delian palm has nodded something sweet all at
once, and the swan sings beautifully in the air. Now you yourselves, door-bars
of the gates, swing open, and you yourselves, bolts; for the god is no longer
far. And you young men, prepare both song and for the dance. Apollo does not
appear to everyone, but to whoever is noble; whoever sees him, that man is
great; whoever has not seen him, that one is of little worth. We shall see, O
Far-shooter, and we shall never be of little worth. Let not the boys have a
silent lyre nor a noiseless step of Phoebus when he is present, if they are
going to complete marriage and to have their gray hair cut, and the wall is to
stand upon ancient foundations. I have admired the boys, since the
tortoise-shell is no longer idle. Keep holy silence, you who hear, at the song
of Apollo. The sea also keeps holy silence, when singers celebrate either the
lyre or the bow, the weapons of Lycorean Phoebus. Nor does Thetis, the mother,
wail dirges for Achilles, whenever she hears ie Paean, ie Paean. And even the
weeping stone puts off its pains, that which stands in Phrygia as a dripping
stone, marble instead of a woman gaping something wretched. Cry ie ie; it is
an evil to contend with the blessed. Whoever fights with the blessed, let him
fight with my king; whoever with my king, let him fight with Apollo too.
Apollo will honor the chorus, whatever it sings according to his heart; for he
is able, since he sits at the right hand of Zeus. Nor will the chorus sing
Phoebus for one day only, for he is well-hymned; who would not easily sing
Phoebus? Golden are Apollo's both the garment and the pin-fastened cloak and
the lyre and the Lyctian bow and the quiver, golden also the sandals; for
Apollo is rich in gold. And he is rich in possessions too; you could judge by
Pytho. And he is always beautiful and always young; never even so much as down
has come upon the tender cheeks of Phoebus. And his locks pour fragrant oil on
the ground. The hair of Apollo does not drip fat, but panacea itself; and in
the city where those drops fall to the ground, all things have become
unharmed. And no one is so abundant in skill as Apollo; he allotted the archer
man, he the singer (for both bow and song are entrusted to Phoebus), and his
are the Thriae and the seers; and from Phoebus physicians have learned the
postponement of death.

We call Phoebus also Nomius from that time, since on the Amphrysus he tended
yoke-mares burned by love for the youth Admetus. Easily would the
cattle-pasture become more abundant, nor would the goats lack young, the ones
that go with the flocks, on which Apollo cast his eye as they grazed; nor
would the sheep be without milk nor barren, but all would be with lambs, and
she who was a bearer of one would quickly become a bearer of twins. And
following Phoebus men measured out cities; for Phoebus always delights in
cities as they are being founded, and Phoebus himself weaves the foundations.
At four years old Phoebus first fixed the foundations in fair Ortygia near the
circular lake. Artemis hunting continuously used to bring the heads of
Cynthian goats, and he, Apollo, was plaiting an altar. He built the base with
horns, and fixed the altar from horns, and laid horned walls around it. Thus
Phoebus first learned to raise foundations. Phoebus also pointed out my
deep-soiled city to Battus and as a raven, on the right, led the people
entering Libya for the founder and swore to give walls to our kings; and
Apollo is always true to his oath. O Apollo, many call you Boedromius, and
many Clarius, and everywhere your name is many; but I Carneius; thus it is
ancestral for me. Sparta, Carneius, is your very first seat, second again
Thera, and third the city of Cyrene. From Sparta the sixth generation of
Oedipus brought you to the Theran colony; and from Thera curly-haired
Aristoteles set you down in the Asbystian land, and built you a very fair
sanctuary, and in the city he established an annual festival, in which many
bulls, O lord, fall last upon the haunch. Ie ie Carneius much-entreated, and
your altars bear flowers in spring as many as the Hours bring in varied kinds
when the west wind breathes dew, and in winter sweet crocus; and always you
have ever-flowing fire, nor does ash ever feed around yesterday's coal. Truly
Phoebus rejoiced greatly, when the girded men of Enyo danced with the
fair-haired Libyan women, when the appointed Carneian seasons came to them.
But the Dorians were not yet able to approach the springs of Cyre, and they
dwelt in Azilis dense with glens. The lord himself saw them, and showed them
to his nymph standing on horned Myrtoussa, where the daughter of Hypseus slew
the lion, the cattle-ravager of Eurypylus. Apollo saw no other chorus more
divine than that one, nor did he allot to a city so many benefits as to
Cyrene, remembering the earlier rape. Nor indeed have the Battiads themselves
honored another god more than Phoebus. We hear ie ie paean, because this the
Delphian people first invented as a refrain for you, when you displayed the
far-shooting of your golden bows. A monstrous beast met you as you were going
down to Pytho, a dread serpent. Him you slew, shooting one swift arrow upon
another, and the people cried out, /ie ie paean, shoot the missile, since your
mother bore you as a helper/; and from that time it is sung. Envy spoke
secretly into the ears of Apollo /I do not admire the singer who does not even
sing as much as the sea/. Apollo kicked Envy with his foot and spoke thus:
/Great is the stream of the Assyrian river, but it draws much filth of the
earth and much refuse upon the water. The bees do not carry water to Deo from
every source, but whatever, clean and undefiled, creeps up from a holy spring,
a little trickle, the choicest flower/. Hail, lord; and let Blame go where
Envy has gone.
