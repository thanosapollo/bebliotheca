#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Megara edited by J. M. Edmonds
#+END_QUOTE

/Mother of mine, why thus do you wound your dear heart/
/grieving terribly, and the former flush no longer/
/is kept upon your face? Why are you so distressed at me?/
/Is it because your glorious son suffers boundless pains/
/at the hands of a worthless man, as a lion under a fawn?/
/Woe is me, why now have the immortal gods so dishonored me?/
/Why did my parents bear me to such an evil fate?/
/Ill-fated I, who when I came to the bed of a blameless husband,/
/honored him equal to my own eyes/
/and still now I revere and respect him in my heart;/
/and of him no other of the living was born more luckless,/
/nor tasted so many cares in his own thoughts./
/Wretched man, who with the bow that Apollo himself gave him,/
/or the dread darts of some Ker or Erinys,/
/slew his own children and took the dear life from them/
/raving through the house, and he was full of slaughter./
/Those I, wretched, saw with my own eyes/
/struck down by their father, and this not even in a dream came to another./
/Nor could I help them when they called loudly/
/upon their own mother, since an unconquerable evil was near./

/And as a bird wails over her own nestlings/
/perishing, whom a dread serpent, while they are still young,/
/devours in the dense thickets; and she over them/
/flies crying very shrilly, the lady mother,/
/nor can she succor her children; for indeed for her herself/
/to go nearer is a great terror of the implacable monster;/
/so I, ill-childed, wailing my dear offspring/
/went ranging much through the house with maddened feet./
/Would that dying together with my children I myself also/
/had lain having a poisoned arrow through the liver,/
/Artemis who greatly rule over women./
/Then our parents, having wept us in their dear hands,/
/would have set us with many funeral gifts upon the same pyre,/
/and gathering the bones of all into one golden urn/
/they would have buried them, where first we were born./
/But now they dwell in horse-nurturing Thebes/
/plowing the deep soil of the Aonian plain;/
/but I in Tiryns, the rocky city of Hera,/
/wretched, am stricken in heart with many pains/
/always alike; and of tears there is not even a little pause for me./
/But my husband I see with my eyes for little time/
/in our house; for ready for him is the work of many/
/toils, which wandering over land and sea/
/he toils at, having a mind of stone or iron/
/strong in his breast; but you melt like water,/
/weeping both nights and as many days as come from Zeus./
/And no other of my kinsfolk standing by could gladden me;/
/for the wall of the house does not shut them in;/

/and indeed all dwell beyond the piney Isthmus,/
/nor have I anyone toward whom looking/
/I might, as a wholly luckless woman, refresh my dear heart,/
/save only Pyrrha my sister; and she herself also/
/grieves more about her own husband Iphicles,/
/your son; for I think you have borne the most wretched children/
/of all, both to a god and to a mortal man./
So she spoke; and her more abundant tears of the cheeks
poured from her eyelids into her lovely bosom,
remembering her children and her parents afterward.
And likewise Alcmene wet her white cheeks with tears;
and she, groaning heavily even from the heart,
spoke thus to her dear daughter-in-law with close-packed words:
/Strange one among children, why now has this fallen upon your/
/prudent mind? How do you wish to stir us both/
/speaking unforgettable cares? And these have not been wept now first./
/Or is it not enough, what we are held by, always arising/
/to the latest day? Truly one would be very fond of lament/
/who would number them upon our griefs./
/Take heart; we have not met with such a fate from the god./
/And I myself see you, dear child, toiling/
/with unwearied pains. And I am one who knows/
/how to be distressed, when indeed even of gladness there is satiety;/
/and I lament you very terribly and pity you,/
/because you have a share in our woeful fortune,/
/which hangs heavy over our head./

/For let Kore and well-robed Demeter know,/
/whom one greatly harmed of his enemies would willingly swear falsely by,/
/that I cherish you in my heart no worse/
/than if you had come forth from my womb/
/and were my late-born maiden in the house./
/Nor do I think this wholly escapes you yourself./
/Therefore do not ever say, my shoot, that I do not care for you,/
/not even if I weep denser than fair-haired Niobe./
/For not even so is it blameworthy for a mother to wail/
/over a child who suffers ill; since I toiled ten months/
/before even seeing him, holding him under my liver,/
/and he brought me near to gate-fastening Aidoneus;/
/so hard-bearing I endured evil birth-pangs for him./
/And now he is gone alone upon a foreign land accomplishing/
/a new labor; nor do I know, ill-fated, whether I shall receive him/
/returned here again, or not./
/And besides a dread dream has startled me through sweet/
/sleep; and I fear terribly having seen a hostile vision,/
/lest it do something hateful to my children./
/For my son appeared to me holding a well-wrought mattock/
/in both hands, the might of Heracles;/
/with it he was digging a great trench, as one hired for wage,/
/at the edge of some flourishing field,/
/naked without cloak and well-girded tunic./
/But when he came to the end of the whole work,/
/toiling at the strong fence of a wine-bearing vineyard,/
/then he was about to set the shovel against the jutting/
/bank and put on the clothes he had worn before;/
/but suddenly over the deep trench there blazed/

/unquenchable fire, and around him unspeakable flame was coiled./
/But he kept drawing back behind with swift feet,/
/eager to escape the destructive might of Hephaestus;/
/and always before his flesh as a wicker shield/
/he was wielding the mattock; and with his eyes here and there/
/he looked about, lest the hostile fire burn him./
/And great-hearted Iphicles, eager to help him, as it seemed to me,/
/fell upon the ground having slipped/
/before he came, nor could he stand upright again,/
/but he lay unmoving, as a feeble old man,/
/whom even unwilling joyless old age forced/
/to fall; and he lies upon the earth firmly so,/
/until some one of those passing by draws him up by the hand/
/respecting the former regard of his gray beard./
/So upon the earth shield-bearing Iphicles had sunk;/
/but I was weeping seeing my children/
/helpless, until sweet sleep fled/
/from my eyes, and at once shining dawn came./
/Such dreams, dear one, startled my mind/
/all night; and may all these turn upon Eurystheus/
/away from our house, and may my heart become a prophet/
/for him, and may the divinity fulfill nothing else besides./
