OF OUR FATHER AMONG THE SAINTS JOHN, ARCHBISHOP OF CONSTANTINOPLE, THE
CHRYSOSTOM. COMMENTARY ON THE EPISTLE TO PHILEMON.

=62.701= ARGUMENT.

First it is necessary to tell the argument of the letter, then also the
things inquired. What then is the argument? Philemon was a certain man of
the admirable and noble (for that he was admirable is clear from this too,
that his whole house was faithful, and so faithful that it was even named a
Church. Therefore also writing he said: And to the Church in your house. And
he testifies to him also much obedience, and that the bowels of the saints
had been refreshed toward him. And he himself writing in this letter charged
him to prepare a lodging. So that house of his seems to me to be a lodging
of saints for all reasons). This admirable man then had a certain servant,
Onesimus. This Onesimus then, having stolen something from the master, ran
away; for that he stole, hear what he says: But if he has wronged you in
anything, or owes, I will repay. Coming then to Paul at Rome, and finding
him in the prison, and enjoying the teaching from him, he obtained baptism
there also. For that he obtained the gift of baptism there is clear from his
saying: Whom I have begotten in my bonds. Paul then writes commending him to
the master, so that there may be a release for all reasons, and =62.702= that
he receive him as now regenerated. But since some say that it is superfluous
that this letter too be attached, if indeed he made request about a small
matter, about one man, let those who bring these charges learn that they are
worthy of ten thousand charges. For not only such small letters, and those
sent about such necessary matters, ought to have been written down; but
would that it were possible to have in plenty him who would hand down to us
the history of the apostles, I do not say about what they wrote and
discussed, but also their other manner of life, and what they ate and when
they ate, when they sat and where they walked, and what they accomplished
each day, in what parts they were, and into what house they entered, and
where they lodged, and to narrate all things with accuracy; so much do all
the things done by them abound with much benefit. But since most do not know
the gain from this, therefore they attempt to blame. For if, seeing only
places, where they sat or were bound, lifeless places, we often =62.703= send
our mind there, and imagine their virtue, and are stirred up and become more
eager; if we heard their words and their other actions, much more. But about
a friend someone asks where he lives, what he does, where he goes forth; and
about the common teachers of the world, tell me, ought we not to have
learned these things? For when someone lives spiritually, even the postures
and gaits, and words and deeds of such a one, and simply all things, benefit
those who hear, and nothing hinders nor becomes an obstacle. Except that it
is useful for you to learn that the letter was sent about necessary things.
See then how many things are set right from this. One and first, that one be
earnest about all things. For if Paul makes so much provision for a runaway,
for a robber and a thief, and does not decline to send him with so many
praises, nor is ashamed, much more neither does it befit us to be slack
about such things.


Second, that one must not despair of the slave race, even if it has gone to
the last wickedness. For if the thief, the runaway, became so virtuous that
Paul wished to make him a partner, and writing said: That he may minister
to me in your stead; much more one must not despair of the free. Third, that
it is not fitting to snatch slaves from the masters. For if Paul, who was so
confident toward Philemon, did not wish to keep Onesimus, who was so useful
and serviceable to him for ministry, against the master's will; much more
must we not do this. For if the household servant is admirable, for this
reason especially he must remain in the slavery, and acknowledge the
mastery, so that he may become a cause of benefit to all in the house. Why
do you bring the lamp from the lampstand onto the bushel? Would that it were
possible to push those outside into the cities. What =62.704= then, he says,
if he himself also becomes worthless? Why, tell me, I ask? because he went
into a city? But consider that being outside he will be more worthless; for
he who became worthless inside, much more being outside; for here he is even
released from the necessary care, the master being anxious; but there the
care about these things will perhaps lead him away even from the more
necessary and more spiritual things. Therefore the blessed Paul also,
bringing in the best counsel to them, said: Were you called as a slave? Let
it not be a care to you; but if you can also become free, use it rather;
that is, Remain in the slavery. And the most necessary of all, that the word
of God be not blasphemed, as he himself also writing said. As many as are
slaves under the yoke, let them count their own masters worthy of all honor,
that the name of God and the teaching be not blasphemed. For the Hellenes
too will say that even while serving he can please God; since many are set
under a necessity of blaspheming and saying that Christianity has been
brought into life for the overturning of all things, masters being deprived
of their household servants, and the matter is violence. Shall I say
something else too? It teaches us not to be ashamed of household servants,
if they are virtuous. For if Paul, more admirable than all men, says so much
about this one, much more we about our own. When then there are so many
achievements, and we have not yet said all, does someone consider it
superfluous that this letter too has been inserted? And how would it not be
of last folly? Let us attend then, I ask, to the letter written by the
Apostle; for having already gained so much, we shall gain more from the
texture.

=62.703= HOMILY 1.

Paul, a prisoner of Jesus Christ, and Timothy the brother, to Philemon the
beloved and our fellow-worker, and to Apphia the beloved, and to Archippus
our fellow-soldier, and to the Church in your house: grace to you and peace
from God our Father, and the Lord Jesus Christ.

αʹ. These things are on behalf of a household servant toward a master.
Immediately from the preface he pulled down his pride, he did not let him be
ashamed, he quenched the anger, calling himself a prisoner he stung and
contracted him, he made the present things seem to be no matters. For if a
bond is not shame because of Christ, but even a boast, much more slavery is
not a reproach. And he says these things not lifting himself up, but doing
this profitably, and showing from this the trustworthiness, not for his own
sake, but that the favor be received more readily; as if he said, For you I
wear this bond; as indeed he said elsewhere, there showing the care, here
the trustworthiness. Nothing is greater than this boast, than to hear that
one is a


branded one of Christ, For I bear, he says, the marks of Christ. The
prisoner of Christ. For he was bound because of him. Who =62.704= would not
reverence, who would not be put to shame, hearing bonds of Christ? who would
not even give up his soul, let alone one household servant? And Timothy the
brother. He takes another also with himself, so that he too, being requested
by many, may rather yield and grant the favor. To Philemon the beloved, and
our fellow-worker. If beloved, the confidence is not daring nor rashness,
but a proof of much friendship; if a fellow-worker, he ought not only to be
requested about such things, but even to know favor; for he grants it to
himself, he builds up the same work. So that even without request you have,
he says, another necessity of granting the favor; for if he is useful to the
Gospel, and you are zealous for the Gospel, then you ought not to be
requested, but to request. And to Apphia the beloved. She seems to me to be
his wife. See the humility of Paul: he takes Timothy too for the request,
and he does not request the man only, but also the woman, and some other
perhaps a friend. And to Archippus, he says, our fellow-soldier. Not wishing
to accomplish such things by command, nor being indignant if he does not
immediately obey when he orders, but what an unknown =62.705= man would do,
these things he asks them also to do, so as to take part in the petition.
For not only being requested by many, but also that the petition be made
toward many, contributes something toward the request being accomplished.
Therefore he said: And to Archippus our fellow-soldier. If you are a
fellow-soldier, you ought to share in these things too. And this is
Archippus, about whom writing to the Colossians he said: Say to Archippus,
Take heed to the ministry which you received in the Lord, that you fulfill
it. He seems to me to be also of those enrolled in the clergy; whom he takes
also into the request, and calls fellow-soldier, so that in every way he may
help. And to the Church in your house. He did not omit even slaves here; for
he knows that often even words of slaves can overturn the master, and
especially when the request is on behalf of a slave; and those who
especially provoke, perhaps were those. He does not then let them fall into
envy, having honored them with the address together with the masters. But
neither does he allow the master to be indignant. For if he had spoken by
name, perhaps he would have been indignant; but if he had not mentioned
them, he would have been displeased. See then how wisely he found through
mentioning these too to honor them by the mention, and not to strike that
one. For the name of the Church does not let the masters be indignant, if
indeed they should be numbered together with the household servants. For the
Church does not know a difference of master, does not know of household
servant; from achievements and sins it defines this one and that. If then it
is a Church, do not be indignant that the slave was addressed together with
you; For in Christ Jesus there is not slave, not free; Grace to you and
peace. He brought him into remembrance of his own sins, having reminded him
of the grace. Consider, he says, how much God forgave you, how you were
saved by grace; imitate the Master. And he prays peace upon him; reasonably.
For then it too is present, when we imitate him, when the grace remains;
since that slave also who became merciless toward the fellow-slave, as long
as he did not demand the hundred denarii, the master's grace also remained;
but when he demanded, he was deprived and given over to the tormentors. βʹ.
Thinking these things then, let us too be compassionate, forgiving toward
those who sin against us. A hundred denarii are here the sins against us,
but those against God from us, ten thousand talents. And you know that sins
are judged also by the quality of the persons. I mean something of this
sort: He who insulted a private man sinned, but not similarly as he who
insulted the ruler; and more than this he who insulted the


greater ruler, but not similarly he who insulted the lesser; and he who
insulted the king, much more; and the insult is the same, but by the
superiority of the person it becomes greater. And if he who insults a king
has unbearable punishment because of the dignity of the person, he who
insults God will be liable for how many talents? So that even if we sin the
same things against God which we do against men, not even so is it equal,
but as much as the interval between God and men, so much of those sins and
these. But now I find the sins even more, becoming great not only by the
superiority of the person, but also by the nature itself; and the word I am
about to say is dreadful, and truly fearful, yet it must be said, so that
even thus it may =62.706= shake and unsettle our mind, showing that we fear
men much more than God, and honor men more than God. For consider: The
adulterer knows that God sees him, and this one he despises; but if a man
should see, he holds the desire. Such a one not only prefers men to God, not
only insults God, but also what is much harsher, fearing those, he despises
this one. For if he sees those, he holds the flame of desire; or rather what
flame? it is not a flame, but insult. For if it were not allowed to use a
wife, reasonably the matter would be a flame, but now it is insult and
wantonness; for if he sees men, he stops from the madness, but of the
longsuffering of God he takes less thought. Again another, the thief is
conscious that he seizes, and he tries to deceive men, and defends himself
toward those who accuse, and puts a form on the defense; but not being able
to persuade God, he does not take thought, nor is ashamed, nor honors. And
if the king should command to abstain from the goods of another, or rather
even to give his own, we all contribute readily; but when God commands not
to seize, nor to gather the things of others, we do not endure. Do you see
that we prefer men to God? The saying is burdensome and grievous; but show
that it is burdensome, flee the deed; but if you have not feared the deed,
how can I believe you when you say, We have feared the words, and you weigh
us down? You weigh yourselves down by the deed, and there is no word; but if
I should say the words, of which you do the deeds, you are indignant; and
how is it not absurd? May what is from me be a lie. I myself wish to receive
the reputation of abuse on that day, as having abused you idly and in vain,
rather than to see you charged about such things. And not only do you
yourselves prefer men to God, but you also force others. Many have forced
many household servants, and children; some dragged them to marriages
unwilling, others to serve in unseemly ministries, and in filthy lust and
seizures and greed and violences; so that the charge is double, and they
cannot even from the necessity find pardon. For if you yourself unwillingly
do the wicked things and because of the command of the ruler, first not even
so is the defense sufficient, yet the sin becomes harsher, when you also
force those to fall into the same. For what pardon would there be for such a
one thereafter? These things I said, not wishing to condemn you, but to show
how many debtors we are to God. For if even honoring man equally with God we
insult God, much more when we prefer men. And if these sins that happen
toward men are shown much greater toward God; how much more, when the sin
itself also is greater and harsher in quality? Let each examine himself, and
he will see that he does all things because of men. We would be exceedingly
blessed, if we did as many things because of God as we do because of men,
and the glory from men and the fear and the honor. If then we are liable for
so many things, we ought with all eagerness to forgive those who wrong us
and defraud us, and not to remember evil. For it is a way of release of
sins, not of labors


needing, nor expense of money, nor anything else, but simply a choice; one
must not send out a journey, not come to a foreign land, =62.707= not undergo
dangers and labors, but only to will. γʹ. What pardon, tell me, shall we
have in the things that seem to be difficult, when even a light matter and
having so much gain and so much benefit and no labor, we do not do? You
cannot despise money? you cannot spend what you have on those in need? Can
you not will some good? can you not forgive the one who has wronged? For if
you were not liable for so many things, and God only commanded to forgive,
ought you not? but now being liable for so many things, you do not forgive,
and this knowing that from what you have you are demanded by him? If then we
come to our own debtor, he knowing this, serves and receives and honors and
shows all kindness with abundance, and this not being released from the
loan, but wishing to make us mild about the demand; but you owing so much to
God, and being commanded to forgive, again so that you may receive, you do
not forgive? why ever, I ask? Woe is me! of how much lovingkindness do we
enjoy! and how much wickedness do we show, how much sleep, how much
sluggishness! how easy is virtue and having much benefit! how toilsome is
wickedness! but we, having fled what is so light, pursue what is heavier
than lead. Here there is no need of strength of body, not of wealth, not of
money, not of power, not of friendship, not of anything else, but it is
enough only to will, and the whole is accomplished. So-and-so grieved and
insulted and mocked? but consider that you too do many such things toward
others, and toward the Master himself, and let go and forgive; consider that
you say: Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors; consider that
if you do not forgive, you will not be able to say this with boldness; but
if you forgive, you demand the matter as a debt, not through the nature of
the matter, but through the lovingkindness of him who has given. And where
is it equal, that those who forgive the things toward fellow-slaves receive
the =62.708= forgiveness of the things sinned against the Master? yet we
obtain so much lovingkindness, since he is rich in mercy and compassions.
And that I may show you that even without these, and without the
forgiveness, from the forgiving alone you reap the gain, consider how many
friends such a one has, how everywhere all go through praises of such a one
saying: a good man, easily reconciled, he does not know how to remember
evil, at once he was struck and at once he was healed. Such a one having
fallen into some circumstance, who will not pity? who will not pardon when
he has sinned? who will not grant a favor when he requests on behalf of
others? who will not wish to be a friend and a slave of a soul so good? Yes,
I ask, let us do all things for this, not toward friends, not toward kinsmen
only, but also toward household servants. Forbearing, he says, the
threatening, knowing that your Lord too is in the heavens. If we forgive the
neighbors the sins, it will be forgiven us also, if we give alms, if we are
humble; for this too takes away sins. For if the publican, that he only say,
Be merciful to me the sinner, went down justified, much more we too, if we
are humble and contrite, shall be able to obtain much lovingkindness; if we
confess our own sins, and condemn ourselves, we shall wipe off the greater
part of the filth. For many are the ways that cleanse. From all sides then
let us war against the devil. I have said nothing difficult, nothing hard;
forgive the one who has grieved, pity the one in need, humble your soul; and
even if you are exceedingly a sinner, you will be able also to obtain the
kingdom, through these cleansing the sins themselves, and wiping off


the stain. And may it be that we all, having been thoroughly cleansed here
of all the filth of sins through confession, obtain there the promised good
things in Christ Jesus our Lord, with whom to the Father the glory, the
power, and the rest.

=62.707= HOMILY 2.

I thank my God always, making mention of you in my prayers, hearing of your
love and the faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the
saints, that the fellowship of your faith may become effectual in the
knowledge of every good thing which is in us unto Christ Jesus.

αʹ. He does not immediately from the preface ask the favor, but first having
admired the man, and praised him for the achievements, and shown no small
token of his own love, the remembering him always in the prayers, and having
said that many are refreshed toward him, and that he obeys and is persuaded
by all; then he also sets it last, putting him to shame especially by this.
For if others obtain what they need, much more Paul; if coming before the
others he was worthy to obtain, much more after the others, and asking a
matter not belonging to himself, but on behalf of anoth =62.708=.40 er. Then
so that it may not seem that he writes only for this, nor someone say, If
Onesimus were not, you would not have written, see how he also sets other
causes of the letter; first showing his love; then also commanding a lodging
to be prepared for him. Hearing, he says, of your love. This is admirable,
and much greater than to see the present. For it is clear that from this
being excessive, it also became conspicuous, and reached even to him; and
yet the interval of Rome and Phrygia was not small; for there he seems to me
to be from Archippus. For the Colossians are of Phrygia, to whom writing he
said, When the letter is read among you, cause that it be read also in the
Church of the Laodiceans, and that you also read the one from Laodicea. And
this is a city of Phrygia. I pray, he says, that the fellowship of your
faith may become effectual. Do you see him first giving rather than
receiving, and before to as =62.709= k the favor, furnishing his own much
greater? That, he says, the fellowship of your faith may become effectual in
the knowledge of every good thing which is in you unto Christ Jesus. That
is, that you may go through every virtue, That nothing be left out. For thus
the faith becomes effectual, when it has works. For without works faith is
dead. And he did not say, Your faith, but, The fellowship of your faith,
joining him to himself, and showing one body, and by this especially putting
him to shame. If you are a partner, he says, according to the faith, you
ought to share also according to the other things. For we have joy and much
consolation upon your love, because the bowels of the saints have been
refreshed through you, brother. Nothing so puts to shame as bringing forward
the good deeds of others, and especially when he is more reverend than
those. And he did not say, If you do for the others, much more for me. But
he hinted the same, yet managed it otherwise more gently. We have joy; that
is, You have given me boldness from the things that happened toward others.
And consolation. That is, we are not only pleased, but also comforted; for
those are our member. If then the harmony must be so great, that at the
refreshments of others those who are in afflictions, even if they obtain
nothing, rejoice because of those, as of one body having been benefited,
much more if you will refresh us also. And he did not say, That you yield,
that you are persuaded, but also more vehemently


and more expressively, That the bowels of the saints, as if about a child
longed for by the fathers and beloved. Thus the love, the affection shows
him also exceedingly loved by them. Wherefore having much boldness in Christ
I command you what is fitting. See how he makes himself safe, lest even
something of the things said from vehement love strike the hearer, so as to
be impatient. Therefore before saying, To command you, since it was heavy,
although said from love it was rather sufficient to soothe, yet still from
abundance he sets much correction, having said, Having boldness, which was
of one showing Philemon great; that is, You have given us boldness; and not
this only, but also, In Christ, having added, which is of one making clear
that not as more splendid in the world, nor as more powerful, but because of
the faith toward Christ; and then he set, To command you, and not this only,
but also, What is fitting, that is, a reasonable matter. And see from how
many things he constructs it. You do well to the others, he says; and to me,
and because of Christ, and that the matter is reasonable, and that love
gives it. Wherefore he also adds: For love's sake I rather beseech. As if he
said, I know indeed that even commanding I accomplish with much authority,
from the things before; yet since I make much eagerness about the matter, I
beseech. He showed both together, that he is also confident toward him (for
he commanded), and that he is exceedingly attached to the matter; wherefore
he beseeches. Being such, he says, as Paul the aged. βʹ. Wonderful, how many
things putting to shame! Paul, from the quality of the person; from the age,
that he is aged; from the more just of all, that also a Prisoner of Jesus
Christ. For who would not receive the athlete, and =62.710= crowned one, with
open hands? who seeing one bound because of Christ, would not grant ten
thousand things? Having then by so many things smoothed his soul beforehand,
he did not even immediately throw in the name, but having made so great a
request he postpones. For you know the angers of masters against runaway
household servants, and especially when they do this with theft, even if
they have good masters, how the wrath increases. This then he smoothed
beforehand with all these; and first having persuaded him to serve anything
whatsoever readily, and having prepared his soul toward all obedience, then
he brings on the petition, and says: I beseech you; and with praises;
Concerning my child, whom I have begotten in my bonds. Again the bonds put
to shame. And then the name; for not only did he quench the anger, but he
also made him be pleased. For he would not, he says, have called him child,
if he were not exceedingly useful. What I called Timothy, this also this
one. And in many places showing the affection, from the time of the
birth-pangs he puts him to shame; A prisoner I begot him, he says. So that
for this too he is worthy to obtain much honor, that he was brought forth in
the very contests, in the trials for Christ. Onesimus, who was once useless
to you. See how much understanding, how he confesses his sin, and by this
quenching his anger. I know, he says, that he was useless. But now he will
be useful to you and to me. He did not say, But now he will be useful to
you, lest that one contradict, but he brought in his own person too, so that
the hopes may be trustworthy. But now, he says, useful to you and to me. For
if useful to Paul who requires such accuracy, much more to the master. Whom
I have sent to you. And by this he quenched the anger, by delivering him.
For masters are especially angry then, when they are beseeched on behalf of
those absent; so that by this very thing too he more soothed. But you
receive him, that is, my bowels. And again he does not set the name naked,
but with a word that puts to shame, which is warmer than the child. He said,
Child; he said,


I have begotten; so that it is likely he loves him especially, because he
begot him in trials. For that we are especially inflamed about those
children is clear, when we have escaped dangers, and give birth in them; as
when Scripture says, Ouaibarochabel; and again when Rachel calls Benjamin,
Son, she says, of my sorrow. But you, he says, that is, my bowels, receive.
He shows his much love. He did not say, Accept, he did not say, Do not be
angry, but, Receive; that is, he is worthy not of pardon only, but also of
honor. Why? He has become a child of Paul. Whom I wished to keep on your
behalf, that he might minister to me in the bonds of the Gospel. Do you see,
how many things he constructed first, then he brought him into the honor of
the master? And see this too with how much wisdom; see also how liable he
makes that one, and how he honors this one. You have found, he says, how to
render the ministry toward me through him. Here he shows that he considered
his own rather than the child's, that he exceedingly reverences him. But
without your mind, he says, I wished to do nothing, that your good might not
be as by necessity, but by willing. This especially relaxes the one
requested, when even, the matter being useful, it is born with that one's
mind. For two good things happen from this, =62.711= that one also gains, and
this one is made more secure. And he did not say, That it might not be by
necessity, but, As by necessity. For I knew, he says, that even not having
learned, but having known it suddenly, you would not have been indignant,
but still from more abundance, that it might not be as by necessity. For
perhaps for this he was separated for an hour, that you might have him
eternally, no longer as a slave. Well the, Perhaps, so that the master may
yield. For since the flight happened from stubbornness and a distorted mind,
and not from choice, he says, Perhaps. And he did not say, For this he fled,
but, For this he was separated, soothing rather with a more fair name. And
he did not say, He separated himself, but, He was separated; for it was not
his contrivance to withdraw for this, nor upon that one. Which also Joseph
defending for the brothers says, that God sent me here; that is, he used
their wickedness for what was needed. For this he was separated, he says,
for an hour. And he contracts the time, and confesses the sin, and turns the
whole into economy. That you might have him eternally, he says, not in the
present time only, but also in the future, that you may have him always, no
longer a slave, but more honorable than a slave; for remaining a slave you
will have him more kindly disposed than a brother. So that you have gained
both in the time and in the quality; for thereafter he will not leap away.
That you might have him eternally, he says, instead of, That you might
receive him back. No longer as a slave, but above a slave, a beloved
brother, especially to me. You lost a slave for a little, and you will find
a brother unto the continual, a brother not yours only, but also mine. Here
the virtue also is much. And if he is my brother, you too will not be
ashamed. Through the child then he showed the affection, and through the
brother the much goodwill and the equal honor. γʹ. These things have not
been written simply, but so that we masters may not despair of the household
servants, nor set upon them exceedingly, but may learn to forgive the sins
to such household servants, so that we may not always be harsh, so that we
may not be ashamed from the slavery and take them as partners in all things,
when they are good. For if Paul was not ashamed even to call him child, and
bowels, and brother, and beloved, how would we be ashamed? And why do I say,
Paul? the Master of Paul is not ashamed to call our slaves his own brothers,
and shall we be ashamed? See how he honors us; he calls our slaves his own
brothers, and friends, and fellow-heirs. See where he came down. What then


having done shall we have accomplished the whole? We shall be able to do

For just as many because of not being vainglorious are vainglorious; so also
through being hum =62.712= bled they are lifted up through thinking great. For
example, a brother came or even a household servant, you received him, you
washed the feet, immediately you thought great; I have done, you say, what
no one has done, I have achieved humility. How then might one remain in the
humility? If he remembers the command of Christ that says: When you have
done all things, say, that We are unprofitable slaves; and again of the
Teacher of the world saying: I do not count myself to have apprehended. He
who has persuaded himself that he has achieved nothing great, whatever he
does, this one alone can be humble, he who does not think to have come to
the end. Many from humility were lifted up. But let us not suffer this. You
have done something humble? do not think great, since you have lost the

Do you see that it did not lift him up, but in every way contracted and
humbled him, and this having gone up to the extreme peak? And the three
children were in fire, and in the middle of the furnace, and what did they
say? We have sinned, we have done lawlessly with our fathers. This is to
have a contrite heart. Therefore they were able to say: But in a contrite
heart, and a spirit of humiliation may we be accepted. Thus even after
falling into the furnace, they were exceedingly humbled, and more than
before falling in. For when they saw the wonder that happened, thinking
themselves unworthy of the salvation, they were brought down into humility.
For when we are persuaded that we have been greatly benefited beyond desert,
then especially we are grieved. And yet what were those benefited beyond
desert? They gave themselves over to a furnace, they were taken captive,
others having sinned (for they were still young) and they did not murmur,
nor were they angry, nor did they say: Yes, for what good is it to us, that
we serve God? what have we benefited worshipping him? This one is impious,
and has become our master; with the idolaters we are punished by the
idolater, we were led into captivity, we fell from fatherland, from freedom,
from all the ancestral things, we have become captives and slaves, we serve
a barbarian king. They said none of these things; but what? We have sinned,
we have done lawlessly; and not even for themselves, but for the others they
make the petition; Because, they say, you have given us over to a most
hateful and most wicked king. Again Daniel a second time having been thrown
into a den, said: For God has remembered me. Why would he not have
remembered, O Daniel, when you glorified him before the king, saying: And I
not in the wisdom that is in me; but when you were thrown into a den of
lions because of not obeying that most wicked decree, why would he not have
remembered? For this very thing then. Have you not been thrown in now
because of him? Yes, he says, but I am a debtor of many. And if that one

thousand achievements of his own to say. And Eli too says: The Lord himself,
he will do what is pleasing before him. =62.713= δʹ. This belongs to
well-disposed slaves, not only in the benefits, but also in


the punishments, and in the chastisements to yield the whole to him. For how
is it not absurd, if we allow masters striking the household servants,
knowing that they spare them, inasmuch as they are their own, but God
punishing will not spare? This Paul also hinted saying: Whether we live, or
die, we are the Lord's. He does not wish, he says, his wealth to be
lessened, he knows how he punishes, he punishes his own children. No one of
us spares more than he who brought us from not being into being, who makes
the sun rise, who rains, who breathes in a soul, who has given his Son for
us. But as I said, and for which all these things have been said by me, let
us be humble, as one must, let us be moderate, as one must; let this not
become an occasion of lifting up for us. Are you humble, and more humble
than all men? do not think great because of this, nor reproach others, lest
you lose the boast. For this you are humble, that you may be released from
madness. If then through it you fall into madness, it is better not to be
humble. For hear Paul saying: Through the good it works death for me, that
sin might become exceedingly sinful through the commandment. When you come
into a thought to admire yourself upon humility, think of your Master, where
he came down, and you will no longer admire yourself nor praise, but you
will even laugh at yourself as having done nothing. Altogether consider
yourself to be a debtor. Whatever you do, remember that parable, Who of you,
he says, who has a slave, not when he has come in, does he say, Recline? I
do not say to you, but, Stand, and minister to me. Do we know favor to our
household servants who minister to us? By no means. But God knows favor to
us not ministering to him, but doing what is profitable for us. Yet let us
not be so disposed, as of him knowing favor, so that he may rather know, but
as fulfilling a debt; for the matter is truly a debt, and all things are of
a debt, whatever we do. For if buying children with silver, we wish them to
live wholly for us, and whatever they have to have for us, how much more he
who brought from not being into being, who after these things bought us with
precious blood? He paid a price for us, as much as no one would even endure
to pay for a child, he poured out his own blood. If then having ten thousand
souls, we should lay =62.714= down all these, shall we then render the equal?
By no means. Why? Because he not owing us did this, but the whole has become
of grace, and we thereafter owe. And he being God became a slave, and not
being under death, became under death in flesh; but we, even if we do not
lay down the souls for him, by the law of nature we shall altogether lay
them down, and unwilling we shall a little later stand away from it. Thus
also upon the money, even if we do not give it because of him, through the
necessity of the end we shall render it. Thus also upon humility, even if we
are not humble because of him, through the afflictions, through the
calamities, through the powers we shall be humbled. Do you see then how much
the grace is? He did not say: For what great thing do the martyrs do? even
if they do not die because of me, they will die altogether; but he knows
much favor to them, that willingly they put away what later from nature they
were going to unwilling. He did not say: For what great thing do those
emptying the money do? they will altogether put it away even unwilling, but
he knows much favor to these too, and he is not ashamed to confess before
all, that the master was fed by the slaves. For this too is glory of a
master, to have well-disposed household servants; and this is glory of a
master, so to love the slaves; and this is glory of a master, to appropriate
their things; and this is glory of a master, not to be ashamed to confess
before all. Let us then reverence so much love of Christ, let us be warmed
with the affection. And if someone is humble, even if cheap, and we hear
that he loves us, most of all


we are warmed toward him, and we bring him into vehement honor; and we love,
but our Master loves us so much, and we are not stirred up? Do not, I ask,
let us become so slack about the salvation of our souls, but let us also
love according to our power, and let us empty all things into his love, both
soul, and money, and glory, and anything whatsoever, with joy, with
gladness, with eagerness, not as furnishing something to him, but as to
ourselves. For such is the law of those who love. They consider themselves
to be well treated, when they are badly treated because of those loved. Let
us then also be so disposed toward our Master, so that we may also obtain
the good things to come, in Christ Jesus our Lord, with whom to the Father
the glory, with the Holy Spirit, now and always, and unto the ages of ages.
Amen.
