TO DEMETRIUS THE MONK ON COMPUNCTION, FIRST DISCOURSE.
=47.393=
αʹ. Seeing you, O blessed Demetrius, continually pressing upon us, and with
much vehemence demanding the discourses concerning compunction, I have always
both counted you blessed and marveled at the purity in your soul. For it is
not possible, unless one has first been well purified, and become superior to
all things of this life, to come to this desire of discourses. And this is
easy to see from those who are held even for a little by this longing, that
they receive so great a change all at once as to be transferred immediately
toward heaven; for as if loosing the soul from certain hard bonds, the cares
of the world, they thus let it fly toward its own place and kindred. But to
the many this is accustomed to happen seldom in all time; but you =47.394= , O
divine head, I know to be held throughout by this fire of compunction. And the
sleepless nights would bear me witness, and the fountains of tears, and the
love of solitude sitting always in your soul and flourishing there. What more
then could come to you from our words? For even this very thing, that you have
come to the very summit, yet think yourself still to be among those who go on
the ground, and call the winged soul a stony one, and continually taking hold
of my right hand, and kissing and weeping, say, Crush my heart that has been
hardened: of how much reverence, and of how much burning, would one set these
as proofs? If then, wishing to wake us who are sleeping, you have drawn us to
this subject, I accept both the philosophy and the great providence =47.395= ;
but if truly looking to your own case, and thinking that you need one who will
raise you up, I do not know how you could otherwise have taught us that you
need nothing of the things from us. Nevertheless even so we will yield to you,
and we will be persuaded for all reasons, both for the boldness toward God and
for the zeal about the request, and for the friendship toward us; but you in
return for these things repay us with your prayers, so that both our life may
be set right for the rest, and that in the present we may be able to say
something noble, and sufficient both to raise the souls that lie fallen and to
tighten and knit together those that are slack. From where then, from where
must we begin this discourse? What foundation, what base shall we set for it?
Is it not clear that the words of Christ, through which he calls the laughing
wretched, and places among the blessed those who mourn, speaking thus: Blessed
are they that mourn, for they shall be comforted; and, Woe to those who laugh
now, for you shall mourn and weep. And very reasonably; for truly of mourning,
of mourning and lamentations is the whole present season; such a calamity has
seized the whole inhabited world, such evils hold all men; which if anyone
should wish to examine with accuracy, if indeed it is possible with accuracy,
he will not cease lamenting and mourning; so has everything been overturned
and confused, and a trace of virtue nowhere. And what is more grievous, that
of the terrible things holding us we neither ourselves have perception, nor
give it to others, but we resemble a body flourishing outside, but wasting
within by much fire; and because of insensibility we differ in nothing from
those out of their minds, who both say and do many dangerous and shameful
things without fear, and are ashamed of none of these, but even pride
themselves, and think they are healthier than those who are in their right
mind. So also we, doing all the things of the sick, do not even know this very
thing, that we are sick. But in the case of the body, even if some chance
affection trouble us, we both call physicians, and spend money


and display endurance, and we do not first stand off, doing all things, until
we put away what pains us; but of the soul, being pricked every day, torn by
the bodily passions, burned, dashed down cliffs, and destroying itself in
every way, we take not even a brief account. The cause is that the disease has
seized all; and just as in the case of those whose bodies are ill, if it
should happen that none of the healthy is present with them, nothing prevents
all from falling into the last corruption, no one restraining their irrational
desires; so also in our case, because there is no one who is purely healthy in
the faith, but all are sick, some from a greater, some from a lesser share,
there is no one who will suffice for those who lie fallen. For if someone
standing over us from somewhere outside should learn well both the
commandments of Christ and the confusion of our way of life, I do not know
whether he will suppose any others more than us to be enemies of Christ; for
as if having been eager to come the opposite way to his commands, so have we
traveled this road. βʹ. And lest anyone think our words to be exaggeration, I
will now try to bring on the proof, from nowhere else, but from the
commandments of Christ themselves. What then does Christ say? It was said to
the ancients: You shall not murder. But I say to you, that everyone who is
angry with his brother without cause shall be liable to the judgment; and
whoever shall say to his brother, Raca, =47.396= shall be liable to the
council; and whoever shall say, Fool, shall be liable to the gehenna of fire.
These things then Christ. But we have trampled this law more than those who
disbelieve him, washing the brothers every day with ten thousand insults. And
what is more ridiculous, that while we guard against the name fool, we often
bring on other insults more bitter than this, as if that word alone had the
punishment. But it is not so, but punishing the insulter once for all, he has
set this penalty; and it is clear from what Paul also says, speaking thus: Be
not deceived; neither fornicators, nor idolaters, nor adulterers, nor the
effeminate nor those who lie with men, nor thieves nor the greedy, nor
drunkards, nor revilers, nor extortioners, shall inherit the kingdom of God.
And if he who says fool to his brother is worthy of the last punishment, he
who slanders him as a criminal and envious and facile and vainglorious, and
ten thousand other such things, how much fire of gehenna will he gather for
himself? For Fool and Raca are much lighter than the things said; therefore
leaving that, Christ has set this, so that you may learn that, if the more
bearable thing when said draws gehenna upon the speaker himself, much more
will the heavier and more unbearable things work this. And if some condemn the
word as exaggeration (for I know some who have suffered this, and think this
threat was spoken only to frighten), it is time for them to release also the
adulterers, those who lie with men, the effeminate, the idolaters from the
punishment spoken. For if he threatened the revilers for fear only, it is
clear that also those; for gathering all together at once, he thus brought on
the falling from the kingdom. What then, he says? Will the reviler lie with
the adulterer and the effeminate and the greedy and the idolater? Whether he
will give the same penalty as they, is for another time to inquire; but that
he will likewise fall from the kingdom as they, I am persuaded by Paul saying,
or rather by Christ working through him, that neither these nor those shall
inherit the kingdom of God. And not in this only, but also in many other
things many have suffered this, and think that the things that will certainly
come to pass have been spoken hyperbolically; but this is a diabolical snare.
For in order to dissolve the fear of the coming punishment of those who have
been pricked in the love of God, and to make them more slack about the
obedience of the commandment, he has introduced the consolation from
exaggeration, sufficient for the present to grant to the more slothful


souls a deceit, but to be convicted at the time of judgment, when there is no
gain. For what profit, tell me, to have been deceived now, and then to see the
deceit, when there is nothing to enjoy from repentance toward the account of
the resurrection? Let us not then vainly deceive ourselves, and cheat
ourselves to our harm, nor call forth another punishment, that from unbelief.
For not only not yielding to the commandments of Christ, but also not
believing them, brings on the most grievous punishment; and the unbelief comes
from being slack toward the fulfillment of the commandments. =47.397= For when
we do not wish to procure for ourselves the security from obedience, nor to do
the things commanded, so as to release the reasoning from the fear of the
things to come, being weighed down by conscience and choked, and striving to
throw off the great fear of the punishments that lie ready, we cast ourselves
into another pit, disbelieving those places of punishment. And just as those
held by a great fever, and leaping into the cold waters, do not shake off the
stifling heat, but add another fire to themselves; so also we, being pricked
by the conscience of sins, are forced to drown ourselves in the Euripus of
exaggeration, so that we may sin all things without fear for the rest. For we
are not only angry at the brothers when present, but we often fight them even
when absent; which is an excess of beastliness. And those greater than
ourselves and more powerful, even when they wrong and insult, we bear with
much gentleness, because of the fear from them, but toward those of equal
honor and lesser who have done us no harm we are hostile. So much greater
strength does the fear from men have than that from Christ. γʹ. Is there then
hope of salvation for us who show such neglect and contempt? From where, tell
me? What then that is burdensome did he demand, and what that is heavy from us
did Christ? Do not be angry, he says, with your brother without cause. And
this is much easier than to bear another who is angry without cause; for there
the matter of the fire has already been gathered, but here, with nothing
underlying, you yourself kindle the flame; and it is not equal, when another
brings a torch, to endure and not be burned, and when no one stirs, to be
still and at rest. For he who has mastered that has shown a test of the
greatest philosophy; but he who succeeds in this would be worthy of no wonder.
When then accomplishing the greater through fear of men, we do not even wish
the lesser through that of God, consider of how great punishment and vengeance
we make ourselves liable. Do not then think the brother to be only the one of
equal honor, nor the free, but also the household slave; For in Christ Jesus
there is neither slave nor free, according to the Apostle. So that even if we
rage against slaves without cause, we shall undergo the same penalty; for this
one too is a brother, and has been counted worthy of the true freedom, having
one spirit. Who then can show his own life clean of insult and of irrational
and vain anger? Do not tell me of the one who suffers this seldom, but of the
one who has never been caught at all; and until you show us this, you will not
even be able to dissolve the threat, because of not suffering this
continually. For also the thief and the fornicator, even if they work that sin
only once, are not released from the punishment because they have not dared
this often, but because they have dared it at all, they are punished. And the
thing lying next after this, who of the unbelievers would not think to be a
myth, seeing it overturned by us with such vehemence? For God having said: If
you bring your gift to the altar, and there remember that your brother has
something against you, leave there your gift before the altar, and go first,
be reconciled to your brother, and then coming, offer your gift; but we,
having become enemies toward one another and being disposed treacherously,
approach the altars. And God has made such haste for our


reconcil =47.398= iation, as to endure his own sacrifice remaining unfinished,
and the service being cut short, for the sake of dissolving the hatred toward
one another and the anger; but we so disregard the matter as even to keep the
enmity many days against our own head. For Christ punishes not only those who
remember wrongs, but also those who have been freed from this passion, yet
despise brothers who have been struck. For since remembering wrongs belongs to
those who have been wronged, but the one who wrongs would not easily be caught
by this passion, for this reason he has brought this one to that one, showing
that he is more liable to that one's punishment who also provided the root to
the sin. But we are not even so schooled, but we grieve the brothers even over
chance things; then as if nothing terrible had happened, so we neglect and
forget those who have been grieved by us, and we overlook the enmity
stretching out to a long length of time, not knowing that we shall pay so much
greater a punishment, by how many more days we allow to enter upon the grief,
both by this itself, and by the reconciliation becoming harder for us
afterward. For just as, when friendship holds us, nothing of the things that
divide will easily be able to prevail and be believed; so, when enmity has
first seized our souls, all things are easy and very ready for those who wish
to dash us together still more, the good things being disbelieved, and only
the wicked being believed. For these reasons he commands us, leaving the gift
upon the altar, first to be reconciled to the brother, so that we may learn
that if at that time one must not postpone this reconciliation, much more in
other times. But we hold the symbols of the things, yet have fallen from the
truth itself, greeting one another when the gift is about to be offered, but
doing this for the most part with lips and mouth only. But the Lord does not
wish this, but that we give the neighbor the kiss from the soul, and the
greeting from the heart. For this is to greet truly, but that is a certain
stage-play and hypocrisy, and he who loves thus would rather provoke God than
reconcile him. For he seeks from us the sincere and rooted friendship, not
this whose shape and form we carry about much, but whose whole power we have
quenched, which itself also is a proof of the lawlessnesses that hold us. For
because lawlessness shall be multiplied, he says, the love of the many shall
grow cold. And these things we men do, who have been commanded neither to be
angry, nor to have any as enemies, and if ever we should even have them, for
days only; For the sun, he says, let it not go down upon your wrath. And we do
not even stop up to this, but we also stitch plots against one another, and by
what we say and by what we do we bite our own members and eat them up; which
is of a bright madness. For we test those incurably possessed and mad
especially from this. And what of the law concerning the adversary, and that
concerning the unseemly desire, and the unchaste eyes, and the irrational and
harmful friendship? For the right eye, and the right hand, hint nothing else
to us than those who love us to our harm; and the law concerning women put
away, by whom has it not been dissolved and trampled often? =47.399= δʹ. For
the things laid down about not needing to swear I am even ashamed to speak,
not because of the oaths only, but also because of the continual perjuries.
For if even to swear, even if one keep the oath, is a charge and a
transgression of a commandment, where shall we put perjury? For if the excess
of yes and of no is of the Evil One, what then will that which is more than
this be? Then again he says: If anyone strike you on the right cheek, turn to
him also the other; and to him who wishes to go to law with you, and to take
your tunic, let him have the cloak also; and if anyone compel you one mile


one, go with him two; and to him who asks you, give, and him who wishes to
borrow from you do not turn away. What could one say to these things? For on
each of the things said only weeping and covering the face remains; so
diametrically have we arrayed ourselves toward the opposite, spending all our
time on judgments and fights and lawsuits and disputes, and bearing not even a
brief injury, neither that from deeds nor that from words, but even becoming
beasts at a chance thing. And if you should have some to name of those who
have spent much on the poor, but later are despised because of poverty, and
suffer ten thousand evils, you will say these are few and easily numbered; yet
not even so have you shown the philosopher written down for us here; for this
one is much more spiritual than that life. For it is not equal to give
willingly, and to bear being deprived of all. And why do I say to bear? For
what Christ said is much greater than this again. And the word has so removed
the souls of those wronged from anger toward those who wrong, that not only
they are not displeased over what has already been taken, but they even
willingly yield the things left, and show a greater eagerness about suffering
ill than those about doing ill. For when he who wishes to wrong finds the one
wronged more prepared to suffer than that one wished, and having filled his
own desire, sees the one injured ambitious from abundance, he goes away
defeated, blushing at the excess of long-suffering; and even if he be a beast,
even if something else more grievous than this, he will be more moderate for
the rest, having seen well from this comparison both his own wickedness and
that one's virtue. This life I seek now, which alone I see lying in the
Scriptures, but elsewhere and in the deeds nowhere. Do not tell me of the one
wronged and keeping silent; for it happens that he suffer this even from
inability. And if he showed this even toward those of equal honor and those
whom he could pursue, still not to such a point as to overshoot that one's
desire, and to give more than he wished, and to show over what was added
willingly the long-suffering shown over what was torn away by force. And what
is again higher than these and touching the very summit, those who work such
things against us, and injure us in money and bodies and all the rest, Christ
commanded to rank in the order of friends, and of genuine friends. For do not
only give in addition to the one who seizes and overreaches, he says, but also
love this one with the most vehement =47.400= and sincere friendship. This
then, this wishing also to establish, he said: Pray for those who despitefully
use you; which we are accustomed to do only toward those vehemently loved. And
lest you again think the words to be exaggeration, and introduce the
diabolical deceit, he adds a reasoning to the matter, and a just cause,
saying: For if you love those who love you, what reward have you? do not even
the publicans the same? And if you greet those who greet you, what excess do
you do? do not even the Gentiles this? When then we differ in nothing from the
publicans and the Gentiles in this, how is it not worthy to mourn and to beat
ourselves? And would that this only were the terrible thing; but now we fall
so short of loving our enemies, that we even turn away from and hate those who
love us. For to envy and to be jealous, and to pull down their glory and good
repute by what we do and by what we say, belongs to those who vehemently hate
and turn away. Thus we not only differ in nothing from the Gentiles, but we
are also disposed much worse than they in this. And Christ commanded to pray
for those who despitefully use, but we stitch plots, and having been commanded
to bless those who curse, we surround them with ten thousand curses. What
could be more vehement than this opposition and this fight, which we fight
against him who legislated these things, standing opposite to all his
commands? For the


tyranny of vainglory, which he himself dissolved through the words that
follow, but we have increased, having drawn its rule not only over the prayers
and fasts and the alms, but also over all the rest, and having subjected
ourselves to this madness more than any bought slave, because it is clear to
all, I pass over, saying only this much, that of men some, having given
themselves over to the last contempt, attend to none of the things commanded,
but others, having wished to obey from a small share, and having been eager to
keep some of the commandments, these too again have turned down into the same
loss as those, because they did not wish to strip off the bonds of vainglory.
And one does not give alms at all, but another, providing some of his own to
those in need, yet doing this with vainglory, is disposed no better than the
one who has not given. Thus the Evil One has cast all from every side into his
own snares. And if someone should be able to slip out of this harm also, held
by arrogance, he has fallen the same fall again, or rather even much more
grievous; for he does not go away having lost only what he did, but also
having taken on some evil. And I know many who are caught not only by these
causes, but also through loving and through being put to shame, and through
many other such pretexts, supplying those in need, not through the fear of God
and the commandment. Since then there are so many things that ruin the good
deeds, who of those who have fallen into these evils will be able to be saved
quickly? εʹ. And the, Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors,
who then is he who will say it with boldness? For even if we do no injustice
to the enemies, still we have the wound incurable. But Christ does not wish us
only to forgive thus, but also to number them among the first of friends. For
this reason at least, as I said before, he also commanded to pray =47.401= for
these. And if you do no injustice, yet turn away, and do not look with
pleasure, and have the wound flourishing in your soul, you have not yet done
the commandment which Christ commanded you. How then do you entreat God
himself to become propitious, you yourself not yet having become propitious to
those who have sinned against you? Such a thing a certain wise man also,
mocking, says: A man keeps anger against a man, and does he seek healing from
the Lord? Upon a man his equal he has no mercy, and concerning his sins he
makes request? He himself being flesh keeps wrath, and who will make atonement
for his sins? I wished to be silent for the rest, and to stop the discourse at
the things said; so much am I ashamed and blush to go further; for this war
which we have taken up against the commandments of Christ, and the undeclared
enmity, as the discourse proceeds, it shows more clearly. But what is the gain
from our silence, when the facts so cry out the fight, and before the facts
themselves he who is going to judge us knows all things clearly? Now not to
treasure upon the earth, but in heaven, even if few, still one might find some
who have succeeded well; for all the rest, as if having heard the opposite,
and having been charged to treasure upon the earth, so leaving heaven, have
glued themselves to all the things on the earth, and are mad about the
gathering of money, and having hated God, they love mammon; but the, Do not be
anxious about tomorrow, I know no one who has either heard or been persuaded,
because of our little faith. Therefore having covered my face I will pass by
this commandment. For it was necessary that Christ be believed even simply
declaring; but now not even having spoken irrefutable reasonings, and having
brought forward examples, both that of the birds and of the grass, is he
believed by us, but like the Gentiles, and perhaps even more small-souled, we
are gnawed over the care of these things, and though not even commanded to
pray for them, we spend all our zeal about these. This then, as I said,
blushing I will pass by the commandment; but I will come to the things after
these, if


perhaps I find some small consolation of this shame. What then does he say
after these? Judge not, that you be not judged. I then thought I would find a
consolation of our shame, but I see an addition not less than the former. For
even if nothing else had been sinned by us, this at least is sufficient to
lead away to the last gehenna; so bitter do we sit as judges of the things
sinned by others, but we overlook our own beams, and our whole life is spent
on being busybodies about the things of others and condemning. And you would
not quickly find anyone, not a man of this life, not a monk, not one of the
clergy, free from this sin; and yet so great a threat lies upon it; For with
what judgment you judge, you shall be judged, he says; and, With what measure
you measure, it shall be measured to you again. But nevertheless, though the
punishment is so great, and the sin has no pleasure, we all run upon the evil,
as if eager and contentious, not only through one, but also through many roads
to en =47.402= ter the furnace of gehenna. And not only toward the things that
seem to be toilsome, but also toward the lighter than these we have likewise
fallen back, transgressing equally both these and those, and showing through
the easiest that we do not accomplish the more toilsome either because of our
own contempt, not because of the heaviness of the commands. For what toil,
tell me, has it, not to be a busybody about the things of others, nor to
condemn the faults of the neighbors? On the contrary, toil is needed for being
curious and judging others. Who then, having heard these things, will ever
believe us, that we came to disobey from sloth, and not from eagerness and
from not wishing? For when the things he commands to do are easy and very
ready for those who wish, but the things he forbids more grievous and
toilsome, and we, leaving the things commanded, do the things forbidden, will
not the enemies say that we sin these things as being contentious against him?
For that the commands of Christ have no toil, he himself declared saying: Take
my yoke upon you; for my yoke is easy, and my burden is light. But we, by
unspeakable sloth, make the light things appear burdensome to the many. To the
one then who wishes to be idle and to sleep throughout, even partaking of food
and of drink seems altogether toilsome; but those who are awake and sober do
not shrink even from the things very marvelous and hard to succeed in, but
these dare them with more courage than the slack and sleepy dare the things
very easy. For there is nothing, nothing so easy, which much shrinking does
not show us very heavy and burdensome, just as there is nothing toilsome and
hard, which zeal and eagerness do not make exceedingly easy. For what, tell
me, was more grievous than to be in danger every day of dangers threatening
death? But nevertheless the blessed Paul called this light, speaking thus: For
the momentary light of the affliction works for us from excess to excess an
eternal weight of glory. For even if by nature the thing is heavy, yet by the
hope of the things to come it becomes light; which cause he himself also set,
saying: We not looking at the things seen, but at the things not seen. ςʹ. And
let us see also the things that follow. Do not give, he says, the holy things
to the dogs, nor cast your pearls before the swine. Christ then has given the
commandment even in the rank of a command; but we, by vainglory and irrational
ambition, have overturned this command also, receiving corrupt and unbelieving
men and men full of ten thousand evils simply and without examination into the
communion of the mysteries, and before giving an exact test of their own mind,
uncovering to them the whole word concerning the doctrines, and leading those
not yet able to see the vestibules all at once into the inner shrine. For this
reason at least some of those so initiated, having quickly leapt away,
wrou =47.403= ght ten thousand terrible things. And not in the case of the
others only, but also in our own case this most dreadful


commandment we overlook, whenever there is need to partake of the immortal
mysteries, often doing this with the uncleanness and shamelessness that lies
in us. And not only these commandments, but also those that follow one might
see wholly overturned by all. For Christ having said, Whatsoever you wish that
men should do to you, so do you also to them; we do all things that we do not
wish to suffer, and having been commanded to enter through the narrow gate, we
seek the broad everywhere. And that some of those of this life should embrace
and love this is not very marvelous; but that men who seem to have been
crucified should track it out more than those, this is what is full of much
astonishment, or rather the matter even resembles a riddle. For of the monks
as a rule you will hear all these words at once first, if ever you invite them
to come to some stewardship, asking whether there is rest to be found, and
whether the one who calls is able to give rest, and up and down much the name
of rest. What do you say, O man? Having been commanded to walk the afflicted
road you ask about rest, and having been ordered to enter through the narrowed
gate you seek a broad one? and what could be worse than this distortion? And
lest you think that I say these things now as condemning others, I will tell
you my own. For when formerly I had resolved, leaving the city, to come to the
tents of the monks, I was much in this, searching and being a busybody, from
where the supply of the necessaries would be, and whether it was possible to
eat bread daily and fresh, and whether there was anyone forcing one to use the
same oil for lamp and for food, whether there was anyone pushing toward the
hardship of the pulses and throwing one into a hard work, such as commanding
to dig or to carry wood or to carry water, and to serve all the other such
things; and in short the word about rest was much with us. And those who take
on the services of rulers and the administrations of public affairs busy
themselves about none of these, but if only the matter should have gain, a
timely gain, and if they should ever be able to be confident about this, they
think no longer of toils, nor dangers, nor dishonors, nor slavish services,
nor long journeys abroad, nor the living in a foreign land, nor insults, nor
tortures, nor change of seasons, nor often failing of the hope at the end, nor
untimely deaths, nor separation from one's own, nor the bereavement of wife
and children, nor any other of the hard things; but having been maddened by
the desire of money, they endure all things, through which they expect to
attain this. But we, when not money nor earth is set before us, but heavens
and the goods in the heavens, which neither eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor
has it gone up upon the heart of a man, we inquire about rest; so much more
pitiable and softer are we than those. What do you say, O man? Being about to
be sent toward heaven, and to receive the kingdom there, you ask whether there
is anything hard along this road and this journey abroad, and you are not
ashamed nor do you blush, nor going away =47.404= do you bury yourself? For
even if all the terrible things among men should be, even if insults, even if
insolences, even if dishonors, even if slanders, even if sword, even if fire,
even if iron and beasts and drownings, even if famine and disease and as many
hard things as life has brought from the beginning until now, will you not,
tell me, laugh at all and despise them? will you even think these at all? And
what could be more old-womanish than this soul, and what more low and more
wretched? For him who has been seized by the desire of the heavenly things I
do not say only that he must not seek rest, but that he must not even perceive
it when present. Or how is it not absurd, that those who love unseemly are so
wholly of the beloved, as to think nothing else of the things in the present
life pleasant except those and the company of those, although there are many;
but we, seized not by some unseemly love, but by that which is above all, not
only do not despise rest when present, but even seek it when absent?


ζʹ. Not yet has anyone, beloved, been seized by desire of the heavenly goods
as one ought to be seized; for otherwise he would have thought all the things
that seem to be hard a shadow and much laughter. He then who admires the
present things would never be counted worthy of the contemplation of the
things to come; but he who looks down on these, and thinks all things to be
disposed no better than a shadow and a dream, will quickly attain those great
and spiritual things. For to whomsoever this good comes genuinely, it shows
such strength as fire in the thorns, and even if it find the soul choked by
ten thousand evils, even if many the ropes of the sins lying around, even if
the flame of the desires burning vehemently, and much the tumult of the things
of this life standing around, driving all those things at once with a certain
vehement scourge it sets them far from the soul. And just as fine dust would
never stand against the force of a rushing wind, so neither will a multitude
of unseemly desires be able to bear the rush of compunction when it has fallen
in, but it vanishes and is dispersed faster than dust and any smoke. And if
the love of bodies so enslaves a soul as to lead it away from all things, and
to nail it to the tyranny of the beloved alone, what would not the longing of
Christ work, and the fear of alienation from there? For just as it is hard, or
rather even impossible, to mix fire with water, so, I think, to bring luxury
and compunction into the same; for these are opposite, and they are
destructive of one another. For the one is mother of tears and of sobriety,
the other of laughter and of derangement; and the one makes the soul light and
winged, the other sets it heavier than any lead. And this I will try to
establish not from my own words, but from him who was held by this good
desire. Who then is this? Paul the fiery lover of Christ, who was so wounded
by this longing, as to groan at the delay and the lingering of the departure
from here; For we who are, he says, in this tent groan; and he longed and
wished still to remain here because of Christ. For to remain, he says, in the
flesh is more necessary for you; that is, that the faith toward Christ may be
increased. For this reason also famine and thirst and nakedness and bonds and
deaths and voyages over the sea and shipwrecks and all the other things which
he listed, he bore, and not only was he not weighed down by these, but he even
rejoiced; and the cause was the love of =47.405= Christ. Therefore he also
said: For in these, he says, in all we more than conquer through him who loved
us. And do not marvel; for if friendship of men has often made one dare death,
what would not that of Christ work? what hardship would it not make easy? Thus
all things were bearable to that man looking only toward the one longed for,
and thinking to suffer all things because of him greater than all pleasure and
enjoyment, as indeed it also is. For he did not even think he was on the
earth, nor in the present life, nor to be turning among men, but as already
having the heavenly lot, and living as a citizen with angels, and having
received the kingdom, and enjoying face to face, so he despised the pleasant
and the painful things in the present, and of rest there was no account with
him, which is continually sought among us, but he cried saying: Until this
present hour we both hunger and thirst, and are naked, and are buffeted, and
have no certain dwelling, and labor working with our own hands; being reviled
we bless, being persecuted we endure, being blasphemed we entreat. And again,
We have become as the filth of the world, the offscouring of all until now.
For having turned the eyes of the soul once for all toward heaven, and having
been struck by the beauty there, he did not endure to return toward the earth
again; but as some beggar and poor man, shut up all the time in a dark and low
little house, when he sees a king gleaming with much gold and with lightnings
of stones, would no longer choose even to take into mind the house of his own
poverty, but will transact all things for the sake of being transferred toward


that dwelling, if it were possible; so also that blessed man, having looked
upon the things in the heavens, despised the poverty here, and though turning
among men because of the necessity of the body, he turned toward none of the
things here, but transferred his whole self toward that city. And why do I
speak of the painful things in the present life? so much did the longing of
Christ incline into him, that if it were even set forth to bear those immortal
punishments for the sake of Christ, he would never have declined even this.
For not as we hirelings, fearing gehenna, and reaching after the kingdom, so
did that man also serve Christ, but seized by some other longing much better
than this and blessed, he both suffered and did all things for nothing else,
but only that he might console the love which he had toward Christ; which so
took his reasoning by force, that even what was more honorable than all to
him, I mean being with Christ, for which he despised both gehenna and the
kingdom of heaven, even from this again he chose to be alienated willingly,
choosing for Christ's sake even the unspeakable falling away, as one of the
most desirable things, to embrace it and to choose it with much eagerness. ηʹ.
And perhaps now the thing said seems unclear to many; but when I say it more
clearly, then again it will seem unbelievable to those to whom it was first
unclear. And nothing marvelous; for that blessed man also, having expected to
be disbelieved about these things, for this reason speaking beforehand said: I
speak truth in Christ, I do not =47.406= lie, my conscience also bearing
witness with me in the Holy Spirit. And nevertheless having added so many
things to the word, and having called such witnesses of his conscience, he is
disbelieved even still now. What then is this which he says? Hear. Having
discoursed about the terrible things in this world, and having said, Who shall
separate us from the love of Christ? tribulation, or distress, or persecution,
or famine, or nakedness, or danger, or sword? and having gone through all the
things upon the earth, he goes up into heaven, and wishing to show that it is
no great thing to despise the punishments here because of Christ, he added,
that Neither angels, nor principalities, nor powers, nor things present, nor
things to come, nor height, nor depth, nor any other creation shall be able to
separate us from the love of God, which is in Christ Jesus our Lord. And what
he says is such: Not only will men not be able to tear me from this
friendship, but not even angels, not even if all the heavenly powers should
come together into the same, will they be strong for this. What do I say? not
even if it be necessary to fall from a kingdom, not even if to fall into
gehenna because of Christ, not even is this fearful to me. For height and
depth, and life and death, hint nothing else than this. And he said these
things, not as if angels were going to attempt to set him apart from Christ,
but he even hypothesizes in the word the things that will never come to pass
at all, so that he may be able to establish the great longing which he had and
make it clear to all. For such is the custom of those who love; they cannot
bear to hold the love in silence, but they carry out the flame to all who are
fit, resting their soul by continually narrating the excess of the longing.
Which then that blessed man also did then; and having embraced all things in
the word, the things that are, the things that will be, the things that have
happened, the things that will never happen, the visible and the invisible,
and every punishment, and every rest, as if these were not sufficient for him
toward the establishing of the passion, having again set up in the word and
placed other such things not existing (for the, Another creation, is of the
things not existing), so he added, making clear that nothing of all the things
said shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ
Jesus our Lord. And that man kindled his own longing to so great a height; but
we who have been commanded to be imitators of him do not even bear the
afflictions here easily, but we are distressed and impatient no less than
those with fever. For this long disease, which has seized our


souls, has become incurable by time, so to speak, and we cannot even take a
thought of the sincere health, but the recovery of this seems to us henceforth
even impossible. And if we hear someone bringing the apostles into the midst,
and speaking of their achievements, when we ought immediately also to mourn
ourselves, that we have stood off so far from them, we do not even think the
matter to be a sin, but we are so disposed as if it were not even possible to
reach that height. And if someone asks the cause, we immediately put forward
this irrational defense =47.407= saying: That one was Paul, and that one was
Peter, and that one was John. What is, That one was Paul, that one was Peter?
did they not, tell me, share the same nature? were they not brought into life
by the same road as we, were they not fed with the same foods? did they not
breathe the same air? did they not use the same things? did not some of them
even have wives and children, others even trades of this life, and others were
even brought down into the very pit of wickedness? But they enjoyed much, he
says, of the grace of God. But if we were commanded to raise the dead, or to
open eyes of the blind, or to cleanse lepers, or to set the lame upright, or
to cast out demons, and to heal the other such diseases, the things of this
defense would have a time for us; but if an examination of way of life is now
set forth, and a display of obedience, what account would these things have
toward that? For you too enjoyed divine grace being baptized, and you partook
of Spirit, even if not toward the doing of the signs, yet as much as suffices
toward receiving a right and accurate way of life; so that the distortion is
of our sloth only. And Christ also on that day does not give the prizes simply
to those who have worked the signs, but to those who have done his commands.
For Come, he says, you blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for
you from the foundation of the world, not because you worked wonders, but
because I hungered, and you gave me to eat; I thirsted, and you gave me drink;
I was a stranger, and you took me in; naked, and you clothed me; I was sick,
and you visited me; I was in prison, and you came to me. And in the beatitudes
also he nowhere places those who do the miracles, but those who have a right
life. θʹ. So that even if that grace has now been contracted, this will not be
able to harm us at all, nor even to apologize for us when we undergo the
accounts of the things done. For we do not marvel at those blessed men because
of the signs; for this was wholly of the power of God; but because they
displayed an angelic way of life; and this is a work with the impulse from
above and their own zeal. And I do not say this now, but also that imitator of
Christ himself. For when he was stretching himself against the false apostles
writing to the disciples, and wished to establish the difference of the
approved and the counterfeit ministry, he did this not from the signs, but
from his achievements, speaking thus: Are they ministers of Christ? I speak as
beside myself; I more; in labors more abundantly, in prisons more abundantly,
in stripes above measure, in deaths often; from Jews five times I received
forty save one, three times I was beaten with rods, once I was stoned, three
times I was shipwrecked, a night and a day I have spent in the deep; in
journeys often, in dangers of rivers, in dangers of robbers, in dangers from
race, in dangers from Gentiles, in dangers in city, in dangers in wilderness,
in dangers in sea, in dangers among false brothers, in toil, in hardship, in
watchings often, in hunger and thirst, in fasts often, in cold and nakedness.
Apart from the things besides, that which comes upon me daily, the care of all
the Churches. Who is weak, and I am not weak? who is made to stumble, and I do
not burn? For these things I marvel at the =47.408= apostles; since those who
without these were permitted by some stewardship to display signs not only did
not become marvelous, but even unapproved, as Christ also shows saying:


Many will say to me in that day: Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name,
and in your name cast out demons, and in your name do many mighty works? And I
will say to them: Depart from me, all who work lawlessness; I never knew you.
For this reason he also exhorted the disciples saying: Do not rejoice that the
demons are subject to you, but that your names have been written in the
heavens. For a right way of life even without these will receive the crowns,
and will have nothing less because of this then; but a lawless life will not
be able to flee the punishment even with signs. So that this word is
superfluous for us, and not only superfluous, but also dangerous, and giving
handles to many of the heretics. For if they did not become so marvelous
through their own choice, but through the grace of Christ only, what prevents
all from becoming such? For the grace, unless it first sought the things from
us, would have been poured out all at once into the souls of all; for God is
no respecter of persons; but since it seeks the things from us, for this
reason it follows some and remains, and from some it flies, and to the rest it
does not even reach at the beginning. And that God, having first examined
choice, thus gave the grace before that blessed man displayed anything
marvelous, hear what he says concerning him: He is a vessel of election to me,
to bear my name before both Gentiles and kings, and the whole race of Israel.
These things he who walks in our hearts testified when the grace was not yet
present. Let us not then deceive ourselves, beloved, saying that it is
impossible for anyone to become according to Paul. For as to the grace and the
signs there will no longer be another Paul henceforth, but as to the exactness
of the life, each of those who wish could become such; and if they are not, it
is only because they do not wish. But I do not know how I fell into this
absurdity, seeking some of those now according to Paul, not even able to see
according to the third or the fourth after him. For these reasons one must
mourn and weep and lament, not one day nor a second only, but the whole life;
for if anyone should set himself into this habit, he will sin with difficulty
for the rest. And if you distrust the word, examine those who mourn, I mean
the worldly mournings, not some of these many who live the toilsome life, but
those of the delicate, who know nothing else than to live in luxury. For
these, these who practice getting drunk and stuffing the belly, who complete
the luncheons until evening, and stretch the dinners into midnight, who seize
the things of others, and spare neither widow, nor poor man, nor the
powerless, who display much cruelty, if ever they should be seized by a
vehement mourning able to turn a soul upside down from below and to throw it
into confusion, having cast out all those sluggish and lawless desires, they
transfer themselves into the life of the philosophers, displaying hard living,
vigil, sleeping on the ground, endurance, fasting, silence, gentleness,
humility, much loving-kindness. And yet those =47.409= seizing the things of
others would even gladly give their own at that time; and even if someone
should wish to set fire to the goods with the whole house, they are not
provoked. And I know many who, after the loss of those dearest, some have
exchanged the way of life of the city for that in the fields and the goods in
this, others have built houses beside the tombs of the departed, and have
ended their life there. But these things later; and until the mourning is at
its height, they have no account of any of the present things, but the madness
which they had about the care and the gathering of money, which they had about
the getting of power and of the glory from the many, like some grass, or even
a flower of grass, having burned it with the flame of despondency, they have
thrown it from the soul, and the mind then changes toward so great a
philosophy, as not even to endure as far as words any of the pleasant things
according to the present life, but all the things that formerly seemed to be
toward pleasure both offend them and


appear most bitter; and no one then, neither of the household slaves, nor of
the friends, would dare to break a voice about the things in this world, even
those very urgent; for all things getting out of the way yield to the words
about philosophy, the soul through the mourning then as in some sacred place
being taught the nothingness of human nature, the temporary nature of the
present age, the easily-destroyed and unstable nature of the things of this
life, the hypocrisy of the stage of the things that happen. Then much contempt
of money, then destruction of anger, then neglect of ambition, and neither
could envy lurk, nor conceit madden him who has been crushed by the mourning,
nor does desire inflame the unchaste, but all these having been banished from
there, one reasoning alone is housed, that which continually holds the image
of the dead; this is both foods, and drink, and sleep, and pleasure, and rest,
and much consolation; this is glory, and wealth, and power, and luxury. ιʹ. So
we ought to mourn the loss of our salvation, that I may say nothing more; with
such longing and eagerness the eye of the soul ought to have been stretched
there from all things, and the memory and impression of that ought to be all
things to us. But now those who have lost children and wives occupy the mind
toward nothing else, than toward imagining those who have been torn from them;
but we who have lost a kingdom of heavens think of all things rather than
this. And of those no one, even if he happen to be of the most royal race, is
ashamed at the law of mourning; but he will even sit on the ground, and will
lament bitterly, and will change the clothing, and will undergo all the other
things of that punishment with much eagerness; and he will not reckon
upbringing, nor habit of body, nor the diseases after these, those from that
hardship, but he will bear all things with much ease. And these things not men
only, but also women, even if they are very weak, display, and more than
these. But we, mourning not children, not wives, but destruction of a soul, of
a soul not another's, but our own, pretend, alleging weakness of =47.410= body
and tenderness of upbringing. And would that this only were the terrible
thing! but now we do not even do those things in which we need nothing of the
activity of the body. For where, tell me, is there need of bodily strength,
when one must crush a heart, when one must pray sober and awakened, when one
must reckon up the sins, when one must dissolve conceit and arrogance, when
one must humble reasoning? For these are the things that also make God
propitious to us, and they do not need much toil; and we do not even do these.
For neither putting on of sackcloth, nor shutting oneself in a little house,
nor sitting in darkness, is this alone the mourning, but continually turning
over the memory of one's own evils, and torturing the conscience with these
reasonings, continually measuring the length of the road, how far we fall
short of the kingdom of the heavens. And how will this be? he says. How? if we
have gehenna always before the eyes, and the angels then running everywhere,
and gathering from the whole inhabited world those who are going to be led
away into gehenna; if we reckon the falling from the kingdom, how great an
evil, even without the gehenna. For even if that fire had not been threatened
to us, nor immortal punishments were waiting for us, this very thing alone, to
be alienated from Christ, the meek and loving toward man, who gave himself
over for us unto death, and suffered all things, that he might deliver us from
that punishment, and reconcile us to his own Father when we were enemies by
the faults, even if we were not going to fall from those unspeakable and
immortal goods set forth, is greater than all punishment, and sufficient to
wake souls, and to persuade them to be sober throughout. For if merely reading
the example of the five virgins, whom the scarcity of oil shut outside the
bridechamber, we grieve equally with those themselves at their evils, and


we are troubled; if we consider that we ourselves also will suffer this if we
are slothful, who then will be so stony, as, when the example itself is
continually turning the soul, to fall into neglect? It was possible then also
to stretch the discourse to greater length, but since it has been said to us
only for the sake of obedience and not of another need, even these things have
been written beyond what is needed. For I know well that you yourself have all
the virtue of compunction exactly, and you would impart it to others even
being silent, if only they should be counted worthy to live a little with your
reverence, and to see the crucified life. So that if those now living must
learn compunction, they must walk to your lodging; but those after these
things, hear your things; for I think the narration of your life alone will
provide much impulse toward this. Therefore I beg and entreat you to pay us
the return at last, and to give your prayers in exchange, so that we may not
only speak about compunction, but also display this thing through the deeds;
since teaching without doing not only has no gain, but also brings much loss
and condemnation to him who administers his own life with such carelessness.
For not everyone, he says, who says to me, Lord, Lord, but whoever shall do
and teach, this one shall be called great in the kingdom of the heavens.
