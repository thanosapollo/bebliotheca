* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Miltiades, son of Cimon, an Athenian, when he most of all flourished both by
the antiquity of his race and by the glory of his ancestors and by his own
moderation, and was of such an age that his fellow-citizens could not only
hope well of him but even be confident that he would be such as they judged
him once they had come to know him, it happened that the Athenians wished to
send colonists to the Chersonese. of which kind when there was a great number
and many sought partnership in that migration, chosen from them were sent to
Delphi to consult, who should consult Apollo by what leader they should
especially use. For then the Thracians held those regions, with whom it had to
be fought with arms. To these consulting, the Pythia instructed by name that
they should take Miltiades as commander for themselves: if they did that,
their undertakings would be prosperous.    With this response of the oracle
Miltiades, having set out with a chosen band by fleet to the Chersonese, when
he had approached Lemnos and wished to bring the inhabitants of that island
under the power of the Athenians, and had demanded that the Lemnians do that
of their own accord, they, mocking, answered that they would then do that,
when he, having set out from home by ships with the north wind, had come to
Lemnos. For this wind, rising from the north, holds adverse for those setting
out from Athens. Miltiades, not having time for delaying, directed his course
where he was heading, and arrived at the Chersonese.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
There in a short time, the forces of the barbarians having been scattered,
having taken possession of the whole region which he had sought, he fortified
places suitable for forts, settled in the fields the multitude which he had
led with him, and by frequent raids enriched them.    Nor was he helped less
in that matter by prudence than by good fortune. For when he had conquered the
armies of the enemy by the valor of his soldiers, he settled affairs with the
highest equity and himself resolved to remain there. For he was among them of
royal dignity, although he lacked the name, nor had he obtained that more by
command than by justice. Nor any the less did he render offices to the
Athenians, from whom he had set out. By which things it came about that he
obtained perpetual command no less by the will of those who had sent him than
of those with whom he had set out. The Chersonese having been established in
such a manner, he returns to Lemnos and from the pact demands that they hand
over the city to him: for they had said that, when he had set out from home
with the north wind and had arrived there, they would surrender, for he had a
home in the Chersonese.    The Carians, who then inhabited Lemnos, although
the matter had fallen out beyond expectation, yet, caught not by the word but
by the favorable fortune of their adversaries, did not dare to resist and
migrated from the island. With equal good fortune he brought the remaining
islands, which are named the Cyclades, under the power of the Athenians.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
In the same times Darius, king of the Persians, having transported an army
from Asia into Europe, resolved to make war on the Scythians. He made a bridge
on the river Ister, by which he might lead across his forces. Of that bridge,
while he himself was away, he left as guards the princes whom he had led with
him from Ionia and Aeolis, to whom he had given perpetual commands of the
several cities.    For thus he thought he would most easily keep under his
power those speaking the Greek language who inhabited Asia, if he had handed
over the towns to be guarded to his friends, to whom, himself having been
overthrown, no hope of safety would be left. In this number then was
Miltiades, to whom that custody was entrusted. This man, when frequent
messengers brought word that Darius was conducting the matter badly and was
being pressed by the Scythians, exhorted the guards of the bridge not to let
go the occasion given by fortune of freeing Greece.    For if Darius had
perished with those forces which he had transported with him, not only would
Europe be safe, but also those who inhabited Asia, Greek by race, would be
free from the domination and danger of the Persians. That could easily be
effected: for the bridge having been cut, the king would perish within a few
days either by the steel of the enemy or by want.    When most were coming
over to this counsel, Histiaeus of Miletus, lest the matter be finished,
opposed, saying that the same thing was not expedient for themselves, who held
the heights of command, and for the multitude, because their domination rested
on the kingdom of Darius: which having been extinguished, they themselves,
driven from power, would pay penalties to their own citizens. And so he so
abhorred from the counsel of the rest that he thought nothing more useful to
themselves than that the kingdom of the Persians be confirmed. When very many
had followed this man's opinion, Miltiades, not doubting that with so many
privy his counsels would come to the king's ears, left the Chersonese and
again migrated to Athens. Whose plan, even if it did not prevail, is
nevertheless greatly to be praised, since he was more friendly to the liberty
of all than to his own domination.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
But Darius, when he had returned from Europe into Asia, his friends exhorting
that he bring Greece under his power, prepared a fleet of five hundred ships
and set Datis over it and Artaphernes, and gave them two hundred thousand of
infantry, ten thousand of cavalry, inserting as cause that he was an enemy to
the Athenians, because with their aid the Ionians had stormed Sardis and had
killed his garrisons. Those royal prefects, the fleet having been brought to
Euboea, quickly took Eretria and sent all the citizens of that people,
snatched away, into Asia to the king. From there they approached Attica and
led their forces down into the plain of Marathon. That is distant from the
town about ten miles.    The Athenians, moved by this tumult so near and so
great, sought aid nowhere except from the Lacedaemonians and sent Phidippus, a
runner of that kind who are called hemerodromoe, to Lacedaemon, that he might
announce that there was need of aid as quickly as possible.    At home however
they create ten praetors, who should be over the army, among them Miltiades.
Among whom there was a great contention, whether they should defend themselves
by the walls or go to meet the enemy and decide by battle-line. Miltiades
alone most strove that camps be made at the first possible time: if that were
done, both spirit would come to the citizens, when they saw that their valor
was not despaired of, and the enemy by the same thing would be slower, if they
noticed that it was dared to fight against them with so small forces.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
At this time no city was of aid to the Athenians except the Plataeans. That
sent a thousand of soldiers. And so by the arrival of these ten thousand of
armed men were completed, which band burned with a wonderful desire of
fighting. By which it was done that Miltiades prevailed more than his
colleagues. Therefore, driven by his authority, the Athenians led their forces
out of the city and made camp in a suitable place. Then on the next day, the
battle-line having been drawn up under the roots of the mountain in a region
not most open (for trees were sparse in many places), they joined battle with
this plan, that they might both be covered by the height of the mountains and
that the cavalry of the enemy might be hindered by the stretch of trees, lest
they be shut in by multitude.    Datis, although he saw the place not equal
for his men, yet relying on the number of his forces desired to clash, and the
more so because he judged it useful to fight before the Lacedaemonians came as
aid. And so he led forth into line a hundred thousand of infantry, ten
thousand of cavalry, and joined battle. In which the Athenians prevailed so
much more by valor that they routed a tenfold number of the enemy, and so
terrified them that the Persians sought not the camp but the ships. Than which
battle nothing more noble has yet existed: for never has so small a band laid
low such great resources.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
Of which victory it does not seem foreign to teach what sort of reward was
given to Miltiades, that it may more easily be understood that the nature of
all states is the same. For as the honors of the Roman people were once rare
and slight and for that cause glorious, but now poured out and worn, so we
find they once were among the Athenians.    For to this Miltiades, who had
freed Athens and all Greece, such honor was given, that in the portico which
is called Poecile, when the Marathonian battle was painted, his image was
placed first in the number of the ten praetors and he was exhorting the
soldiers and joining battle.    That same people, after it had obtained a
greater command and was corrupted by the largess of magistrates, decreed three
hundred statues to Demetrius of Phalerum.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:
After this battle the Athenians gave a fleet of seventy ships to the same
Miltiades, that he might pursue with war the islands which had aided the
barbarians. In which command he compelled most to return to duty, some he took
by force.    From these, when he could not by speech reconcile the island
Paros, elated by wealth, he led his forces out of the ships, shut the city
with works and deprived it of all supply, then, penthouses and tortoises
having been set up, he approached nearer the walls.    When he was already at
the point of taking the town, far off on the mainland a grove, which was seen
from the island, I know not by what chance was set on fire in the night time.
When the flame of which was seen by the townsmen and by the besiegers, it came
into the opinion of both that a signal had been given by the royal marines. By
which it was done that both the Parians were deterred from surrender and
Miltiades, fearing lest the royal fleet should arrive, the works which he had
set up having been burned, returned to Athens with as many ships as he had set
out, with great offense of his own citizens.    He was therefore accused of
treachery, because, when he could have taken Paros, corrupted by the king he
had departed with the matter unfinished. At that time he was sick with wounds
which he had received in besieging the town. And so, when he himself could not
speak for himself, his brother Stesagoras made the words. The cause having
been heard, he was acquitted of the capital charge, fined with money, and that
suit was estimated at fifty talents, as much as the expense that had been made
on the fleet. Because he could not pay this money at present, he was thrown
into public chains and there met his last day.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:
This man, although he was accused on the Parian charge, yet there was another
cause of the condemnation. For the Athenians, on account of the tyranny of
Pisistratus, which had been a few years before, greatly feared the excessive
power of their own citizens.    Miltiades, much versed in commands and
magistracies, did not seem able to be a private man, especially since by habit
he seemed to be drawn to desire of command.    For in the Chersonese all those
years which he had inhabited he had obtained perpetual domination and had been
called tyrant, but a just one. For he had not obtained it by force, but by the
will of his own, and he retained that power by goodness. But all are both
called and held tyrants who are of perpetual power in that state which has
used liberty.    But in Miltiades there was both the highest humanity and a
wonderful openness, so that no one was so humble to whom access to him did not
lie open; great authority among all states, a noble name, the highest praise
of military affairs. The people, looking back at these things, preferred that
that innocent man be punished rather than that they themselves be longer in
fear.
