HOMILY On the inscription of the fiftieth Psalm, and on the repentance of
David, and on that of Uriah.
=55.565=
1. Painters imitate nature by their art, and mixing the colors, they paint the
images of the bodies that are seen, and they make men, and beasts, and trees,
and wars, and battles, and torrents of blood, and spears, and breastplates,
and shields, and kings, and private men, and a royal throne, and a king
seated, and a barbarian lying subject, and a sharpened spear-point, and rivers
flowing, and varied plains, and simply, imitating all things that are seen
through their art, they furnish a spectacle to those who look. So also the
prophets are certain painters of virtue and vice. For they too paint with the
word the sinner, the righteous man, the one who repents, the one who stands,
the one who falls, the one who is raised, the one who is shaken. And just as
the painters make wars and slaughters, so these too make sin now attacking,
now being thrown down, and demons again, now warring, now being warred
against, and the devil now plotting, now also being defeated.

Since then such is their art, let us learn it with accuracy. For seeing the
tablets of the painters, we go away gaining nothing. For what benefit is it to
me to see the image of a king and a consul? For I neither can become king, nor
do I wish to. What benefit then is the spectacle to me, whose desire I do not
have, for which even if I desire I am punished? What benefit is it to me to
see a man being rich? If I am poor, I envy; if I am rich, I am jealous.
Suffering the same things again, I also lament. Leave those images, and look
at this spectacle. If I see a consul, what is that to me? If I see a sinner
who has fallen, and risen again, the spectacle becomes for me a subject of
teaching. If I see a righteous man standing, the spectacle leads me by the
hand toward philosophy. From the spectacle I learn the battle, and I am
trained for the victory. If I see a righteous man being tripped, again I
become wiser from the spectacle.

All these things have been said by me not simply nor at random, but because
the image of the psalm read today leads us to this subject of teaching. For
what does the prophet say? Have mercy on me, O God, according to your great
mercy; and according to the multitude of your compassions blot out my
transgression. This is the image. For I call the psalm an image. For I see
various persons in the psalm: sin attacking and being slain, repentance
standing by and fighting together with God, loving-kindness shining from
above, furnishing philosophy, and having many springs of tears from
confession.

These are the persons of the image. But before I begin the psalm, it is
necessary to narrate its inscription to you more accurately. For just as
images have inscriptions, =55.566= so also in the case of the psalms. For the
images standing above have below purple boards, who the king is, and of whom,
and what war he accomplished; but in the case of the psalm the inscription
lies above, what the subject is, and what the teaching, and what the root, and
for whose sake the psalm has been spoken. Give me your hearing with accuracy
for the word. For our narration is not about chance matters. A psalm of David,
when Nathan the prophet came in to him, when he had gone in to Bathsheba the
wife of Uriah. This is the inscription. Let us learn then who Nathan is, and
who Bathsheba is. Who then is the wife of Uriah? and when


did the prophet go in to her? and for whose sake did he write this psalm? For
not all the psalms have been spoken unto David. For all are from him, but not
all have been spoken unto him. For he did not narrate only his own sufferings,
but he also sang as tragedy the misfortunes of others. For example, By the
rivers of Babylon, there we sat down and wept; it is quite clear that the
psalm was not spoken about himself; for he neither went away to Babylon, nor
saw a barbarian land, nor sat down in a foreign land and wept; but he narrates
the sufferings of the Jews. Again when he says, They dug my hands and my feet,
they numbered all my bones; he is not setting forth his own suffering, but he
was proclaiming the cross, the sacrifice of the world, that which was for the
salvation of our race. For it is an image having varied teaching. Sometimes
then he narrates his own things, sometimes those of others. Since then this
psalm also narrates the things of others, for this reason it has been
inscribed, When Nathan the prophet came in to him. So that the inscription
does not allow us to grow forgetful, nor to carry the mind elsewhere; but it
stands commanding and teaching such things, and it also teaches other
thoughts, which the inscription says to us.

2. But pay attention with accuracy, both you who are righteous and you who are
sinners. For the psalm is useful to either part: to the righteous man, that he
may not be slothful; to the sinner, that he may not despair of his own
salvation. For both are hard to lay hold of. For the righteous man, having
become slothful, is thrown down, and the sinner, having despaired, does not
rise. In order then that neither this one remain in a fall, nor that one fall
into a fall, this psalmody of this singer is given as saving to either part.

I am about then to speak of a great and grievous sin of the prophet. But let
none of us condemn. For the loving-kindness of God, taking thought for the
common race of men, turned both the achievements of the saints and the sins
=55.567= into medicines, so that the wounds of some might become medicines for
others, and the shipwreck of the righteous man might be established as a
harbor for the sinner. For when the righteous man sins, I no longer despair of
my own salvation; when I see that he was brought down from the heavens, his
fall becomes for me a subject of resurrection.

For this reason also the grace of the Spirit remembered not only the
achievements of the righteous, but also the sins. For it did not remember
beforehand only those, but also those that come after these. I wish then to
show this noble man wounded, fallen, risen, wrestling, defeated, victorious,
crowned. For I do not show him lying in the fall only, but also raised, so
that you may gain from both sides, so that when you sin you may not despair,
but may run at once to the resurrection; nor say, Woe is me, I have perished,
I committed adultery, I fornicated, I murdered. For you have a harbor of
salvation beating back the sin. For when you see such a man receiving wounds
and being torn apart, run at once to repentance, and speak freely pouring out
springs of tears, so that through that man's repentance you may learn your own
salvation more accurately. What I mean is something like this: Many have often
fallen into a grievous and incurable disease, both men and women. Tears were
poured out, medicines were spent, the medical art was at a loss, beauty was
consumed, the body has given up. Then the woman goes about: Where am I to go?
what am I to do? I spent money, I called physicians, medicines were spent; the
disease remains, the sore was not healed.

It has often happened that one of those who visit said: I too fell into such a
suffering, and this man, or this woman, put a medicine on me, and they
released me from the pain. Then she begs: Show me the woman. Go to the alley.
So also now, since he committed adultery, and committed murder, and the sore
is grievous, I show you


how he was healed, and what sort of medicine he applied. And I do not say: Go
to the alley, ask this man, or this woman. There is no need of toil, no need
of labor, nor is it necessary for you to cross thresholds, but standing here
to receive the medicine. Only attend to what is said. For it is necessary to
introduce the account, and to speak the time at which he sinned, and the
place, and the person, and the repentance, and the correction, and the
medicine, so that having learned all things with accuracy, you may go away
thus.

This David then, being young and altogether a lad, and of an undistinguished
father, and attending to the land and the flocks, fled cities and countries
and tumults, and in the desert displayed philosophy, having nothing in common
with the affairs of life, but from first age living together with the quiet
that is the companion of virtue, and sitting in the desert as in a harbor, by
the oversight of the sheep he practiced the kingdom of the heavens. This man
then was young, altogether a lad, young in age, but gray-haired in mind; and
the incompleteness of age hindered nothing for so great a virtue; but being
very young he conquered not by power of body, but by power of faith. But
attend with accuracy to the word: There was need once for a king to come to be
for the Jews, since the first who came to be was unworthy. God sends Samuel at
once to his father.

Samuel then having come to Jesse the father of David, the child was among the
flocks, very small. I do not cease saying the youth continually, so that no
one may either slander a young man, or praise an old man. For neither is gray
hair virtue, nor is youth vice. Many even in youth displayed philosophy, and
many even in old age were tripped. For the three children were in the fire,
and they quenched the flame. And Daniel being twelve years old made the lions
sheep, not changing their nature, but bridling their savagery by faith; and
having refuted the =55.568= senseless elders, he is shown forth as a judge of
Israel. And Stephen the foremost of the deacons, full of grace, and filled
with power from youth, was discoursing with the Hebrew people; but Paul was
aged and gray-haired, and at first he became a plunderer of the Churches, but
after these things he seized the prize of faith, and became a sheep of
goodness.

3. Do not then slander the young man, nor praise the one who has grown old.
For neither is virtue and vice in the difference of age, but in the difference
of purpose; and if you are sober, the untimeliness of age harms you nothing;
and if you are slothful, old age will benefit you nothing. For gray hair is
not white hairs, but virtue of soul. This man then being young possessed the
ripe fruit of virtue. Samuel came to Jesse, not clothed with a flood of
bodies, but having the horn, where the holy oil was. And what does he say? God
sent me to make a king from your sons. Jesse hearing king, thinking that
dignity to be suitable to that one, brings the first son. Pass him by, he
says; God was not well pleased in him. The second; nor this one. The third,
the fourth, the fifth. The chorus of children was spent, and the one sought
was not found.

He says to him: Have you another? The father was ashamed. I have one, he says,
smaller and cheap, a shepherd. Do you seek to make a small and cheap man king?
Man sets at nought, but God crowns. For God will not see as man will see; man
looks at the face, but God at the heart. For are we seeking much flesh? We
long for nobility of soul. Attend with accuracy to the word. God did not say
to him, Go, anoint David for me; but, Go, anoint for me one of the sons of
Jesse. And why is he at a loss? and why does he not say the name? So that
David may not suffer what Joseph suffered. For just as those men, having
learned that the one sought was their king, plotted,


made war, sold him; so there was fear that these too might work this. So that
ignorance became a mother of safety. The prophet stood at a loss: Give me one
of your sons. I do not have one, he says, but a small child shepherding the
sheep. Man will not see thus, as God will see. Where are those who adorn
themselves upon beauty of face? Do not tell me beauty of body, but look at the
beauty of the mind. For what is a well-formed woman? A whitened tomb, if she
is not chaste. And beauty is a pit dug, and a poison prepared for others, if
she is not chaste; or it withers by disease and is cut off by death; but the
beauty of the soul remains immortal and unstained, but then rather it displays
its own fairness, when it has stopped the passions, and the mind has come to
be in a calm. If then you see a man, or a beautiful woman, do not marvel.

Since even the oaks stretched up to a height have no fruit useful to men, but
only to beasts; but the vine lying on the ground has a ripe fruit of clusters.
What then do you wish, to have a vine, or an oak? Clearly a vine; for the oak
standing in height has as fruit food of irrational beasts. What is cheaper
than a bee? and what more fair than a peacock? But what is better, a bee, or a
peacock? Clearly a bee. Take away a peacock, and you have harmed nothing; take
away a bee, and many things go lame. David then came, the oil is poured out,
and he is anointed by a vote of God, but by a ministry of man. He set the horn
upon him, and it became for him in place of purple and a diadem. And he went
away having the kingship, not clothed with a breastplate, not a shield, nor
spears, but having the very vote of God, which is more powerful than all. This
man then, with war also calling, and affairs being in confusion, and the
barbarians pressing upon the Jews, went away to see the array and the
barbarian summoning to single combat. And no one dared to leap forward and go
out. David came, he saw the enemy flashing and being unbearable, and all
cowering. The shepherd in the war, and those practiced in war could not even
bear the voice of the enemy; but he who conversed with trees and =55.569=
shades and springs stood before his face, and says: Who is this uncircumcised,
who has reproached the array of the living God? And they say to him: From
where is this madness of yours? But he: Not madness, but faith; I possess not
arrogance, but piety; I do not arm myself with a breastplate, but I have put
on faith; I do not look at that man's size, but the desolation of his mind; I
do not look that the ship is great, but that it has no helmsman. I have not
feared him (for he is an enemy of God), even if he be ten thousand times
powerful. For
through the inclination from above this uncircumcised man is weaker than all.
In what weapons do you take courage, O David? Not fleshly, but spiritual.

For our weapons are not fleshly, but spiritual. And how, that you are small
and weak? But the Master is great. The weapon is cheap; but the one who sent
it is strong. He made a sea dry, he split a rock, he changed the elements;
much more does he prevail over one man.

4. He came then to the men, and says to them: What shall be given to the man
who takes his head? He discourses about rewards, not in order to receive a
reward, but in order to lead these men to faith. What then the brothers? Again
envy. Again jealousy. For what have you left those small sheep? We know the
arrogance and the wickedness of your heart. O envy filled with passions, fire
not being quenched! For just as a worm eats through the wool, so also envy
melts the one who envies, and makes the one envied more splendid. So also it
happened in the case of Joseph and his brothers. For those who envied were set
down from freedom into slavery; but the one envied became king. Again then the
brothers:


We know the arrogance and the wickedness of your heart, that you came down for
the sake of seeing the war. Why did they envy? why were they jealous? They
heard a word, they did not bear the prosperity of words. But what did this
wise man? He stood away from them cutting off the sore, and went away to the
other part of the war, and says: What will there be for the man who takes his
head? and they led him in to the king. He saw then the king seated, cowering,
fearing, trembling, and he says to him: Why has the face of my lord fallen,
and in prosperity he confesses slavery? Who is this? shall I not go, and take
away his head? What then the king? And how can you, that you are a small
child, but this man a warrior from his youth? Do not look at the things seen,
but the things thought; do not see that he is small in appearance, but that he
is great in mind. What then is to happen? The king disbelieves; David is
forced then to tell him his own achievements. I, he says, was a small child
beside my father's flock; and when a lion came, or a bear, and they were
taking sheep from the flock, I went out after them, and I pulled out from
their mouth, and I killed the lion and the bear; and this one will be as one
of these beasts, because he has reproached the array of the living God. And
Saul said to David: Go, the Lord is with you. And he gave him weapons, and
David was not able to bear them. Why? God does not allow this, so that he may
conquer naked, so that the weapons may not share the victory of the
achievements, so that the king may not say that My weapons conquered. He
throws the weapons, and puts on faith; he goes out naked, and breastplated
with piety; he goes out a shepherd, and not a soldier; he lets fly a throw,
not taking courage in the nature of the body, but by the power of faith he
carried off the victory; he ran, he took his =55.570= sword, he cut off his
head.

And the thing written was fulfilled: In the works of his hands the sinner was
caught. Again envy, again jealousy. Continually I dig up the spring, so as to
find the very eye. This man then, such and so great a man, concerning whom God
says: I have found David the son of Jesse, a man according to my heart. O high
encomium! O demonstration of virtue! His Master says: I have found David the
son of Jesse, a man according to my heart. This man then who prevailed over
the beasts, who killed Goliath, who throttled the lion, who overpowered the
bear, who was gray-haired in youth, who was small in body and high in spirit,
who in the royal robe displayed the exactness of monks; for he washed, not
from pearls, but from tears wetting his bed each night. For men now even sleep
by day, but that man even by night was full of care. Why by night? Because by
day being king he was flooded round by ten thousand affairs; but by night
spending time as in a harbor, then he conversed alone with God alone, and
poured out springs of tears, and washed his own bed.

This man then, so great and of such stature, who drew to himself the grace of
the Spirit, who was gray-haired in the age of youth, who touched the vault of
the heavens, who enjoyed the gift and grace from above, who dissolved wars,
who displayed ten thousand achievements against the devil, after temperance,
after gentleness, after gravity, when he held the kingship, when he conquered
ten thousand enemies, when he subjected nations, when there was peace from
every side, he became more slothful, so that you may learn of how many good
things affliction is a procurer, and of how many evils ease. And when? In the
middle of midday. After breakfast having risen from the pallet he was walking
about, and he saw a woman naked, bathing, beautiful and very well-formed. But
beauty is not an accusation; for beauty is a gift of God. He saw a woman
bathing in an upper room, a soldier's wife, beautiful, fair. He saw, he was
winged by the eye, he received the dart.


5. Let the busybodies hear, those who busy themselves with others' things and
learn beauties thoroughly. Let those hear who are mad about the theaters, who
say: We look, but we are harmed in nothing. David was harmed, and you are not
harmed? That man was conquered, and am I to take courage for you? He who had
so great a grace of the Spirit received a dart; and you remain unwounded in
the spectacle of unseemly things? And yet that man did not see a harlot, but a
chaste and grave woman; and not in a theater, but in a house; but you in a
theater, where even the place punishes a soul; where you not only look, but
also hear shameful words, and see a harlot's dress and devilish songs; and
from every side your mind is struck; by the sight from what you saw; by the
hearing, from what you hear; by the nose, from what you smell. So many
corruptions, so many pits, and am I to take courage for you, that you do not
become a prey of beasts? For are you a stone? are you iron? You are a man,
subject to the common weakness of nature. Do you hold fire, and not burn? and
how does it have reason? Set a lamp into hay, and can you tell me that the hay
is not burned? What the hay is, this also is our nature. He saw her bathing
naked, he was caught by the beauty, he received the dart, he took the wound,
he sent for her; and as soon as she came to him, he worked the lawlessness, he
fulfilled the desire. He commands her to go away to her own house. And saying
these things I also bring the wounded man into the midst, so that you who
stand around may all see how he is healed. For a physician operating has all
those standing around, so that they may see how by the art he conquers the
disease; much more, when a prophet is being operated on, all must run
together, so that =55.571= we may see how we ought to be released from the
putrefaction and the worms.

The sin is grievous; desire was fulfilled, the sin was completed, the one who
heals was not there. The woman conceived. For a while the king thought to
escape the notice of her husband. For even if he was a prophet, still the
desire wholly darkened him. The woman conceived, she became pregnant; she
comes running to the king. King, I have perished. What is the matter with you?
I am pregnant. The fruit of the sin has sprouted; I carry the accusation in
the womb; the proof of the sin has grown; I have the demonstration from home.
If my husband comes and sees, what do I have to say? or what shall I speak?
Consider how great an evil sin is. The king fears the soldier. For there is no
slave, except the one who does the sin. He was clothed with a diadem, and he
feared the reproach. Are you not a king? do you not have authority of a sword?
Yes, but I also committed a grievous sin. O sin, mother of cowardice! O proof
of dishonor! O subject of silence! I fear, I tremble, because you have put me
to shame; I have the proof within. If my husband comes, what shall I say? what
shall I speak? He will find me pregnant, and he will kill me having within the
proof of the sin. Do you see the shipwreck that has been practiced? do you see
how great an evil sin is? What then the king? He adds sin upon sin. For the
word of the narration must be broadened. Uriah her husband came from the war,
and Uriah went in to the king. And the king says to him: What is it, Uriah?
how is the war? He says: Well; we have conquered. The king heard that the
soldier conquered, and he himself was defeated; the soldier subjected the war,
and the king was defeated by pleasure; the soldier prevailed over the war, but
he himself had been thrown down by the sin; the soldier was speaking the
victory, but he himself from shame did not even dare to speak the defeat. We
have conquered, he says, and we have prevailed. The king was at a loss, he did
not perceive the victory of the war because of the shame of the defeat by
which he himself was defeated. He says to the soldier: Go to your house;
bathe, and enjoy a table, and enjoy your wife. Why? So that he may overshadow
the sore, so that the child may be thought to be born from him. But the
unsleeping eye did not allow this to happen, sparing the wounded man. What
then does the soldier say? And how can I leave you, and live in luxury? Israel
is in the


war, the commander-in-chief is in the array, the ark of the Lord is in the
camp; and shall I go away to my house, and am I about to live in luxury? The
soldier was saying these things not knowing. But it became a reproach to the
king, that the soldier did not even wish to see his own house having come from
the war, but the king had so transgressed, as to bring necessity upon his own
soldier toward seeing his own wife, so that he might be able to cover his own
wound. He went away to the war, and the child was born. What then does he do?
Since he was not able to overshadow the sore, he writes to the general of the
war: Taking Uriah, set him in the strong part of the war. Why? So that he may
be killed. And he puts the letter into his hands. O the wantonness! to how
great a cruelty did the sin lead that most gentle man! The one wronged was
carrying the sword against himself, and he was bearing a letter full of blood,
and he was going away. For one must cut short. What then happens? In the war
he fell, he was slain. Behold two sins, adultery and murder. The murder became
the fruit of the adultery.

6. And I say these things because of the sinners and those who have many evils
on their conscience, so that hearing they may not despair of their own
salvation. For it is written: You shall not murder, you shall not commit
adultery. For he transgressed these two first commandments. He committed
adultery, and then he murdered the one who had been wronged. The sore is
grievous, the wound is great. Since then Uriah having gone away to the war was
slain, and at once the sin that had been overshadowed leaped =55.572=, then
afterward Nathan the prophet went away to him. The desire was completed, the
lawlessness was fulfilled, the one wronged had been killed. The prophet
refutes a prophet. Why? And yet David was a prophet; how then does he not heal
himself? Just as then physicians, when they are sick, need other physicians,
and the weakness damages the art; so also here that man is a prophet, this man
a prophet. But that man not lying on a bed, but spending time in sin. They say
to him: Nathan the prophet is outside. He says: Let him come in. The prophet
comes in. He sees him seated with much splendor and royal pride. And what does
he say? I have a case, O king. Hear me. O wisdom of a physician! He came in
having taken an iron, so that he may cut the sore; but he does not show it to
the sick man, so that he may not be deprived of the healing; he hides it
within, not under the garment, but under the drama of the narration. For
having come in at once he did not say to him: O lawless man, O profane man, O
adulterer, O man-slayer; he does not strip the refutation, nor the freedom of
speech without a mask; but he brings the iron hidden and says: I have a case,
O king, hear me.

What then the king? Speak the case. And he says: There was a certain man rich;
this one had many flocks and herds; and there was a certain man poor. Two men,
Uriah and David. Learn the masks in the truth. The poor man had nothing except
one ewe-lamb (he means the woman against whom adultery was committed), which
ate from his table, and drank from his cup, and slept in his bosom. Do you see
the poverty having a subject of affection? For in the case of those who are
rich there is much pride; but in the case of the poor the wife becomes a
servant and a minister, and she bears a child, and she herself becomes both
mother and nurse. But in the case of those who are rich it is not so, but she
bears a child, and gives it outside, and pride cuts through the affection. The
mother bears, and does not become a nurse. For she is ashamed to become a
nurse who has become a mother. But Christ is not so. For he begot us, and he
himself became our nourisher. For this reason also instead of foods he
nourished us with his own flesh, and instead of drink he gave us his own blood
to drink. The ewe-lamb then slept in his bosom. The tragedy was increased by
the narration of the affection. A stranger man then came to that rich man. Who
is the stranger?


Desire. In order then to show his gravity, by the method he called desire a
stranger. For he had never undermined another's marriage, nor had he looked
with unjust eyes; but having been wounded then toward that woman he lost the
gravity, and having been a little slothful he fell from what was fitting. The
stranger came, the unbridled desire, that savage one came to him. A stranger
came to that rich man, and he did not take from his flocks and herds; but he
sent and took that ewe-lamb, the one sleeping in the bosom of the poor man,
and eating from his table. What then? The king was angry.

For he thought he was judging a case belonging to another. The king was angry,
and he uttered the just thing with much splendor. For there was not affection
damaging the just. By the sword, he says, he shall die, and he shall pay the
ewe-lamb fourfold. He commands beyond the law. For the law commands the one
who steals to give back fourfold; but he himself has gone beyond the law; for
he commands both that he himself be killed, and to pay in fourfold. He brought
out the vote, and completed the condemnation. The prophet then throws the
mask, and says: You are the man, O king. O cutting, not cutting the sense of
the pain with a sword, but in the shortness of the refutation! For when it was
needed, he acted the drama; but when there was need of the refutation, at once
he gave and cut off, not working much pain: You are the man, O king. What then
he himself? I have sinned against the Lord. Do you see even in the disease the
nobility of the soul? He did not say to him: O wretched and miserable man, nor
did he wish to refute openly. What then does the king say? I have sinned
against the Lord. And what the prophet =55.573=? And the Lord has taken away
your sin. The repentance is sharp; but the forgiveness is sharper. Speak you
your lawless deeds, he says, first, so that you may be justified. You sinned
through deeds, and through words repent.

7. I say these things, so that the sinners may hear and those who have fallen
into sins, and may not despair of their own salvation. I have sinned, he says,
against the Lord. And the Lord has taken away your sin. You said: A sword
shall fall upon him; but I, You shall not die. Did you see how God dissolved
the case of the prophet? Speak you your lawless deeds first, so that you may
be justified. But let us run back to the beginning. A psalm of David, when
Nathan the prophet came in to him.

Here his good-mindedness and forgiveness are shown. See then, you have learned
how he was healed, and what sort of medicine he received. And when God
justifies, who condemns? For he did not say that I forgive, but that The Lord
has taken away your sin. He cut the sore, he corrected the wound. You shall
not die, he says. Having said these things he went away. But I wish then to
show also this man's good-mindedness, and the correction of the mind, what
sort of forgiveness of the sin he received, what sort of remission of the
trespass he found. For even when Uriah came from the alliance, and announced
the victory of the war to the king, the king wishing to escape the notice of
the man who had been wronged, that he had committed adultery against his wife,
set so great an eagerness to hide the sin, that he also wove murder together
with the adultery; and after these things he became a philosopher from the
repentance, he who formerly was hiding his own sin came to repentance; and
after the forgiveness that came to be from the nod of God, after the prophet
came, and said that The Lord has taken away your sin, you shall not die, not
only does he not hide the suffering, but even after these things he made it
quite clear to all the generations, and he wrote a psalm, so that those after
these things also may learn how great a tragedy the suffering has. For when
the repentance came, it brought the freedom of speech. I was saved, he says;
why are others also not saved through my narration? God forgave me. For this
reason I write these things, so that they may learn both the sore and the
medicine. I do not fear the shame, since I desire my own salvation. So also
Paul does; he posts up the sins that were forgiven him, and


says: I have thanks to Christ who strengthened me, that he counted me
faithful; me who formerly was a blasphemer and a persecutor and an insolent
man, now he counted faithful. For this reason I post myself up, so that if
someone is an unbearable blasphemer, he may see that he has a hope of
salvation.

And you, O David, since you committed adultery and committed murder, and
received forgiveness from God, for this reason do you post yourself up? Yes,
he says, so that if someone is an adulterer and a man-slayer, he may learn how
he can obtain salvation. For this reason he puts away the sin, and received
the repentance. This then alone is what is sought, that he made it quite clear
to all, and was corrected. Or also something else? Yes, also something else;
and how, I say. For just as you heard that repentance has much forgiveness,
and brings us great and much freedom of speech; so it is necessary for you
also to learn this too, that even the one clothed with many sins possesses
much shame, and is not only content with the repentance, but also fears and
trembles at falling no longer into the same things. I wish then to show that
the psalm makes known to you not only the one who sins, but also the one who
repents. And I will say this to you, so that no one may take courage for
himself after the repentance, but rather may establish his own salvation more
accurately in the harbor.

I wish then to show him, that he was healed, and became better than himself,
and more powerful and stronger. How then again after this desire did he fall
into another disease? Again war, again sin, again battle, again a pancration,
again the desire came upon him, again a war has been joined, a grievous war,
and camps =55.574= stand.

And there was a spring in those places. And there being a violent war, David
saw it, and was brought down into a most grievous thirst; he desired then to
drink. Again desire, but not forbidden; then of a body, now of water. He
desired to drink, he was burning with the thirst, and he says: I thirst, I
perish. Some then of those wishing to heal him went away, and brought him
water at the cost of their blood, and through the enemies they carried the
water. And when the water was brought, the king considered what he had done,
that in that war he did not restrain his thirst. And what did he do? Taking
the water he did not drink, but he poured it as a libation to God. For it did
not seem to be water, but blood, not attending to the nature, but to the
purpose. Do you see then how having been trained by the former desire,
afterward he became more chaste? He who desired bodies does not desire water;
or rather he desired, but he mastered it. For I do not say that he was outside
the desire, but by philosophy he prevailed over the desire, and did not touch.

8. I counsel these things because of those who fornicate. Did you fall in love
with a harlot? did you fornicate? do you wish to repent? Do not add sin to
sin, and do not say to me: How can I not fornicate? I tell you how you can.
Does the desire burn you? But you have a wife; and if you do not have one,
take one, so that you may have pleasure with safety. But I cannot master it.
But these things are of sloth. For when I bring you another who does not have
a wife, but is chaste, what defense do you have, or what pardon? That man in
the open sea does not endure shipwreck, and you in the harbor endure the fall?
You have a wife, and you fornicate, or rather you commit adultery? And how am
I to prevail over the sore? he says. Enjoy your wife. Am I to enjoy my wife?
Entirely. Consider what the blessed Paul says: But because of the fornications
let each man have his own wife, and each woman her own husband. The wife does
not have authority over her own body, but the husband; and likewise also the
husband does not have authority over his own body, but the wife. If then a
harlot calls you, consider the saying with yourself, and say, that The husband
does not have authority over his own body, but the wife; and write the saying
in your heart, and say to the harlot: Why do you call me? the body is not
mine, but my


wife's; I do not dare then to betray what belongs to another. I do not dare to
diminish her dowry, nor do I dare to spend her affairs, and do I have daring
to defile the body? How? I cannot. Say to the harlot: Why do you call me? you
shall not come near it; for this is not mine, it is another's. For is the body
the harlot's? I do not receive a harlot's body; this is my wife's. Fear
Christ, and say, that our body is members of Christ. Let the women also hear
this, and let them consider where Christ has called them, that it is wholly in
sanctification, and let them say, that The wife does not have authority over
her own body, but the husband. Consider the wisdom of Paul, what he says.

Since many practice continence, and have grave and chaste wives, and they
practice continence beyond what seems good, and the philosophy of these
becomes a subject of adultery, for this reason he says: Let each enjoy his own
wife. And he is not ashamed, but he comes in, and sits down at the bed night
and day, and he holds the husband and the wife, and so joins them, and he
cries and shouts: Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement. Do
you practice continence, and do you not wish to sleep with your husband, but
that man does not enjoy you? He goes away and sins, and then that man's sin is
reckoned to your continence. It is better for him to sleep with you, than with
the harlot. Your enjoyment is not forbidden; but hers is forbidden.

If he sleeps with you, there is no accusation; if he sleeps with that woman,
you have destroyed your members. He sits then almost at the bed of the husband
and the wife, and cries: Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by
agreement. For this reason you took =55.575= a husband, for this reason you
also took a wife, so that you may be chaste. Do you wish to practice
continence? Persuade your husband also, so that there may be two crowns,
chastity and concord; so that there may not be chastity and battle, so that
there may not be peace and war. For when you practice continence, but that man
is burned by desire, and Paul says, Do not fornicate, at once he is troubled,
he is waved. Do not deprive one another, except perhaps by agreement. For
where there is peace, there all the good things; where there is peace,
chastity also shines; where there is concord, continence also is crowned; but
where there is war, chastity also is undercut. Do not then deprive one
another, except perhaps by agreement. For Paul conquers every bridesman, the
bridesman of the world. For this reason he is not ashamed saying: Marriage is
honorable in all, and the bed undefiled. For his Master also came to a
marriage, honoring the marriage by his own presence, and he brought gifts.

For he changed the water into wine. Run then as much as you wish; but when you
grow faint, take as a medicine intercourse, so that Satan may not tempt you.
Let each then have his own wife. For these are three: virginity, marriage,
fornication. Marriage is in the middle, fornication below, virginity above.
Virginity has a crown, marriage a measured praise, fornication punishment and
vengeance. See then how you practice continence, if you can bear the weakness
of the body, and do not wish to go up beyond the measure, so that you may not
go down beyond the measure.

Just as then the one who is about to swim goes in so far as he is able to come
out; and when he goes in, he considers how far he ought to come out; so also
you be chaste so far as you can endure, so that you may not go down contrary
to the measure. And for all these things let us give thanks to Christ, because
to him is the glory and the power, with the Father and the Holy Spirit, now
and always, and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
