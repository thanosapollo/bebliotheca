ORATION XX.
On dogma and the appointment of bishops.
A. When I see the present talkativeness, and the same-day wise men, and the
ready-made theologians, for whom it is enough merely to wish in order to be
wise, I long for the highest philosophy, and I seek a last lodging-place,
according to Jeremiah, and I want to be with myself alone. For nothing seems
to me such as this: having closed the senses, having become outside flesh and
world, touching none of human things except as far as all necessity, speaking
to oneself and to God, to live above the things seen, and always to bear in
oneself the divine impressions pure, unmixed with the characters below and
wandering, as an unstained mirror of God and of the divine, both being and
always becoming, receiving light with light, and a clearer with a dimmer,
until we come to the source of the radiances here, and attain the blessed end,
the mirrors being dissolved by the truth; so that scarcely anyone, either
having trained himself by long philosophy and tearing away little by little
the noble and light-like of the soul from the low and yoked with darkness, or
having found God gracious, or both these, and making practice as much as
possible to look above, would prevail over the matter that drags down. But
before overcoming this, as much as power allows, and sufficiently cleansing
both the ears and the mind, =35.1068= either to receive oversight of a soul,
or to attempt theology, I know it is not safe.
B. And whence I was led into this fear, that you may not suppose me more timid
than is due, but even praise me for the forethought, I hear Moses himself,
when God was giving him oracles, that when more had been summoned to the
mountain, of whom one was also Aaron with his two sons the priests, all the
rest were commanded to worship from afar, but Moses alone to draw near, and
the people not to go up with him. And a little before these things,
lightnings, and thunders, and trumpets, and the whole mountain smoking, and
dreadful threats, and such terrors held the others below; and it was a great
thing for them to hear the voice of God only, and this after they had been
well purified. But Moses both goes up, and enters within the cloud, and is
with God, and receives a law, for the many that of the letter, and for those
above the many that of the spirit.
C. I know also Eli the priest, and a little later a certain Uzzah; the one
even required to pay the penalty for the lawlessness of his sons, which they
dared against the sacrifices, and this though he did not accept their impiety,
but often rebuked them much; the other, because he dared only to touch the
ark, when it was being dragged aside by the ox, saved it, but himself
perished, God clearly guarding the reverence for the ark. And I know that it
is not safe even for the many to touch the walls of the temple, and for this
they needed other walls, the outer ones; nor that the sacrifices themselves be
consumed by those by whom, and when, and where it was not fitting; so far is
it from being for everyone to approach the Holy of Holies boldly, or the
veil, or the mercy-seat, or the ark, or to look upon them, or to touch them.
D. Knowing these things then, I, and that no one is worthy of the great God,
and victim, and high priest, who =35.1069= has not first presented himself to
God a living sacrifice, or rather, has become a holy temple of the living God
and living: how should I myself readily undertake the words about God, or
accept the one who undertakes them rashly? The desire is not praiseworthy; the
attempt is fearful. And for this one must first purify himself, then converse
with the Pure; unless we are going to suffer the thing of Manoah, and say when
we have come into a vision of God: We have perished, O woman, we have seen
God; or, as Peter, to send Jesus away from the boat, as not worthy of such a
visitation; or, as that centurion, to demand the healing, but not to receive
the healer in. Let one of us also say, while he is a centurion, and ruling
over many in wickedness, and still serving Caesar the world-ruler of those
dragged below: I am not sufficient that you should enter under my roof. But
when I behold Jesus, though I am small in spiritual stature, as that
Zacchaeus, and am lifted above the sycamore, having put to death the members
upon the earth, and having wasted the body of humiliation: then I will also
receive Jesus in, and I will hear: Today salvation to this house; and I will
attain salvation, and I will philosophize the more perfect things, scattering
well what I gathered badly, whether money or doctrines.
E. And since we have cleansed the theologian by the word, come then let us
also speak about God as in brief, having taken heart in the Father himself,
and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, about whom the discourse is. And I pray to
suffer the thing of Solomon, to think nothing of my own about God, nor to
utter it. For when he says, For I am most foolish of all men, and there is no
prudence of man in me, he does not say this, I suppose, condemning himself for
lack of understanding. For how, he who asked this also from God before every
other thing, and received wisdom, and contemplation, and breadth of heart,
both richer and more abundant than sand? And he who is so wise, and has
obtained such a gift, how does he name himself most foolish of all? As not
having his own prudence, that is, but being worked by the divine and more
perfect. For Paul also, saying, And I live, yet no longer I, but Christ lives
in me, was not speaking of himself as altogether dead, but as living a life
better than that of the many, by having partaken of the life that truly is,
and is bounded by no death. =35.1072= We worship then Father, and Son, and
Holy Spirit, separating the properties, but uniting the Godhead; and we
neither blend the three into one, lest we suffer Sabellius' disease; nor do we
divide them into three alien and foreign, lest we rave the things of Arius.
For why must we, like a plant leaning all to one side and bent, forcibly shift
it to the other part, correcting distortion by distortion, and not,
straightening toward the middle, stand in the bounds of godliness?
F. And when I say a mean, I say the truth, toward which alone we do well to
look; both declining the poor contraction, and the more absurd division; so
that the discourse, neither contracted into one hypostasis, from fear of
polytheism, may leave us bare names, for those who suppose the same one to be
Father, and Son, and Holy Spirit, and who define that all are not more one
than that each is nothing (for they would flee from being what they are,
passing over and changing into one another); nor divided into three foreign
and unlike essences and torn apart, according to Arius' well-named madness, or
without beginning and unordered, and so to speak, rival gods; on the one side
being shut up into Jewish smallness, circumscribing the Godhead by the
unbegotten alone; on the other falling into the opposite, but an equal evil,
positing three beginnings, and three Gods, which is more absurd than the
things said before; it being necessary that some be neither so father-loving
as even to take away being Father (for of whom would he even be Father, if the
Son's nature has been made foreign and alienated along with the creation?);
nor so Christ-loving as not even to keep this, being Son (for of whom would he
even be Son, if not referred to the Father as cause?); nor to belittle for the
Father the dignity of the beginning, that as Father and begetter; for he would
be beginning of small and unworthy things, if he is not cause of the Godhead
contemplated in Son and Spirit. Since one must both keep the one God, and
confess the three hypostases, or then three persons, and each with its
property. =35.1073=
G. And it would be kept, as my discourse has it, one God, both Son and Spirit
being referred to one cause, not being compounded, nor being blended together;
and according to the one and the same of the Godhead, that I may so name it,
both movement and will, and the identity of essence. And the three hypostases,
no blending being conceived, or dissolution, or confusion, lest the whole be
destroyed, through those things by which the one is honored more than is well.
And the properties: of the Father, both without beginning, and conceived and
called beginning (beginning, as cause, and as source, and as everlasting
light); and of the Son, without beginning in no way, but beginning of all
things; and when I say beginning, do not insert time, nor set anything in the
middle of the begetter and the offspring, nor divide the nature by what is
badly inserted among the co-eternal and joined. For if time is older than the
Son, of that, clearly, the Father would first be cause. And how is he maker of
times who is under time? And how also Lord of all, if he has been anticipated
by time and is mastered? The Father then is without beginning; for being is
not from elsewhere for him, nor from himself. And the Son, if you take the
Father as cause, is not without beginning; for the Father is beginning of
the Son as cause; but if you mean the beginning from time, he is also without
beginning; for the Master of times does not begin under time.
H. And if, because bodies are under time, you will claim for this that the Son
also lies under time, you will also put a body around the bodiless; and if
because the things begotten among us, not being at some time, then coming to
be, for this you will also force the Son to have passed from things that are
not into being, you compare the incomparable, God and man, body and the
bodiless; then he will also suffer and be dissolved, because our bodies also
do. You then claim that God is begotten thus for this reason, because bodies
are; but I say not thus for this reason, =35.1076= that bodies are thus. For
of those whose being is not alike, neither is the begetting alike; unless the
other things also will be enslaved to the materials, such as suffering, and
grieving, and hungering, and thirsting, and as many passions as are of body,
or of both together. But your mind does not accept these; for the discourse is
about God. Do not then accept the generation otherwise than as divine.
I. But if he has been begotten, how has he been begotten, he says? Answer me,
you dialectician and inescapable one. If he has been created, how has he been
created? And demand of me also the, How has he been begotten? Passion about
the generation? Passion also about the creation; for is not the forming, and
the care, and the sudden unfolding of what was thought into the particular, a
passion? Time about the generation? The things created are also in time. Place
here? Place there. Failure about the generation? Failure also about the
creation. These things I heard from you philosophizing; for what the mind
sketched, often the hand did not complete. But by word, he says, he brought
the all into being, and by will. For he spoke, and they came to be; he
commanded, and they were created. When you say that all things have been
created by the word of God, you do not introduce the human creation; for none
of us does the things that come to be by word. For nothing would be higher
than we, nor more without toil, if speaking were completion of a work; so that
even if God creates the things created by word, creating is not human for him.
For either show also a man doing something by word; or accept that God does
not create as a man. Since sketch a city by will, and let a city stand forth;
will a son to come to be for you, and let a child stand forth; will some other
of the things done, and let the will pass into work. But if none of these
follows upon willing, and for God the willing is action, a man creates in one
way, and God the creator of all in another. How then does he create not
humanly, but is forced to beget humanly? You, not being, then came to be, then
you beget; for this you bring things that are not into being. Or, that I may
say something deeper, perhaps you yourself do not even bring them from things
that are not; since Levi also, he says, was still in the loin of his father,
before he passed into being. And let no one abuse the discourse. For I do not
say that the Son exists from the Father thus, as being first in the Father,
and after this traveling into being; for neither imperfect first, then
perfect, as is the law of our generation. =35.1077=
J. These things belong to those who abuse; these to those who leap readily
upon all that is said. But we do not think thus, we do not believe thus; but
together with the Father being unbegottenly (and he always was, for the mind
does not overshoot into not being at some time), the Son also was begottenly.
So that the being of the Father runs together with the having-been-begotten of
the Only-begotten, existing from him, and not after him, except in the thought
alone of the beginning; and beginning, as cause. For I often turn the same
discourse, fearing the thick and material of your mind. And if you do not busy
yourself over the Son's, whether one must say generation, or hypostasis, or
something else more proper than these that someone conceives (for what is
thought and said conquers my tongue); do not be over-curious about the
Spirit's procession either. I am content to hear that he is Son, and that he
is from the Father; and that the one is Father, the other Son; and I busy
myself about nothing beyond this, lest I suffer the same as the voices, which
by being overpowered altogether fail, or as the sight straining toward a
sunbeam. For by how much more and more exactly someone would wish to see, by
so much is the sense harmed, and he is deprived of seeing even somehow,
through the greater of what is seen conquering the sight, if he should wish to
see the whole, and not as much as it is safe to see.
K. You hear generation? Do not be over-curious about the how. You hear that
the Spirit proceeds from the Father? Do not busy yourself about the manner.
And if you busy yourself over the Son's generation, and the Spirit's
procession, I too busy myself over your mixture of soul and body: How are you
dust, and image of God? What is it that moves you, or what is moved? How does
the same both move and get moved? How does sense remain in the same, and draw
in the outside? How does the mind remain in you, and beget a word in another
mind? How is a thought distributed by word? And I do not yet say the greater
things: What is the circuit of heaven? What is the motion of stars, or order,
or measures, or conjunction, or distance? And what are the bounds of the sea?
And from where the streams of winds, or the turns of seasons, or the pourings
of rains? If you have understood none of these, O man (and you will perhaps
=35.1080= understand sometime, when you receive the perfect. For I will see,
he says, the heavens, works of your fingers; so that the things now seen are
to be supposed not to be the truth, but images of the truth); if you have not
known yourself, who you are who discourse about these things, if you have not
grasped these things, of which sense also is witness, how do you suppose you
know God exactly, both what and how great he is? This is of much unreason.
L. But if you are persuaded at all by me, the theologian who is not rash, you
have grasped the one; ask to grasp the other. Love what remains in you,
and let what is in the treasuries above remain there. Through a way of life,
go up; through purification, acquire the pure. Do you wish to become a
theologian sometime, and worthy of the Godhead? Keep the commandments; journey
through the precepts; for practice is a stepping-up to contemplation; from the
body labor for the soul. Is there any of men who can be lifted so much as to
come to the measure of Paul? Yet still he says he sees through a mirror and
a riddle, and that there is a time when he will see face to face. Are you more
philosophical in words than another? Yet you are altogether lower than God.
Are you perhaps more understanding than another? Yet you fall short of truth
by as much as your being is second to the being of God. We have a promise to
know sometime, as much as we have been known. If it is not possible here to
have the perfect knowledge of the things that are, what is the remaining thing
for me? What is the hoped-for? The kingdom of the heavens, you will altogether
say. And I think this is nothing else than to attain the most pure and most
perfect; and most perfect of the things that are, knowledge of God. But let us
hold the one, and grasp the other, while we are upon earth; and let us store
up the rest from there, that we may have this fruit of the labor, the whole
illumination of the Holy Trinity, what it is, and of what kind, and how great,
if it is lawful to say this, in Christ himself our Lord, to whom be the glory
and the might unto the ages of ages. Amen.
