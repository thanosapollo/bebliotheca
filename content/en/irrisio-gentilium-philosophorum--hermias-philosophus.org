OF HERMIAS THE PHILOSOPHER
A mockery of the outside philosophers.
The blessed apostle Paul, writing to the Corinthians who sojourn in Laconian
Greece, declared, saying: "O beloved, the wisdom of this world is foolishness
with God," not speaking without purpose. For it seems to me to have taken its
beginning from the apostasy of the angels. For which reason the philosophers,
saying things neither consistent nor in agreement with one another, set forth
their doctrines. For some of them say the soul is fire, others air, the
Stoics, others mind, others motion, Heraclitus, others the exhalation, others
a power flowing from the stars, others a kinetic number, Pythagoras, others
generative water, Hippon, others an element from elements, others harmony,
Dinarchus, others the blood, Critias, others the breath, others the monad,
Pythagoras, and the ancients the opposites. How many discourses about these
things, how many attempts, how many lawsuits of sophists wrangling rather than
finding the truth? But be it so: they quarrel about the soul *** and for the
rest they have declared in agreement about it. And as for its pleasure, one
calls it a good, another an evil, and another again a mean between good and
evil, and as for its nature, some say it is immortal, others mortal, others
that it persists for a little while, others turn it into a beast, others
dissolve it into atoms, others embody it three times, others assign it periods
of three thousand years. For even those who do not live a hundred years make
proclamations about three thousand years to come. What then ought one to call
these things? As it seems to me, marvel-mongering, or folly, or madness, or
faction, or all of them together. If they have found the truth, let them agree
or give their assent, and then I too will gladly be persuaded by them; but if
they pull the soul against itself and drag it, one into one nature, another
into another substance, and change matter out of matter, I confess I am
overburdened by the ebb and flow of the things. Now I am immortal and I
rejoice, now again I become mortal and I weep; just now I am dissolved into
atoms, I become water, and I become air, I become fire; then after a little I
am neither air nor fire, he makes me a beast, he makes me a fish, so again I
have dolphins for brothers. And when I look at myself, I fear the body and do
not know how to name it, a man or a dog or a wolf or a bull or a bird or a
snake or a dragon or a chimera; for I am changed by those who philosophize
into all the beasts, land, water, winged, many-formed, wild, tame, voiceless,
well-voiced, irrational, rational; I swim, I fly, I soar, I creep, I run, I
perch. And Empedocles besides even makes me a bush.
Where then it is not possible for those who philosophize to find the soul of
man with one mind, they would hardly be able to declare the truth about the
gods or about the world. For they have this courage, not to say this rashness.
For those who cannot find their own soul do not seek that of the gods
themselves, and those who do not know their own body busy themselves with the
nature of the world. At any rate they stand quite against one another
concerning the principles of nature. When Anaxagoras takes me in hand, he
teaches me these things: Mind is the principle of all things, and this is
cause and lord of the whole, and it furnishes order to the unordered, motion
to the unmoved, separation to the mixed, and cosmos to the uncosmic. When he
says these things Anaxagoras is my friend and I am persuaded by the doctrine.
But Melissus and Parmenides stand against him. Parmenides, for his part, even
in poetic verses proclaims that being is one and everlasting and infinite and
unmoved and altogether alike. So again I am changed, I know not how, into this
doctrine; Parmenides has driven Anaxagoras out of my judgment. And when I
think I have an unmoved doctrine, Anaximenes taking it up cries out against
it: But I tell you, the all is air, and this, being condensed and compacted,
becomes water and earth, and being rarefied and dispersed becomes aether and
fire, and returning into its own nature it is air, rare; and if it is also
condensed, he says, it is altered. And again I am retuned to this and I love
Anaximenes.
But Empedocles stands right opposite, snorting, and shouting loudly from Etna:
The principles of all things are enmity and friendship, the one gathering, the
other separating; and their strife makes all things. And I define them as both
like and unlike, both infinite and having a limit, both everlasting and coming
to be. Well done, Empedocles, I follow you even as far as the craters of the
fire. But on the other side Protagoras stands and drags me the other way,
saying: Man is the measure and judgment of things, and what falls under the
senses is things, and what does not fall under them is not among the forms of
being. Flattered by this account by Protagoras I am delighted, because he
assigns the all, or the greater part, to man. And from another quarter Thales
nods the truth to me, defining water as the principle of the all. And from the
moist all things are composed and into the moist they are dissolved, and the
earth is borne upon water. Why then should I not be persuaded by Thales, the
eldest of the Ionians? But his fellow-citizen Anaximander says that an older
principle than the moist is everlasting motion, and by this some things are
generated and others perish. Well then, let Anaximander be trusted. And yet
does Archelaus not find favor, declaring the principles of the whole to be hot
and cold? But to this again the loud-voiced Plato does not agree, saying that
the principles are God and matter and the paradigm. Now indeed I am persuaded.
For how should I not believe a philosopher who has made the chariot of Zeus?
And behind him his pupil Aristotle stands, jealous of the teacher for the
chariot-making. This man defines other principles: acting and being acted
upon. And the acting, impassible, is the aether, and the being-acted-upon has
four qualities, dryness, wetness, heat, cold; for by the change of these into
one another all things come to be and perish. We are already worn out, being
changed up and down by the doctrines. Still, I will stand upon Aristotle's
judgment, and let not even one account trouble me any longer. But what then
would I suffer? For older men than these pull the strings of my soul.
Pherecydes saying the principles are Zena and Chthonie and Cronus: Zena the
aether, Chthonie the earth, Cronus time; the aether the acting, the earth the
being-acted-upon, and time that in which the things that come to be. So there
is jealousy of the old men toward one another. For Leucippus, thinking all
these things nonsense, says the principles are the infinite and ever-moving
and smallest things; and the fine-particled, having gone up, became fire and
air, and the thick-particled, having settled below, water and earth. How far
am I taught so many things, learning nothing true? Unless perhaps Democritus
will release me from the error, declaring the principles to be what is and
what is not, and what is is the full, and what is not is the void. And the
full in the void, by turning or by rhythm, makes all things. Perhaps I would
be persuaded by the noble Democritus and would wish to laugh with him, if
Heraclitus did not persuade me otherwise, weeping at once and saying: The
principle of the whole is fire, and it has two affections, rarity and density,
the one acting, the other being acted upon, the one combining, the other
separating. It is enough for me, and I am already drunk with so many
principles.
But Epicurus calls me from that quarter too, not to outrage his noble doctrine
of the atoms and the void. For by their many-turned and many-shaped
intertwining all things come to be and perish. I do not contradict you, best
of men, Epicurus; but Cleanthes, lifting his head from the well, laughs your
doctrine to scorn and himself draws up the true principles, God and matter.
And that the earth changes into water, the water into air, the air is carried
upward, the fire proceeds to the regions about the earth, and the soul extends
through the whole cosmos, of which we, partaking of a part, are ensouled.
While there are so many of these, another crowd streams upon me from Libya,
Carneades and Clitomachus and as many as are their companions, trampling down
all the doctrines of the others, and themselves declaring expressly that all
things are incomprehensible and that a false appearance always lies beside the
truth. What then am I to suffer, having toiled so long? And how am I to pour
out of my judgment so many doctrines? For if nothing were comprehensible,
truth has gone from among men, and the celebrated philosophy fights with
shadows rather than having knowledge of the things that are.
Others then from the old tribe, Pythagoras and his fellow-tribesmen, solemn
and silent, hand down other doctrines to me as if mysteries, and this great
and secret thing, the "he himself said": The principle of all things is the
monad. And from its figures and from the numbers the elements come to be. And
of each of these he declares the number and the figure and the measure somehow
thus: Fire is filled out by twenty-four right-angled triangles, being
contained by four equilateral. And each equilateral is composed of six
right-angled triangles, whence they also liken it to a pyramid. And air is
filled out by forty-eight triangles, being contained by eight equilateral. And
it is likened to an octahedron, which is contained by eight equilateral
triangles, each of which is divided into six right-angled, so that all become
forty-eight. And water is filled out by one hundred twenty triangles, being
contained by twenty equal and equilateral, and it is likened to an
icosahedron, which is composed of equal and equilateral triangles, twenty and
a hundred. And the aether is filled out by twelve equilateral pentagons and is
like a dodecahedron. And earth is filled out from forty-eight triangles and is
also contained by six equilateral squares. And it is like a cube. For the cube
is contained by six squares, each of which is divided into eight triangles, so
that all become forty-eight. So Pythagoras measures the cosmos. And I again,
having become inspired, despise house and fatherland and wife and children,
and these things no longer concern me. And I myself go up into the aether
itself, and taking the cubit from Pythagoras I begin to measure the fire. For
Zeus measuring is not enough, but unless I myself go up into heaven and
measure the aether, the great living thing, the great body, the great soul,
the rule of Zeus is gone. And when I have measured and Zeus has learned from
me how many angles fire has, I come down again from heaven, and having eaten
olives and figs and greens I set out with all speed upon the water, and by
cubit and finger and half-finger I measure the wet substance and I measure out
its depth, so that I may teach Poseidon too how much sea he rules. And I go
around the whole earth in one day, collecting its number and the measure and
the figures. For I am persuaded that I will not pass over even a span of the
whole cosmos, being such and so great. I know, and of the whole smos I will
not pass over even a span, being such and so great. And I know also the number
of the stars and of the fishes and of the beasts, and setting the cosmos on a
balance I can easily learn its weight. About these things, then, until now my
soul has been eager to rule over the whole. But Epicurus, leaning out, says to
me: You have measured one cosmos, my friend, but there are many worlds, and
infinite. So again I am forced to measure other heavens, other aethers, and
these many. Come then, delaying no longer, having provisioned myself for a few
days I will go abroad to the Epicurean worlds. The limits, Tethys and Ocean, I
fly over easily. And having entered a new cosmos, as into another city, I
measure all things in a few days. And from there I pass over again into a
third cosmos, then into a fourth and fifth and tenth and hundredth and
thousandth, and how far? For already a darkness of ignorance meets me, and
black deceit, and infinite wandering, and unfinished appearance, and
incomprehensible folly. Still, why do I delay even to count the atoms
themselves, from which so many worlds have come to be, so that I may leave
nothing unexamined, especially of things so necessary and useful, from which
house and city are happy?
These things, then, I have gone through, wishing to show the opposition that
is in their doctrines, and that the inquiry of things proceeds for them into
the infinite and undefined, and that their end is unguessable and useless,
confirmed by no evident deed and no clear account.
