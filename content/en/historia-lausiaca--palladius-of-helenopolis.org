THE LAUSIAC OF PALLADIUS Many have left behind to the world many and varied
writings at different times, some from the inspiration of the God-given grace
from above, for the building up and security of those who with faithful
purpose follow the doctrines of the Saviour, others from a man-pleasing and
corrupted purpose having run wild in matter, for the consolation of those who
ivy themselves with vainglory, and others from a certain madness and operation
of the beauty-hating demon, with pride and wrath, for the ruin of light-minded
men and as a stain of the undefiled and catholic church, having crept into the
minds of the foolish in spite against the reverent way of life; Pref.2 it
seemed good also to me the humble one, having respected the command of your
greatness of mind, most eager for learning, the command that is for the
progress of the mind, being in the thirty-third year in the way of life of the
brothers, as it were, and in the solitary life, and in the twentieth of the
episcopate, and in the fifty-sixth in the whole of life, to you who desire the
narratives of the fathers, both males and females, both those I have seen and
those I have heard about, and those with whom I lived in the desert according
to Egypt and Libya and the Thebaid and Syene, under which are also the
so-called Tabennesiotes, then in Mesopotamia and Palestine and Syria, and in
the parts of the West, Rome and Campania and those around it, to set forth
this book to you from the beginning in the form of a narrative; Pref.3 so
that, having a reverent and soul-profiting reminder as an unceasing medicine
of forgetfulness, putting away through this every drowsiness that is from
irrational desire, and all double-mindedness and niggardliness in the
necessities, and all sloth and smallness of soul of those in the character,
and irascibility and disturbance and grief and irrational fear, and the
distraction of the world, you may progress with unceasing desire in the
purpose of piety, becoming a guide both of yourself and of those with you and
of those under you and of the most pious emperors; through which
accomplishments all who love Christ are eager to be united to God; also
expecting daily the departure of the soul, according to what is written;
Pref.4 "It is good to depart and to be with Christ"; and "Prepare your works
for the going out, and make ready for the field"; for he who always remembers
death, that it will come of necessity and will not delay, will not fail
greatly; neither reasoning away the deposit of the instructions nor spitting
upon the homeliness and the unadorned quality of the diction. For it is not
the work of divine teaching to speak with sophistry, but to persuade the mind
with thoughts of truth, according to what is said: "Open your mouth with the
word of God"; and again: "Do not miss the narrative of elders, for they also
learned from the fathers." Pref.5 I therefore, most eager for learning, man of
God, following in part this saying, have met with many of the saints, not
using a curious reasoning, but even completing a journey of thirty days and
twice as many, having walked, as before God, the whole land of the Romans by a
journey on foot, I welcomed the hardship of the traveling for a meeting with a
God-loving man, that I might gain what I did not have. Pref.6 For if Paul, far
better than I, having surpassed in way of life and knowledge and conscience
and faith, undertook the journey from Tarsus to Judaea for a meeting with
Peter and James and John, and narrates it in the form of a boast, publishing
his own labors to provoke those who live with sloth and idleness, in saying:
"I went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas," not being content with the report of
the virtue but longing also for the meeting of his person: how much more ought
I, the debtor of ten thousand talents, to do this, not benefiting those men
but profiting myself. Pref.7 For those also who


have recorded the lives of the fathers, both Abraham and those in succession,
Moses and Elijah and John, did not narrate in order to glorify those men, but
in order that they might also profit those who come upon them. Knowing these
things therefore, most faithful servant of Christ Lausus, and admonishing
yourself, bear also with our chatter for the guarding of the pious mind, which
is naturally tossed by various evils both visible and invisible, able to be at
rest only by continuous prayer and by minding one's own work. Pref.8 For many
of the brothers, even while flourishing in labors and alms, and boasting of
unmarried life or virginity, and having trusted in the study of divine oracles
and in earnest efforts, missed passionlessness, having without discernment,
under a pretext of piety, fallen sick with certain busy-bodinesses, from which
are born much-busynesses or evil-busynesses driving away good-work, the mother
of minding one's own work. Pref.9 Be a man therefore, I entreat, not fattening
the wealth; which indeed you have also done, having sufficiently lessened it
by the distribution to those in need, through the service of virtue that comes
from this; nor with some impulse and irrational prejudice, in a man-pleasing
way, having bound the purpose with an oath, as some have suffered,
contentiously, by love of glory of not eating or drinking, having enslaved the
self-determining will to the necessity of the oath, and having fallen under
this again pitiably, by love of life and acedia and pleasure, bringing forth
perjury. Taking therefore with reason and abstaining with reason you will
never sin. Pref.10 For reason is divine among the movements in us, driving out
the harmful things, and taking on the beneficial; for "a law is not laid down
for a righteous man." For wine-drinking with reason is better than
water-drinking with pride. And look at the holy men who drank wine with
reason, and the profane men who drank water without reason, and no longer
blame the matter or praise it, but bless or call wretched the mind of those
who use the matter well or badly. Joseph once drank wine among Egyptians, but
he was not harmed in the mind, for he had secured the purpose. Pref.11 But
Pythagoras and Diogenes and Plato drank water, among whom are also Manichaeans
and the rest of the company of would-be philosophers, and they drove to such a
point of light-glory by incontinence as even to be ignorant of God and to
worship idols. And those around the apostle Peter also touched the use of
wine, so that even their teacher the Saviour himself was reproached for the
partaking, the Jews saying: "Why do your disciples not fast as also those of
John?" And again, attacking the disciples with reproaches, they said: "Your
teacher eats and drinks with tax-collectors and sinners." And they would not
have taken hold of him over bread and water but over cooked dishes and wine,
clearly; Pref.12 to whom again, unreasonably admiring water-drinking and
blaming wine-drinking, the Saviour said: "John came in the way of
righteousness, neither eating nor drinking" (that is, meats and wine, for
without the other things he could not live) "and they say: He has a demon. The
son of man came eating and drinking, and they say: Behold a man glutton and
wine-drinker, a friend of tax-collectors and sinners," because of the eating
and drinking. What then shall we do? Let us follow neither those who blame nor
those who praise, but either with John let us fast with reason even if they
say: They have a demon; or with Jesus let us drink wine in wisdom, if the body
should need it, even if they say: Behold men gluttons and wine-drinkers.
Pref.13 For neither is eating anything according to truth nor is abstinence,
but faith extending through love to the works. For when faith attends upon
every action, the one who eats and drinks is uncondemned because of the faith;
"for everything that is not from faith is sin." But since every one of those
who go wrong will say that he partakes by faith or does some other thing with
irrational assurance, the conscience being corrupted, the Saviour
distinguished saying: "From their fruits you will know them." And that the
fruit of those who live by reason and understanding according to the divine
apostle "is love and joy and peace and long-suffering, kindness,


goodness, faith, meekness, and self-control," is confessed. Pref.14 For Paul
himself said: "For the fruit of the Spirit is" such and such. And that the one
who is eager to have such fruits will not unreasonably or aimlessly or
untimely eat meats nor drink wine, nor will he dwell with anyone with an evil
conscience, the same Paul said again that "Everyone who contends is
self-controlled in all things," while the flesh is healthy abstaining from the
things that fatten, but when it is sick or in pain or also sharing in griefs
and circumstances he will use foods or drinks as medicines for the healing of
the things that distress, but he will abstain from the things harmful
according to the soul, anger, envy, vainglory, acedia, slander and irrational
suspicion, giving thanks in the Lord. Pref.15 Having therefore treated of this
sufficiently I bring again another exhortation to your eagerness for learning.
Flee with all your power meetings with men who have no profit and who adorn
the skin unsuitably, even if they should be orthodox, much more of heretics,
who harm by hypocrisy, even if they should seem by grey hairs or wrinkles to
drag along a length of time. For even if you should be harmed in nothing by
them through nobility of manners, you will at least, the lesser thing, be made
vain or be lifted up laughing at these, which is harm to you. But more than a
bright window pursue holy meetings of men and of women, so that through these,
as a finely written book, you may be able clearly to see also your own heart,
being able through the comparison to test your own slackness or negligence.
Pref.16 For both the color of the faces blooming upon the grey hair and the
array of the clothing and the unpride of the words and the reverence of the
expressions and the grace of the thoughts will strengthen you, even if you
happen to be in acedia. "For the array of a man and the step of a foot and the
laugh of teeth will announce the things about him" as the wisdom says.
Beginning therefore the narratives I will not leave unknown to you by the word
either those in the cities or those in the villages or deserts. For it is not
the place that is sought where these dwelt, but the manner of the purpose. 1
.tConcerning Isidore 1.1 Having first set foot in the city of the Alexandrians
in the second consulship of Theodosius the great emperor, who now is among
angels through his faith in Christ, I met in the city a wonderful man adorned
from every side both in character and in knowledge, Isidore the presbyter,
being xenodochos of the church of the Alexandrians; who was said to have
accomplished the first contests of youth in the desert; whose cell also I saw
on the mountain of Nitria. And I found this man an old man of seventy years,
who having lived fifteen years more dies in peace. 1.2 This man until the very
end did not wear linen besides a handkerchief, did not touch a bath, did not
partake of meats; who had such a little body held together by the grace, that
all who did not know his diet expected that he lives in luxury. If I should
wish to narrate the virtues of this man's soul in part, time will fail me; who
was so philanthropic and peaceful, that even his enemies themselves, the
unbelievers, respected his shadow because of the extreme kindness. 1.3 And he
had such knowledge of the holy scriptures and of the divine doctrines, that
even at the very meals of the brothers his mind would go out and he would be
as a child; and being asked to narrate the things of the ecstasy, he would say
that "I went abroad in the mind, having been snatched by a certain
contemplation." I also knew this man often having wept at table, and having
inquired the cause of the tears I heard him saying that "I am ashamed
partaking of irrational food, being rational and owing to live in a paradise
of luxury through the authority given us by Christ." 1.4 This man, being known
to the whole senate according to Rome and to the wives of the great men, when
he had gone with Athanasius the bishop the first time, then with Demetrius the
bishop, and overflowing with wealth and abundance of necessities, did not
write a will when dying, did not leave a coin, nor a thing to his own sisters


who were virgins; but he committed them to Christ saying: "He who created you
will manage your life, as also me." And there was with his sisters a company
of seventy virgins. 1.5 This man, when I had gone to him being young and
asking to be grounded in the solitary life, the age still being vigorous and
not needing word but labors according to the flesh, as a good colt-tamer led
me out of the city to the so-called desert places five miles away. 2
.tConcerning Dorotheus 2.1 And he handed me over to a certain Dorotheus, a
Theban ascetic spending the sixtieth year in the cave, and he commands me to
complete three years with him toward the taming of the passions; for he knew
that the old man lived with great hardness of life; having commanded me to
return again to him for the sake of spiritual teaching. And not having been
able to complete three years, having fallen into illness, thus before the
three I was taken from him; for his diet was dry and most arid. 2.2 For
through the whole of the day in the heat in the desert by the sea he would
gather stones, and always building these and making cells he would yield them
to those not able to build, finishing one cell each year. And when I had once
said to him: "What are you doing, father, in such old age killing your little
body in these heats?" he answered saying: "It kills me, I kill it." For he
would eat six ounces of bread and a bunch of small vegetables, and he would
drink of water the due measure. With God as witness I did not know him to have
stretched out feet, nor to have slept upon a mat, nor upon a bed; but through
the whole night sitting he would plait a rope of palm fronds for the account
of food. 2.3 And having expected that he does this on my account, I inquired
carefully and having ascertained from other disciples of his, who remained by
themselves, that from youth he had this way of life, never having slept on
purpose, unless perhaps while working or eating he closed the eye, having been
carried down by sleep, so that often even the morsel would fall from his mouth
at the time of eating by excess of drowsiness. And when I once compelled him
to lie back a little upon the mat, being somewhat grieved he said: "If you
persuade the angels to sleep, you will persuade also the earnest man." 2.4 At
his well around the ninth hour he sent me once to fill the jar for the account
of the partaking of the ninth hour. And it happened that I, having gone, saw
an asp in the well below, and no longer drew the water, but having gone said
to him that: "We have died, abba; for I saw an asp in the well." And he,
having smiled a reverent smile, looked at me for a long time, and having
shaken the head said: "If it should seem good to the devil to become a serpent
or a tortoise at every well and to fall into the springs of the waters, do you
remain never drinking?" and having gone out and having drawn through himself,
fasting he first swallowed saying: "Where a cross visits, the evil of anyone
has no strength." 3 .tConcerning Potamiaena 3.1 This blessed Isidore, having
met with the blessed Antony, narrated to me a matter worthy of writing, having
heard from him that a certain Potamiaena so called, at the time of Maximian
the persecutor, was a most beautiful girl, a servant of someone; whom her
master, having entreated with many promises, was not able to persuade; 3.2 and
last, having gone mad, he hands her over to the then prefect of Alexandria,
giving her up as a Christian and as blaspheming the times and the emperors
over the persecutions, having suggested to him with money that "If she should
agree with my aim, keep her unpunished"; but if she should persist in the
severity, he asked that she be punished, so that she might not living laugh at
his debauchery. 3.3 And having been brought before the tribunal she was
stormed in the purpose with various punishing instruments. Among which
instruments the judge, having also filled a great cauldron with pitch,
commanded it to be kindled underneath. The pitch therefore boiling and being
fiercely burned, he held out to her: "Either go, submit to the wishes of your
master; or that you may know that I command you to be plunged in the
cauldron." And she answered saying: "May there never be such a


judge who commands to submit to debauchery." 3.4 Having therefore gone mad
that man commands her, having been stripped, to be thrown into the cauldron.
And she lets out a voice saying: "By the head of your emperor whom you fear,
if it has been judged by you to punish me thus, command me to be let down
little by little into the cauldron, that you may know how much endurance
Christ whom you do not know grants me." And being let down little by little
over the space of an hour she expired, the pitch having reached about her
neck. 4 .tConcerning Didymus 4.1 Very many indeed both men and women were
perfected in the church of the Alexandrians, worthy of the land of the meek.
Among whom also Didymus the writer, who became from eyes; of whom I also had
four meetings, going to him at intervals over ten years. For he dies at
eighty-five years. This man was from eyes, as he himself narrated to me,
having lost the sight at four years, having learned neither letters nor having
gone to teachers; 4.2 for he had the teacher according to nature strong, his
own conscience; who was adorned with such grace of knowledge that historically
the thing written was fulfilled upon him: "The Lord makes the blind wise." For
he interpreted the old and new testament word for word; and of the doctrines
he took such care, having set forth the word about them finely and firmly, as
to surpass all the ancients in knowledge. 4.3 And when he once compelled me to
make a prayer in his cell, and I was not willing, he said narrating that "Into
this cell the blessed Antony entered a third time visiting me; and having been
asked by me to make a prayer he immediately bent the knee in the cell, and he
did not prepare me to say the word a second time, having taught me by deed in
the obedience. So that if you walk according to the track of his way of life,
as being a monk and living as a stranger through virtue, put away the
contentiousness." 4.4 And he narrated to me also this, that "When I was
anxious about the life of Julian the wretched emperor as a persecutor, and was
in distress one of the days and until deep evening had not tasted bread
because of the anxiety, it happened that I, sitting on the chair, was carried
down by sleep, and I saw an ecstasy, white horses running through with the
riders and proclaiming: 'Tell Didymus, today at the seventh hour Julian has
died; having risen therefore eat, and send, they say, to the bishop
Athanasius, that he also may know.' And I noted, he says, both the hour and
the month and the week and the day, and it was found thus." 5 .tConcerning
Alexandra 5.1 And he narrated to me also about a certain servant-girl named
Alexandra, who having left the city and having shut herself in a tomb received
the things toward the need through a hole, meeting neither women nor men by
sight for ten years. And in the tenth year she fell asleep having arranged
herself, so that the one who had gone in the usual way and had not obtained an
answer announced to us. Having therefore torn away the door and having entered
we found her having fallen asleep. 5.2 And the thrice-blessed Melanium, about
whom I will speak later, also said about her, that "I did not see her by
sight, but having stood about the hole I asked her to say the cause for which
she shut herself into the tomb. And she through the hole spoke to me saying
that 'Someone was harmed in the mind toward me; and that I might not seem to
grieve him or to slander, I chose to bring myself living into the tomb, rather
than to scandalize a soul made according to the image of God.' 5.3 And when I,
she says, had said: 'How then do you endure meeting no one, but boxing with
acedia?' she said that 'From dawn until the ninth hour I pray by the hour,
spinning the flax; and the remaining hours I go around in the mind the holy
patriarchs and prophets and apostles and martyrs; and having eaten my morsel I
wait the other hours, persevering and expecting the end with a good hope.'" 6
.tConcerning the rich virgin 6.1 And I will not omit in the narrative also
those who have lived in contempt, for the praise of those who have succeeded
and for the security of those who come upon it. A certain virgin came to be in
Alexandria humble in the schema


but in purpose haughty, rich in money to excess, not having † given a dowry †
to a stranger nor to a virgin, nor to a church, nor to a poor man an obol.
This woman by many admonitions of the fathers was not putting away the matter.
6.2 And she had also a family, among whom a daughter of her own sister is
born, to whom night and by day she promised her things, having fallen from the
heavenly desire. For this also is a form of the devil's deceit, preparing one
under a pretext of love of kin to bring forth into greed; for that he does not
care about family, teaching brother-killing and mother-killing and
father-killing, is confessed. 6.3 But even if he should seem to insert care of
kin, he does not do this toward goodwill for those, but in order that the soul
may be trained in injustice, knowing the sentence that "The unjust will not
inherit the kingdom of God." And one moved by divine understanding can both
not despise his own soul, and give consolation to his kin, if indeed they are
lacking. But when someone tramples his whole soul with care of kin, he falls
under the law, reckoning his own soul upon a vain thing. 6.4 And the sacred
singer sings about those who take thought of the soul in fear, that "Who will
go up to the mountain of the Lord?" instead of, rarely; "or who will stand in
his holy place? Innocent in hands and clean in heart, who did not take his
soul upon a vain thing." For these take it upon a vain thing, as many as
neglect the virtues, thinking that it is dissolved together with the little
flesh. 6.5 This virgin, wishing, as the saying is, to phlebo 6.5 tomize for
the lightening of the greed, the most holy Macarius, the presbyter and leader
of the poorhouse of the mutilated, devises such a drama; for he was in youth a
stone-worker whom they call a kabidarios. And having gone he says to her:
"Necessary stones, emeralds and hyacinths, have fallen to me, and whether they
are finder's goods or stolen goods I cannot say. They are not subjected to a
price, being above valuation; and the one who has them sells them for five
hundred nomismata. 6.6 If it seems good to you to take them, from one stone
you can save the five hundred nomismata, and use the rest for the adornment of
your niece." The virgin hanging upon it is baited and falls at his feet, "By
your feet," saying, "let no other take them." He therefore urges her that
"Come as far as my house and see them." And she did not bear it, but throws to
him the five hundred nomismata saying: "As you wish, take them; for I do not
wish to see the man who sells." 6.7 And he, having taken the five hundred
nomismata, gives them for the needs of the poorhouse. And time having ridden
by, since the man seemed to have a great reputation in Alexandria being
God-loving and merciful, for he flourished until a hundred years, with whom we
also were contemporaries, she was in awe to remind him. At last having found
him in the church she says to him: "I beg you, what do you command about those
stones for which we have given the five hundred nomismata?" 6.8 And he
answered saying: "From then since you gave me the gold, I put it down for the
price of the stones; and if you wish to come and see them in the hospice, for
there they lie, come and see, if they please you, otherwise take your gold."
And she came most gladly. And the poorhouse had in the upper rooms women, and
in the lower rooms men. And having led her he brings her into the gateway and
says to her: "What do you wish to see first? the hyacinths, or the emeralds?"
She says to him: "What seems good to you." 6.9 He leads her up to the upper
rooms and shows her women mutilated having leprous faces; and he says to her:
"Behold the hyacinths." And he leads her down again below and says to her,
having shown her the men: "Behold the emeralds, if they please you, otherwise
take your gold." Thus that woman, having been put to shame, went out, and
having gone from much grief, because she had not done the matter according to
God, she fell ill; later having given thanks to the presbyter, the girl for
whom she took thought having died after marriage childless. 7 .tConcerning
those in Nitria 7.1


Having therefore met with and spent time at the monasteries about Alexandria
three years, with most excellent and most earnest men about two thousand,
having withdrawn from there I came to the mountain of Nitria. And between this
mountain and Alexandria lies the lake called Maria, of seventy miles; which
having sailed through in one and a half day I came to the mountain on the part
of the south; 7.2 to which mountain the all-desert lies alongside stretching
as far as Ethiopia and the Mazices and Mauretania. On which mountain dwell men
about five thousand having various ways of life, each as he can and as he
wishes; so that it is possible both to remain alone, and as a second, and as
many. On this mountain there are seven bakeries serving both those and the
withdrawers into the all-desert, six hundred men. 7.3 Having therefore dwelt
on this mountain a year and having been much profited from the blessed fathers
Arsisius the great and Poutoubastes and Asion and Cronius and Sarapion, and
having been spurred by many narratives of fathers from them, I entered into
the desert the innermost. On this mountain of Nitria there is a great church,
in which stand three palms, each having a scourge hanging. And one is for the
account of monks who fail, and one of robbers, if indeed they should fall in,
and one of those who happen by; so that all who fail and are convicted as
worthy of blows embrace the palm, and having received stated blows upon the
back are thus released. 7.4 And there is attached to the church a guest-house,
into which they receive the stranger who has gone, until he goes out of his
own will, all the time, even if he should remain for a two-year or three-year;
and having allowed him one week in idleness, the remaining days they occupy
him in works, either in a garden or in a bakery or in a kitchen. And if he
should be of note, they give him a book, not having allowed him until the hour
to meet with anyone. On this mountain also physicians dwell and cake-bakers.
And they also use wine, and wine is sold. 7.5 And all these work linen with
the hands, so that all are without need. And indeed also about the ninth hour
it is possible, having stood, to hear how from each monastery psalmodies come
out, so as to expect that one is lifted in paradise. And they overtake the
church on Saturday only and on the Lord's day. And eight leading presbyters of
this church there are, in which until the first presbyter lives no other
offers, does not speak, does not judge, but they sit by him quietly only. 7.6
This Arsisius and many other old men with him, whom we have seen, were
contemporaries of the blessed Antony. Among whom they narrated also that they
knew Amoun the Nitriote, whose soul Antony saw being taken up and being led by
angels. This man said also that he knew Pachomius the Tabennesiote, a prophet
man, archimandrite of three thousand men, about whom I will narrate later. 8
.tConcerning Amoun the Nitriote 8.1 And he said that Amoun had lived in such a
manner: that being an orphan, a young man about twenty-two years, he was yoked
by force by his own uncle to a wife; and not having been able to resist the
necessity of the uncle, he seemed both to be crowned and to sit in a bridal
chamber, and to have endured all the things according to the marriage. And
after all had gone out, having put them to sleep in the bridal chamber and the
bed, Amoun having risen shuts the door, and having sat he calls his blessed
partner and says to her: 8.2 "Come, lady, henceforth I will narrate the matter
to you; the marriage which we married is this, having nothing superfluous. We
will therefore do well if from now each of us should sleep by himself, that we
may also please God having kept the virginity untouched." And having brought
out from his bosom a little book from the person of the apostle and of the
Saviour he read to the girl being inexperienced of scriptures, and for the
most part adding all things by his own mind he introduced the word about
virginity and purity; so that she, having been assured by the grace of God,
said: 8.3 "I also have been assured,


lord; and what do you command henceforth?" "I command, he says, that each of
us from now remain by himself." And she did not bear it, saying: "Let us
remain in the same house, but on different beds." Having therefore lived
eighteen years with her in the same house, through every day he was occupied
with the garden and the balsam-plot; for he was a balsam-worker. Which balsam
is planted like a vine, being cultivated and pruned, having much labor. In the
evening therefore entering into the house he made prayers and ate with her;
and having made again a night prayer he went out. 8.4 These things being thus
accomplished, and both having been driven to passionlessness, the prayers of
Amoun operated, and she says to him last: "I have something to say to you, my
lord; that, if you should hear me, I may be assured that you love me according
to God." He says to her: "Say what you wish." And she says to him: "It is a
just matter, you being a man and practicing righteousness, and I likewise
having been zealous for the same way as you, to remain by ourselves. For it is
out of place that such virtue of yours be hidden, you dwelling with me in
purity." 8.5 And he, having given thanks to God, says to her: "Have you
