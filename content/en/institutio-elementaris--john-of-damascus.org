An elementary introduction to the dogmas, from the voice of John the humble
monk, to John the most holy bishop of Laodicea.
Preface
In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, of the one
Godhead in three hypostases and to be worshipped, of the consubstantial and
life-originating Trinity, of the one God, from whom every good gift and every
perfect endowment comes down to us from above, I open my stammering and
slow-tongued lips, I the sinner and wretched one, trusting in him, that
because of the humility of those who asked for the word and for the benefit of
those who hear, the Lord of grace and the storehouse of all good things, the
God who is over all, blessed unto the ages, will give a spirit of wisdom and a
word in the opening of my mouth. Amen.
1 Concerning substance and nature and form.
Substance and nature and form, according to the holy Fathers, are the same.
And again hypostasis and person and individual are the same. And difference
and quality and property are the same. Our aim, then, is to speak about these
and such things, concerning which your Holiness also deigned to ask.
Beginning, then, we speak thus concerning substance and nature and form. There
is a superessential substance and nature and form, the incomprehensible
Godhead, and its hypostases and persons are Father, Son, and the all-holy
Spirit; for each of them is a perfect hypostasis and is a perfect person.
Further, every creature of God is a substance. But the creatures are many and
have much difference from one another; for the angel is one creature and man
another, and the ox is another and the dog another, heaven is another and
earth another, and fire is another and air another and water another; and, to
speak simply, there are many species among the creatures. Each species,
therefore, is one nature: for example, all men are one nature, and all angels
are one nature, and all oxen are one nature; and likewise the remaining
creatures. And the nature of the angels is one, and that of men another, and
that of oxen another; and likewise in the rest.
2 Concerning hypostasis and person and individual.
Since men are many, each man is a hypostasis: for example, Adam is a
hypostasis, and Eve is another hypostasis, and Seth is another hypostasis.
Likewise also in the rest. Each man is a hypostasis other than the remaining
men, and each ox is a hypostasis, and each angel is a hypostasis. So that
nature and form and substance is the common, which contains the consubstantial
hypostases, while hypostasis and individual and person is the particular, that
is, each of those contained under the same species.
3 Concerning difference and quality and property.
We said that there are many species and various substances, and likewise also
under each substance and species various hypostases. Therefore both each
substance differs from the other substance, and each hypostasis from the other
hypostasis. That thing, then, in which substance differs from substance and
species from species or hypostasis from hypostasis is called difference and
quality and property: for example, man differs from the angel, because the
angel is immortal, while man is mortal; the immortal and the mortal is a
difference and quality and property. Again Adam differs from Seth, because
Adam is father of Seth, while Seth is son of Adam. Fatherhood, then, and
sonship are differences; for in these Adam and Seth differ from one another.
And all such things are called differences and qualities and properties: for
example, the rational and the irrational, the mortal and the immortal, the
two-footed, the four-footed, the sensitive and the insensitive, the snub-nosed
and the hook-nosed, the white and the black, the tall and the short, and all
such things.
4 Concerning essential and non-essential difference, that is, natural
difference and accident.
Every thing by which a species differs from another species and a substance
from another substance is called an essential and natural and constitutive
difference and quality and natural property and property of nature. For
example, the angel and man differ from one another according to the immortal
and the mortal; the immortal is an essential and natural and constitutive
difference of the angel and a natural property. Likewise also the mortal of
man. Again man and the ox differ from one another, because man is rational,
while the ox is irrational. See: the rational is an essential and constitutive
difference of man, and the irrational of the ox. Likewise also in the
remaining species and substances and natures and forms. But every thing in
which a hypostasis differs from a hypostasis of the same species and of the
same substance is called a non-essential difference and quality and hypostatic
property and characterizing property; this is the accident: for example, a man
differs from another man, because the one is tall and the other short, the one
white and the other black; the one is healthy and the other is ill, the one is
a father and the other a son. These and all such things are non-essential
differences and qualities and hypostatic and characterizing properties and
accidents. And, to speak simply, whatever things are observed alike in all the
hypostases under the same species, and without which it is impossible for the
substance and the species to subsist, are called essential difference; but
whatever things are in some of the hypostases of the same species and are not
in others are accidents and non-essential.
5 Concerning separable and inseparable accident.
Of accidents some are separable, and some inseparable. Inseparable are those
in which a hypostasis differs from another hypostasis, but does not differ
from itself: for example, this particular man differs from another man,
because he himself is snub-nosed while that one is hook-nosed, because the one
is grey-eyed and the other black-eyed. But he does not differ from himself;
for the snub-nosed is always snub-nosed and the hook-nosed is always
hook-nosed, and it is not possible for the same man to be at one time
snub-nosed and at another hook-nosed. And these are called inseparable
accidents, because, once they come to be in someone, they are no longer
separated from him. Nevertheless they are accidents; for it is possible both
not to be snub-nosed and to be a man, and not to be hook-nosed and to be a
man; for not every man is snub-nosed nor is every man hook-nosed, but every
man is rational, and he who is not rational is not a man. Separable are those
which even the same man, that is the same hypostasis, at one time has and at
another does not have, for example being ill and being healthy, sitting and
standing up, rejoicing and grieving, and such things.
6 Concerning things of the same substance and of different substance.
Of the same substance and of the same nature and of the same form and of the
same species and of the same genus are those things which are under the same
species and under the same substance, for example Peter, Paul, and the
remaining particular men, and all oxen and all dogs, and likewise the rest. Of
different substance and of different nature and of different form and of
different species and of different genus are those that are under this species
and that, and this substance and that, for example man and horse and ox and
dog and such things.
7 Concerning genus and species.
Genus is divided into species, for example the animal is either rational or
irrational; see: the animal is genus of the rational and of the irrational,
while the rational and the irrational are species of the animal. Again the
rational is divided into mortal, man, and immortal, angel. The rational is
genus of the mortal and of the immortal, while the mortal and the immortal are
species of the rational. In order, then, that what is said may become clearer,
let us take up the account from above. The most generic genus is the first
genus, which, being a genus, is not a species, because it has no other genus
above it; and the most specific species is the last and subordinate species,
which, being a species, is not a genus, because it has no other species
divided from it. And between the most generic genus and the most specific
species there are both genera and species, genera of those below, species of
those above, which are called subaltern. For example, substance is the most
generic genus; for it has no other genus above it, and therefore it is not a
species either. This is divided into body and incorporeal; body is divided
into animate and inanimate; the animate is divided into sensitive and
insensitive; the sensitive into rational and irrational; the rational into
mortal and immortal. The incorporeal, then, is rational and immortal; and here
I mean by incorporeal a substance such as soul, angel, demon; each of these is
a most specific species. And an animate sensitive body is the animal, and a
rational mortal animal is man, who is a most specific species, and an
irrational barking animal is the dog, who is a most specific species. Then an
animate insensitive body is the plant. A body wholly inanimate is earth, air,
water, fire, each of which is a most specific species. And for greater clarity
we speak thus. Substance, which contains in a superessential way the uncreated
Godhead, and in a cognitive and encompassing way the whole creation, is the
most generic genus. This is either incorporeal and intellective and immortal,
as God, angel, soul, demon, or an inanimate body, as fire, air, water, earth,
or a vegetative body, as vine, date-palm, olive, and such things, or an
animate sensitive body, that is an animal, as horse, dog, elephant, and such
things, or composed of body and incorporeal soul, as man. Of these, substance,
as first and more universal and encompassing of all, is the most generic
genus. And of this most generic genus the species are the incorporeal, the
inanimate body, the vegetative body, the animate and sensitive body, that is
the animal. These are called species and subaltern genera; for they are
species of substance as divided from it. And each of them is divided into
other species and is encompassing and more universal than they; for the
incorporeal substance contains God, angel, soul, demon; the inanimate body,
earth, water, air, fire; the vegetative body, vine, olive, date-palm, and such
things; the animate and sensitive, that is the animal, horse, ox, dog, and
such things, and further also man, composed of incorporeal soul and body.
A hypostasis, then, and individual and person is Father, Son, and the Holy
Spirit; and the species that contains them is the superessential and
incomprehensible Godhead. Further, hypostases are Michael, Gabriel, and the
remaining particular angels; and the species that contains them is the angelic
nature. Further, hypostases are Peter, Paul, John, and the remaining
particular men; and the species that contains them is humanity. Again,
hypostases are this horse and that; and the species that contains them is
horse simply. Again, hypostases are this and that plant of the olive; and the
species that contains them is the olive simply. One must know, then, that
those which contain individuals only are called most specific species, for
example the angel, man, the horse, the dog, the olive, the date-palm, and such
things; while those which contain the most specific species only are called
genera and subaltern species, for example the incorporeal, the body, the
inanimate, the insensitive, the vegetative, the animate, the sensitive, the
animal, the rational, the irrational, the mortal, the immortal, the
two-footed, the four-footed, the creeping, the winged, and all such things,
which contain the most specific species and are genera of them and their
constitutive differences, but are contained by the most generic genus, which
is substance. And the same things must be treated in another way. All things
that came to be by one command of God are of one genus: for example, God
commanded fishes to come to be, and many differences of fishes came to be; all
fishes are of one genus, and each difference of fishes is a species. Again God
commanded the waters to bring forth winged things, and many differences of
winged things came to be; all winged things are of one genus, and each
difference of the winged things is a species of that genus of winged things.
Likewise also in reptiles and cattle and wild beasts and herbs and vegetables
and trees. And there is said to be also one genus of all animals and one genus
of all plants and one genus of things wholly inanimate and having no
growth-power or nutritive power at all, and again one genus of the incorporeal
and one genus of bodies; and the whole creation is also said to be one genus,
and the differences of the creatures are many species under the genus of
creation, and the whole substance is one genus.
8 Concerning energy.
Energy is an active motion of that which acts. And natural energy is an active
motion of nature, for example the rational motion of the mind, the vital, the
sensitive, the nutritive, the growth-giving, the generative, and motion
according to impulse, that is the motions of the body, and the imaginative and
the memorial, and the irascible and the concupiscible and the volitive, that
is the appetitive, and such things. And this is of five kinds: either
intellective, as in angels and all incorporeal substances, who think by a
simple application; or rational, as in men composed of incorporeal soul and
body, who think not by a simple but by a varied and discursive application; or
vital, as in irrational animals that have sensation but neither reason nor
think; or vegetative, as in things without sensation that have nutritive and
growth-giving and generative power; or wholly lifeless, as in earth and
stones, which have neither vital nor nutritive nor growth-giving power, yet
are not without a share in certain natural energies.
9 Concerning passion.
Passion is a natural passive motion of the nature of that which suffers, which
is an energy of the nature, but a passion of that about which the energy is,
for example generation, joy, grief, anguish, distress, fear, hesitation, toil,
sleep, pleasure, pain, anger, desire, cutting, flux and corruption, and such
things. These and such things are natural passions and not to be blamed, not
being in our power. Sin alone is against nature, a most evil voluntary and
self-chosen passion of the soul, and corruption.
10 Concerning will.
Will is a natural rational appetite. And again, will is a natural rational and
self-determining appetitive motion of the mind. For the irrational animals are
not moved toward appetite in a self-determining way; for when the appetite is
moved naturally in them, since they do not have reason as overseer, they are
led by nature, being overcome by its appetite. Hence they rush at once to the
act, unless they are hindered by another. Therefore they are not
self-determining, but under another's power. But the rational nature has
authority to yield to the natural appetite and not to yield but to overcome
it, and nature makes this plain, being a natural desire of life and yet
yielding to reason; for many, having overcome this by the ruling reason, have
gone willingly to death and have deferred the appetite for food and for sleep
and for the rest, leading nature by self-determining reason, not being led by
it. As, then, the natural appetite is present in every sensitive nature, so
self-determination is present in every rational nature; for every rational
thing is self-determining, and this is what is according to the image of God.
Will, then, is a natural, that is a rational and self-determining, appetitive
power and motion. And the things subject to the will, which the will desires,
are called gnomic wills; for by its own gnome the mind, being
self-determining, rushes toward the use of these. And these are such as to eat
or not to eat, to drink or not to drink, to journey or not to journey, to
marry or not to marry, and such things, which are in our power, which we are
able both to do and to do their opposites.
