When Valerian had been captured (for where, indeed, should the life of
Gallienus begin, if not from that especial disaster by which his life was
crushed?), with the state tottering, when Odaenathus had already seized the
empire of the East, and Gallienus, on learning of his father's captivity, was
rejoicing, the armies were wandering everywhere, the generals were murmuring
in every province, and there was huge grief among all that Valerian, a Roman
emperor, was being held in Persia like a slave. Yet there was even greater
sadness among all that Gallienus, once he had obtained the empire, had ruined
the state by his character, just as his father had ruined it by fate.

Therefore, in the consulship of Gallienus and Volusianus, Macrianus and
Ballista came together, called up the remnants of the army, and, since Roman
power in the East was tottering, asked whom they should make emperor.
Gallienus was conducting himself so carelessly that his name was not even
mentioned among the army. At last it was decided that they should name
Macrianus, together with his sons, emperors, and take up the defense of the
state.

settled, came first to Asia and made for Illyricum. In Illyricum he joined
battle with a general of the emperor Aureolus (who had assumed imperial power
against Gallienus), a man named Domitianus, having one of his sons with him
and leading thirty thousand soldiers. But Macrianus was defeated together with
his son named Macrianus, and the whole army was surrendered to the emperor
Aureolus.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Meanwhile, with the state in turmoil throughout almost the whole world, when
Odaenathus learned that Macrianus had been killed with his son, that Aureolus
was reigning, and that Gallienus was conducting affairs more slackly, he
hurried with an army to seize Macrianus's other son, if fortune should grant
it.

But those who were with Macrianus's son, named Quietus, agreeing with
Odaenathus at the instigation of Ballista, Macrianus's prefect, killed the
young man and, having thrown the body over the wall, all surrendered
themselves fully to Odaenathus.

Odaenathus therefore became emperor of almost the whole East, while Aureolus
held Illyricum and Gallienus Rome. The same Ballista killed many of the people
of Emesa, to whom Macrianus's soldiers had fled, together with Quietus and the
keeper of the treasures, so that the city was almost destroyed.

Meanwhile Odaenathus, as if he were acting for Gallienus's side, had
everything reported to him according to the truth.

But Gallienus, learning that Macrianus had been killed with his children, as
if secure in his affairs and as if his father had already been recovered, gave
himself to lust and pleasure. He gave circus games and stage plays, gymnastic
games, a show-hunt as well, and gladiatorial games, and he called the people
to festivity and applause as if on days of victory. And while most were
mourning his father's captivity, he, under a show of honor (that his father
seemed to have been deceived by zeal for valor), rejoiced beyond measure. It
was well known, however, that he had not been able to bear his father's
severity, and that it had been a thing he wished for, that he no longer had
that paternal gravity hanging over his neck.

* Chapter 4

About the same time Aemilianus assumed imperial power in Egypt and, having
seized the granaries, pressed many towns with the evil of famine. But
Theodotus, Gallienus's general, after a battle captured him and sent him alive
to Gallienus, stripped of the imperial ornaments. Egypt after this was given
to Theodotus; Aemilianus was strangled in prison; there was also savage
treatment of the Thebaid soldiers, a good many being killed.

When Gallienus persisted in luxury and wickedness, gave himself to mockeries
and gluttony, and governed the state no otherwise than as boys play at powers
in their games, the Gauls, in whom it is inborn not to bear princes who are
frivolous, falling away from Roman virtue, and given to luxury, called
Postumus to imperial power, the armies also agreeing, because they complained
that the emperor was occupied with lusts. Against him Gallienus himself led an
army; and when he had begun to besiege the city in which Postumus was, the
Gauls defending it fiercely, Gallienus, going around the walls, was struck by
an arrow. For Postumus reigned for seven years and most stoutly defended the
Gauls from all the barbarians flooding around them. Forced by these evils,
Gallienus made peace with Aureolus out of eagerness to attack Postumus, and
with a long war drawn out through various sieges and battles he conducted
affairs now successfully, now unsuccessfully. Besides, these evils had been
added: that the Scythians had invaded Bithynia and destroyed cities. Finally
they burned Astacus, which was later called Nicomedia, and laid it waste
heavily.

Finally, as if by a conspiracy of the whole world, with the parts of the globe
shaken, even in Sicily there arose something like a slave war, with brigands
ranging about, who were scarcely

prescribed. For so great a pestilence had arisen both at Rome and in the
Achaean cities that in one day five thousand people perished of the same

There was fighting in Achaea under the general Marcianus against the same
Goths, from where they withdrew, defeated by the Achaeans.

The Scythians, however, that is a part of the Goths, were laying waste Asia.
Even the temple of Diana at Ephesus was stripped and burned, a work whose fame
is well enough known among the peoples.

It is shameful to report, amid these times, while those things were being
done, what Gallienus, to the harm of the human race, often said as if in jest.
For when it was announced to him that Egypt had revolted, he is said to have
said:

/What? Cannot we do without Egyptian linen?/
when he learned that Asia had been laid waste both by shocks of
the elements and by Scythian raids,
/What,/ he said, /cannot we do without saltpetre?/
When Gaul was lost he is reported to have laughed and said:
/Is the state safe without Atrebatic cloaks?/
So, in short, about all parts of the world, when he was losing them, he joked
as if he seemed to be affected by the loss of cheap household services. And
that no evil might be lacking to Gallienus's times, the city of the
Byzantines, famous in naval wars, the lock of the Pontus, was so wholly laid
waste by the soldiers of this same Gallienus that absolutely no one survived.
In short, no old family is found among the Byzantines, unless someone occupied
in travel or military service escaped, who might represent the antiquity and
nobility of his line.

* Chapter 7

Against Postumus, therefore, Gallienus entered on war with Aureolus and the
general Claudius, who afterward obtained the empire, the founder of the line
of our Caesar Constantius. And since Postumus was being helped by many Celtic
and Frankish auxiliaries, he went to war with Victorinus, with whom he had
shared the imperial power. Gallienus's side was victorious after several
battles run through with varying outcomes. There was in Gallienus a sudden
daring of courage, for sometimes he was deeply moved by injuries. In short, he
marched to avenge the Byzantines, and although he did not think he could be
received within the walls, being received on another day he killed all the
soldiers, unarmed, surrounded by a ring of armed men, having broken the pact
he had promised. In the same period the Scythians also in Asia, laid waste by
the valor and leadership of Roman generals, withdrew to their own lands.

When the soldiers at Byzantium had been killed, Gallienus, as if he had done
some great thing, flew to Rome at a rapid pace and, having called the senators
together, celebrated his ten-year festival with a new kind of games, a new
sort of processions, an ex-

pretended nations, such as Goths, Sarmatians, Franks, Persians, so that no
fewer than two hundred were led in each troop.

By this procession the foolish man believed he was making sport of the Roman
people. But, as Roman witticisms are, one favored Postumus, another
Regalianus, another Aureolus or Aemilianus, another Saturninus, for he too was
already said to be ruling. Amid this there was huge complaint about his
father, whom the son had left unavenged, and whom foreigners had somehow
avenged. Yet Gallienus was not moved at such things, his heart stupefied by
pleasures, but he asked those around him:

/What have we for lunch? What pleasures are prepared? And what will tomorrow's
stage-play be, and what the circus games?/
So, the procession finished and the hecatombs celebrated, he returned to the
palace and, after running through banquets and feasts, he assigned other days
to public pleasures. Nor should a not ignoble kind of jest be passed over. For
when a flock of Persians, as if captives, was being led through the procession
(a ridiculous thing), certain jesters mixed themselves among the Persians,
searching everything most carefully and peering at each man's face with a
marvelous gaping. When they were asked what they were doing with that cunning,
they answered:

/We are looking for the prince's father./
When this reached Gallienus, he was moved neither by shame nor by grief nor by
piety, and he ordered the jesters to be burned alive. The people took this
deed more bitterly than anyone would guess. The soldiers, indeed, grieved so
that not long after they paid him back.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:

:END:

In the consulship of Gallienus and Saturninus, Odaenathus, king of the
Palmyrenes, obtained the empire of the whole East, chiefly because by brave
deeds he showed himself worthy of so great a majesty among the unfaithful,
while Gallienus was doing either nothing, or luxurious things, or silly and
ridiculous things. In short, he at once declared war on the Persians in
vengeance for Valerian, which his son was neglecting. He at once occupied
Nisibis and Carrhae, the people of Nisibis and Carrhae surrendering themselves
and railing at Gallienus. Yet reverence of Odaenathus toward Gallienus was not
lacking. For he sent the captured satraps to him almost for the sake of insult
and of displaying himself. And when they had been brought to Rome, while
Odaenathus was winning, Gallienus triumphed, with no mention made of his
father, whom he did not even enroll among the gods except under compulsion,
when he had heard that he was dead; but he was still living, for he had
learned of his death falsely.

Odaenathus, however, besieged the multitude of the Parthians at Ctesiphon and,
having laid waste all the places around, killed countless people. But when all
the satraps from all regions had flocked there for common defense, there were
long and varied battles, yet the Roman victory lasted longer still. And since
Odaenathus was doing nothing else than to free Valerian, he pressed on daily,
but the best of emperors was struggling with the difficulties of the ground on
foreign soil.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:

While these things were being done among the Persians, the Scythians burst
into Cappadocia. There, after capturing cities and after a varied war long
waged, they moved to Bithynia.

Wherefore the soldiers again thought of making a new emperor. All of them
Gallienus, in his usual way, when he could not appease them and win them back
to his favor, killed.

Yet while the soldiers were seeking a prince worthy of themselves, Gallienus
was archon at Athens, that is the highest magistrate, with that vanity by
which he desired both to be enrolled as a citizen and to take part in all the
rites, which neither Hadrian in the height of prosperity nor Antoninus in a
mature peace had done, although they were so learned in Greek letters that
they rarely yielded to any of the most learned men in the judgment of the
great. Besides, he longed to be thrust into the number of the Areopagites,
with the state almost despised. For Gallienus was, which cannot be denied,
distinguished in oratory, in poetry, and in all the arts.

His is that wedding-song which was foremost among a hundred poets. For when he
was joining the children of his brothers in marriage, and all the Greek and
Latin poets had spoken wedding-songs, and that for very many days, he, while
he held the hands of the betrothed, as some say, is reported to have spoken
thus more than once:

Go on, then, boys, and sweat as one,
marrow to marrow, each in each;
let doves not beat your murmurings,
nor ivy your arms, nor shells your kisses.

It would be long to string together his verses and speeches, by which in his
time he shone among poets as among rhetors. But one thing is sought in an
emperor, another is demanded in an orator or a poet.

His best deed is indeed praised. For in the consulship of his brother Valerian
and of Lucillus his kinsman, when he learned that the Persians had been laid
waste by Odaenathus, that Nisibis and Carrhae had been brought back into Roman
power, that all Mesopotamia was ours, that Ctesiphon finally had been reached,
that the king had fled, that satraps had been captured, that very many
Persians had been killed, he named Odaenathus Augustus, sharing the imperial
power, and ordered coin to be struck for him, on which he should drag captured
Persians, which both the senate and the city and every age received with joy.

He was besides a man of the greatest cleverness, of whose showing of wit it is
worth putting a few things. For when he had sent a huge bull into the arena,
and a hunter had gone out to strike it and could not kill it though it was
brought out ten times, he sent a crown to the hunter. And when all were
murmuring what it meant that a most inept man should be crowned, he ordered it
said through the herald:

/Not to hit a bull so many times is hard./
The same man, when someone had sold glass gems as real ones to his
wife, and she, the thing being found out, wished it punished, ordered
the seller to be snatched as if to a lion, then a capon to be let out
of the cage, and when all wondered at so ridiculous a thing he ordered
it said through the herald:
/He practiced a fraud and he suffered one./
Then he dismissed the dealer.

Yet while Odaenathus was occupied with the Persian war, and Gallienus, as
usual, was brooding over the silliest affairs, the Scythians, having built
ships, reached Heraclea and from there returned with booty to their own soil,
although many perished by shipwreck or were overcome in a naval fight.

About the same time Odaenathus was killed by the treachery of his cousin,
together with his son Herodes, whom he had also named emperor. Then Zenobia,
his wife, because the remaining sons, Herennianus and Timolaus, were little
children, herself took up the imperial power and ruled it long, not in a
womanish way nor after a woman's manner, but not only more bravely and more
skillfully than Gallienus, than whom any girl could have ruled better, but
even than many emperors.

Gallienus, indeed, when it was announced to him that Odaenathus had been
killed, prepared a war against the Persians for a vengeance of his father that
was far too late, and, having gathered soldiers through the general
Heraclianus, he was conducting the business of a skillful prince. Yet this
Heraclianus, when he had set out against the Persians, was defeated by the
Palmyrenes and lost all the soldiers he had prepared, while Zenobia was ruling
the Palmyrenes and most of the Easterners in a manly way.

Meanwhile the Scythians, sailing through the Euxine, entered the Danube and
did many grave things on Roman soil. When this was learned, Gallienus put the
Byzantines Cleodamus and Athenaeus in charge of restoring and fortifying the
cities, and there was fighting around the Pontus, and the barbarians were
defeated by the Byzantine generals. Likewise under the general Venerianus the
Goths were overcome in a naval war, though Venerianus himself died a soldier's
death. And from there they laid waste Cyzicus and Asia, then all Achaea, and
were defeated by the Athenians under Dexippus, the writer of these times.
Driven from there they ranged through Epirus, Macedonia, and Boeotia.
Gallienus meanwhile, scarcely roused by the public disasters, met the Goths
wandering through Illyricum and by chance killed a great many. When this was
learned, the Scythians, having made a wagon-fort, tried to flee over Mount
Gessaces. From there Marcianus all the Scythians with varying fortune of wars

killed, whom many say was Augustus, many Caesar, many neither, which is not

/In the consulship of the emperor Valerian./

family is agreed. Yet there is not enough agreement about the rank, or, as
others have begun to speak, about the majesty.

When Gallienus had therefore been killed there was a huge mutiny of the
soldiers, since, in hope of plunder and public devastation, they said, in
order to stir odium, that an emperor useful to them, necessary, brave, and
effective had been snatched away. Wherefore it was the counsel of the leading
men that the soldiers should be given of that kind by which they are usually
appeased. And so, twenty gold pieces each having been promised through
Marcianus and received (for a supply of treasure was at hand), they entered
Gallienus as a tyrant in the public fasti by military judgment. Thus, the
soldiers being quieted, Claudius, a holy man and justly venerable and dear to
all good men, a friend of the fatherland, a friend to the laws, acceptable to
the senate, well known to the people, received the imperial power.

This was the life of Gallienus, briefly set down by me in writing, a man born
for the belly and for pleasures, who wasted days and nights in wine and
debauchery, who caused the world to be laid waste by nearly twenty tyrants, so
that even women ruled better than he. And that his pitiable ingenuity may not
be passed over: in springtime he made bedchambers of roses, he composed
castles of fruit, he kept grapes for three years, at the height of winter he
produced melons, he taught how new wine might be had through the whole year,
he always offered green figs and fruit fresh from the trees in months not
their own, he always spread golden tablecloths. He made double vessels, and
those of gold. He sprinkled gold-dust on his hair. He often went forth wearing
the radiate crown. He was seen at Rome with a purple cloak and jeweled and
golden brooches, where princes were always seen in the toga. He had a purple
and gilded man's tunic, sleeved as well. He used a jeweled belt. He fastened
jeweled straps, when he called his military boots hair-nets. He banqueted in
public.
He softened the people with largesses. Sitting, he distributed a dole to the
senate. He invited matrons to his council, and to the same women, as they

** Saloninus Gallienus
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h19-h1
:END:

This man was the son of Gallienus, grandson of Valerian, about whom indeed
there is almost nothing worthy of being put into writing, except that he was
born nobly, brought up royally, then killed not for his own cause but for his
father's. About his name there is great ambiguity. For many have handed him
down to history as Gallienus, many as Saloninus. And those who say Saloninus
report that he was so surnamed because he was born at Salonae. Those who say
Gallienus, that he was surnamed with his father's name and that of his
grandfather Gallienus, once a most eminent man in the state. There was, in
short, until now a statue at the foot of the Romulean Hill, that is before the

and from this the custom was drawn, that thereafter they dined with the

seems to have been from elsewhere. They say that the military lunch, which was
called parandium from the fact that it prepares soldiers for war, was begun by
men still belted. And the proof of this is that even with the emperor dinner
is eaten by men with belts off. I have set these things down for this reason,
because they seemed worthy both of being remembered and of being known.

Now let us pass to the twenty.1 tyrants who 2 arose in Gallienus's times from
contempt of a bad prince. About them a few things must be said briefly. For
most of them are not worthy that a volume should be occupied even with the
names of such men, although some seem to have had no small virtue in them, and
even to have benefited the state much.

So various also are the opinions about Saloninus's name, that whoever thinks
he is speaking more truly says he was named from his mother Salonina, whom he
loved to distraction, and he loved a barbarian king's daughter named Pipara.
Wherefore Gallienus with his household always dyes his hair yellow.

About the years of Gallienus and Valerian belonging to the imperial power,
however, the tradition is so uncertain that, although it is agreed they
ruled fifteen years, that is Gallienus had reached the fifteenth, but Valerian
was captured in the sixth, others put it into writing that Gallienus ruled
nine years, others scarcely ten, although it is agreed that the ten-year
festival was celebrated by him at Rome and that after the ten-year festival
the Goths were defeated by him, that peace was made with Odaenathus, that
concord was entered with Aureolus, that there was fighting against Postumus,
against Lollianus,
and that many things also were done by him which belonged to valor, yet more
which belonged to disgrace. For he is said always to have frequented cookshops
at night and to have lived with pimps, mimes, and jesters.
