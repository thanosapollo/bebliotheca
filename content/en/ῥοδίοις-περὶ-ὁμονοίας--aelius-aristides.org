If I had my body, men of Rhodes, as I would have wished and you would join in
praying, I myself would have crossed over and come into speech with you for
many reasons; but since perhaps even to you word has come of how we are
disposed, for my own affairs I leave it to the gods, through whom I have also
been saved to this time; but toward you what remained was to send the book,
and in this way to be together for the first time. And reasonably I would not
be thought a busybody in counting your city in the portion of a fatherland.
For of Greeks seeming to rank not among the last, to think it belongs to him
to care for the whole Hellenic race is no vain reckoning, and of the private
claims that exist for me toward you you yourselves have not forgotten; in
return for which, thinking it right both to say and to do anything whatsoever

on your behalf I would justly meet with pardon. In what way then I was
affected at the disaster concerning the earthquake, and what sort of man I
showed myself to those of you who went as ambassadors to Egypt in those times,
you would best learn from them. But when things much more terrible, if it is
possible to say so, than the present were announced to me, that you distrust
yourselves and have been divided and are stirring disturbances that do not
become you, I had no way either how I ought to believe or how to disbelieve.
For those announcing were your own citizens and otherwise among the
trustworthy, and it was not possible readily to accept such charges against
you. If then Terpander of Lesbos were still alive, I would have joined in
praying that he come to you; but since one must settle the affairs by
persuading with bare speech, I make the contest my own. First then consider
this, that concerning any other matter it is possible even to go through the
opposing arguments, but concerning concord no one ever disputed that it is not
the greatest of goods for cities. But concerning the constitutions themselves
and the laws in them each think separately, yet that in every constitution
nothing is more contrary than faction nor anything that more ruins the
established things, this as if with one voice has prevailed. And yet how is it
not strange that all agree about this very thing, that concord is noble and
saving, but having neglected it they think it right to strive against one
another, and that too without end, and that things which none of the other
Greeks or barbarians can well be ignorant of, these the Rhodians should have
escaped?

Nor indeed is it human to claim to know, yet willingly to neglect salvation.
And I think I would most gladly be reproached for this, that I speak ancient
things and have discovered nothing new whatsoever. For how is it not strange
to blame the speaker as advising things too well known and old and seeming
good to all, yet yourselves not to dare to use what is so evident, but not
only to be factiously disposed toward yourselves, but even to be at variance
with all time up to this? But I think that neither the counselor nor those who
use him ought to look to this, the one how he will say those things which no
one,

and the others how they will hear those things which they have not before, but
whatever is going to benefit in common, this both to choose to say. For not
even in the needs of the body has each of us been eager that he should hear
something new, but this man is the best physician who knows how to make people
healthy; nor is there anyone of you who will be angry if he is saved through
the same things through which someone already even before. Nevertheless I at
least would wish, just as even here I think I have it in me to gratify you by
being able to say even something more than those before us who have attempted
similar things, so also that you should be willing to gratify yourselves and
not only during this time in which you now hear this speech to think the same
about what is said, [but also in what follows] (for you will clearly obey
it, I well know your dexterity and love of hearing) but also concerning your
relations with one another to use one mind for the rest and to add the proof
in deed that you were pleased with the speech. I think you would all agree
that, just as of the speeches in the lawcourts you hold those most true of
which there are the most and the most notable witnesses, so also of those that
come for counsel, those for which there are the most and the most serious
witnesses, these one must especially trust. This then refer to Homer, the
common counselor and champion of the Greeks, who made the wisest of the Greeks
of that time praying together with Nausicaa while she was still a girl that
the gods grant them husband and house and like-mindedness; /for nothing is
stronger and better than this, than when a man and woman keep a house,
like-minded in thoughts; many pains for ill-wishers, and joys for
well-wishers; and they themselves hear it most./ And yet do not think that one
house would be well inhabited in one way, and the whole city in another, but
if of the houses one by one the salvation of one is to think the same things,
much more must the cities be so, by as much as when one house is divided a
cure is easily found, but of a city it does not even become easy to find; and
of one house doing well and worse

there is little benefit to all, but when they flourish in common it is likely
that they also do better in their private houses. If then even for those about
to inhabit a house the counselor already thought it the season to pray
together concerning concord, surely those who from of old inhabit a city, and
this one so illustrious, must count all else second to this.

Well then. Let us divide it three ways, and having first considered one house
from a city, then let us go up in the argument to one man and examine of what
sort he is to whom one would not be ashamed to be likened. But here we would
especially also see the good of concord that has escaped. For who is so
outside of sense or who so inexperienced of things in nature that he would
prefer for use the man who changes at random and easily and never remains in
the same nor keeps what he has decided, rather than the man who is good and
steadfast through to the end? For example who would wish either to make a
marriage-alliance with such a one before the other, or to contract, or to
share a tent, or any of the things common to all, as many dealings as there
are among men to share in, whoever does not even himself know what he wants,
but like Euripus is carried up and down, warring and being at faction with
himself? Therefore the opposite of this man wins with all the judges, whoever
is simple, noble, truthful, knowing the same things about the same things,
being of one mind with himself as much as possible. How then is it not
strange, two men being set forth, the one according to the city at faction,
the other as the city of one mind ought to be, that while blaming the one as
incurable, and setting the other as best, they should not preserve the likely,
but the one whom someone would pray not to meet, how must they appear
imitating this man in common affairs? And indeed no reproach so belongs to
private persons to flee as to a city in common. For by as much as the figure
is greater and by as much as those who will know it together are more, by so
much more it is likely to be clean of all shameful things. And apart from
these the danger is not even similar. For a private person not standing in his
judgment becomes no more his own murderer, but is penalized only so far as to
seem to be base; but a city divided first bears a double folly,

then what it does it is shown suffering, and it pays the penalty upon the
affairs themselves. Which things I think Hesiod also, foreseeing, forbids all
strifes from afar, whichever does not call to work; reasonably. For there is
no iron, no palisade, no weapon at all fearful to cities, if only they are
willing to be of good sense; but even these things through folly most people
make strong against themselves. The other things then, when overlooked, make
the penalty timely and it has not been missed by all the votes, but to turn
against one another and, dissolving this settlement that came to be for common
salvation and tearing their own things apart, for each to think they are
setting their own well, this lifts all human folly; so much does it exceed.

And indeed I think I would not be doing out of season even in mentioning
Solon, especially also where there is an examination of laws and constitution.
He then in the elegies, going through the things he had done as a statesman,
prides himself most of all upon this, upon mixing the people with the
powerful, so that they might inhabit the city with one mind, neither being
stronger than is of common benefit. And yet this is not only of one exhorting
to concord, but also of one showing how it is fitting to acquire it. And what
need is there to speak of each? For there is no poet, no lawgiver, no orator,
who, so to speak, begins from anywhere else, nor champions anything else than
friendship and trust toward one another; reasonably and necessarily. For if of
allies those are most trustworthy and most necessary who would think the same
man enemy and friend, surely to look upon themselves as enemies is not saving.
For whom of those outside is it possible even to use for those who do not have
themselves? And one would know even judging by the name, for this alone at
least of a city's diseases has been so called. And yet how is it not strange
to have such forethought of the ailments in bodies, which ruin some part, as
to cease as quickly as possible, but of those that destroy the cities publicly
not to seek the swiftest riddance,

but how the most possible will come to be, and of the involuntary things to
ask the gods for turnings-away, such as famine and plague and such things, but
those which harm more than these, yet it is possible to heal by being of good
sense, not to wish these? Will you then say that you do not even pray for
concord? What other good then will you pray together for yourselves? But by
Zeus you do this eagerly? Then it is strange to trouble the gods, but
yourselves not to wish to do the things which even to the gods themselves seem
to be in your power. And indeed if for bodies those of the diseases are
heaviest and need most care which would touch the inward parts, how must one
not also think these to be for a city the last of misfortunes by which it is
destroyed within? Or do we judge enemies who have come inside the wall more
fearful, but ourselves warring against one another in the middle of the city
shall we think it some small thing? And indeed I shall not even accept that
argument, that civil faction is by so much harder than war, by as much as war
is than peace. For I see war often turning out more choiceworthy than peace,
but faction never than concord. For having made war it has already benefited
many both toward acquisition of money and toward glory and toward strength and
all that one might say, but using faction and hatred toward one another none
ever yet enjoyed any of the goods that exist, so far were they from acquiring
others besides. To speak concisely, when peace exists it is by so much worse
to be at faction than to be stirred to war, by as much as it is worse to do
oneself harm than others. And in war this same thing is by so much worse, by
as much as war is than peace. And indeed it is not that it now seems, but
formerly did not seem, to be the last of evils. For tyranny is by so much more
moderate than faction, by as much as first one man doing a city harm is
lighter than all in a circle, then no one of good sense ever yet thought it
right to free a city so as to be at faction, but for the sake of not being at
faction some have already deliberately set up tyrants. And with a tyrant even
some of the lawgivers themselves have already said to bring in laws,

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

but that a constitution should either first be composed with faction or remain
with it has never yet seemed to anyone to be possible. And indeed some of the
cities already, being under tyrants, advanced both in size and in equipment,
but those who were not destroyed by faction we would not be able to find. And
yet how is it likely to have proclaimed gifts for those who kill the tyrants,
but for those who put down the factions not even to wish so much as to be
persuaded?

You then pride yourselves as being free and praise the democracy so that you
would not even accept to become immortal, if someone will not allow you to
remain in this constitution. And yet how is it not unreasonable so to honor
it, but not to be willing to live with it while insulting it, and if someone
should construct a monarchy, to be bitter, but what has been shown harder than
this by as many things as I have said, this to increase willingly, and not
even to be able to calculate that there is no way, if these things proceed
thus, that you will not be in danger of being deprived of this seeming
freedom; and if you do not turn of your own will, another will come who will
save you by force. For neither is it a law for the rulers to be ignorant of
such things nor to make no account of them. So that if for no other sake, at
least for being free to do what you wish, stand off from these present things,
in order that you may not fear at once as much as you now are confident and
may not throw away an ancient ambition. It is then of the time that has gone
by to enjoy this, using the most notable examples for the present, and these
things to go through toward some others would perhaps have been boyish, but
toward you who are purely Greeks and have been reared from children in these
things it is not useless. This then look to the city of the Lacedaemonians,
being of the same tribe as you, which while it was at faction was better than
none of its neighbors, but even warring was defeated, as it is said, for the
most part; but when it was settled through the words and laws of Lycurgus, it
led the Greeks both by cities and in common. And again the things of the

Athenians consider, who being at faction were able to be useful neither to
themselves nor to others, but when having been freed of the tyrants they came
to concord with one another, they freed not only the city but also Greece in
the greatest crises. And having taken the empire, as long as they had one
mind, they held out against almost all men both by land and by sea, but when
they were divided, they razed the walls and handed over the ships. And the
people, having suffered this so great misfortune, having been driven out and
having returned again, though it was possible for it to take vengeance on
those who had risen up and against whom they had drawn up, chose to be of one
mind, making the common salvation of the city prior to the private complaints.
And so indeed it came back again into the affairs, and stood at the head of
the Greeks of almost all in turn. So great an evil is faction for cities and
so great a good to be willing to cease as quickly as possible. And if one must
also recall your ancestors the Argives, it is said that these too, having been
disturbed among themselves, when ambassadors of the Athenians came to them and
taught how they themselves had set their own things, obeyed and having put the
faction out of the way came again to the same. Now then, men of Rhodes, you
must think that from all these cities a common embassy has come to you calling
you to the settlements, and that we speak as advocates joining in asking what
we think is in every way best for you. And indeed you must not do the same as
the Athenians nor jointly pay with those who borrowed against you so many
talents as a sign of the trust, but the temperance is set forth without
penalty for those who take to heart these things I have said and no longer
look to one city or a second, but to the common affairs of the Greeks and all
the Greek cities, for whom neither phalanxes of hoplites opposite nor a
multitude of horsemen nor anything else of the things that seem to be fearful,
but the faction against one another and distrust and neither thinking the same
in common nor again being sound each by each became the cause of so great a
change. And let none of you in security

make the times being other, but let him take to heart that common thing
against all faction, that it is always by nature to deprive of the present
goods. And apart from these, men of Rhodes, the disaster is also greater, if
in such times in which there is no fear nor danger, but it is possible to
enjoy as much prosperity as one wishes, you will deliberately let go the part
that falls to you as if of a theoric fund. For as long as the affairs of the
Greeks were divided, it was likely that it would also benefit, and that some
of you should choose these things, others those. But now what either beginning
of faction, or not an abundance of ease? Is not all the earth common, and one
king, and laws common to all, and to take part in politics and to be silent
and to set out and to remain as much license as one wishes? Why then need of
imported evils, or of madness superfluous for all?

But indeed it is not as the last of evils that one must show faction most
hard, but how you will be rid of it, this perhaps is the work to find. For you
do not seem to me to err so much by ignorance of the advantageous, as, if it
is possible to say so, by a certain desperation and willingly being defeated
by the differences. I say then as a summary, first that all anger go out of
the way as if a siege, having its own things; since affairs are not judged
most finely by spirit. Then to take away envy and greed from both sides; envy
I mean of the lesser toward the greater, and greed of the greater toward the
weaker; and to speak concisely, to imitate the figure and type of a house.
What then is this? There are rulers in a house, fathers of children and
masters of slaves. How then do these inhabit well? When the rulers do not
think everything is permitted to themselves, but willingly take away from the
authority, and the others receive whatever seems good to the stronger as being
permitted. And without this good-sense from both sides it is not easy to find
a house which will be saved. This then I at least think one must now also
bring upon the common affairs of the city, the ones claiming to be stronger
calculating that whatever

they willingly destroy the weaker they ruin by their own ambition. For it is a
gain to the stronger that the weaker exist, if indeed they will have those of
whom they will show themselves stronger, and those left behind either in
properties or according to the rest of fortune, that if they insult the
greater they ruin their own salvation much more than if they willingly took
down the wall. For this is a law truly laid down by nature, shown by the
stronger, that the weaker listen to the stronger. And if someone makes it a
symbol of freedom to destroy the law, he deceives himself and does something
similar as if all men in common having envied the gods then should not believe
in them. And if any of you, because he has suffered many and terrible things
before taking justice, thinks it intolerable to settle, first he is disposed
the opposite way for himself. For if faction becomes the cause of so many and
so great evils that one thinks it unlivable unless someone in any way
whatsoever will take justice, how is it not best to flee from it? For it
belongs to those of good sense of the things they have experienced as
gladdening not to wish to stand off, but of those they have found so hard and
turbulent and everywhere full of unpleasantness to cease as quickly as
possible, not thinking the evil the drug of the evil, but thinking memory and
forgetfulness two opposite names of two opposite things, and that one must
mark the useful things by memory, but strike out the base by forgetfulness.
Then whenever the spirit altogether also stands against, taking on his ancient
adversary, reason, one must speak thus to themselves: we are Rhodians, and if
any of Greeks or barbarians disputes with us about nobility of birth, we would
wish to prevail over him. Therefore since we wish this, our fatherland must be
saved in common, in order that we may have that on which we may pride
ourselves. Therefore of those at faction it is destroyed, but of those who
have used one mind it becomes at once more beautiful and greater. Why then do
we put ourselves down? These things if each one should be willing to say to
himself, he will not give

room to the provocations. For how is it not truly strange to be in the same
way toward all the speeches on behalf of the fatherland, if it should be
needed, but for the very saving of the fatherland and to be able to use the
fair-speaking of the speeches about it not to dare to think the same, but in
deed to destroy, and in speech to adorn, and to wait for the victory of
Cleomenes the Laconian, who gutted his own body beginning from the feet? Or
what by the gods do you think, if each having set this limit before himself,
whenever he has put the enemies out of the way, then to be temperate? Is
anything else left than that of so many men and women one man and one woman be
left, as they say those from the flood? And how will you differ from the women
who tore Pentheus apart, whenever you yourselves tear apart the common body of
the city with your own hands? But those who have suffered must give the things
that have happened to some season and fortune, and think no gain greater than
this, and those again who have done the deeds must be ashamed of what they
have done, not pride themselves, and by having willingly begun the concord
think it sufficient to loose the former things, setting as the wage of those
things not to pay the penalty, thinking they are receiving it.

And I think there are so many evils in faction that concerning any other thing
if someone should wish to turn away, it is fitting to say as many as would
harm, but concerning this the whole contrary it is profitable rather to be
silent than to move the memory of what each have suffered, whoever is not
going to do the opposite of what he wishes. But the speeches which someone
would have to say on behalf of the other, of concord, are already countless,
yet one must imitate the enchanters, who uttering the same voices many times
thus seem to accomplish. For she alone confirms the seasons from Zeus, and she
alone seals all things, the things in the country adorning with farmings and
giving both fruits of what exists and acquisition of others, and the things in
the city leading according to prayer, through whom both marriages in season
and giving in marriage and taking into those one wishes and from where one
wishes, and rearings of children and education according to the ancestral
laws, and security concerning women and trusts

of contracts, and receptions of strangers and services of gods and processions
and choruses and delights, and further assemblies and council-chambers, which
the eldest of gods, Themis, gathers, and for the poor beginnings of life and

willingly to stand off from so many goods, as if ruining some others, and not

well-formed and well-compact and of good color and of good grace and agreeing

the other of all spectacles the most bitter, the head thrown back, the lips
livid, the eyes turned away, ruined, swollen, always held by newly-sprinkled
tears, the hands without power, bringing the sword inward toward the breast,
swaying upon thin and crooked feet, and about her as a net in a circle gloom
and darkness, under which for the most part she dwells in tombs instead of
temples. Looking toward these things one must thus make the choice, judging at
leisure with which it is better to live. And indeed for all men it is worth
the whole to be in earnest concerning concord; and by how much for you
especially of the others there is not much need to show, who are of old
Dorians from the Peloponnese, and alone Greeks through all to this time, and
have used Heraclids and Asclepiads as founders and kings. And besides these
your city is the most beautiful of the Greek ones through all its equipment.
And yet it is terrible that of beautiful bodies even for those of others there
is more forethought, but that you

should not spare your own beautiful things. And consider how much ambition the
things said by the poets toward you have for you, of which I, passing over the
speeches, will mention the testimony of Homer alone. And how does this have
it? /who dwelt about Rhodes arranged in three parts, Lindos and Ialysos and
white Camirus./ I myself am already stung toward the saying, considering how
unworthily of it we are disposed, being led now with no order. And the rest of
the testimony of what sort? /and they were settled in three parts by tribes
and were loved from Zeus, who rules over gods and men./ Consider that the
poet, having spoken well of almost all things toward the Greeks, dedicated the
greatest of all to you. Whether then being at faction and disturbed and hating
yourselves would you think more to be dear to the gods and to confirm the
things from the poet, or leading the city through good order and concord?

Well then. And being dear to the gods whether would you think more to be at
faction and to be disturbed and to live with the curses of enemies, or the
contrary to fare as the prayers of the temperate, to honor the gods, and to
recognize one another, and to flourish in all things? Therefore since through
both faction appears alien to you and hateful to the gods, and concord alone
saving, it is fitting I suppose as in an account of money to accept what
follows. And indeed that too is not reasonable, that your ancestors, having
formerly been divided in three, came together to the same for the sake of
concord and trust toward one another and inhabited one city from all, but that
you from one city should show many by having been divided. And it would be an
excess, if dwelling in three you were shown thinking one and the same, but
being together you will war against one another. Are you not ashamed before
Helios, who for the others is a spectator of what happens, but of you is also
founder? Will not each of you think the present day this to be the first for
the island of its birth, when it was coming up from the sea as a gift for the
god? Will he not take thought how he may not of the

temple-robbers appear more base, ruining the lot of the god? Will he not
having raised his hands up with shame and fear ask the gods pardon of what has
been erred up to this, but as in a moonless night do you perish inhabiting the

indeed no chorus so out of tune is so untimely a spectacle as the people of
the Rhodians not uttering the same. Well then. And those who pursued the
pirates and adorned the dockyards with the rams and those great triremes,
which we saw before the earthquake, by the gods if they fought not against
Tyrrhenians nor against the pirate ships, but against themselves ship by ship,
would they ever have ruled the sea, or have brought these ornaments into the
city, or have left it to those from themselves to take pride in these deeds?
But, I think, it would have been about salvation for them first. Then in a
ship faction is not saving, but in a city and this one sea-girt to destroy one
another yoked together is temperate, or for Rhodians to imitate the evils of
the Lesbians and the Mytileneans? But it was much better that those be seen
desiring your good order than that you endure to become imitators of their
misfortune. And yet one would have things fitting also to say toward those and
reaching them: men of Lesbos, where have you been carried? who

say that the whole island is musical for you and you blame the head of Orpheus

for those, as it seems, this unreasonable thing of the misfortune has been
