But, dearest Heracles, to praise you is the sweetest of labors; and in every
way you are much-hymned. For many are those who sing your deeds in prose, and
poets have hymned many things in every manner; yet greatest is the daily
praise from all, always arising on every occasion that presents itself. And it
is no wonder at all that he so far surpassed human nature, he whose father is
first among beings, and whose mother is she whom that father preferred above
all. And he took such care over his begetting that for three days and nights
in succession, as they say, he consorted with her, wishing to deposit into the
seed as much of his own nature as possible, and that most exactly. For the
counsel was concerning the whole, that the affairs of men might be set in
order and earth and sea made productive. And so Heracles is born,
fellow-worker with his father and viceroy of all under the pole of the moon,
and the signs followed at once. For when the serpents came upon him in his
swaddling-clothes, instead of any other motion of the hand he destroyed them;
and while still a child, instead of any other play, he freed the Thebans from
the tribute which they had already for a long time to the Orchomenians

were paying. And beginning from his own hearth he purified Greece, and then
also the common race of men, going against all in succession, both those in
the islands and those on the mainland, omitting nothing that tended to
benefaction, but mastering the wild beasts, by whose number and size most of
the country was uninhabitable, and destroying the tyrants each in the manner
that fitted him, and bringing the cities to temperance, some by laws and some
by arms, and utterly destroying land-robbers and sea-robbers and all who,
trusting in bodily strength, committed outrage against the weaker, alike in
Greece and in the barbarian land. For he counted all things hostile to nature
as his own enemies, and he alone of those who have come to earth legislated
for the common good of all, not only for men but even, so to speak, for
beasts, he who even found how to drive out the birds around Stymphalus that
were destroying most of Arcadia, as if it were his part to free not only earth
and sea but the air as well. And further, whatever was oppressed by rivers'
streams or by lakes he made dry land, and whatever in turn needed cleansing by
water, by sending in the rivers he made it not only bearable to look upon but
also productive. And so he worked earth and sea and rivers and lakes and air
and rocks and men and cities, blending laws with arms, so that of his dominion
there is nothing either more splendid or more profitable to use. For by
unbreakable force nothing of what was entrusted to him escaped him, and by
excess of justice all things came about for the benefit of the common race.
And from these things poets composed both Prometheuses being loosed by him, as
if whatever Zeus bound, it was possible for Heracles to loose, and Atlas, how
he relieved him by taking over and himself holding up the heaven, and
Cerberus, how he brought him up from Hades and with him Theseus of the
Erechtheids, and how he wounded Pluto and Hera, and the Giants, how he
mastered them, becoming ally to the gods; and these things, I think,

mean to say by way of hyperbole that Heracles searched through all earth and
all sea, and came to every boundary and every farthest edge, and neglected
neither the things below the earth nor those as far as heaven, but so greatly
did he come in season for all men that even the gods had need of Heracles to
set their own affairs in order. And it seems to me he was also first to mark
off the outer sea against the inner, betrothing this whole region to the
Greeks as their own to settle, and that this is the meaning of the pillars
which even now we name of Heracles.

And not only by following what is said by the Greeks would one know his nature
and that he became greater than the measure of men, but we know how great a
god the Egyptians hold Heracles, and that the Tyrians worship him first of the
gods. And yet if all, thinking him the same, honor him thus, what greater
token of his power could one name? But if some think him one of the oldest
gods, and we have deemed this one, as like to that one, worthy of the same
honor, even so he is shown to have become greater than a man. And it is clear
also from the god's oracles. For when Heracles departed from among men,
purified in the manner that is told, the god at once directed that temples of
Heracles be founded and that they sacrifice to him as to a god; and this,
moreover, addressing the city of the Athenians, which both is the oldest of
the Greek cities and was as it were leader to all in piety toward the gods and
in the other most serious things. And further she had also very many tokens of
friendship with Heracles, among them that she first initiated him as a
stranger, while he was still among men. And so clear was the zeal, and so
strongly was his affair judged of the better portion, that even all the
Theseia that were in the demes they converted and established as Heracleia
instead of Theseia, thinking Theseus the best of the citizens, but Heracles
above human nature.

But why should one say the many things? For even now the movement of the god
is clear. On the one hand, as we hear, around Gadeira he works such wonders,
and is held second to none of all the gods; on the other, in Messene of Sicily
he releases from all diseases, and those who have escaped dangers at sea
ascribe the benefaction equally to Poseidon and to Heracles. And one could
also recount many other places and sanctuaries of the god and manifest powers.
But what need to speak of things far off? Our headquarters itself seems to
belong to Heracles. And in this he has already often been seen playing with
certain Heraclean balls. These are round stones of no small weight. A clash of
them is heard, and carrying them from one place to another he sets them down.
Marvelous too are the things of his other appearing; so that although the
headquarters is so public, by the exact observances concerning the god it has
stood in place of an inner shrine. And what would you say of the far-famed
invocation, in comedies and tragedies and all speeches, even now almost
everyone crying "O Heracles" at every thing that stands in need, this indeed
the saying of Sophocles: is it not the greatest reminder and token of his
justice and power, and that he stood over human nature, leading all toward the
best? For which reasons those who lived in his time also called upon him for
everything, and now the word has remained from the ancient custom. Then the
epithets Callinicus and Alexikakos: the one is given to him alone of the gods,
the other among the first. And the Coans, as I remember, even hold Heracles as
Alexius. And among them Heracles stands, from an oracle, with the club raised
on his back, as being master even to bring the heaven into due measure. Nor
then would one have only so much to say about the god, of which some things,
when he was among men, and some even now he still appears to do of himself.
But the begetting of his children would also be among the first; whom he made
common saviors of Greece and heirs of his own

polity. Of whom some, remaining, adorned the Peloponnese, and others advanced
Italy and Sicily to so great a form. And others, having crossed into Asia,
both settled and administered the Dorian cities there. And the city of the
Lacedaemonians too seems to have become a kind of image of Heracles, as to
liken small to great; and very reasonably. For the Leonidases and the
Leotychidases and Archidamuses and Agesilauses and Agises, and before these
Lycurgus who set them their laws, all these are of Heracles' outflow, who
accustomed those men, though few, to be instead of many, and to stand over all
in need without pay, and a Spartiate general to do more than an army of
others. Thus both through himself and through his descendants he contrived, as
it were, springs of virtue for the Greeks, and prepared some to practice the
finest things, and others to have those by whom they would be saved. Nor then
would one have Heracles to remember only in deeds and contests, but even in
the cheer of life he is of much worth. And the drinking statues of him make
this clear. And he received a wondrous portion from all the gods, he whom Zeus
brought into light, and Athena taking him over watched over him and directed
the labors, and Aphrodite and Dionysus welcomed him and worthily bestowed the
rests from toils. The kinship with Hera and the marriage of Hebe are ancient
to tell. For so much, as it seems, of vigor was allotted him that, though all
gods are unaging through eternity, Heracles especially seems to have youth.
And indeed there are now common statues of Hermes and Heracles; to such a
point of companionship have they come. And you might see Heracles also in the
midst of mountains beside the Mother of the Gods, and in cities, and again
with the Dioscuri. And of baths the sweetest have the name Heraclean; and
besides, springs of river waters are themselves named after the god. So great
among the Nymphs

has he received the presidency. And if the opinion of the Thasian stranger, or
rather Macedonian, is right, who said that once he seemed to sing a paean
composed by me, and that this was the refrain for it, "Ie Paean, Heracles,

his due season. To all men then Heracles is in memory and honors, and I have
also something private of friendship toward him, from a certain divine voice
that came. And it seemed to come from the Metroon. And it exhorted me to bear
what befalls, since even Heracles, being son of Zeus, endured. This, dear
Heracles, is the speech from us, sung in place of another song according to
the seeming of the dream, in which I seemed to speak a praise of Heracles in
the forecourts of Apollo.
