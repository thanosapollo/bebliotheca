The things you asked me the other day about the Nile: since I answered you
briefly and on the surface, and at the same time those who came in took me
away, I wished to take it up and go through it for you and, as if paying some
other debt, to render the whole account. And these things too will be said as
shortly as possible. For since, having gone even as far as the Ethiopian
country and having examined Egypt itself four times in all, and having left
nothing uninvestigated, not pyramids, not the labyrinth, not a temple, not
canals, but having supplied from the books the measures of those things for
which they were in the books, and having measured myself, with the priests and
prophets in each place, those that could not be taken ready to hand, I was
then unable to preserve these things for you, the notes having been destroyed
which I had ordered the slaves to make: this one small question at least I
would be able to discharge, how the Nile rises and what the occasion is that
it has suffered the opposite of the other rivers about the seasons of the
year. You remember, then, that even then I answered at once that it is likely
no one has anything clear to say about the Nile, but they talk nonsense at
random, some insisting on what seems good to themselves, others not even
themselves very much convinced, pretending for the sake of a pose before the
many, so that they may seem to know something about the obscure. And I will go
through it for you now as well in this way: not the cause by which this
happens, but that it is not because of those things which each of them says.

It is necessary also to mention some of the things which Herodotus, speaking
against them, refutes, first that it is not the etesian winds, checking the
stream, that make the flood. For if the

the notable ones. For if again they will say that the continuity of the
etesian winds pushes the Nile back, I pass over that the winds I mentioned
have lasted countless times for no less a time, but the Nile is not filled
when the etesian winds are at mid course, nor when they are near ending, so
that we might grant the excess to time, but both when they begin and often
before they begin. So the continuity of the blasts would not be the cause,
things which have been set in motion before they exist. And indeed the etesian
winds do not blow entirely in the mouth of the Nile, but toward the eastern
bank; the majority of them, at any rate, are west winds, I suppose. And these
stretch from the west toward the rising sun. He cannot then be checked because
of the etesian winds, and if because of these, why in the world do not all
suffer it who flow the same way as the Nile? And besides, the argument of the
etesian winds already becomes a joke. For if we accept this account, we must
already believe that too, that east winds blowing stop the Eridanus and as
many as meet the Ionian in the same way; and when west winds blow the Rhine
flows inward and does not pass through to the outer sea; and of those about
the north, those toward the south fill, and of south winds those toward the
north in the same way; and thus what we seek the cause of, why in the world it
happens in one only, we shall add to all in a circle. And how is it not
monstrous, or rather wholly forgetful, to seek why the Nile of all, and to
declare all suffering the same from the same things? And indeed if no other

And indeed this too is clear, that the Nile is not among the poorest of
rivers, nor so easy to handle beyond all that the mouths of this one alone are
checked by the winds and change. For when not even the smallest are shut off
completely, but have an outflow, how is it likely that the Nile, which is able
to flood all Egypt, does this as one worsted by the winds? And we see that
neither the shores of the sea nor as many shallows and lakes as there are, not
to say so great a thing, are held in by winds, at least the many and usual
ones, but I will not say how much they go out of their own place; but waves
that have washed up recede, and shallows remain still. And yet things on the
level are easier to be tilted than things flowing toward the downslope to be
turned about. Nor indeed can one say this, that the etesian winds strike these
places most strongly; but whether one must grant them to be west winds or
north winds or whatever they are, they fall more fiercely and more violently,
I suppose, on those nearer and facing them. When none of these suffers the
same, how is it likely that the Nile alone does, which besides being furthest
from the run of the etesian winds is so much so also in its current? And
further it is wholly shameless. For it is not that they claim the etesian
winds furnish the cause, and for this reason the Nile does not go out into the
sea, but if one comes to the mouths themselves he will appear everywhere
flowing bright in bright etesian winds. This account ought no longer to be
spoken by those who know even a little about the Nile. So of the absurdities,
not to see what is at one's feet and to dispute about things outside, and to
bring the clear under the obscure, rather than confirm those by these, if it
is possible. All the mouths then, whether the sophists say so or not, go out
(and this is judged by eyes, not by speech), and not for a short way like
this, nor for as far as those of the other and many rivers, but as far as it
is likely the greatest of rivers to reach, and again much more in summer than

Let us go now to the opinion of the many and the clever men who have made it;
who say that it rains about the upper places when there are etesian winds, and
the clouds driven from us burst there, and from the rains the Nile, being
filled, is reasonably greater in summer than in winter. You must then learn
the whole of what happens about the rise. For the Nile, when it is the due
season for it, comes down the heights, and it comes down not with a visible
addition nor so that the eye may know the water being brought on, but
beginning from a few fingers it increases so much that in little short of four
months it fills these four or fifteen cubits about Memphis, and in this always
making the addition obscure and signaling by this one thing, by occupying the
land in succession. Why then do I say this? Because they say that just as the
rivers among us increase from the rains of winter, so the Nile in summer from
the rains. If then this were true, first it ought to appear coming down
suddenly raised. For just as when a stream is carried over land or solid
ground, so the water from the rains runs on top of the old, arched. Why then
does this same thing not also happen about the Nile? For one must not, I
suppose, at once refer it to the other rivers and judge by the same signs, and
at once not use the same signs on both. For if it once happened so, there
would be an account, but if it is always raised in this way, what would they
say? First then and greatest, this sign which I mention,
