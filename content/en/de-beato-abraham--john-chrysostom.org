Homily on the Blessed Abraham.
=50.737=
Did you see gray hair in vigor, and old age in its prime? Did you see a noble
athlete drawn up against nature and the bowels? Did you see a man in extreme
old age harder than adamant? The tone of his flesh had slackened, but the tone
of his faith had been sinewed. For such are the achievements of the Church,
that the slackness of the body in no way harms the eagerness of faith; for the
ornament of the Church is gray hair composed, and faith on the wing, and in
this the Church rather rejoices. For in outward affairs the old man is
useless, fit for nothing, but everywhere enjoys pardon, being unable through
slackness to serve the necessary things. What I mean is this: The old man
cannot draw up for war, cannot mount a horse, cannot move a spear, cannot
shake a shield, cannot bear the heat of the sun, cannot endure the length of a
journey, cannot endure the terror of famine, nor the bulk of tumults; but he
sits in a quiet place, having his gray hair as advocate. And this one may see
also in the case of ships: The old man cannot sit at the tillers, cannot cut
the seas, cannot pull an oar, cannot hang a sail, cannot draw up against
contrary winds, cannot endure the sharpness of cold, nor can he do any other
such thing; but he sits in the ship, having the pardon that belongs to his
age. And this one may see also in the case of husbandmen: for he can neither
draw a plow, nor cut a furrow, nor become a horse-breaker, nor bear the
strength of strong oxen, nor the war of burning heat, nor the bulk of heat,
nor the weight of the mattock, nor anything else of the things that serve the
earth; but he sits in the house, having his gray hair as advocate. But the
things of the Church are not such; rather, when those who live in virtue grow
old, then they become more useful; for it is not vigor of flesh that is
sought, but intensity of faith. Such was Abraham, slackened in the tone of the
flesh, but sinewed in the tone of faith. He was an old man, but in extreme old
age he fought against nature, setting up great trophies of victory; he was an
old man, but more vigorous than iron, and harder than adamant. And when he was
in youth, he accomplished none of these things; but when the time of his age
had run past, then he set up the trophy of victory; he was in extreme old age,
but the gray hair did not become a hindrance. Therefore God also, knowing his
vigor, appears to him, and says: Go out from your land, and from your kindred,
and from your father's house, and come into a land which I will show you.
Abraham obeyed the word, though he was already an old man, and fee =50.738=
ble in body, and he did not say to himself: Where am I going in extreme old
age? Shall I leave my father's house, and the land in which I was born, where
there is abundance of money, and the distinction of parents; where there is
costly possession, and the prosperity of friends? but he was grieved for the
present, yet he did not disobey; for as one fond of his place he was in
anguish at leaving, but as a lover of God he was persuaded and obeyed; and the
wonder, God did not even tell him to what place, but by the indefiniteness of
the name he exercised his choice. For if he had said to him, that I have to
lead you into a land flowing with milk and honey, Abraham would have been
found not to have obeyed the voice of God, but to have preferred land to land.
Abraham went out then, not knowing where he was going. Did you see gray hair
honored in achievements? For such, as I said before, are our things; weakness
of old age does not cast them down, time does not wither them; for the gray
hair is not in bodies, but in the soul; therefore they are unaging. Abraham
went out then having taken nothing, but still dwelling in tents; and he did
not


disbelieve, nor was he in anguish; for he had a trustworthy pledge, the voice
of him who called. He himself was childless, and he was not begetting, nor was
Sarah his wife; but he received Isaac as a son from promise; when nature had
given up, then grace bestowed; he received a trustworthy reward of obedience,
but not knowing this; for if he had known, he would have done nothing
trustworthy. And that you may learn that it was not for this that he obeyed,
see how even when commanded to slay him he does not disobey, but casts nature
aside, and embraces the love of God, tramples the bowels, and does not stand
away from him who called. What then does God say to him? Abraham, Abraham. And
he said: Here am I; and he said: Take your son, the beloved, whom you have
loved, Isaac, and offer him up for a whole-burnt-offering on one of the
mountains, which I will tell you. He did not even declare the mountain to him,
but by the indefiniteness of the mountain he cast him into greater
despondency. But nonetheless none of these things threw Abraham into
confusion; or rather they did throw him into confusion; for he suffered the
things of a man, but he did not suffer the things of sin; he was tossed as a
father, but he was not sunk as a lover of God; the bowels were kindled, but
faith conquered. For do not say that Abraham suffered nothing; consider how he
was set on fire, driven by the bowels, and see his philosophy. He does not
even tell Sarah; for he feared she might hinder the mystery being
accomplished. For if he had spoken, she would likely have stopped his mouth,
and said: Where are you leading up your son, whom I received beyond hope, whom
I accepted according to promise, whom I received in return for hospitality,
whom God granted me in extreme old age? where are you leading him up? where
are you taking him? no one appeared to you =50.739= ? for how could God have
appeared to you, and seek the son whom he gave me beyond hope? he himself
granted him, and he himself takes him? if he gave him in order to take him, it
would have been better if he had not given; for the pain of not having is not
so great as that of receiving and losing. Consider Sarah kindled and set on
fire, and driven by nature, and her womb twisting, and her bowels altered; for
women are compassionate in such things; for as much as she is softer and more
compassionate, so much was she going to contend firmly with Abraham, and the
sacrifice would have been hindered, and the mystery made void. For what would
Sarah not have done to snatch away her son? for she could not have borne what
was happening, the son beyond hope about to be slain, and the one granted her
in that very old age about to be slaughtered, a father's hands about to work
child-murder; Sarah would not have borne such things, but would have made a
great fight with Abraham, and when the fight had come about a snare would
necessarily have followed, and when a snare followed the mystery would have
been hindered. Abraham therefore did not tell his wife, lest contention arise,
lest, contention having arisen, the matter proceed to a fight, lest, the
matter having proceeded to a fight, it advance to a division over the son,
lest, the son advancing to a division, the promise be delayed, lest, the
promise having been delayed, the matter be snared, lest, the matter having
been snared, the mystery remain unfinished.

the athlete, both contending against nature, and boxing against the bowels,
and arming himself against nature, did not disobey, but yielding to the
command of God, he heard the message, and at once takes the son. I gladly
linger over these words: Take your son, the beloved, whom you have loved,
Isaac, and offer him up to me for a whole-burnt-offering on one of the
mountains, which I will tell you; and Abraham, having taken Isaac, and the
ass, and the two servants, went off; then when they had arrived at a certain
place, he says to the servants: Sit, he says, here; but I and the lad will go
yonder, and having worshiped we will return again to you. Unwillingly Abraham
prophesied, the divine grace


cooperating with him. You go up to slay your son, and you say, We will return?
As he seeks to hide it from the servants, he is made a prophet. And he laid
the wood upon Isaac's shoulders, and taking fire and a knife he went up onto
the mountain. And what does Isaac say to the father? Father. And he, What is
it, child? And he says: behold the fire and the wood; where is the sheep for
the whole-burnt-offering? And he to him: God will see for himself a sheep for
a whole-burnt-offering, child. Behold a second prophecy also; I cannot bear
either to pass over the words, or to run past what happened. Consider names
about to be desolated of their realities. Thus that one calls the father, and
that one again the son, and for a season the names were, but they were about
to be stri =50.740= pped. For consider what Abraham suffered, being called by
Isaac, whom he was about to slaughter, father. How were his knees not
shattered? how were his limbs not broken? how did he not suffer these things
in his mind, having heard them from his son Isaac? Nonetheless he offered him
up, and bound Isaac's feet together, and took the knife in his right hand,
that he might slay his son. O that right hand arming itself against the son; I
have no way to set forth in speech what happened. How did the hand not grow
numb? how did the sword not fall from the hand? how was he not wholly
dissolved, and shattered? how did he suffice to stand and see Isaac bound? how
did he not remain suddenly dead? how did the sinews serve him? how did the
mind suffice, I have no way to set forth in speech what happened. As many as
are fathers or mothers, come together, stretch out a hand, help the word; for
I am weak before the facts; pardon me, but you join the fight. Often someone
has five or even six sons and daughters; and when one of them falls sick, the
father goes around the whole bed, kissing the child's eyes, clasping the
hands, counting the day as night, and the light as darkness, not changing the
elements, but by the disgust of grief not taking pleasure in them; beds spread
soft, physicians sitting beside, much is the attendance of the one who is
unwell, the father melts through and through; and if he has abundance of
money, it is accursed to him, and if he has countless cares, he drives them
all away, unable to recover, drunk with grief; and the whole world to him is
the incurable thing of the evil. Likewise the mother also goes around, cut
through, set on fire, and kindled wholly, seeking power, that she may share
the pain; or rather that she may take it all upon herself, that the child who
is suffering may be delivered from the disease. The present life is nothing to
her, the future is nothing to her, but she counts it more precious than all
these to take upon herself the whole disease of the child. I have no way to
set forth this passion. Consider Abraham also suffering the same things, yet
philosophizing, and obeying the command; he was trampling nature, and the
commandment became more powerful than the bowels. For he suffered these
things, as I said before, as a man, but he philosophized as a lover of God.
And one might see Isaac a martyr living and not living, dead and not dead. For
as far as the father's purpose, he died; but as far as God's loving-kindness,
he did not die; for he was a type of the Master and an image; and the type
served its use, and awaits the truth. Then Isaac is bound foot to foot, and is
not sacrificed; for there came from above a voice full of mercy and
loving-kindness, and it holds back the patriarch who is wholly intent and
ready for the slaughter, and it says: Abraham, Abraham, do not lay your hand
upon the lad. Why not upon the child, but Upon the lad? why does he cheapen
him? For he was a man; but there was need of a Son, not of a lad; not of a
slave, but of a true only-begotten Son, sent to us from the Father. Do not lay
your hand upon the lad. You have had enough of the type, await the tru

consider what you are doing, consult with your wife, the partner of the
begetting, who endured the birth-pangs for him; sacrifice us first, and so the
son. But the wise old man does not take along those who would hinder him, but
he makes the victim bear the wood, that he may have the type of the Savior
bearing the cross. And the child, bearing it and speaking, moves the father's
bowels, but does not drive him from the longing toward God. For what does he
say? Father. And consider what sort of voice it was, Father. For we too, when
we are about to sacrifice either a lamb or some other of the animals, and have
seen it bleating, and speaking to us with a quiet voice, we pity, we have
compassion, though it utters an inarticulate voice. Consider then what this
sheep would have done, if it were speaking to a weak man; Father, behold the
fire and the wood, where is the sheep for the whole-burnt-offering? and he
himself, according to the father's mind, was the speaking sheep, stirring the
priest to compassion. For he had not been a disciple of Christ who says: He
who loves father or mother, or son or daughter, more than me, is not worthy of
me. But he was imitating the Father concerning whom it has been said: He who
did not spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all. Where is the
sheep? the child, with understanding. What then does the father say? God will
see for himself a sheep for a whole-burnt-offering, child; not a
household-slave, but a Son. That one an only-begotten, and God an
only-begotten; that one the wood upon the shoulders, and Christ the cross upon
the shoulders; that one, having committed no sin, was about to be slaughtered,
and the Son of God, having committed no sin, was crucified; that one both was
slaughtered and was not slaughtered, and the only-begotten Son of God both was
slaughtered and was not slaughtered, and died and did not die; for as far as
purpose, he died, but as far as the outcome of events, that one did not die;
but the Son of God died in the flesh, but the Godhead remained impassible.
γʹ. For always God, when he is about to administer a great matter among men,
anticipates, and pre-paints its shadow, so that when the truth comes they may
not disbelieve concerning it, so that, the image having come first, they may
not disbelieve the truth. What I mean is this: the all-holy virgin was about
to give birth, and the matter was unbelievable; how will a virgin give birth
contrary to the law of nature? for what has been begotten is according to the
sequence of nature. Who from of old has seen a virgin giving birth? In order
then that the Jews may not go into much unbelief, =50.742= he anticipates and
sketches the truth in Sarah, so that when they are at a loss, how the virgin
gave birth, they may rather, having remembered, say, how did Sarah give birth?
or rather by that word, how did the earth, being a virgin, give birth? There
was no mattock, the one who farms it did not exist, rain did not come down,
herbage did not come up into it, its furrow was not cut, it was fallow, it did
not receive a casting of seeds, a drop did not gladden it. Tell me then, how,
being a virgin, did it sprout the countless kinds of plants? But you cannot
interpret what happened, but you say, as God willed. In the same way then also
in the case of the virgin, since you are at a loss, learn not to be
over-curious when God works; do not ask the things according to nature, when
the craftsman of nature is at work; do not be a busybody. He said, Let the
earth put forth, and at once the word entered the flanks of the earth, and
woke its birth-pangs, and the virgin was giving birth. It was one word that
went forth, and countless kinds of plants sprouted, and the earth was taking
up its own adornment through the word; And one might see the earth billowing,
the winged things, the water-things, the land-things, the meadows, the
flowers, the trees, the vine, the olive, the countless kinds of


trees, the untimely, the ripe, those in plains, those by the sea, those by
lakes, those by rivers, those by springs, those in the mountains. It was one
word that was at work, and the earth was receiving back its own comeliness.
Interpret this for me, O Jew; but you cannot. Do you see how the error is
tripped up by itself? Attend exactly. For as I was saying, when God is about
to administer some matter that seems unbelievable to men, he anticipates the
type and the shadow, lest the truth, having come suddenly, cast them into
unbelief. The Son of God had to come down, and baptize the race of men, and
drown the old man, and sink him who wants to renew sin, and bring the blessing
back, and make the curse vanish, and grant righteousness, and make men angels;
he was about to take an adulterer, and make him a son; which indeed he also
did; one not worthy of earth he was about to take, and make him worthy of
heaven. Since then this wonder was great and unbelievable to the many, how the
same nature drowns and justifies the one man, makes sin vanish, and brings the
truth back; in order then that Jews may not say, the things of the Christians
are a myth, how is it possible that the same water drown the one, and make the
other live? the water is one, and how can it have two operations? What then
does God administer beforehand, that the shamelessness of the Jews may be
convicted? The sons of Israel were being worn down in Egypt, in the works, and
nailed to the brick and to the other toils; and they were crying out weeping
and saying, who will deliver us from the slavery of the Egyptians? we have
perished, we have been utterly destroyed in the wicked works. What then? God
heard them, and sends Moses, having armed him with signs and wonders. Moses
came bearing the punishments against Pharaoh; he brought on an army of wicked
locusts, he commanded the devouring locust, and it arrived; he changed the
water into blood, he made blisters boil up in =50.743= the men and in the
four-footed beasts. Then since after these plagues Pharaoh did not yield, God
at last brings on the death of the firstborn; then, that I may cut the
discourse short (for one must cut it short, lest winding about the same things
we not fulfill the promise), they went out from the land of Egypt, and came
beside the sea. Pharaoh encamped behind them with a multitude of chariots and
horses; and when Israel saw the array, he was afraid. And they came upon the
lip of the sea; and God says to Moses, Why do you cry to me? and yet Moses was
saying nothing. But he says, I am God, not hearing the things from the lips
only, but also knowing the things in the heart, that you may learn that prayer
is not in crying out, but in the mind. Why do you cry to me? For his lips were
not speaking, but the heart was crying out; for prayer is sought from a good
mind, not from a strong cry. Why do you cry to me? Stretch out the rod that is
in your hand upon the water, and pass through, you and all Israel. And Moses
stretched out the rod that was in his hand, and struck the water, and the
disorderly rush of the waters was made dry land, and it forgot its own nature.
For such is the element, when God nods, even these forget their own nature,
and bridle the operation. For like a well-disposed maidservant the sea, having
seen a slave of the Master, was persuaded and obeyed; and it obeyed not from
respect for the dry wood, but because of him who was going to be crucified on
the wood. It saw the image then, and at once conceived the truth, and
withdrew. Pass, he says, you and all the people of Israel. Then he passed, and
all Israel; and the Egyptians also followed, pursuing them, both Pharaoh and
his chariots and the horsemen. Then when the sons of Israel had passed, the
water came on and covered the Egyptians. Tell me then, O Jew, how the water at
baptism has two operations, and drowns the one, and makes the other live? tell
me, how that sea, being one in nature, made some live, and killed others,
drowned some, and became a road for others, and yet the disorderly rush of the
waters was one? and

the cross, how the same water drowned those, and saved these. The water was
