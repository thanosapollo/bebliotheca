OF THE SAME, AN ENCOMIUM ON OUR HOLY FATHER EPHRAIM.
The parable in the divine Gospels moves me toward the present subject, the
mystagogy of the lamp, and it looses the tongue serving under the muzzle of
speechlessness, and it levels the paths of the thoughts as horse-driven
tracks, and the most many-formed chariot of the discourse is prepared to run
upon a highway, crying out clearly somewhere thus: They do not light a lamp
and set it under the bushel, but on the lampstand, and it shines for all who
are in the house. And it adds the thing understood in depth from this, saying:
So let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works. Would
it not then be blameworthy to hide oneself and to fear, in the things in which
the Master himself charged us to be bold? Who, having kindled as it were a
bright lamp and one brighter than the solar, the manner of life of our present
divine Father for narration, does not wish this to be hidden under the bushel
of silence, but to lie forth above on the very summit of the Church, so as to
shine for all who turn about in the worldly house; and that those who see from
this may glorify the Father who is in the heavens.
One must not then revere that paternal bond of his, because a Masterly
ordinance is being trodden down; for it is necessary to take the beginnings of
the praises from there, even if the present taking-up has been assigned by
position after the encomia. Since for the one who knows how to distinguish the
matters well, that is not simply a bond, forbidding one who turns away from
human glory to be crowned with encomia; but a bond that is no bond, and an
unholding hold, crowding the lovers toward the opposite. For through the
things by which he aimed to escape our praises, walling off the attempt with a
bond, through these we =46.821= take the starting-points of the encomia. Each
of his advantages then is toward a starting-point of perfect praise, and most
of all the not rejoicing in praise. For he wished not to seem, but to be,
good. For this very thing then and this alone, even if he had done nothing
else worthy of praises, it is just that he be eulogized, because he said he
rejoiced in the encomia so far as even to seal with a bond the rebuke upon
them. And this first opens for us the stadium of the discourse, and fully
assures us not to walk aimlessly, nor to be carried down a cliff, but to
travel a royal road, reasoning well that if the wondrous man of God did not
see himself worthy of praises, he would not have forbidden those who wished to
speak well of him. For no one who is not admired, while still living, on
account of greatest advantages charges those afterward to darken his own
memory by a fall of forgetfulness. Besides these it is possible to say this
also: that just as Paul the orator of grace, the bridesman of the Church, the
mouth of Christ, did not lose being called Apostle because he said, I am not
worthy to be called an Apostle; rather, through this very thing, the being
moderate-minded, he carried off greater glory. So then also our great Father,
having judged himself unworthy of praises through reverence, will be found
worthy according to what follows.
Since then our law of the Church knows how to crown the zealots of virtue from
their pursuing the forms of it; and of these, as many as especially were
spiritually raised by the humble measure of thought, according to what was
said by the Lord in the Gospels in some such way: He who humbles himself will
be exalted; those who write down the virtues of the present God-bearing
Father, and as it were raise up his life for us into a breathing and living
stele, are altogether outside blame and mockery and even condemnation itself.
And the wise


hearer of the things promised will see the unlying, gathering after the
discourse the various forms of his virtues, of which we, having wrought as
some one gold-inlaid crown, flowered round with precious and diverse stones,
will bring as a longed-for gift to the Church the bride of Christ; for she
rejoices to be greeted with such gifts, when the yearly memory of the
righteous meets her. And running time has now brought Ephraim round to eulogy;
and how will she not receive his memorial with great joy? That Ephraim, who is
carried about in all the mouths of Christians. I mean Ephraim the Syrian; for
I am not ashamed of the race in whose ways I take pride. Ephraim, whose light
of life and of contemplation shone out into all the earth; who is known to
almost all under the sun, and unknown among as many as the great luminary of
the Church Basil =46.824= is unknown; Ephraim, the truly intelligible
Euphrates of the Church, from which the irrigated fullness of the faithful
yields the seed of faith a hundredfold; Ephraim, the many-bearing vine of God,
which flowers with fruits of teaching after the manner of sweet clusters, and
gladdens the nurslings of the Church with the satiety of divine love; Ephraim,
the good and faithful steward of grace, who measures out the words of the
virtues analogously to his fellow-slaves, and administers the Master's house
most excellently. Whose race and the land that bore him and the distinction of
ancestors, and the good fame of parents, and birth, and rearing, and increase
of age, and bodily aptitude, and fortune, and arts, and the rest of the
solemnities gleaned for praises by the outside chroniclers, we have supposed
it superfluous to bring into the midst; because we have not received the
custom of praising divine men from such things; even if from abundance the
praiseworthy belongs to him from there also, from the things by which he made
himself distinguished in life and in word, from these we too will bind on him
the crown of the discourse. For the praises are of the things in our power,
and the prizes of the things that are free-willed.
But of the things enumerated, both the blames are unreasonable and the
eulogies unfitting.
For how did this man accept being eulogized from race, who abhorred all
worldly nobility, and embraced becoming a child of God through taking up of
best works? Or how to be made solemn from fatherland, who reckoned all the
earth alien to himself, and turned away from the material creation as from
enmity, because of the eternal blessedness laid up in the heavens? And how
again would he take pleasure in the good fame of ancestors or parents, who
simply trampled down bodily attachment, and was weighed down even by the
soul's little tunic, that is the little flesh, as found an obstacle to him
toward the sharpest courses of virtue? And how at all would he wish to be
praised from bodily increase and manner of life, or aptitude, or art, or any
other despised occupation about life, who was reared from first age and grew
in the study of the divine Scriptures, and was watered by the ever-flowing
channels of grace, and arrived, to speak apostolically, at the measure of the
stature of Christ? Knowing then that our great Father does not rejoice to be
magnified by such things, with which the bodily are magnified, by laughable
praises, let us try, even if only moderately, to make him solemn from his own
labors; for the discourse has not nature to stretch itself to things beyond
power. So that neither speechlessness may fetter our longing, nor again,
traveling a track foreign to the Fathers, we may slip from the royal road,
running the side-tracks, we will craft the discourse with measure. And what
are his things, through which we proposed to weave the praise? Practice and
contemplation, which the swarm of the virtues in part follows: faith, hope,
love, =46.825= piety toward God, study of the divine Scriptures, purification
of soul and body, continual tears, desert living, withdrawal from place to
place, flight from things harmful, ever-flowing teaching, unceasing prayer,
fasting and vigil having no measure,


sleeping on the ground and hard training that overpass speech, poverty and
humility that have driven to the extreme, almsgiving that lifts him out of
human nature itself, God-inspired zeal against those who rage against piety;
and, to speak simply, everything that knows how to characterize a man
according to God.
Our Father is adorned with such praises, and he knows the things said, and he
understands his own advantages, and he loves the words, as bringing benefit,
not to him, but to us; since even his bare name becomes a subject of virtue
for the zealous. For we have been taught these things from nowhere else than
from what he himself sowed in words concerning himself. From which we, as the
praiseworthy bee, having collected the useful from many flowers, have worked
this spiritual honeycomb. And he will not be angry at us at all for the
attempt, because he no longer fears the wicked demon, on account of having
tripped many even toward the very end of the contests, having once arrived at
the waveless harbors of the bodiless, he is outside both storm and surge. Come
then, having lingered a little in the discourse upon each of the things
enumerated, let us show to those gathered of what sort the wondrous man
became, and to what measure of spiritual ascent he arrived. For he held the
things of faith very rightly, not nodding somewhere far from piety; as we have
received both from his writings and from the opinion concerning him held by
the Church; for equally he loathed the unreasonable confusion of Sabellius and
the mad division of Arius. And he stood within the bounds of piety; dividing
by number, but joining by essence, the one and unconfused and all-holy
Trinity; so that he might neither be reproached in Jewish fashion with poverty
of godhead, nor revel in Greek fashion with a populace of gods; which those
who have run riot about the incomprehensible Trinity are convicted of
suffering. And so much did he reject the irrational dogma of Apollinarius, as
to set all zeal to drive it out of every Christian soul. But indeed also by
many demonstrative and Scriptural words he shut the ungated mouths of the
Anomoeans, leaving us as greatest safety his God-taught oracles. And if anyone
should wish to see the defeat of most rash Novatus, let him look at the fall
of the adversary in the wrestling through the words of Ephraim, in which he
will find the surplus of the power of our Teacher in the presentation of the
discourse as great as that of a pancratiast man and of a child not strong
through softness.
And not only did he refute by the right word of the faith the weedy heresies
that sprang up at that time or even before from the sower of wickedness; but
also those that were going to sprout badly afterward =46.828= he saw with
prophetic glance and harvested beforehand. And every writing and poetry of his
is full of these proofs. Thus the son of the truth never went mad away from
the truth; and hope for him was toward God alone, from whatever time the
graces of the hopes stored up for the worthy. For studying the psalmic saying
through life in word and in deed he used to say, My heart hoped upon him, and
I was helped. Because he hoped upon the Lord in such a way, mercy will
encircle him. And the trust toward him likens this man to mount Sion; but it
also sets the one who possesses it within a most high beatitude, as it is
possible to learn from the prophets themselves, David saying: Blessed is the
man whose hope is the name of the Lord. And Jeremiah: Blessed is the man who
has trusted upon the Lord, and the Lord will be his hope; and he will be as
the tree flourishing beside waters, and he will cast his root upon moisture.
And Isaiah: The Lord is our King, the Lord our Savior, he himself will save
us. Behold God my Savior the Lord, I will be trusting upon him and I will be
bold. And the blessed Paul exhorting and saying: We hold the promise of the
hope; for faithful is the one who promised us.


Turning about in this divine and stored-up hope, he despised all worldly
things, and longed for each eternal glory. And he guarded so exactly the love
toward God and toward the neighbor, as for him to say thus when departing from
life; for it is good to bring into the midst the very words of the God-bearing
Father, which happen to be better than every proof. In no way, he says, in all
my life did I revile the Lord, and a foolish word did not pass my lips. In all
my life I cursed no one, and I did not fight at all with any faithful man. O
blessed tongue, which having been bold uttered such a voice, which happens to
be most fitting to the angels alone, because of the immaterial and unfactioned
character of the life. But to us who are enslaved to the flesh, it is foreign
and beyond nature, and hard to be accomplished. And you might labor much
searching the lives of those famed for virtues, but nowhere would you find
anything such as upon the present Father, a sign for narration of pure and
unspotted love. If then love is greater than all the virtues; and the blessed
Ephraim accomplished this as no other of the Fathers; and each will receive
according to his works; we will yield to others to conclude the rest from
these things, lest we seem to compare Fathers with Fathers. For we have not
taken up what was said for the sake of comparison, but only that we may set
before the eyes of the many that our teacher, or rather the teacher of the
inhabited world, Ephraim, arrived at the very peak of the spiritual ladder of
the virtues. =46.829=
And having trafficked piety in the acquisition of true wisdom (according to
what was said in Job: Behold, piety is wisdom) through it, as we showed a
little above, describing the purity of his faith, he went up to a third
heaven, according to Paul, and he has been enriched with immortal memory and
lasting good fame in the Church; and the study of the divine Scriptures was
kindled for him from the Davidic lamp, who says, In my study a fire will be
kindled. For a love of spiritual contemplation having sold him raised the
longing into a high flame. For having studied through every Old and New
Scripture, and having dwelt in such contemplations as no other, he interpreted
the whole accurately toward the letter, from the genesis of the world even to
the last book of grace, bringing into the open the depths of the hidden
contemplations, using the Spirit as a lamp. And not only did he drink out the
whole spiritual mixing-bowl of this our God-inspired wisdom, and give a share
to the others, but also of the outside worldly wisdom, as much as concerns the
well-tongued and correct of discourse, and as much as concerns the depth of
thoughts, having trained himself very much, he took to himself as much as was
useful; and as much as was unprofitable he threw away, trafficking both
practice and contemplation with a balance of righteousness. And he
accomplished purity of soul and of body, as much as nature admitted; or rather
even beyond nature; for the gift is of grace. For he did not allow the soul to
slip from the habit according to right reason, but he was truly reigning with
the soul, and flashing forth splendidly with the body. And the change of the
harlot who is told of bears witness to the things said, whom Beliar the
man-slayer used as a bait of wickedness toward the deceit of the enlightened
man, and he fell so far short of what was hoped, that even the adulteress
herself was arrayed against that exceeding Wicked one, and having been changed
by exhortations and turns, and by the charms of the divine words, and having
been remolded toward the better, she was shown temperate instead of unchaste,
solemn instead of unsolemn, and pure instead of defiled. And as I am eager to
speak of that ever-flowing stream of tears of his, it truly comes upon me to
weep; because not without this would anyone sail through the sea of his tears
with the discourse. For as to breathe is inborn to all men, and always in
activity; so for Ephraim was the flowing of tears.


For there was not day, not night, not midnight, not an hour, not the finest
point of a moment, when his unsleeping eye was seen dry; bewailing now the
common, now the private, as he used to say, offenses; and he wisely channels
the streams of the eyes by groanings; or rather he called out the groanings by
pourings of the eyes. And the going-forth of both was paradoxical, each
appearing as cause of the other; for in him tears begot groanings =46.832=,
and groanings again tears; and the cause was unknown to the many. For time not
dividing these, but tears always succeeding the groanings, then again these
succeeding groanings, and a certain circle coming to be, the beginning was
unclear, and the cause of either. And one will see this having conversed with
his writings. For he will find him bewailing not only in the words concerning
repentance and character and good manner of life, but even in his panegyric
discourses themselves, where most are accustomed to show much gladness in the
words. But he was the same everywhere, and he was continually rich in the gift
of compunction. Whence even now he almost calls all the hearers of his words
back to the true life. So great a force does his God-inspired discourse
possess, mixed with tears. For what man unsoftened, or stony in heart, having
heard his sayings, will not be softened, and having thrown off the hardness of
character, will not look grimly on his own vices? What man wild in character,
or beastlike in manner, having given ear to his soul-profiting teaching, will
not at once be good and gentle, and a lover of the good in his ways? What man
practicing to rejoice in the sweet passions of life, and turning away from
weeping, having heard a few of his sayings, will not lament and weep, and come
into remembrance of the things lived of the coming recompense?
You are boiling a stone, the proverb of those outside has taken up concerning
impossible things. But for us experience has presented this clearly. For this
divine old man persuaded the unmelting and unyielding souls both to be on
guard and to obey. Who, having met his discourse concerning humility of mind,
will not at once hate all conceit, but will he not even proclaim himself
humbler than everyone? Who, having set himself upon the things concerning
love, will not be eager to run risk beforehand for love? Who, having conversed
with the things concerning virginity, will not contend to present himself pure
in soul and body to God? Who, having stooped into the things concerning
judgment, that is the second coming of Christ, will not think he is standing
at the tribunal there, and having become trembling, will he not suppose that
the last sentence is already being brought out against him? For this
celebrated and most prophetic man so presented the coming court of God, as for
nothing else of knowledge to be left remaining, but only to learn this by the
deed and by experience. Always held by such imaginings concerning judgment,
the blessed man fled the world and the things in the world; and fleeing he
made himself far, as the oracles say, and he lodged in the desert, attending
to himself alone and to God, and receiving from there the increases of the
virtues. For he knew exactly that desert living frees the one who wishes from
the worldly tumults, and by quiet makes him a fellow-conversant with the
angels, and raises the mind as far as it is reachable to the contemplations
=46.833= concerning God. And to change places from places, when the Spirit
that moved him was managing it toward the building of the many, he did not
disobey, nor did he strain against it. For he was obedient to divine commands,
if anyone else was. From there, as that divine Abraham when commanded, he left
the land that bore him and came to the city of the Edessenes; for it was not
just that the sun be hidden under earth for long. And for two causes: both to
embrace the holy things there, and before this to meet a learned man, and
either to receive or to give a share of a fruit of knowledge.
In which, as it has been told, having come to the city, and going up through
the city, he met with a certain harlot instead of the lover of words whom he
was praying to meet; and as what appeared of the expectation


was other, the meeting turned out grievous to him. Therefore having gazed
intently at the little harlot, as having been cheated of the hope he was led
away *; but she, seeing him gazing intently at her, was herself also looking
carefully and inspecting him. And the wise man to her: Tell me, woman, for
what reason do you gaze a little at me. And she: I correspondingly and
fittingly; for I have been taken from you the man, but do you not inspect me,
but rather the earth, from which you were taken. The wise man having heard
this beyond expectation, confessed that he had been greatly benefited; and he
over-glorified the incomprehensible power of God, who grants the things of
hope even through the things contrary to hope. And having gone from there to
Caesarea of Cappadocia, led by the Holy Spirit, he saw the great Basil, the
mouth of the Church, the golden nightingale of the dogmas; whom the old man,
having beheld, began to honor with many eulogies. For with the discerning eye
of the soul he saw a splendid dove sitting toward his right shoulder,
supplying him words of teaching, and him ferrying these through to the people.
Who, having been initiated by the reverend dove that was teaching him, both
perceived the visit and recognized that he was Ephraim the Syrian. And indeed
both were thought worthy of a timely spiritual sojourn together; thus the
trouble did not turn out unprofitable for Ephraim.
And the natural integrity of life was showing him the flight from things
harmful; teaching both the foresight of the better and the guarding against
the worse, and to admit those of the reasonings that are approved and pure,
and more profitable toward a choice of good, as many as especially hinder
toward the need of teaching. For the talent of the word had been given
abundantly by Christ to the divine old man, which it was owed to him before
all the other things to lay down in the tables of the heart of the many. And
this he himself appears saying concerning himself, that having just passed the
softness of childish age, he beheld mystically toward his tongue how a very
fruitful vine came up, having grown so much as to fill =46.836= the earth, and
every bird of heaven coming was filled with food from it. And the vine itself,
in being pruned, and receiving every winged thing, was rather richer in
clusters. And another of the very discerning men then also bore witness of
this concerning him; saying that he saw a multitude of angels coming down from
heaven, bearing a volume upon their hands, written outside and inside. And
that divine array that appeared was saying to itself: Who then ought to be
entrusted with this volume? From which some answering preferred this man, and
some that, and others another, whom that time held as chosen men. Then after
the testing all said: "Truly these are holy and servants of God; yet they
cannot be entrusted with this volume." And when they had mentioned many of the
saints then, they were not well pleased among them; at last, having agreed,
they said: No one is able to be entrusted with this volume, except Ephraim.
And indeed that seer is said to have seen this too, the divine angels handing
over the volume to Ephraim; and the one initiated in the vision, having risen
at night, came to church, heard Ephraim teaching in an abundant word and
seasoned with grace; and that man, judging the vision that had been seen, both
glorified God and was struck by the rich gift of the word given to the saint.
For so great a surplus of wisdom had been granted him, that the channels of
the words were ever-flowing; but he was slow toward the manifestation of the
thoughts, not by numbness of the tongue, but by density of the conceptions; so
that the tongue was able to equal the speed of the conceptions in others, but
was less than his own contemplations. Therefore the great old man himself
also, asking God concerning himself, upon the forbearance of the unholdable
gift of chosen words, is said to have spoken such things: Stay, O


Master, the waves of your grace. For under the tongue the abyss of teaching,
rolling as a sea, did not permit him to bear the successive waves of the
thoughts, the organs for speaking not serving in the eager ministry. And
nothing else was cutting the words, but prayer alone, and the words this, and
the tears these, and these again prayer. And the word was of the word, or
rather to speak more properly, in a word, being continually occupied through
all these in the contemplations concerning God. For having put to death the
body with the pleasures, and having worked it a slave through self-control to
the ruling reasoning, he had it unmoved toward the things not needed, tamed
beforehand by fasting, but firm toward the things profitable and furnishing
salvation of soul. But neither did nights cut his course of virtue, cheating
him with the imaginings from sleep. For receiving him sober from the days,
they left him watching as they ran past, accomplishing this, that the hand of
the ruler of this darkness might not overtake him sleeping. And he partook of
sleep so much as was sufficient toward living; so that the little flesh might
not, the natural sequence having been altogether overturned, =46.837= undergo
a violent dissolution. And many other things also were overturning this and
driving it from the eyes, but most of all sleeping on the ground and hard
training, and the manifold ill-treatment of the body.
For by these especially the passion of sleep is naturally banished. And he
acquired such poverty as you hear the divine apostles to have. Therefore if
anyone will call this man an archetype of those who possess nothing, he will
not miss what is due. For we have that sweetest and blessed voice of his
himself, which, being about to depart toward the heavenly things, he left us
as a teacher of poverty, having somehow thus: There never belonged to Ephraim
a purse, not a staff, not a wallet. Nor indeed silver, nor gold, but neither
did I acquire any other possession upon the earth. For I heard the good King
in the Gospels saying to his own disciples to acquire nothing upon the earth.
Whence I did not even have a passionate relation toward any such thing. So
then, careless of both glory and money, and a lover of the better more than of
everything, competing from there also in the equal course with the apostles.
And what shall we bear witness to him concerning humility of mind; where
indeed every discourse and writing of his expressly has this virtue
proclaimed, even though it is pursued by him from a great surplus? For when
would he who calls forth tears with tears, and eats ash as bread, mixing the
rough and unpleasurable of the diet, and the drink with weeping, as the
oracles say, strike the soul's foot against a stone of lifting or conceit? Or
he who turns away from all human glory, and while still going about in life,
when someone was praising him, being distressed, and changing from one color
to another, and bowing to the earth, and dripping with a fine sweat, and held
by a terrible speechlessness, as if the shame itself were making his tongue
idle? And departing toward the blessed and unceasing life, again forbidding
this very thing with a strong rebuke, and saying: Do not sing a song over
Ephraim, do not eulogize, do not bury me with costly clothing, do not work a
tomb for my body privately; for I have a word with God, that I lodge with the
strangers. I am a stranger and a sojourner, as all my fathers. You have then
from abundance also of this virtue, as indeed also of the rest, clear the
proofs. And the definition and law of truth will vote this man not only a doer
of almsgiving and sympathy, but also a teacher.
For by perfect poverty, not having to provide to those in need, he practiced
almsgiving in stirring up the others toward mercy by frequent exhortations.
For truly his word was, even without the sight, a God-forged key, opening the
treasures of the rich =46.840= and furnishing the needed things to the
moderate; and the angelic appearance sufficed even alone when seen toward
sympathy and compassions,


and to move even those very unsoftened, mixed with simplicity, and gentleness,
and much nobility of goodness. And who was so devoted to shamelessness, who
having looked at him did not blush, and had somehow become more orderly than
himself? Perhaps then someone, having heard so great a multitude of
accomplishments, will suppose that this divine man did not go into the depth
of the ecclesiastical dogmas. For what leisure, I would say, did such a man
have, being divided among so many virtues? But the knowledge of the divine
dogmas was not superficial in him. For he did not pursue this only so far as
to speak and to exhort the others; but he was well educated in both, both the
dogmas of the Church themselves, and the things that attempt to contradict
these; the one, as much as he learned by practice; the other toward refutation
of the heretics. For zeal was moving him against the wild beasts of the
Church; whence also a certain unwritten account has reached us, showing us his
zeal toward the truth, having thus, how the light-minded, or rather mindless
and irrational Apollinarius, having made many innovations and having belched
from the belly, drafted an unjust Scripture of piety, composing this in two
books. And he entrusted these for keeping also to a certain woman who, as was
rumored, was serving his pleasures. And when the things of the writing became
known to the Great one, the wise man, having molded a figure of that man's
opinion, approaches the woman who was keeping the profane books, making
himself known, and bringing her blessings as from the desert, and perhaps also
crafting certain other things. Later then he begs that the writings of the
teacher be given him as a favor of benefit, as he said, so that he might
easily be able to be borne against the heretics, thus indeed calling us.
But she, not en[light]ened in mind, and having been cheated into thinking this
man also of the company of Apollinarius, provided the books, having asked that
the return of them be swift. And this great Jacob, having tripped the profane
Esau, and having taken the wicked firstborn of his thoughts, went out wisely;
for having joined leaf to leaf, and having smeared the whole with glue of
fish, he worked the whole of the book as one codex, the one part not being
allowed to be torn from the other by the violent binding. Having thus crafted
with the two books, he gave them back again to the woman who had used them;
who, as a woman, and inexperienced in wise thoughts, having seen the
appearance of them sound, did not examine closely concerning the depth. And
when not very many days had passed, the divine old man suggests to some of the
orthodox to call the impious Apollinarius to converse. And he, having received
the summons, and trusting in his godless books =46.841=, met on the appointed
day, and he himself declined to converse as worn out by old age; he thought it
right that his own books be brought forward, and through them to answer and to
contradict. When then his fellow-initiates brought the books, greatly
swaggering over them; that judge of injustice, aged in days of evils, taking
them attempted to unfold the one of the books. And as it did not yield, being
pressed by the glue, he made the attempt about the middle of it; and it was
similarly unfolded. When then he missed the first, he proceeded then to the
second, and indeed having seen it also wholly undivided and altogether
unyielding, his face was changed by the shame; and by the perplexity his soul
was struck, and the deranged man, having separated from the council by
despondency and disease, and at last drew near to death, not bearing the
outrage. Such was our great father and teacher Ephraim also toward the zeal of
piety; showing in other things the meek and gentle, when there was no need of
war; and in other things the astringent and austere, when especially the
danger was about the faith, pursuing all things wisely, and as the time
needed.
For this too was not less than his many fastings and tears and unceasing
prayers; I mean the zeal according to God, or rather, even beyond those;
inasmuch as those confine the benefit to the doer alone; that


but passes over to common benefit. Since the true word tests the good hunter
toward the fighting beasts, and the good helmsman toward winds blowing against
the ship, and the wise physician toward the hard-to-heal sufferings, and the
noble soldier toward a heavy opposing array, and the zealous and jealous man
of piety, in the peak of dangers and difficulty, how he may pull out both
himself and the others from the plot. It is not possible then to find a form
of virtues laboriously worked of old among any, which was not also
accomplished in this man. And it is possible to liken his soul to a spring
bubbling with all kinds of streams, adorned with benefit and sweetness and
delight; or a meadow blooming, making fragrant with various flowers; or an
earthly heaven diversified with different lamps, or a paradise; such as you
hear the one of Eden made splendid with countless fruit-bearing trees; except
not passable for a most wicked serpent, and the one who procures the exile; or
if nature has honored any other of the fair and delightful things with many
and different goods; reckon the blessed soul of the great Ephraim to be such,
circled from all sides by many forms of virtues. For the wondrous man, having
set it as a study through all life to acquire virtue entire, was eager to
embrace all the advantages alone. For having imitated as a priest the
God-chosen offering of Abel the first among the righteous, he did not bring
sacrifices from flocks, nor fat to the Lord, but a bloodless sacrifice, the
reasonable worship, consumed as a whole-burnt offering by purity of life
=46.844=, having competed with that man so far as not to be killed by the
wicked murderer, but having run through the snares of the man-hating demon to
escape, and to be transferred, as that man, toward unending life, having
appeared above the plot.
And having been zealous for the hope of Enoch, he has not only called upon the
name of the Lord God, but he also taught the others to call upon it with him.
Enoch, he did not practice the foreign transfer from earth toward paradise,
but the passage from material attachment upon the spirit. Noah, not the small
saving of the race in a wooden chest, but the securing of his own soul from
every side, so as to pass unharmed the surge of life, and not to be damaged in
the freight of virtue. Of Abraham he became an imitator in many other things
also, in the faith and meekness, and in the love toward God, but most of all
in the flight from the world, as that man exchanged his own land and kinship
for a friend, and to take the sacrifice of the Only-begotten into that man's
voluntary offering of the body through God, having put to death the members
upon the earth. Of Isaac, in the willing and untroubled death, except that man
not by a father. For this man, sacrificing himself everywhere apostolically,
was whole-fruited with the body, as far as to purpose, but he lived in spirit,
and lives to God through the pure offering of the body as in a ram. Jacob, in
tripping the profane Esau, that is the begetter of the heresies, and taking
the firstborn, the right dogmas of the Church, and in seeing not a ladder set
from earth into heaven, but a pillar of fire heaven-high, showing somehow more
finely a depth of mystery. And still also in distributing to the disciples as
that man to the sons, when about to depart from the body, the blessings; into
which if anyone stoops laboriously, he will suppose these to be of that great
Jacob himself. Of Joseph, most of all in the chastity and purity of the body;
but much more in the giving of a share of the word, as that man of the grain.
Of Moses similar either in all things or in most; for this man also fled the
sorcerer Pharaoh, and lodged in a desert, and saw God, as far as reachable by
contemplation, and worked wonders, and led a people, having been named a
teacher; and he outwitted the Egyptians, having seized their wealth, having
taken captive and triumphed over the books of the heretics, and he split the
sea, the salty and undrinkable unbelief, and he brought the people across, the
orthodox


body. And he drowned the Pharaonites, the godless offspring of the heretics.
And he routed Amalek (if you wish to call any of the heretics by this name);
he received from God the law of orthodoxy, and gave a share to us all. He
beheld the type of the tabernacle on the mountain, not of the one in the time
of Moses, but of the coming fearful judgment and constitution. He perfected
the priests, legislating the things concerning priesthood. He made water
spring from a rock, having prepared the stony hearts to drip tears =46.845=.
He fed bread as that man, heavenly, having set forth for all the words of
love, through which especially every soul is supported, and comes boldly to
the divine and mystical bread, which came from the bosom of the Father for our
salvation. He gave quail, teaching us the faithful from below to travel heaven
by the memory concerning God, and to imagine the beauties there. And simply,
with whatever words of contemplation you may wish to equal the solemnities of
the Father to the ancient ones, you will not find him falling short at all.
Since Jesus the son of Nave also split Jordan, having opened toward well-doing
the hands of the rich that had been shut toward sharing, and he allotted to
the people the land, not of the promise below, but of the heavenly kingdom. As
Samuel he was dedicated to God from an infant, and heard a divine voice. As
the great Elijah, he made an example of the priests of shame, and he brought
down intelligible fire upon the reasonable sacrifice, not once, but many
times; and by a fiery chariot of the virtues he was led up, not an ethereal,
but a heavenly charioteer. As Elisha, he has been enriched with a double grace
of the Spirit; and as the prophets he has often been thought worthy of divine
appearances. The discourse dares to set this man even beside the one higher
among those born of women, the mediator of the law and of grace. As a
forerunner he lodged in a desert; and a word of God came also to this man, and
he was named a herald of repentance, and he taught those who approached to
confess the sins. As Paul the vessel of election, he underwent all kinds of
trials, and he was not numb to cast down the seeds of repentance, as that man
of the faith. And what need is there to compare this man with those in part,
while his accomplishments still fill all the inhabited world? And when the
works appear, long speech is superfluous; and the length of the discourse is
turned toward discredit of the virtues, as if they were not sufficient to
indicate themselves adequately through works, but needed the help from words.
But it is good to add to the discourse, as some most delightful seasoning,
also the wonder sung concerning his end.
For this God-bearing man, being about to depart toward the heavenly things,
was charging those present that it was not permitted them to bury his body in
costly clothing. And if even someone being a lover of the father had planned
ahead some such thing, or had prepared it, by no means to bring the counsel
into deed, but that very thing defined for his burial to be given to the poor.
One then of those standing by, and this one of the distinguished, having
prepared costly clothing, and having planned beforehand to bury with it the
body of the divine old man, having heard the rebuke, was pained, and postponed
giving to the poor the garment prepared, having judged it more holy according
to mind rather to give a worthy honor than to those in need. Who at once,
having been struck by a wicked demon, was reaping the bitterest sheaves of the
disobedience =46.848=, tearing himself before the bed of the holy man, and
boiling up the foam from the mouth. And the most sympathetic man of God says
to the one who had suffered: Since, O man, what unreasonable thing has been
done by you, and has thrown you toward this danger? And he, having risen at
the command, even though darkened in mind by the demon, spoke out the hidden
reasoning, and made the disobedience manifest.
Whom, when he had confessed, the most sympathetic old man restored healthy,
having released him from the suffering by laying on of holy hands and by
prayer; then also addressing:


Fulfill your promise, man, which you long ago planned ahead. Having worked
such a wonder toward [the things] at the end of life, and having raised those
with him toward the zeal of virtue by many exhortations, as his last discourse
makes clear, he breathed out, well-accepted also, into the waveless harbor of
the eternal kingdom. And where else must one suppose that soul of his to have
lodged, or clearly in heavenly tents, where orders of angels, where choirs of
prophets, where thrones of apostles, where joy of the martyrs, where gladness
of the holy, where brightness of teachers, where the festal gathering of the
firstborn, and a pure sound of those keeping festival there. Into those good
things, which angels desire to stoop into, into that holy place the
much-blessed and holy soul of our blessed and celebrated Father went. And I
think that at its ascent toward heaven the virtues through his life led the
way, and each showed it those unspeakable and unseen beauties, and perhaps
love, the greater of all the virtues, having approached, spoke thus: See,
dearest soul, what sort of beauty I procured for you; and with the word to
show the delight. And humility of mind having run up to say: Look, soul longed
for by God, what sort of place of rest I too have prepared for you. And all of
them in part both to speak and to show what sorts of recompenses they, having
first been laboriously worked, procured for it later. O much-hymned and
zealous departure! O death not needing tears! O separation that procures the
longed-for union! O transfer that does not give regret to the one transferred!
O funeral that has not acquired regret! For from the things by which we admire
his ways, from these we take the consolation. For in the case of the other
men, death is recognized as a cause of tears for those left behind; but in the
case of the saints, it becomes a procurer of gladness and of festival; because
such a thing is not death, but rather a transfer and a passage toward the
better things.
These encomia are brought to you from us, best of the Fathers, and teacher of
the inhabited world, by a daring tongue, as gifts below worth; not as to one
in need (for what good fame does a discourse grant, walking behind the worth
=46.849= of the one praised), but rather toward benefit of the living; because
the eulogy of good men is a very great encouragement and exhortation to the
many toward the better things. And many other things also moved us toward
these words and made us daring. For I let go the many-formed advantages, and
what is spoken through all the world of the life and of the word; more than
these your wondrous oversight, and the deliverance toward the one who became
your namesake, who also urged us to strip for these contests, who having been
led away captive by barbarian offspring of Ishmael, and having been separated
from the land that bore him not a small time, returning toward his own, and
not knowing the profit of the road, obtained your paradoxical oversight, and
was taught the road more suitable toward safety, and truly did not miss the
aim. Yes indeed, even having arrived at the very peak of the dangers, and
awaiting death, having been hemmed in by a barbarian outrage upon the road, as
soon as he remembered your name, saying besides: Holy Ephraim, help me. And he
passed unharmed over the wandering of the dangers, and despised the fear, and
obtained a salvation beyond mind, and has been given back to the fatherland
beyond hope, being guarded by your providence. For this reason we dared to
stretch out more extensively into the things said before, and we dared to
touch the praises with unclean lips. And if we came far beside what is due in
the words, we will inscribe you as having become responsible for the success,
and we will confess the favor to you. And if the praises are far beside the
worth, even so again we will accuse you as responsible for the failure, even
if the word is somehow daring. For you, wishing to flee the praises, and as
living in life, thus


even having departed, embracing to be humble-minded, you hinder those who wish
to eulogize you. Except whether this, or whether the thing before them, we
discharge the debt, as far as to power, and we believe that you will not turn
away from us the warm lovers of the father; but as parents receive the
affectionate lispings of children, you will receive them. And you, standing at
the divine altar, and serving with angels the life-originating and all-holy
Trinity, remember all of us, asking for us forgiveness of sins, and enjoyment
of the eternal kingdom, in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be the glory with
the beginningless Father and the divine and life-giving Spirit, now and
always, and unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
