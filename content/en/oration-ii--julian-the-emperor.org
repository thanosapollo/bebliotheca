* Section 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Poetry says that Achilles, when he grew angry and quarreled with the king, let
fall from his hands the spear and the shield, and, fitting a psaltery and a
lyre, sang and hymned the deeds of the demigods, and made this the pastime of
his quiet, having planned this very wisely. For to become hated and to provoke
the king is overly arrogant and savage; yet perhaps the son of Thetis is not
even free of that other blame, that in the season of deeds he wastes himself
on songs and striking of strings, when it was possible then to cling to the
arms and not let them go, and later in quiet to hymn the king and sing the
successes. Nor yet does the father of those speeches say that Agamemnon dealt
moderately and politically with the general, but used threat and outraged him
in deeds, stripping him of the prize. And bringing them together to the same
place in the assembly, repenting, the son of Thetis crying out /Ἀτρείδη, ἦ ἄρ
τι τόδ’ ἀμφοτέροισιν ἄρειον Ἔπλετο, σοὶ καὶ ἐμοί,/ then cursing the pretext of
the feud and numbering the disasters from the wrath, and the king blaming Zeus
and Fate and Erinys, he seems to me to teach, using the men set forth as
images, as in a drama, that kings must do nothing by outrage nor use their
power toward everything nor give the rein to anger, as to a bold horse carried
along for want of bit and charioteer, and again to advise the generals not to
take it hard at royal disdain, but to bear the rebukes with self-control and
mildness, so that their life may not be full of regret.

Thinking these things by myself, O dear king, and seeing you displaying the
Homeric education in deeds and wishing in every way to do some good to all in
common, and preparing for us privately honors and prizes one after another,
and wishing, I think, to be so much better than the king of the Greeks that he
used to dishonor the best men, while you, I think, grant pardon even to many
of the worthless, praising Pittacus for the saying that set pardon before
punishment, I would be ashamed if I did not appear more reasonable than the
son of Peleus and did not praise according to my power what belongs to you,
not gold, I say, and a purple cloak, nor, by Zeus, richly wrought robes, works
of Sidonian women, nor the beauty of Nisaean horses and the flashing splendor
of gold-inlaid chariots, nor the Indian stone, flowery and graceful. And yet
if someone, giving his mind to these, should wish to think each worthy of
speech, having almost drawn off all of Homer's poetry, I think he will still
need words, and the encomia composed for all the demigods together will not
suffice for you alone.

:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

Let us begin first from the scepter, if you will, and from the kingship
itself. For what does the poet say when he wishes to praise the antiquity of
the house of the Pelopidae and to show the greatness of the rule? /ἀνὰ δὲ
κρείων Ἀγαμέμνων Ἔστη σκῆπτρον ἔχων, τὸ μὲν Ἥφαιστος κάμε τεύχων,/ and he gave
it to Zeus, and he to the child of Maia and of himself, and lord Hermes gave
it to Pelops, and Pelops /δῶκ’ Ἀτρέι ποιμένι λαῶν· Ἀτρεὺς δὲ θνήσκων ἔλιπε
πολύαρνι Θυέστῃ· Αὐτὰρ ὅγ’ αὖτε Θυέστ’ Ἀγαμέμνονι δῶκε φορῆναι, Πολλῇσιν
νήσοισι καὶ Ἄργεϊ παντὶ ἀνάσσειν·/

This is the genealogy of the house of the Pelopidae for you, not even
remaining through three whole generations. But that of our kinship began from
Claudius, and, the rule having left a small gap in the middle, your two
grandfathers succeed. And the father of your mother governed Rome and Italy,
and Libya besides, and Sardinia and Sicily, a dominion in no way worse than
the Argive and Mycenaean; while the begetter of your father governed the most
warlike nations of Gaul and the western Iberians and the islands within Ocean,
which are as much greater than those seen in the sea about us as the sea that
overflows the Pillars of Heracles is greater than the inner sea. And they
showed all these lands clear of enemies, campaigning in common whenever there
was need of this, and at times also each by himself going against the
neighboring barbarians they cut out outrage and injustice. Those men were
adorned with these things. But your father acquired the portion that belonged
to him very piously and holily, waiting for the fated end of the one who begot
him, and the rest, which had slipped from kingship into tyrannies, he stopped
from a harsh slavery, and he ruled all together, taking you three, his own
sons, as co-rulers. Is it then worth comparing the greatness of power and the
time in the dominion and the number of those who reigned? Or is this truly
ancient, and must one pass to wealth and admire your cloak with the clasp,
things which even furnished Homer a pleasant pastime? And must one think
worthy of much speech the horses of Tros, which being three thousand /ἕλος
κάτα βουκολέοντο,/ and the thefts from there? Or shall we be cautious of the
Thracian horses, whiter than snow and swifter to run than the winter winds,
and the chariots among them? And we can praise you in these things, and the
house of Alcinous, I think, and the halls of Menelaus that struck with wonder
even the son of much-minded Odysseus and persuaded him to talk such nonsense,
shall we think it right to compare with yours, lest you seem to have less in
these things, and shall we not thrust away the idle talk? But see that someone
not indict us for pettiness and ignorance of the things truly beautiful and
catch us.

* Section 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

Therefore one must leave such things for the Homeridae to busy themselves
with, and go with confidence to what is nearer than these to virtue, and of
which he makes greater provision, strength of body and experience in arms.

To which, then, of those hymned by the Homeric siren shall we yield? There is
an archer in him, Pandarus, a man faithless and weaker than money, but also
weak in the hand and a poor hoplite, and after him Teucer and Meriones, the
one using the bow against the dove, the other being best in the battle, yet
needing as it were a defense and a wall. That is why he also puts forward the
shield, not his own, but his brother's, and aims at the enemy at leisure,
showing himself a ridiculous soldier, who needed a greater guardian and did
not set the hopes of safety in his arms. You indeed I have seen, O dear king,
bringing down bears and leopards and many lions with the missiles sent forth,
using the bow for hunting and play, but in the battle-line you have a shield
and a breastplate and a helmet; and I would not fear Achilles made splendid in
the Hephaestean arms and trying himself and the weapons, /Εἴ οἱ ἐφαρμόσσειε
καὶ ἐντρέχοι ἀγλαὰ γυῖα·/ for the successes proclaim your experience to all.

And as for horsemanship and lightness in the races, is it worth comparing for
you those of the men before who took a greater name and glory? Or had the one
not even been invented yet? For they used chariots and not yet unyoked colts.
And whoever excelled in speed, for him the judgment against you has become
disputed. To array a formation and dispose a phalanx well Menestheus seems
strongest, and to him, because of age, the Pylian does not yield the
experience. But of those men the enemy often threw the ranks into confusion,
and they could not even hold out arrayed on the wall; but for you, who have
joined in countless battles and with many enemies, barbarians, and no fewer
than these of those who revolted from home and joined in attacking the one who
chose to seize the rule, the phalanx remained unbroken and undissolved, not
yielding even a little.

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

And that this is not nonsense nor a pretence of words stronger than the truth
in the deeds, I wish to go through for those present. For it is ridiculous, I
think, to narrate to you about your own deeds; and I would suffer the same as
a poor and inelegant spectator of the works of Pheidias trying to go through
to Pheidias himself about the Maiden on the acropolis and the Zeus among the
Pisaeans. But if I should bring out to the others the most august of the
deeds, perhaps I would escape the fault, and I will not be liable to the
slanders; so that now one must speak with confidence.

And let no one take it hard at me as I try to take hold of greater actions,
even if the length of the speech should run out with them, and that though I
wish to hold it back and force it, so that the weakness of the words, poured
around the greatness of the deeds, may not ruin them; just as they say the
gold laid on the wings of the Eros at Thespiae took away the precision of the
art. For the successes truly need the Homeric trumpet, and much more than the
deeds of the Macedonian. And it will be clear when we use the manner of
speeches which we set before ourselves from the beginning. There appeared a
great kinship of the king's deeds with those of the heroes, and we said that
he surpasses all in that in which each of the others especially excelled, and
how he is more kingly than the king himself, if we remember at all what was
said in the proem, we showed, and it will be very clear again. But now, if you
will, let us look at the things about the battles and the wars.

Whom then did Homer hymn especially among Greeks and barbarians together? I
will read you the most timely of the verses themselves. /Τίς τ’ ἂρ τῶν ὄχ’
ἄριστος ἔην, σύ μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, Ἀνδρῶν ἠδ’ ἵππων, οἳ ἃμ’ Ἀτρείδαισιν
ἕποντο. Ἀνδρῶν μὲν μέγ’ ἄριστος ἔην Τελαμώνιος Αἴας, Ὄφρ’ Ἀχιλεὺς μήνιεν· ὁ
γὰρ πολὺ φέρτατος ἦεν./ And again he says about the Telamonian: /Αἴας, ὃς περὶ
μὲν εἶδος, περὶ δ’ ἔργ’ ἐτέτυκτο, Τῶν ἄλλων Δαναῶν μετ’ ἀμύμονα Πηλείωνα. /
These then he says came as the best of the Greeks, and of those about the
Trojans, Hector and Sarpedon. Do you wish, then, selecting their most splendid
deeds, that we look around at the greatness? For somehow the battle of the son
of Peleus at the river and the war about the wall of the Achaeans are brought
together to the same point with some of the king's; and Ajax fighting over the
ships and standing on the decks would perhaps meet with some worthy image. But
I wish to narrate to you the battle at the river which the king fought
recently. And you know from where the war broke out, and that it was fought
with justice and not from desire of more. And nothing prevents recalling it in
a few words.

* Section 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

A faithless and bold man, reaching after a rule that did not belong to him,
kills the brother of the king and partner of the rule, and was lifted with
shining hopes, as if he would imitate Poseidon and show Homer's speech not a
myth but more true than anything, who said about the god: /Τρὶς μὲν ὀρέξατ’
ἰών, τὸ δὲ τέτρατον ἵκετο τέκμωρ, Αἰγάς,/ and how from there, taking up the
panoply and yoking the horses, he was carried through the sea. /Γηθοσύνῃ δὲ
θάλασσα διίστατο· τοὶ δ’ ἐπέτοντο Ῥίμφα μάλ’, οὐδ’ ὑπένερθε διαίνετο χάλκεος
ἄξων,/ since nothing was in the way, and all stood aside and withdrew in joy.
So he thought nothing hostile or matched was left for him, nor that even one
thing held him from standing at the outlets of the Tigris. And there followed
him much infantry, and horsemen not fewer, but those who are valiant, Celts
and Iberians and those of the Germans who live by the Rhine and by the sea
toward the west, which whether one must call Ocean or the Atlantic sea or it
is fitting to use some other additional name, I do not insist; except that
there live by it races of barbarians hard to fight and excelling the other
nations in strength, not by hearing only, which happens to be an unsafe trust,
but I know this having learned it by the experience itself. Having raised from
these nations a multitude not less than the army that followed him from home,
or rather the one part followed as his own and akin to him, much of it, and
the other our part; for so it is right to call it; as much of the Romans as
followed by force and not by judgment, like allies and mercenaries, followed
in the rank and figure of a Carian, ill-disposed, as is likely, to a barbarian
and a stranger, who in drunkenness and hangover thought over the dominion and
took it up, and a ruler, as was fitting for one who began from such preludes
and privileges.

And he himself led, not according to Typhos, whom poetic marvel-mongering says
Earth, angry at Zeus, brought to birth, nor as the strongest of giants, but
such as the wise Prodicus in myths fashions Vice competing with Virtue and
wishing to persuade the son of Zeus that she of all things was most to be
honored by him. And leading on to the battle he put forward the things of
Capaneus, barbarianizing and being foolish, yet not, like that man, trusting
in the strength of soul nor in the prowess of body, but in the multitude of
the barbarians who followed, to whom he threatened that he would set all as
plunder, chiliarch to chiliarch and captain to captain and soldier to soldier
of those opposite, baggage and possessions, not even leaving the body free.
And the king's skill increases his mind, and from the difficult ground leads
him down to the plains, rejoicing and not understanding, judging the thing to
be sheer flight and not generalship. That is why he is also caught, like birds
and fishes in nets. For when he came to the open space and the plains of the
Paeonians and it seemed better to fight it out there, then the king arrays the
horsemen on the wing, each apart.

And of these some are spear-bearers, covered with hammered breastplates and
helmets made of iron; and greaves fitted very well to the ankles, and
coverings about the knees, and about the thighs other such coverings of iron;
and they themselves are carried on the horses just like statues, needing no
shield. These were followed by a multitude of the other horsemen carrying
shields, and some shooting from the horses. And of the infantry the hoplite
was in the middle, joining on either side to the horsemen; and behind were the
slingers and archers and as much as throws from the hand, bare of shield and
breastplate. When the phalanx was thus arrayed, the left wing having come
forward a little, the whole enemy was thrown into confusion and did not keep
the formation. And as the horsemen pressed on and did not let up, the one who
had seized the kingship more shamefully flees shamefully, and leaves his
hipparch and chiliarchs and taxiarchs, very many and fighting stoutly, and
after all the poet of the monstrous and accursed drama, who first put it into
his mind to recast the kingship and take the prize from us.

And for a while he rejoiced, not having failed of the first attempt nor having
missed, but then he is asked, with justice, for the penalties standing over
the deeds, and an incredible punishment is exacted. For of all who took part
with the tyrant in the war, death was manifest, and flight was clear, and the
repentance of others; for many supplicated, and all obtained pardon, the king
surpassing the son of Thetis in greatness of soul. For that one, when
Patroclus fell, no longer thought it right even to sell the enemy who were
taken, but killed them as they supplicated about his knees; but this one
proclaimed safety for those who denied the conspiracy, taking away the fear
not only of death or exile or some other punishment, but as if from some
misery and luckless wandering of the life with the tyrant he thought it right
to bring them back with their former things unimpaired.

This will obtain speech again later.

But that must already be said, that the trainer of the tyrant was neither
among the fallen nor among those who fled. For not even to hope for pardon was
reasonable for one who had planned things so unjust and done things impious,
having laid hold of unjust murders of men and women, many private persons, and
almost all who shared in the royal race, not with fear, nor as one would plan
doing a kindred murder, fearing and suspecting some avenging spirits and
polluters from the pollution, but as if washing off the former with some new
and strange purifications, killing man upon man and women upon those dearest,
he reasonably despaired of the supplication. These things it is likely he
thought, and it is likely it is otherwise. For we do not know what he suffered
or did and went unseen, vanished. But whether an avenging daimon, snatching
him up, as Homer says of the daughters of Pandareos, leads him to the ends of
the earth to exact penalties of the plans, or the river, receiving him, bids
him feast the fishes, is not yet clear. For up to the battle itself and when
the companies were being arrayed against a phalanx he was bold, turning about
in the middle; but when the things of the battle were done, as was fitting, he
went vanished, I do not know by which of gods or daimons hidden, except that
it is quite clear it was not for better fortunes. For he was not going to
appear again and, outraging in security upon license, be happy, as he thought,
but, vanished utterly, to undergo a punishment luckless for himself, but
useful to many and toward correction.

The things about the contriver of the whole plot, thought worthy of more
speech, taking away the continuity of the narrative in the middle of the
action, must here again somehow be let go. And one must go back from where I
went out and give the end of the battle.

For the spirits of the soldiers do not fall together with the cowardice of the
generals, but when the things of their formation were destroyed, not by their
baseness, but by the inexperience and ignorance of the one arraying, they
fought it out standing together by companies; and the work was greater than
all hope, some not yielding utterly to those who held the upper hand, others
ambitious to follow out the victory completely, and a mixed confusion rose,
and a shout and a clash of arms, swords being broken about the helmets and
shields about the spears. And man closed with man, and throwing away the
shields they pushed with the swords themselves, caring little for suffering,
and turning all their anger to doing something terrible to the enemy, so as
not to furnish them a victory clean and without tears, exchanging even dying.
And they did this not only as infantry against those pursuing, but also as
many of the horsemen as had their spears become wholly useless from the
breakings. They are long lances, which they broke together and, leaping off,
changed themselves into hoplites. And for some time they held out with
difficulty and barely; but when the horsemen shot from bows riding from far
off, and the cuirassiers used dense charges against them, as on a plain clean
and smooth, and night overtook, then some fled glad, and the others pursued
stoutly as far as the palisade, and they take it with the baggage itself and
the slaves and the cattle. And when the rout of the enemy, as I said, had just
begun, and those pursuing did not let up, they are pushed to the left, where
the river was on the right for those who held the upper hand. And there the
great slaughter happened, and it was filled with corpses of men and horses
mixed. For the Dravus was not like Scamander, nor was it kindly to those
fleeing, so as to thrust out the dead with their arms and throw them from the
streams, and to cover the living and hide them safely in the eddies. For this
the Trojan river did, perhaps from goodwill, perhaps being of such a size as
to furnish the crossing easy both to one wishing to walk and to one swimming;
since it is even bridged when one elm is thrown into it, and all murmuring
with foam and blood it beat the shoulders of Achilles, if one must believe
this too, yet it did nothing more violent; and when a little burning took it,
it gives up the war and swears off the aid.

And this too seems to be a play of Homer, having invented a new and strange
manner of single combat. Since in the other things also he is clear in
favoring Achilles, and, as if leading the army as spectators, he brings him
alone, invincible and irresistible, against the enemy, killing those he meets,
and turning simply all by voice and figure and the assaults of the eyes, both
at the beginning of the array, I think, and on the banks of Scamander, until
those who escaped were gathered glad into the wall. These things that man,
narrating in many verses and inventing battles of gods and adorning the poetry
with myths, bribes the judges and does not allow them to cast a just and
unlying vote. But whoever wishes to be deceived in nothing by the beauty of
the words and the fictions brought on from outside, as at the beginning about
certain spices and colors, let him be an Areopagite judge, and we will not be
cautious of the judgment. For we agree that the son of Peleus is a good
soldier, being persuaded from the poetry. He kills twenty men, /Ζωοὺς δ’ ἐκ
ποταμοῖο δυώδεκα λέξατο κούρους, Τοὺς ἐξῆγε θύραζε τεθηπότας ἠύτε νεβρούς,
Ποινὴν Πατρόκλοιο Μενοιτιάδαο θανόντος./ Yet the victory brought such a turn
into the affairs of the Achaeans that it did not even cast a greater fear into
the enemy nor make them despair utterly about themselves. And about these
things shall we need some other witness, leaving Homer aside? And is it not
enough to recall the verses which that man composed when Priam came to the
ships bringing the ransom for his child? For when, after the settlements for
which he had come, the son of Thetis asked /Ποσσῆμαρ μέμονας κτερεϊζέμεν
Ἕκτορα δῖον,/ he goes through the other things and about the war says: /Τῇ δὲ
δυωδεκάτῃ πολεμίξομεν, εἴπερ ἀνάγκη./ So he does not even shrink from
announcing the war after the truce. But the ignoble and cowardly tyrant put
forward high mountains of his own flight and, having built forts on them, does
not even trust the strength of the places, but supplicates to obtain pardon.
And he would have obtained it, if he had been worthy and had not been caught
often faithless and bold, adding injustices one upon another.

The things in the battle, then, if someone should not wish to attend to the
opinion of those who narrate nor to verses well made, but to look to the deeds
themselves, let him judge. Next, if you will, set the fight of Ajax over the
ships and that of the Achaeans on the wall against the deeds at that city; to
which Mygdonius, most beautiful of rivers, adds his own fame, being also named
for king Antiochus; and it has also another barbarian name, customary to the
many from the mixing with the barbarians here; this city an army of Parthians
with Indians, unmanageable in multitude, surrounded, when it was set before
them to march against the tyrant; and what they say happened to Heracles going
against the Lernaian beast, the sea-crab, this was the king of the Parthians,
having crossed the Tigris from the mainland and walling the city around with
mounds; then receiving Mygdonius into these he showed the place about the town
a lake and held the city in it as an island, the battlements a little
overtopping and showing above. And he besieged bringing ships and engines on
ships; and it was not the work of a day, but of almost four months, I think.
And those in the wall continuously beat back the barbarians, burning the
engines with fire-bearers; and they hauled up many ships from the wall, and
others were broken by the force of the engines sent forth and the weight of
the missiles. For stones of the weight of seven Attic talents were carried
against them. And when this was done for many days, a part of the mound breaks
and the flood of the waters flows in, and upon it a part of the wall not less
than a hundred cubits came down together.

There he arrays the army in the Persian manner. For they preserve and imitate
the Persian things, not thinking it worthy, as it seems to me, to be
considered Parthians, but pretending to be Persians. That is why they also
delight in Median dress. And they go to battles like those men, glorying in
such arms and in gilded and purple garments. And from this they contrive not
to seem to have revolted from Macedonians, but to have taken up again the
kingship belonging to them from of old. So the king also, imitating Xerxes,
sat on a certain handmade hillock, and the army advanced with the beasts.
These followed from India, and carried towers of iron full of archers. And the
cuirassier horsemen and the archers led them, another unmanageable multitude
of horsemen. For their infantry has been set down as useless for warlike
things, sharing neither an honored rank nor being in need for them, the land
they inhabit being a plain and bare. For such things seem to be thought worthy
of honor and dishonor toward the needs of war. As being useless by nature it
is not even from the laws thought worthy of much care. And it happened thus
also about Crete and Caria and in countless other nations that the things
about war were prepared. So also the plain of the Thessalians showed itself
suitable for horsemen to contend and practice in. For the things of our city,
as having stood against opponents of every kind and having prevailed by good
counsel and fortune, were reasonably fitted to every kind of arms and other
preparation.

But these things perhaps are nothing toward the speech, as those assigned to
the arts of praises as to laws would say; but I, if any of these also belongs
to you, I will examine in due time, and the reproaches of men I easily clear
away. For I say that neither do I claim the arts nor does whoever has not
agreed with some to abide by them do injustice in not keeping these; and
perhaps we will not even lack other decent excuses. But it is not worth
detaching the speech at more length into nothing needed and wandering from the
subject. Let us then step back again into the track and from where I went out.

For when the Parthians, themselves and horses arrayed in arms, with the Indian
beasts, advanced to the wall, shining in hopes as if they would snatch it at
once, and the signal to go forward had been given them, they all pushed, each
himself wishing to leap first onto the wall and go carrying the glory upon it;
and they guessed there was no fear; for those within would not even await
their onset. So much surplus of hope the Parthians had. But the others had the
phalanx dense at the broken part of the wall, and over the part still standing
they set as much as was a useless multitude in the city, mixing in a portion
of the soldiers not less. And when the enemy rode up and no missile was sent
against them from the wall, they had a firmer hope of taking the city from the
top, and they struck the horses with whips and bloodied the flanks with the
goads, until they made the mounds at their backs; and those had been made by
them earlier toward holding the outflows of Mygdonius, and there was mud about
the place very deep, the place itself not being wholly so from the wood, and
because the earth is fat and able by nature to hold the springs. And there was
also an old defense for the city, a wide ditch, and in it a deeper swamp had
collected. And when the enemy were already laying hold of this too and trying
to cross, many came out from within, and many from the walls threw with the
stones; and of them there was a great slaughter, and they all turned the
horses to flight, by only wishing and showing the mind through the figure. For
as they turned they fell at once and brought down the horsemen; and being
heavy in the arms they were held more in the swamp. And there a slaughter of
them happens, as great as has never yet happened in such a siege.

And when the things of the horsemen had gone thus, they try the elephants,
thinking they would more strike with the strangeness of the fight; for the
things of their eyes were not so destroyed that they did not see the beast
being heavier than a horse, and carrying a load not of two horses or more, but
of many wagons, I think, archers and javelin-men and an iron tower. And all
these were hindrances toward the place that had become a handmade swamp, and

For think how about the child he has become better and more just than the one
who begot him, and about that man's friends more trustworthy than the one who
agreed the friendship. For the one gave all up, and the other saved all. And
if that man, having known these things about the king's manner as having
understood it in much time, trusted strongly that the things of the child were
safe for him, and the things of the friends moored firmly, he understood
rightly, but he was often knavish and base and unlucky, wishing to be an enemy
to such a one and hating and plotting against one whom he knew as very good
and especially mild, and taking away what in no way he ought. And if, the
child's safety happening to be unhoped for for him, and that of the friends
and the kinsmen hard and impossible, he nonetheless chose the faithlessness,
he was for these things also base and mindless and wilder than the beasts, and
the other tame and mild and great-souled, having pitied the age and the manner
of the infant, and being mild to those not convicted, and looking over and
despising the knaveries of the other. For he who grants what not even some one
of the enemy hopes, through the greatness of the injustices he is conscious of
in himself, is reasonably a victor of virtue, transferring the justice toward
the better and milder, and surpassing in temperance those who set the moderate
upon the punishments, and differing in courage by taking no enemy as
sufficient, and displaying prudence by dissolving the hatreds together and not
sending them on to the children nor to descendants on the pretext of the exact
justice and of wishing, very reasonably, like a pine, to make the seeds of the
base vanish. For that deed is theirs also, and upon it an ancient speech
showed the image. But the good king, imitating the god simply, knows that
swarms of bees fly out even from the rocks, and from the sharpest wood the
sweet fruit grows, the graceful figs, I say, and from thorns the pomegranate
and other things growing from others unlike those that beget and bring them
forth. So he does not think one must destroy these before the prime, but wait
for the time and allow them, having thrust away the mindlessness and the folly
of the fathers, to become good and temperate, and those who have become
emulators of the paternal practices to undergo the justice in due time, not
having been spent alongside others' deeds and disasters.

Do we then seem to you to have completed the true praise enough? Or do you
long to hear also the endurance and the solemnity, and how he is not only
unconquered by the enemy, but has never yet been taken by a shameful desire,
nor, having desired a beautiful house or a costly country-place or emerald
necklaces, has he taken them by force or even by persuasion from those who
possessed them, nor even of a free woman nor of a servant-girl, nor did he
love unjust Aphrodite at all, and how he does not even ask the unbounded
filling of the goods which the seasons grow, nor in the season of summer is
ice a care to him, nor does he change the dwelling toward the seasons, but he
is always present to the laboring parts of the rule, holding out both toward
the cold and toward the noble heats? And if you should bid me bring you the
manifest proofs of these things, I will say things known and I will not lack,
but the speech is long and huge, and I have no leisure to serve the Muses so
far, but it is time at last to turn toward work.
