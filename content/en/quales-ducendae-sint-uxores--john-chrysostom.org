ENCOMIUM ON MAXIMUS And concerning what kind of women one ought to take as
wives.
=51.225=
αʹ. That I was left behind from you at the last assembly, I was pained; but
that you enjoyed a richer table, I was glad. For the one who
draws the yoke with me both cut the furrow for us the other day, and cast the
seeds with a lavish tongue, and with much care farmed your souls. You saw a
tongue thoroughly cleansed, you heard a word turned on the lathe, you enjoyed
water leaping unto eternal life, you saw a fountain sending forth rivers of
pure gold. It is said that a certain river carries gold dust to the men who
dwell about it, not because the nature of the waters begets the gold, but
since it happened that the sources of the river pass through mining mountains,
the river flowing through those mountains and sweeping along the gold-bearing
earth becomes a treasure to those who dwell beside it, furnishing wealth
unplanned. This river the teacher here imitated the other day, flowing as
through mining mountains, through the Scriptures, and bringing thoughts more
precious than all gold to your souls. And I know that our things appear poorer
to you today. For he who continually enjoys a poor table, if somewhere in
between he happens upon a more abundant one, then returns again to his own,
will see its poverty all the more. Yet not for this will I strip for the
contest more sluggishly. For you know, having been disciples of Paul, both to
be filled and to hunger, both to abound and to be in want, and to admire the
rich, and not to spit upon the poor. And just as the lovers of wine and the
lovers of drink welcome the better wine, yet do not despise the inferior; so
also you, being mad for the hearing of the divine oracles, receive the wiser
of the teachers, and to the more cheap you also furnish no ordinary zeal and
eagerness. For the slack and dissolved even nauseate at the more costly table;
but those who are awakened and sober, and hungering and thirsting after
righteousness, run even to the poorer with much eagerness. And that the words
are not flattery, you showed this most of all from the discourse that took
place for you the other day. For since we were making many words to you about
marriages, showing that it is a completed adultery to cast out wives, or to
take those who have been cast out, while the former husbands are still living,
and reading the law of Christ that says that He who has married her who has
been put away commits adultery, and he who puts away a wife, except for the
cause of fornication, makes her to be adulterated, I saw many bending down,
striking the face, and not even able to look up; then indeed looking up into
heaven I said: Blessed be God, that we are not sounding into dead ears, but
the things said take hold of the mind of the hearers with much vehe =51.226=
mence. For it is better not to sin at all; but it is no small thing toward the
account of salvation that the one who sins be cut to the heart, and condemn
his own soul, and scourge the conscience with much exactness; for such a
condemnation is a part of righteousness, and altogether leading toward sinning
no longer. Therefore Paul too, having grieved those who had sinned, was glad,
not because he grieved them, but because by the grief he corrected them. For I
rejoiced, he says, not that you were grieved, but that you were grieved unto
repentance; for the grief according to God works repentance unto salvation not
to be regretted. For whether on behalf of

your own, or on behalf of others' sins you were then pained, you would be
worthy of myriads of praises. For when someone is pained on behalf of others'
things, apostolic bowels are shown, and he imitates that holy one who says:
Who is weak, and I am not weak? who is scandalized, and I am not on fire? But
when he is stung on behalf of his own, he has both quenched the punishment for
the things already dared, and has made himself more secure toward the things
to come through this grief. For these reasons I too, seeing you bending down,
and groaning, and striking the face, was glad, considering the fruit from this
grief. Therefore today also I will discourse to you on the same subject; so
that those who wish to come into marriage may make much provision concerning
this matter. For if, when we are about to buy houses and servants, we inquire
and busy ourselves both with those who sell and with those who possessed them
before, and of the things sold themselves, of some the construction, and of
others both the habit of the body and the choice of the soul; much more, when
we are about to take wives, must we display so much provision and much more.
For a house that is poor it is possible to sell again, and a servant who has
appeared clumsy is allowed to be given back again to the one who sold him; but
when one takes a wife it is not possible to give her back again to those who
gave her out, but there is every necessity either to have her within to the
end, or, casting her out if she is wicked, to be caught in adultery according
to the laws of God. When therefore you are about to take a wife, do not read
the outside laws only, but also before those, the ones laid down among us; for
according to these, not according to those, God is about to judge you on that
day; and those, if overlooked, have often brought the loss upon money, but
these, if they suffer this, bring upon the soul the inescapable punishments
and that unquenchable fire.

βʹ. But you, when you are about to take a wife, run to the outside lawyers
with much zeal, and sitting beside them you examine with all exactness what
will be if the woman dies childless, and what if she has a child, and what if
two and three, and how, having a father, and how, not having one, she will use
her =51.227= own affairs, and what of the inheritance will come to the
brothers, and what to the one living with her, and when he will be master of
the whole, that no one may snatch away any part of her things, and when he
will fall from the whole; and many other such things you busy yourself about
with them and seek, going around everything and looking about, that in no way
any of the woman's affairs may come to any of those belonging to her; and yet,
as I said before, even if something unexpected should happen, the loss will be
in money, but still you cannot bear to overlook any of these things. How then
is it not absurd, when money is about to be lost to us, to display so much
zeal, but when the danger for us is of the soul and of the accountings there,
to make no account, though we ought before all other things to seek these and
inquire and busy ourselves? Therefore I exhort and advise those who are about
to take wives to go to the blessed Paul, and to read the laws about marriages
laid down with him, and having learned first what he commands to do when a
woman happens to be wicked and treacherous, and weaker than drink, and
abusive, and full of folly, and having any other such defect, then to
discourse about marriage. For if you see that he gives you authority, having
found one of these defects, to cast her out and to bring in another, take
courage as being freed from every danger; but if he does not permit this, but
commands you to love and keep within her who has all the other defects except
fornication, then fence yourself, as being about to


bear all the wickedness of the wife. But if this is heavy and burdensome, do
everything and busy yourself so as to take a good and reasonable and obedient
wife, knowing that of two things one is necessary, either having taken a
wicked wife to bear her oppressiveness, or, not wishing this, having cast her
out to be caught in adultery. For he who casts out a wife, he says, except for
the cause of fornication, makes her to be adulterated; and he who marries her
who has been put away commits adultery. If we have well considered these
things before marriage and know these laws, we will make much zeal so as from
the beginning to take a wife well ordered and agreeing with our ways; and
having taken such a one we will reap not this only, that we will never cast
her out, but that we will also love her with much vehemence, and with as much
as Paul commanded. For having said, Husbands, love the wives, he did not stop
at this only, but also gave us a measure of love, As also Christ loved the
Church. And how did Christ love? tell me. In that he delivered himself up for
her. So that even if it is necessary to die for the wives, do not refuse. For
if the Master so loved the maidservant as even to give himself up for her,
much more must you so love the fellow servant. But let us see whether perhaps
the beauty of the bride drew the bridegroom, and the virtue of the soul. It is
not possible to say this. For that she was ill formed and unclean, hear what
follows. For having said, He delivered himself up for her, he added, That he
might sanctify her, having cleansed her with the washing of the water. And
having said, Having cleansed her, he showed that she was unclean before and
polluted, and not as it happened, but with the last uncleanness; for with
fat-smoke and smoke and =51.228= gore and bloods, and myriads of other such
stains she was defiled. But nevertheless he did not loathe the deformity, but
he changed the unpleasantness, he remolded, he reordered, he let go the things
that had been sinned. Imitate this one also. And if the one living with you
sins myriads of sins against you, let all go and forgive; and if you take one
ill tempered, reorder her with reasonableness and gentleness, as also Christ
did the Church. For he not only wiped off her uncleanness, but also scraped
off the old age, having stripped off the old man, the one composed of the
sins. And this again Paul himself, hinting, said: That he might present the
Church to himself glorious, not having spot or wrinkle; for he made her not
only beautiful, but also young, not according to the nature of the body, but
according to the habit of the choice. And this is not the only marvel, that
having taken one shapeless and ill formed and shameful and aged, he neither
loathed the deformity, but even delivered himself unto death, and reordered
her into beauty beyond contrivance; but that even after this, seeing her often
being defiled, being stained, he does not cast her out, nor tear her from
himself, but even remains healing and correcting. For how many, tell me, have
sinned after believing? but nevertheless he did not loathe them; as, the one
among the Corinthians who had committed fornication was a member of the
Church; but he did not cut off the member, but he corrected it. The Church of
the Galatians all started away, and fell toward Judaism; but nevertheless he
did not cast that one out either, but having healed her through Paul, he
brought her back to the former kinship. Just as therefore in our bodies,
whenever a disease comes to be, we do not cut off the member, but we drive out
the disease; so let us do also in the case of the wife. If some wickedness
comes to be in her, do not cast out the wife, but drive away the vice. And yet
it is possible to correct a wife, but often a member that has been maimed it
is not possible to heal; but nevertheless, knowing that its maiming is
incurable, not even so do we cut it off; but even having a twisted foot, many
often, and a lame leg, and a hand dry and deadened, and an eye


put out, neither dig out the eye, nor cut off the leg, nor chop off the hand,
but seeing that no profit comes to the body from them, and much shame to the
remaining members, they remain having them because of the fellow feeling
toward the rest. How then is it not absurd, where the correction is incurable,
and there is no profit, to make so much provision; but where the hopes are
good, and the change is much, to give up the healing? For the things maimed by
nature it is impossible to recover again, but the choice that has been twisted
it is possible to reorder.

γʹ. But if you should say that she herself is sick incurably, and having met
with much care uses her own way, not even so is she to be cast out; for
neither is the member that is sick incurably cut off. And she too is your
member; For they shall be, he says, the two into one flesh. And in the case of
the member, there will be no profit to us of the healing, whenever it has
already become uncorrectable by the illness; but in the case of the wife, even
if she remains sick incurably, much wage will be laid up for us who teach, who
train. And if she gains nothing from our teaching, at least of the patience we
will receive from =51.229= God the much wage, because through fear of him we
displayed so much patience, and bore her vice gently, and held our member. For
the wife is a necessary member of ours; and for this reason one must love her
especially. Which thing Paul again, teaching this very thing, said: So ought
husbands to love their own wives as their own bodies. For no one ever hated
his own flesh, but nourishes and cherishes it, as also Christ the Church;
because we are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones. For just
as Eve, he says, came to be from the side of Adam, so also we from the side of
Christ. For this is, Of his flesh, and of his bones. But that Eve came to be
from the side of Adam himself, we all know, and the Scripture has said this
clearly, that he cast an ecstasy upon him, and took one of his sides, and
built the woman; but that the Church also was constituted from the side of
Christ, whence would one be able to show it? The Scripture shows this too. For
when Christ was brought up onto the cross, and was nailed, and died, One of
the soldiers came and pierced his side, and there came out blood and water;
and from that blood and water the whole Church has been constituted. And he
himself bears witness saying that Unless someone is born again of water and
spirit, he cannot enter into the kingdom of the heavens. And the blood he
calls spirit. And we are born through the water of baptism, but we are
nourished through the blood. Do you see how we are of his flesh, and of his
bones, being born and nourished from that blood and water? And just as, while
Adam was sleeping, the woman was being prepared, so, when Christ died, the
Church was being formed from his side. And not for this reason only must one
love the wife, that she is our member, and had from us the beginning of her
fashioning; but also because God has laid down a law for this very thing,
speaking thus: For this a man shall leave his father and his mother, and shall
be joined to his wife, and they shall be the two into one flesh. For this
reason Paul also read this law to us, that from every side he might drive us
together toward this love. And consider apostolic wisdom; for he leads us into
the love of the wives neither from the divine laws only, nor from the human
only, but from both these and from those, setting both of these by turns; so
that the one who is


higher and more philosophical may be led into affection from the things above,
and the weak from the things below and from those of nature. Therefore
beginning from the achievements of Christ, he introduces the exhortation
speaking thus: Love the wives, as also Christ loved the Church; then again
from the human: So ought husbands to love their own wives, as their own
bodies; then again from Christ: because we are members of his body, and of his
flesh, and of his bones; then again from the human: For this a man shall leave
his father and his mother, and shall be joined to his =51.230= wife; and
having read this law he says: This mystery is great. How is it great? tell me.
Because the girl, being kept in the chamber the whole time, having never seen
the bridegroom, from the first day so longs for him and loves him as her own
body; again the man, her whom he never saw, whose company in speech he never
shared, this one he too from the first day prefers to all, both to friends,
and to household, and to those who begot them. The parents again, if from some
other occasion money is taken from them, are stung, are pained, drag those who
took it into court; but to a man often never seen, nor known, they both put
into his hands their own daughter and a dowry of much money. And they rejoice
doing this, and they do not think what is happening to be a loss; but seeing
the daughter being led away, they do not remember habit, they are not pained,
they are not stung, but they even give thanks, and they think it a work of
prayer even to see the daughter being led out of the house, and much money
with her. All these things therefore Paul considered, that both leaving the
parents they are bound to one another, and the then meeting becomes more
tyrannical than so much time of habit, and having seen that this is not human,
but God sowed these loves in, and prepared both those who give out and those
who are given out to do this with joy, he says: This mystery is great. And
just as in the case of children, the little child having been born knows the
parents at once from the sight, not speaking; so also bridegroom and bride, no
one bringing them together, no one exhorting and advising, from the sight are
woven together with one another. Then having seen that this also came to be in
the case of Christ, and in the case of the Church especially, he was struck
and marveled. How then did this come to be in the case of Christ and the
Church? Just as the bridegroom leaving the father comes to the bride, so also
Christ leaving the paternal throne came to the bride; he did not call us up,
but he himself arrived to us. And when you hear that he left, do not
understand a change of place, but a condescension; for even being with us, he
was with the Father. Therefore he says: This mystery is great. It is great
even when it comes to be among men; but when I see it happening unto Christ
and unto the Church, then I am struck, then I marvel. Therefore having said,
This mystery is great, he added: But I speak, unto Christ, and unto the
Church. Knowing then how great a mystery marriage is, and of how great a thing
it is a type, do not counsel about this simply, nor as it happens, nor seek
abundance of money when you are about to take a bride. For one must think
marriage to be not huckstering, but a sharing of life.

δʹ. For I have heard many saying, So-and-so has become more well off from the
marriage, being poor; since he took a well off wife, he is rich and lives in
luxury now. What are you saying, O man? you desire to gain from a wife, and
you are not ashamed, nor do you blush; nor do you


sink into the earth seeking such ways of gains? and where are these the words
of a man? Of a woman there is one thing only, to guard the things collected,
to keep the revenues, to care for the house; for for this God also gave her,
that in these things she might help us along with all the others. For since
these two things are accustomed to compose our =51.231= life, the civic and
the private affairs, God having divided both of these assigned to her the
presidency of the house, and to the husbands all the affairs of the city, both
those in the marketplace, courts, councils, generalships, all the rest. The
woman cannot hurl a spear, nor send a dart; but she can take a distaff, and
weave a loom, and arrange well all the other things throughout the house. She
cannot introduce a motion in a council; but she can introduce a motion in a
house, and often what the husband understood of the things throughout the
house, she understood better. She cannot arrange the public things well; but
she can rear children well, the capital of the possessions; she can see
through the knaveries of maidservants, and take care of the temperance of
those who serve, furnish all the rest of freedom from care to the one living
with her, and free him from every such thought in the houses, caring for
store-rooms, wool-work, preparation of breakfast, seemliness of garments, all
the other things, which it is neither seemly nor easy for a man ever to
handle, even if he contends myriads of times. For this too is a work of the
munificence and wisdom of God, that the one useful in the greater things be
inferior and useless in the lesser, that the need of the wife may become
necessary. For if he had made the man fit in both, the race of women would
have become contemptible; again, if he had entrusted the greater and more
useful to the woman, he would have filled the women with much madness.
Therefore he neither gave both to one, that the other race might not be
diminished, and seem to be superfluous; nor did he distribute both equally to
each, that again from the equal honor some battle and contention might not
come to be, the women contending to be thought worthy of the same presidency
as the men; but at once both providing for peace, and keeping the fitting rank
for each, having divided our life into these two, he gave the more necessary
and more useful to the man, and the lesser and inferior to the woman; that he
through the necessity of the need might be much sought after by us, and she
through the inferiority of the service might not rise up against the one
living with her. All therefore knowing these things, let us seek one thing
only, virtue of soul and nobility of ways, that we may enjoy peace, that we
may luxuriate in concord and continual love. For he who has taken a well off
wife has taken a mistress rather than a wife. For if, even apart from this,
women are filled with pride, and easily fall toward a love of glory, if they
also take this addition, how will they be bearable to those who live with
them? But he who has taken a wife of equal honor or poorer has taken a helper
and ally, and has brought all the good things into the house. For the
necessity of poverty persuades her to serve the husband with much care, and to
yield and obey in all things, and takes away every occasion of strife, and
battle, and madness, and insolence; and becomes a bond of peace, and concord,
and love, and agreement. Let us not then seek this, how we may have money, but
how peace, how we may enjoy pleasure; for marriage is for this, not that we
may fill the houses with war and battle, not that we may have strifes and
contentions, not that we may be at faction with one another, and make life
unlivable, but that we may enjoy help, and have a harbor and =51.232= a
refuge, and a consolation of the evils that lie upon us, that we may converse
with the wife with pleasure. How many, being rich,


having taken well off wives, having increased the substance, have dissolved
the pleasure and the concord, making daily battles at the table, having
contentions? how many poor, having taken poorer women, enjoy peace, and with
much good cheer look upon this sun? but the well off, luxury lying about them
from every side, have often wished because of the wives to die, and to be
released from the present life? so there is no profit of money, when we do not
happen upon a good soul. And what need is there to speak about peace and
concord? For even unto the very acquisition of money, taking a richer one has
often harmed us. For when, having given over all the surplus toward the
account of the dowry, then an untimely death having happened, he is forced by
the in-laws to pay down the whole dowry; just as those who suffer shipwreck
upon the sea save the body only, so also this one, after many contentions, and
battles, and insolences, and courts, scarcely comes out having the body free.
And just as the insatiable of the merchants, having filled the ship with
myriads of cargoes, and having set on a bulk more than the power, have sunk
the vessel, and lost all; so also these, taking oversized marriages, having
often seemed to throw about themselves a greater substance through the wife,
have fallen even from what they had; and just as there a small onset of a wave
having fallen in baptizes the vessel, so also here an untimely death having
fallen upon him has taken away with the wife even all the things that were
his.

εʹ. All these things therefore considering, let us not look about for money,
but reasonableness of ways, and gravity and temperance. For a temperate wife,
and reasonable and moderate, even if she is poor, will be able to arrange the
poverty better than wealth; just as the corrupted, and unchaste, and
strife-loving, even if she finds myriads of treasures lying within, having
blown them out quicker than every wind, also throws the husband around with
myriads of disasters together with the poverty. Let us not then seek wealth,
but the one who will use well the things that are. First learn what the cause
of marriage is, and for the sake of what it has been brought into our life,
and demand nothing more. What then is the occasion of marriage, and for the
sake of what did God give it? Hear Paul saying: But because of the
fornications let each have his own wife. He did not say, But because of
release from poverty, or Because of the acquisition of abundance; but what?
That we may flee fornication, that we may put down desire, that we may live
together with temperance, that we may please God, being content with our own
wife. This is the gift of marriage, this the fruit, this the gain. Do not then
leaving the greater things seek the lesser; for wealth is much less than
temperance. For for this one thing only must one take a wife, that we may flee
the sin, that we may be released from all fornication; toward this must all
marriage be established, that it may work together with us unto temperance;
and this will be, if we take such brides as are able to bring in to us much
reverence, and much temperance, and much reasonableness. For the well-form of
the body, when it does not have the virtue of the soul yoked with it, will be
able to take the husband both twenty and thirty days, but it will not go
further, but having shown the vice, it will dissolve all the affection; but
those who shine with the beauty =51.233= of the soul, the more the time goes
forward, and they furnish trial of their own nobility, so much the more they
make the love hotter for their own husbands, and kindle the love toward him.
And this being, and a hot and genuine friendship lying between, every kind of
fornication is driven out, and not even some thought of unchastity will ever
fall upon the one who loves his own wife, but he remains continually loving
his own and through


the temperance drawing God unto the goodwill and protection of the whole
house. Thus the noble of the men of old took wives, seeking nobility of soul,
and not abundance of money. And that this is true, for the sake of an example,
I will mention one marriage: And Abraham was older and advanced in days, he
says, and he said to his servant, the elder of his house, the ruler of all his
things: Put your hand under my thigh, and I will adjure you by the Lord the
God of heaven and of earth, that you not take a wife for my son Isaac from the
daughters of the Canaanites, with whom I dwell with them, but into my land
where I was born you shall go, and into my tribe, and you shall take a wife
for my son from there. Did you see the virtue of the righteous one, how much
provision he made concerning marriage? For he did not, having called procuress
women, as those now, nor matchmakers, nor old wives telling tales, but his own
servant, to this one he entrusted the matter; which itself also is a very
great sign of the reverence of the patriarch, that he prepared the servant
such that he thought him a trustworthy minister even of such a matter. Then he
seeks a wife, not well off, nor well formed, but noble in ways, and for this
he sends him forth so great a road of travel. And see also the good sense of
the servant; for he did not say, What ever is this? so many nations near us,
so many daughters of well off men, of notable and illustrious; but you send to
so far a land, to unknown men; to whom shall I speak? who will know me? and
what if they stitch a trick for me, if they work a deceit? for nothing is so
easy to attempt as a stranger. He said none of these things, but having
overlooked all these, what especially was to be suspected, this he suspected,
from answering nothing back to him displaying the obedience, and from asking
only that which most of all it was necessary to seek, showing his own
understanding and provision. What ever then is this? and what did he ask his
own master? But if the woman is not willing, he says, to go with me, shall I
turn your son back into the land from which you came out? Then Abraham: Do not
turn my son back there. The Lord the God of heaven and of earth, who took me
from the house of my father, and from the land in which I was born, who spoke
to me and swore to me saying: To you I will give this land and to your seed,
he himself will send his angel before you, and will prosper your way. Do you
see faith of a man? He did not call upon friends, or kinsmen, not anyone else,
but he gave him God as mediator and fellow traveler. Then wishing to encourage
the servant, he did not simply say, The Lord the God of heaven and of earth,
but he adds, Who took me from the house of my father. Remember, he says, how
we made so great a =51.234= journey, how having left our own we enjoyed more
prosperity upon the foreign, how the impossible things became possible. And
not only wishing to show this did he say, Who took me from the house of my
father, but that he also has God as a debtor. He is a debtor to us, he says;
he himself said, To you I will give this land and to your seed. So that even
if we are unworthy, at least because of his own promise, and that he may bring
it to an end, he will be present with us, and will make easy all the things
set before us, and will set an end upon this which we pray. Having said these
things he sent the servant. Then that one having come into that country, did
not approach any of those inhabiting the city, did not converse with men, did
not call women; but see how he too being faithful held the mediator whom he
received, conversed with him alone, and having stood he prayed, he says, And
he said, Lord the God of my lord Abraham, prosper before me today; and he did
not say, Lord my God, but what? Lord the God of my lord Abraham. Even if I am
cheap and cast away, he says,


yet I put forward my master; for I have come not ministering to myself, but to
him; having then respected his virtue, help me accomplish all that is set
before me.

ςʹ. Then that you may not think that he demands it as a debt, hear also what
follows: And do mercy with my lord Abraham. Even if we have achieved myriads
of things, he says, we ask to be saved by grace, and to receive this from
loving-kindness, not from some due and debt. What then do you wish? Behold I
