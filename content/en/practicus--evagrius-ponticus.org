OF EVAGRIUS THE MONK

Since you recently made known to me from the holy mountain, while I was
sitting in Scetis, most longed-for brother Anatolius, and asked that the
symbolic habit of the monks in Egypt be made clear to you; for you have not
considered it idle or superfluous, having so great a difference from the other
habits of men; come, let us declare as much as we have learned about this from
the holy Fathers.

The cowl is a symbol of the grace of our Savior God covering their ruling
faculty and cherishing the infancy in Christ because of those who are always
trying to strike and wound. Those then who wear this on the head in power
chant these things: Unless the Lord build a house and keep a city, in vain has
the builder labored and the one trying to keep it. And such utterances produce
humility, and they uproot pride, the ancient evil, which shook down to the
earth Lucifer who rises at dawn.

And the baring of the hands shows the unhypocritical character of the way of
life; for vainglory is terrible at covering and shading the virtues, always
hunting the glories from men and driving away faith. For how can you believe,
he says, receiving glory from one another, and not seeking the glory from the
only God? For the good must be choiceworthy not for the sake of another thing
but rather for itself; for if this is not granted, that which moves us toward
the working of the good will appear much more honorable than what is produced,
which would be among the most absurd things, to think and to say that
something is better than God.

And the analabos again, which is wound crosswise about their shoulders, is a
symbol of the faith in Christ that takes up the meek and always gathers in
what hinders and furnishes them unimpeded work. And the belt tightening their
loins drives away all uncleanness and commands this: It is good for a man not
to touch a woman. And they have the sheepskin who always carry about the dying
of Jesus in the body and muzzle all the irrational passions of the body, and
by sharing in the good cut away the vices of the soul; and loving poverty, and
fleeing greed as mother of idolatry. And the staff is a tree of life to all
who hold it, and safe for those who lean on it as on the Lord.

And of these things the habit is a symbol, as in an epitome of the realities;
and these are the words which the Fathers always say to them: Faith, O
children, is confirmed by the fear of God, and this again by self-control, and
endurance and hope make this unswerving, from which impassibility is born,
whose offspring is love, and love is a door of natural knowledge, which
theology and the last blessedness succeed. And concerning the sacred habit and
the teaching of the elders let this much be said by us for the present. And
concerning the life, both the practical and the gnostic, we now give an
account, not as much as we have seen or heard, but as much as we have learned
from them to tell also to others, dividing the practical things briefly into a
hundred chapters, and the gnostic into fifty besides six hundred; and some
things hiding, and some shading, so that we may not give the holy things to
the dogs nor throw the pearls before the swine. And these things will be
manifest to those who have stepped into the same track as they.



OF THE SAME, A PRACTICAL DISCOURSE, CHAPTERS 100

1 Christianity is the doctrine of our Savior Christ, consisting of the
practical and the natural and the theological. 2 The kingdom of heaven is
impassibility of soul with true knowledge of the things that are.

3 The kingdom of God is knowledge of the Holy Trinity coextensive with the
constitution of the intellect, and surpassing its incorruption.

4 Whatever one loves, that he also longs for entirely, and what he longs for,
that he also strives to obtain; and desire is the beginning of every pleasure,
and sensation begets desire; for what is without share in sensation is also
free of passion.

5 To the anchorites the demons wrestle naked, but to those working virtue in
coenobia or in companies they arm the more careless of the brethren; and the
second war is much lighter than the first, because it is not possible to find
on earth men more bitter than demons, or men who receive all their malice at
once.

t6-14 On the eight thoughts

6 There are eight in all the most generic thoughts, in which every thought is
contained. First that of gluttony, and after it that of fornication; third
that of love of money; fourth that of grief; fifth that of anger; sixth that
of acedia; seventh that of vainglory; eighth that of pride. That these all
disturb the soul or do not disturb it is not in our power; but that they
linger or do not linger, or move passions or do not move them, is in our
power.

7 The thought of gluttony suggests to the monk a swift fall from ascesis;
depicting stomach and liver and spleen and dropsy, and a long disease, and
scarcity of necessities, and want of physicians. And it often also brings him
to the memory of certain brethren who have fallen into these passions. And
sometimes it even persuades those who have suffered to approach those who
practice self-control, and to recount their own misfortunes, and that they
have become such from the ascesis.

8 The demon of fornication constrains one to desire various bodies; and it
stands more vehemently against those who practice self-control, so that they
may cease as accomplishing nothing; and defiling the soul it bends it down
toward those works; and it makes it both say certain words and hear them
again, as if the thing were seen and present.

9 Love of money suggests a long old age and inability of the hands for work,
and famines that will come and diseases that will happen, and the bitter
things of poverty, and that it is shameful to receive from others the things
needed.

10 Grief sometimes occurs according to deprivation of desires, and sometimes
also follows upon anger. And according to deprivation of desires it occurs
thus: certain thoughts going before bring the soul to memory of house and of
parents and of the former way of life. And when they see it not resisting but
following and spreading itself in the pleasures according to the mind, then
taking it they plunge it in grief, as the former things no longer existing nor
being able any longer to exist because of the present life; and the wretched
soul, as much as it was spread out upon the former thoughts, so much is it
contracted upon the second, having been humbled.



11 Anger is a most sharp passion; for it is called a boiling and movement of
wrath against the one who has wronged or seems to have wronged; which
throughout the day makes the soul savage, and especially in the prayers it
snatches the intellect, mirroring the face of the one who has caused grief.
And sometimes remaining long and changing into resentment, it furnishes
disturbances by night, and wasting of the body and paleness, and onsets of
venomous beasts. And these four things that happen after resentment, one would
find accompanying more thoughts.

12 The demon of acedia, which is also called noonday, 12 is the heaviest of
all the demons; and it stands by the monk about the fourth hour, and it
circles his soul until the eighth hour. And first it makes the sun appear
slow-moving or motionless, showing the day as fifty hours. Then it forces one
to look continually toward the windows and to leap out of the cell, and to
stare at the sun how far it is from the ninth hour, and to look about here and
there whether any of the brethren. And further it casts hatred toward the
place and toward the life itself, and toward the work of the hands; and that
love has failed among the brethren and there is no one who consoles; and if
anyone in those days has caused grief to the monk, the demon adds this also to
the increase of the hatred. And it also brings him to desire of other places
in which it is easy to find the things needed and to pursue a craft more
comfortable and prosperous; and it adds that it is not in a place that one is
well-pleasing to the Lord; for everywhere, it says, the divine is to be
worshipped. And it joins to these also memory of the household and of the
former way of life; and it sketches a long time of life, bringing the labors
of ascesis before the eyes; and it moves every device, as the saying is, so
that the monk, having left the cell, may flee the stadium. After this demon
another demon does not follow immediately; but a peaceful state and
unspeakable joy succeed the soul after the contest.

13 The thought of vainglory is a most subtle one and it stands beside those
who succeed, easily wanting to make their contests public and hunting the
glories from men, and fabricating demons crying out and women being healed and
a crowd touching the garments; and it prophesies priesthood to him hereafter
and sets those seeking him at the doors; and that if he should not be willing
he will be led away bound. And having thus made him airborne with empty hopes
it flies away, leaving him either for the demon of pride to try him or for
that of grief, which also brings him thoughts opposing the hopes; and
sometimes it even hands over to the demon of fornication the one who a little
before was a bound and holy priest.

14 The demon of pride becomes the cause of a most grievous fall to the soul;
for it persuades it not to confess God as helper, but to think itself the
cause of the things accomplished and to be puffed up against the brethren as
fools, because not all know this about it. And there follow upon this anger
and grief, and the last evil, derangement of mind and madness and a multitude
of demons seen in the air.

t15-33 Against the eight thoughts

15 Reading and vigil and prayer still a wandering intellect; hunger and toil
and withdrawal wither a desire that is flaming; psalmody and long-suffering
and mercy still a wrath that is being stirred; and these things



being done at the fitting times and measures; for the unmeasured and untimely
are short-lived; and the short-lived are rather harmful and not beneficial.

16 Whenever our soul longs for various foods, then let it be straitened in
bread and water so that it may become thankful even for the mere morsel
itself; for satiety desires various meats, but hunger thinks the satiety of
bread to be blessedness.

17 Want of water contributes greatly toward chastity; and let the three
hundred of the Israelites with Gideon who mastered Midian persuade you.

18 As it is among things not possible that life and death happen together to
the same, so it is among things impossible that love coexist with money for
anyone; for love is destructive not only of money, but even of this our
temporal life itself. 19 The one who flees all worldly pleasures is a tower
inaccessible to the demon of grief; for grief is deprivation of pleasure
either present or expected; and it is impossible to drive this enemy away
while we have attachment toward any of the earthly things; for there he sets
the snare and works the grief where he sees us especially inclined.

20 Anger and hatred increase wrath; but almsgiving and meekness diminish even
what is there.

21 Let not the sun go down upon our provocation, lest the demons standing by
at night terrify the soul and make the intellect more cowardly for the war on
the following day; for the fearful phantasms are wont to come from the
disturbance of wrath; and nothing else so makes the intellect a deserter as
wrath being disturbed.

22 Whenever our irascible part of the soul, having laid hold of a pretext, is
disturbed, then the demons also suggest withdrawal to us as a good, so that we
may not, having resolved the causes of the grief, free ourselves from
disturbance. But when the desiring part is heated, then again they make us
humane, calling us hard and savage, so that desiring bodies we may meet with
bodies. To whom we must not be persuaded; but rather do the opposite.

23 Do not give yourself to the thought of anger in the mind, fighting with the
one who has caused grief; nor again to that of fornication, imagining the
pleasure at length; for the one darkens the soul, and the other calls it
toward the burning of the passion; and both make your intellect filthy; and
imagining the images at the time of prayer and not offering a pure prayer to
God you immediately fall in with the demon of acedia; which especially leaps
upon such states and like a dog snatches the soul as a fawn.

24 The nature of wrath is to fight the demons and to contend for any pleasure
whatsoever. Therefore the angels, suggesting to us the spiritual pleasure and
the blessedness from this, exhort us to turn the wrath against the demons; but
those again, drawing us toward worldly desires, force the wrath contrary to
nature to fight against men, so that the intellect, darkened and fallen from
knowledge, may become a betrayer of the virtues.

25 Take heed to yourself lest you ever put to flight one of the brethren
having provoked him, and you will not escape in your life the demon of grief,
becoming always a stumbling-block to you at the time of prayer.



26 Gifts quench remembrance of wrongs; and let Jacob persuade you, who, having
gone to meet with gifts, met Esau coming out with four hundred. But we, being
poor, will fulfill the need with a table.

27 When we fall in with the demon of acedia, then dividing the soul with tears
let us make one part the one that consoles and the other the one that is
consoled, sowing good hopes for ourselves and charming with the word of holy
David: Why are you very grieved, O my soul, and why do you trouble me? Hope in
God, for I will confess to him; the salvation of my face and my God.

28 One must not at the time of temptations leave the cell, fabricating
seemingly reasonable pretexts, but sit inside and endure and receive nobly all
that come, and especially the demon of acedia, which being heaviest above all
makes the soul especially most proven. For fleeing such contests and going
around them teaches the intellect unskilled and cowardly and a runaway.

29 And our holy and most practical teacher used to say: 29 thus must the monk
always prepare himself as one who will die tomorrow, and thus again use the
body as one who will live together with it for many years. For the one, he
says, cuts away the thoughts of acedia and prepares the monk more eager; and
the other keeps the body sound and always maintains its self-control equal.

30 It is hard to escape the thought of vainglory; for what you do for its
destruction becomes for you a beginning of another vainglory. And the demons
do not oppose every right thought of ours, but to some these vices themselves
also oppose, according to which we have been qualified.

31 I have known the demon of vainglory pursued by almost all the demons and
standing shamelessly upon the falls of those pursuing, and displaying to the
monk a greatness of virtues.

32 The one who has laid hold of knowledge and is harvesting the pleasure from
it will no longer be persuaded by the demon of vainglory, bringing him all the
pleasures of the world; for what greater thing could it even promise than
spiritual contemplation? But while we are without taste of knowledge, let us
work the practical life eagerly, showing our aim to God that we do all things
for the sake of his knowledge.

33 Remember your former life and the ancient transgressions, and how being
passionate you have passed over to impassibility by the mercy of Christ, and
how again you went out from the world which humbled you much and often. And
consider this also for me: who is the one who keeps you in the desert, and who
is the one who drives away the demons gnashing their teeth against you. For
such thoughts produce humility, and they do not receive the demon of pride.

34-39 On passions

34 Of those things whose memories we have as passionate, of these we also
first received the things themselves with passion; and again, as many of the
things as we receive with passion, of these we will also have the memories
passionate. Whence the one who has conquered the demons that are at work
despises the things worked by them; for the immaterial war is harder than the
material.



35 The passions of the soul have their starting-points from men; and those of
the body from the body; and self-control cuts away the passions of the body,
and spiritual love those of the soul.

36 Those who preside over the psychic passions persist until death; but those
of the bodily withdraw more quickly; and the other demons are like a sun
rising or setting, laying hold of some one part of the soul; but the noonday
one is accustomed to encompass the whole soul and to choke the intellect.
Therefore withdrawal is sweet after the emptying of the passions; for then the
memories are only bare; and the wrestling no longer prepares the monk for
contest, but for contemplation of it.

37 Whether the thought moves the passions, or the passions the thought, one
must attend; for to some the former seemed so, and to some the latter.

38 The passions are by nature moved by the senses; and when love and
self-control are present they will not be moved, but when absent they will be
moved; and wrath needs more medicines than desire, and for this reason love is
called great because it is a bridle of wrath; and that holy Moses in the
natural things named it symbolically a fighter of serpents.

39 Toward the prevailing stench among the demons the soul is accustomed to be
kindled toward thoughts, when it perceives them drawing near, having been
qualified by the passion of the one that is disturbing.

t40- 53 Counsels

40 It is not possible at every time to complete the customary canon, but one
must attend to the time and try as much as possible to do the commandments
that are possible; for the demons themselves also are not ignorant of such
times. Whence being moved against us, they hinder the things that can be done,
and they compel us to do the things that cannot be done; for they prevent
those who are sick from giving thanks in the pains and being long-suffering
toward those who serve; and again they urge those who are exhausted to
practice self-control and those who are weighed down to stand and chant.

41 When we are compelled to spend a little time in cities or in villages, then
especially holding more vehemently to self-control we will be with the
worldly, lest our intellect, having become thick and deprived of the customary
care because of the present time, do something of the unwilling things and
become a fugitive, being struck by the demons.

42 You will not pray first when being tempted, before saying certain words
with anger toward the one who is afflicting; for your soul having been
qualified by the thoughts, it happens that the prayer also is not pure; but if
you say something with anger toward them, you both confuse and make vanish the
thoughts of the opposing ones. For this anger is also by nature able to work
upon the better thoughts.

43 And one must also recognize the differences of the demons, and note their
times; and we will know from the thoughts, and the thoughts from the things,
which of the demons are rare and heavier, and which are continual and lighter,
and which are those that leap in suddenly and snatch the intellect toward
blasphemy. And it is necessary to know these things, so that when the thoughts
begin to move their own matters, before we are thrown far from our own state,
we may utter something toward them and indicate the one who is present; for
thus we ourselves also may easily with God



make progress, and make those, marveling at us and being in pain, fly away.

44 When the demons, contending against the monks, are unable, then having
withdrawn a little they watch which of the virtues has been neglected in the
meantime, and having entered suddenly by that they snatch the wretched soul.

45 The wicked demons draw the more wicked demons among them to help; and being
opposed to one another according to the dispositions, they agree only upon
destruction of soul. 46 And let not the demon that snatches the intellect
toward blasphemy of God and toward those forbidden fantasies which I have not
even dared to hand over to writing disturb us, nor cut off our eagerness; for
the Lord is knower of hearts and knows that not even when we were in the world
did we ever rage with such madness. And the aim of this demon is to make us
cease from prayer, so that we may not stand opposite the Lord our God, nor
dare to stretch out the hands against whom we have thought such things.

47 Of the passions in the soul a symbol becomes either some word brought
forth, or a movement of the body that has happened, through which the enemies
perceive whether we have their thoughts within and are in labor, or having
thrown them off we are concerned about our salvation. For God who made us
alone knows the intellect, and he himself does not need symbols in order to
know the things hidden in the heart.

48 To those in the world the demons wrestle rather through the things, but to
the monks for the most part through the thoughts; for they are deprived of
things because of the desert; and as much as it is easier to sin according to
the mind than according to deed, so much harder also is the war according to
the mind than that constituted through the things; for the intellect is a
thing easily moved and hard to hold toward the lawless fantasies.

49 We have not been commanded to work always and to keep vigil and to fast,
but it has been legislated for us to pray without ceasing; because those
things, healing the passionate part of the soul, also need our body for the
work, which through its own weakness does not suffice for the labors; but
prayer prepares the intellect strong and pure for the wrestling, being by
nature able to pray even without this body and to fight the demons beyond all
the powers of the soul.

50 If any of the monks should wish to be tried by savage demons and to get a
habit of their craft, let him keep the thoughts, and note the intensifications
of these, and the relaxations, and the interweavings, and the times, and which
of the demons are the ones doing this, and which demon follows which, and
which does not follow which; and let him seek from Christ the reasons of
these. For they are very angry at those who pursue the practical life more
gnostically, wanting to shoot in a moonless night those who are straight in
heart.

51 Observing you will find two of the demons most swift, and almost outrunning
the movement of our intellect: the demon of fornication and the one that
snatches us toward blasphemy of God; but the second is short-lived, and the
former, if it should not move the thoughts with passion, will not hinder us
toward the knowledge of God.

52 To separate body from soul belongs only to the one who bound them; but soul
from body, also to the one who longs for virtue. For our Fathers name
withdrawal a meditation of death and a flight of the body.



53 Those who nourish the flesh badly and make provision of it unto desires,
let them blame themselves, not this; for they know the grace of the Creator
who have acquired the impassibility of the soul through this body and who in
some measure apply themselves to the contemplation of the things that are.
t5456 On the things that happen in sleep

54 When in the fantasies according to sleep the demons, warring against the
desiring part, themselves show, and we run toward them, meetings of
acquaintances and drinking-parties of kinsmen and choruses of women and as
many other such things as complete pleasures, we are sick in this part and the
passion has strength. But when again they disturb the irascible part,
compelling us to travel cliff-paths, and bringing on armed men and venomous
and flesh-eating beasts, and we are terrified toward the paths, and being
pursued by the beasts and the men we flee, let us make provision of the
irascible part, and calling upon Christ in vigils, let us use the medicines
said before.

55 The imageless natural movements of the body in sleep indicate that the soul
is in some measure healthy; but a fixing of images is a mark of sickness; and
consider the undefined faces of the old passion, and the defined a symbol of
the immediate blow.

56 The proofs of impassibility we will recognize by day through the thoughts,
and by night through the dreams; and we will say that impassibility is health
of soul, and knowledge food, which alone is accustomed to join us to the holy
powers; if indeed the conjunction of the incorporeals is by nature from the
like disposition. t5762 On a state approaching impassibility

57 There are two peaceful states of the soul, one that is given forth from the
natural seeds, and another that comes about from withdrawal of the demons; and
to the former there follows humility with compunction, and a tear and infinite
longing toward the divine, and unmeasured eagerness about the work; and to the
second vainglory with pride, in the destruction of the remaining demons,
dragging the monk down. He then who keeps the bounds of the former state will
recognize more sharply the onsets of the demons. 58 The demon of vainglory is
opposed to the demon of fornication, and it is among things not possible for
these to attack a soul at the same time; if indeed the one promises honors,
and the other becomes a cause of dishonors; whichever then of these, having
drawn near, presses you, fabricate in yourself supposedly the thoughts of the
opposing demon; and if you are able to drive out the nail with the nail, as
the saying is, know that you are near the bounds of impassibility; for your
intellect has been strong enough to make vanish thoughts of demons by human
thoughts. But to drive away the thought of vainglory through humility, or that
of fornication through chastity, would be a proof of deepest impassibility.
And try to do this upon all the demons opposed to one another; for at the same
time you will also know by which passion you have been more qualified. Yet as
much as is in your power ask from God to ward off the enemies by the second
manner.

59 As much as a soul progresses, so much greater antagonists succeed it; for I
am not persuaded that the same demons always remain with it; and those know
this especially who apply themselves more sharply to the temptations, and see
the impassibility present to them being pried up by those who have succeeded.
60 The perfect impassibility for the soul comes about after the victory
against all the demons opposed to the practical life; and the imperfect
impassibility is said so far according to the power of the demon wrestling
with it.



61 The intellect would not go forth, nor will it sojourn that good sojourning,
and come to be in the country of the incorporeals, unless it has set right the
things within; for the disturbance of its own things is accustomed to turn it
back toward the things from which it has gone out.

62 Both the virtues and the vices make the intellect blind; the former, so
that it may not see the vices; and the latter, so that it may not again see
the virtues.

t63-70 On the signs of impassibility

63 When the intellect begins to make the prayers without distraction, then the
whole war is constituted about the irascible part of the soul by night and by
day. 64 A proof of impassibility, an intellect that has begun to see its own
light, and remaining quiet toward the phantasms according to sleep, and seeing
the things evenly.

65 An intellect is strong that imagines nothing of the things of this world at
the time of prayer.

66 An intellect that with God has succeeded in the practical life and has
drawn near to knowledge little or not at all perceives the irrational part of
the soul, the knowledge snatching it aloft and separating it from the
sensibles.

67 A soul has impassibility, not the one that does not suffer toward the
things, but the one that remains also undisturbed toward the memories of them.

68 The perfect one does not practice self-control, and the impassible one does
not endure, if indeed endurance is of the one who suffers, and self-control of
the one who is troubled.

69 It is a great thing to pray without distraction, but a greater to chant
also without distraction.

70 The one who has established the virtues in himself, and has been wholly
mixed with them, no longer remembers law or commandments or punishment, but
says and does as many things as the best habit dictates.

t70-90 Practical contemplations

71 οαʹ The demonic songs move our desire, and throw the soul into shameful
fantasies; but the psalms and hymns and the spiritual songs always call the
intellect to memory of virtue, cooling our boiling wrath and withering the
desires.

72 If those who wrestle are in being afflicted and afflicting in return, and
the demons wrestle with us, then they also, afflicting us, are afflicted in
return by us. For I will squeeze them out, he says, and they shall not be able
to stand. And again: those who afflict me and my enemies, they themselves were
weakened and fell.

73 Rest is yoked to wisdom, but toil to prudence; for it is not possible to
acquire wisdom without war, and it is not possible to succeed in the war
without prudence; for this has been entrusted to stand against the wrath of
the demons, compelling the powers of the soul to work according to nature, and
preparing beforehand the way of wisdom.

74 A temptation of a monk is a thought that has gone up through the passionate
part of the soul and darkens the intellect.

75 A sin of a monk is the consent of the thought toward the forbidden
pleasure.



76 Angels rejoice when vice is diminished, but demons when virtue is; for the
former are servants of mercy and love, and the latter are obedient to anger
and hatred; and the former, drawing near, fill us with spiritual
contemplation, and the latter, approaching, throw the soul into shameful
fantasies.

77 The virtues do not check the onsets of the demons, but they keep us
unharmed.

78 Practice is a spiritual method purifying the passionate part of the soul.

79 The workings of the commandments are not sufficient for perfectly healing
the powers of the soul, unless also suitable contemplations succeed the
intellect corresponding to these.

80 It is not possible to stand against all the thoughts thrown into us by
angels, but it is possible to overturn all the thoughts from demons; and a
peaceful state follows the former thoughts, and a disturbed one the latter.

81 Love is offspring of impassibility; and impassibility is a flower of the
practical life; and the keeping of the commandments constitutes the practical
life; and the guardian of these is the fear of God, which is an offspring of
right faith; and faith is an inward good, which is by nature present even in
those who have not yet believed in God.

82 As the soul, working through the body, perceives the members that are sick;
so also the intellect, working its own activity, both recognizes its own
powers and through the one that hinders it discovers the healing commandment
of it. 83 The intellect warring the passionate war will not contemplate the
reasons of the war; for it is like one fighting in the night; but having
acquired impassibility, it will easily recognize the methods of the enemies.

84 The end of the practical life is love; and of knowledge, theology; and the
beginnings of each are faith and natural contemplation; and as many of the
demons as lay hold of the passionate part of the soul, these are said to be
opposed to the practical life; and as many again as trouble the rational part
are named enemies of all truth and contrary to contemplation.

85 Nothing of the things that purify the bodies remains after this with those
who have been purified; but the virtues both purify the soul together and
remain with it when it has been purified. 86 A rational soul works according
to nature when its desiring part longs for virtue, and the irascible part
contends for it, and the rational part applies itself to the contemplation of
the things that have come to be.

87 The one who progresses in the practical life diminishes the passions, and
the one in contemplation, ignorance; and of the passions there will sometime
also be complete destruction, but of ignorance they say of one kind there is
an end, and of another there is not.

88 The things that according to use are good and evil become productive of the
virtues and of the vices; and it belongs to prudence thereafter to use these
toward the one or the other. 89 The rational soul being tripartite according
to our wise teacher, when the virtue comes to be in the rational part, it is
called prudence and understanding and wisdom; and when in the desiring part,
chastity and love and self-control; and when in the irascible part, courage
and endurance; and in the whole soul, justice. And the work of prudence is to
take the field against the opposing powers, and to shield the virtues, and to
array against the vices, and to administer the intermediates according to the
times; and of understanding, to manage fittingly all the things that
contribute to us toward the aim; and of wisdom, to contemplate the reasons of
bodies and of incorporeals; and the work of chastity, to look impassibly at
the things that move irrational fantasies in us; and of love, to present
itself to every image of God such as almost also to the archetype, even if the
demons try to defile them; and of self-control,



to shake off every pleasure of the throat with joy; and not to fear the
enemies and to endure the terrible things eagerly belongs to endurance and to
courage; and of justice, to work a certain agreement and harmony of the parts
of the soul.

90 The fruit of seeds is the sheaves, and of virtues, knowledge; and as tears
follow the seeds, so joy the sheaves.

t91100 Sayings of holy monks

91 And it is necessary also to inquire closely the ways of the monks who have
gone before rightly and to succeed according to them; for it is possible to
find many things both said and done well by them; among which one of them also
says this, that the drier and not uneven diet, yoked with love, more quickly
leads the monk into the harbor of impassibility. And the same delivered one
of the brethren who was being disturbed at night from the phantasms, having
commanded him to serve the sick with fasting; for by nothing so, he said when
asked, are such passions quenched as by mercy.

92 One of the wise men of that time approached the righteous Antony and said,
How do you endure, father, being deprived of the consolation from books? And
he says: My book, philosopher, is the nature of the things that have come to
be, and it is present whenever I wish to read the reasons of God.

93 The Egyptian elder Macarius, the vessel of election, asked me: Why ever,
remembering wrongs against men, do we destroy the memorial power of the soul,
but remaining remembering wrongs against demons we remain unharmed? And I
being at a loss toward the answer and asking to learn the word, because, he
says, the former is contrary to nature, and the latter is according to nature
of wrath. 94 I visited the holy father Macarius at the very standing noon and
being greatly burned by thirst I asked to drink water; and he says: Be content
with the shade; for many now traveling or sailing are deprived even of this.
Then as I was exercising words toward him concerning self-control: Take
courage, he says, O child, in twenty whole years I have received satiety
neither of bread, nor of water, nor of sleep; for I used to eat my bread by
weight, and I used to drink the water by measure, and leaning myself against
the walls I used to snatch a little part of sleep.

95 Death of his father was announced to one of the monks; and he to the one
who reported, Cease, he says, blaspheming; for my father is immortal.

96 One of the brethren inquired of one of the elders whether he would bid him
to eat with his mother and his sisters when he visited the house; and he said,
With a woman you shall not eat.

97 One of the brethren possessed a gospel only, and having sold this he gave
it for food to those who hungered, uttering a word worthy of memory; for I
have sold, he says, the very word that says to me: Sell your possessions and
give to the poor.

98 And there is an island beside Alexandria lying at the very northern part of
the lake called Maria, and there dwells by it a monk of the camp of the
gnostics, the most proven; who declared that all the things done by the monks
are done for five causes: because of God, because of nature, because of
custom, because of necessity, because of the works of the hands. And the same
said again that virtue is one by nature, but it is given form in the powers of
the soul; for the solar light also



is unshaped, he says, but it is by nature conformed to the windows through
which it enters.

99 And another again of the monks: For this reason I take away the pleasures,
he said, so that I may cut away the pretexts of wrath; for I know it always
fighting for the pleasures and disturbing my intellect and driving away
knowledge. And one of the elders used to say that love does not know how to
keep deposits of foods or of money. And the same: I do not know, he says, that
I have been deceived by demons twice in the same. 100 It is not possible to
love all the brethren equally, but it is possible to meet with all impassibly,
being free of remembrance of wrongs and of hatred; the priests must be loved
after the Lord, who purify us through the holy mysteries and pray for us; and
our elders must be honored as the angels; for they themselves are the ones who
anoint us for the contests and heal the bites of the savage beasts. epi But
now let this much have been said by me to you concerning the practical life,
most longed-for brother Anatolius, as much as by the grace of the Holy Spirit
we have found gleaning in the seed while our grape is turning dark; but if the
sun of righteousness will shine upon us standing, and the cluster become ripe,
then we will also drink its wine that gladdens a man's heart, by prayers and
intercessions of the righteous Gregory who planted me and of the holy Fathers
now who water me, and by the power of Christ Jesus our Lord who increases me,
to whom be the glory and the might unto the ages of the ages. Amen.
