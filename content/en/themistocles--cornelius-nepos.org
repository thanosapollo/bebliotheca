* Caput 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Themistocles, son of Neocles, an Athenian. The faults of his early youth
were corrected by great virtues, so much so that no one is ranked above
him, and few are thought his equals. But he must be treated from the
beginning. His father Neocles was of good birth. He married a woman who
was a citizen of Acarnania, and from her Themistocles was born. Because
he was less approved by his parents, living too freely and neglecting
the family property, he was disinherited by his father. That insult did
not break him, but raised him up. For when he judged that it could not
be wiped out without the utmost industry, he gave himself wholly to
public affairs, serving his friends and his reputation more carefully.
He was much engaged in private lawsuits, and often came forward in the
assembly of the people. No greater business was done without him. He
quickly found what was needed and easily set it out in speech, and he
was no less ready in executing affairs than in conceiving them, because,
as Thucydides says, he judged most truly of present things and
conjectured most shrewdly of the future. By this it came about that in a
short time he became famous.

* Caput 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

The first step in taking up public affairs was the Corcyraean war. Made
praetor by the people to conduct it, he made the city more warlike not
only for the present war but for the time that followed. For when the
public money that came in from the mines was being wasted year by year
through the largess of the magistrates, he persuaded the people that
with that money a fleet of a hundred ships should be built. When this
was quickly completed, he first crushed the Corcyraeans, then, by
hunting down the pirates, made the sea safe. In this he both enriched
the Athenians and made them most skilled in naval war. How great a
salvation this was to all Greece was learned in the Persian war. For
when Xerxes was bringing war by sea and land against all Europe, he
invaded it with such forces as no one has had either before or since:
his fleet was of twelve hundred ships of war, followed by two thousand
transports, and the land army was seven hundred thousand infantry and
four hundred thousand cavalry. When the report of his coming had been
carried into Greece, and the Athenians especially were said to be aimed
at because of the battle of Marathon, they sent to Delphi to ask what
they should do about their affairs. To them as they deliberated the
Pythia answered that they should fortify themselves with wooden walls.
When no one understood what this response meant, Themistocles persuaded
them that Apollo's counsel was that they should carry themselves and
their goods onto ships: for that was what the god meant by the wooden
wall. When this plan was approved, they added as many triremes again to
those they already had, and they carried all their movable goods, some
to Salamis, some to Troezen. They handed the citadel over to the priests
and a few of the older men to tend the sacred rites, and they left the
rest of the town.

* Caput 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

This plan displeased most of the states, and they preferred to fight on
land. So chosen men were sent with Leonidas, king of the Lacedaemonians,
to occupy Thermopylae and not allow the barbarians to advance farther.
They could not withstand the force of the enemy, and all perished in
that place. But the common fleet of Greece, of three hundred ships, of
which two hundred were Athenian, first fought at Artemisium, between
Euboea and the mainland, with the king's seamen. For Themistocles sought
the narrows, lest they be surrounded by numbers. Although they had left
this fight on even terms, they still did not dare to remain in the same
place, because there was danger that if part of the enemy's ships
rounded Euboea they would be pressed by a double peril. So they left
Artemisium and stationed their fleet at Salamis, opposite Athens.

* Caput 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:

But Xerxes, after taking Thermopylae, at once approached the city, and,
none defending it, after killing the priests whom he had found in the
citadel, destroyed it by fire. Terrified by that blaze, the seamen, not
daring to remain, and most urging that they should go home and defend
themselves behind walls, Themistocles alone stood against them and said
that united they could be a match, while if scattered they would perish,
and he affirmed this to Eurybiades, king of the Lacedaemonians, who then
held the supreme command. When he moved him less than he wished, at
night he sent the most faithful of his slaves to the king, to tell him
in Themistocles' name that his enemies were in flight: if they departed,
he would finish the war with greater labor and in a longer time, since
he would be forced to hunt them down one by one; if he attacked them at
once, he would crush them all in a short time. The point of this was
that all should be forced, against their will, to fight it out. Hearing
this, the barbarian, believing no trick lay under it, on the next day
fought in a place most unfavorable to himself and most convenient for
the enemy, in a sea so narrow that the multitude of his ships could not
be deployed. He was therefore beaten more by the counsel of Themistocles
than by the arms of Greece.

* Caput 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:

Though he had fared badly, he still had such remnants of his forces that
even then he could have crushed the enemy with them. He was driven from
that footing a second time. For Themistocles, fearing that he would
persist in the war, informed him that a plan was afoot to break up the
bridge he had made on the Hellespont and shut him off from return to
Asia, and he persuaded him of it. So by the same road on which he had
marched in six months he returned to Asia in less than thirty days, and
judged that he had been not conquered but saved by Themistocles. Thus by
the prudence of one man Greece was freed, and Asia yielded to Europe.
This is a second victory that may be compared with the trophy of
Marathon. For in the same way at Salamis, with a small number of ships,
the greatest fleet within the memory of men was defeated.

* Caput 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

Themistocles was great in this war and no less in peace. For since the
Athenians were using the harbor of Phalerum, which was neither large nor
good, by his counsel the triple harbor of the Piraeus was established
and surrounded with walls, so that it equaled the city itself in dignity
and surpassed it in usefulness. The same man restored the walls of the
Athenians at especial risk to himself. For the Lacedaemonians, having
found a suitable pretext in the raids of the barbarians, by which they
denied that any city outside the Peloponnese ought to have walls, lest
there be fortified places which the enemy might occupy, tried to stop
the Athenians from building. This aimed at something far other than they
wished it to appear. For the Athenians, by the two victories of Marathon
and Salamis, had won such glory among all nations that the
Lacedaemonians understood there would be a contest with them for the
leadership. Therefore they wished them to be as weak as possible. When
they heard that walls were being built, they sent envoys to Athens to
forbid it. While these were present the Athenians stopped and said they
would send envoys to them about the matter. Themistocles undertook this
embassy and at first set out alone. He instructed the rest of the
envoys to leave only when the walls seemed built high enough for
defense; meanwhile all, slaves and free, should work and spare no place,
whether sacred or private or public, and gather from every side whatever
they thought fit for fortifying. So it came about that the walls of the
Athenians consisted of shrines and tombs.

* Caput 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:

When Themistocles came to Lacedaemon, he would not go to the magistrates
and took care to draw out the time as long as possible, putting forward
the excuse that he was waiting for his colleagues. When the
Lacedaemonians complained that the work was going on nonetheless and
that he was trying to deceive them in the matter, the rest of the
envoys arrived in the meantime. When he heard from them that not much of
the fortification remained, he went to the ephors of the Lacedaemonians,
in whose hands was the supreme power, and argued before them that false
reports had been brought to them: therefore it was fair that they send
good and noble men who could be trusted to look into the matter;
meanwhile they should keep him as a hostage. This was granted him, and
three envoys who had held the highest offices were sent to Athens. With
these Themistocles ordered his colleagues to set out, and he instructed
them not to let the Lacedaemonian envoys go before he himself had been
sent back. When he judged that these had reached Athens, he went to the
magistrates and senate of the Lacedaemonians and declared before them
most freely that the Athenians, by his counsel, as they might do by the
common law of nations, had enclosed with walls their public gods and
their ancestral household gods, so that they might more easily defend
them from the enemy, and that they had done nothing in this that was
useless to Greece. For their city stood as a bulwark set against the
barbarians, and at it the king's forces had already twice been wrecked.
The Lacedaemonians, however, were acting badly and unjustly in looking
rather to what was useful to their own domination than to what was
useful to all Greece. Therefore, if they wished to receive back their
own envoys whom they had sent to Athens, they should send him back,
since otherwise those men would never be received into their country.

* Caput 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:

Yet he did not escape the envy of his own citizens. For from the same
fear by which Miltiades had been condemned, he was driven from the state
by the votes of potsherds and withdrew to live at Argos. While he was
living there with great dignity because of his many virtues, the
Lacedaemonians sent envoys to Athens to accuse him in his absence of
having made an alliance with the Persian king to crush Greece. On this
charge he was condemned in absence. When he heard it, seeing that he was
not safe enough at Argos, he moved to Corcyra. There, when he noticed
that the chiefs of the island feared that because of him the
Lacedaemonians and Athenians would declare war on them, he fled to
Admetus, king of the Molossians, with whom he had no guest friendship.
When he had come there and the king was at the moment away, in order
that he might be received and protected with greater religious awe, he
seized the king's little son and threw himself with him into a shrine
that was worshipped with the highest ceremony. He did not leave it until
the king, giving him his right hand, received him into his protection,
which he kept. For when he was demanded publicly by the Athenians and
Lacedaemonians, he did not betray the suppliant, and he advised him to
look to his own safety: for it was hard for him to live safely in so
near a place. So he ordered him to be conducted to Pydna and gave him a
sufficient guard. Here he boarded a ship, unknown to all the sailors.
When it was being carried by a very great storm to Naxos, where the
Athenian army then was, Themistocles perceived that if he arrived there
he must perish. Forced by this necessity, he revealed to the master of
the ship who he was, promising much if he saved him. The man, moved by
pity for so famous a man, held the ship at anchor day and night out at
sea far from the island, and allowed no one to leave it. From there he
came to Ephesus and landed Themistocles. To him Themistocles later
returned thanks according to his deserts.

* Caput 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:

I know that most have written that Themistocles crossed into Asia while
Xerxes was still reigning. But I trust Thucydides most of all, because
he was both nearest in time of those who have left a history of those
times and of the same city. He says that Themistocles came to Artaxerxes
and sent a letter in these words: "I, Themistocles, have come to you, I
who of all the Greeks brought the most evils upon your house, so long as
it was necessary for me to war against your father and defend my
country. I also did far more good once I myself was in safety and he
began to be in danger. For when, after the battle at Salamis, he wished
to return into Asia, I informed him by letter that a plan was afoot to
break up the bridge he had made on the Hellespont and that he would be
surrounded by the enemy: by that message he was freed from danger. Now I
have fled to you, driven out by all Greece, seeking your friendship: if
I obtain it, you will have me no less a good friend than he found me a
brave enemy. I ask you to give me a year's time for those matters I wish
to discuss with you, and when it is past to allow me to come to you."

* Caput 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:

The king, admiring the greatness of his spirit and wishing to win such a
man to himself, granted the request. Themistocles gave all that time to
the letters and speech of the Persians: in these he became so learned
that he is said to have spoken before the king far more readily than
those who had been born in Persia could. When he had promised the king
many things, and that most welcome of all, that if the king were willing
to use his counsels he would crush Greece by war, he was presented with
great gifts by Artaxerxes, returned into Asia, and established his home
at Magnesia. For the king had given him this city, in these words, to
furnish him bread (from which region fifty talents came in each year),
Lampsacus from which he might take wine, and Myus from which he might
have his provisions. Two monuments of him have remained to our time: a
tomb near the town in which he was buried, and a statue in the forum of
Magnesia. About his death it has been written in many ways among most
authors, but we approve Thucydides most of all as authority, who says
that he died of disease at Magnesia, and does not deny that there was a
report that he took poison of his own accord when he despaired of being
able to perform what he had promised the king about crushing Greece.
The same writer has handed down that his bones were buried secretly in
Attica by his friends, since it was not allowed by the laws, because he
had been condemned for treason.
