** Oratio Lepidi cons. ad populum Romanum
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1-h1
:END:

Your clemency and honesty, citizens, the very qualities that have made you
greatest and most renowned among the nations, fill me with the deepest fear in
the face of Lucius Sulla's tyranny: that you may be trapped by refusing to
believe of others the crimes you yourselves count unspeakable. All the more,
since his whole hope lies in crime and treachery, and he thinks himself safe
only if he has become worse and more infamous than your fear of him, so that
once you are caught, misery may strip you of all care for liberty; or, if you
do see it coming, that you may be held to fending off danger rather than to
revenge.
I cannot marvel enough at his followers, men of the greatest names, with the
finest examples of their ancestors before them: they pay with their own
slavery for dominion over you, and prefer both wrongs to living free under the
best of rights.
The famous offspring of the Bruti, the Aemilii, and the Lutatii, born to
overthrow what their ancestors won by valor.
For what did we defend against Pyrrhus, Hannibal, Philip, and Antiochus, if
not liberty, each man's own home, and that we should obey no one except the
laws?
All of this that perverse Romulus holds as if it were plunder from foreigners,
not sated by the destruction of so many armies, nor of consuls and other
leading men whom the fortune of war had consumed, but more cruel at the very
time when success turns most men from anger to pity.
Indeed he alone of all men since human memory began devised punishments for
those yet unborn, for whom wrong was certain before life itself; and most
perversely he has so far been kept safe by the very enormity of his crime,
while fear of a heavier slavery keeps you from reclaiming liberty.

We must act and go to meet it, citizens, lest your spoils remain in their
hands. There must be no delay, nor help sought by prayers; unless perhaps you
hope that Sulla is already weary or ashamed of tyranny, and that he will let
go, as involving greater danger, what he seized by crime.
But he has gone so far that he counts nothing glorious except what is safe,
and everything honorable that serves to keep his dominion.
And so that quiet and leisure with liberty, which many honest men used to
choose rather than toil with honors, are no more.
In this season one must be a slave or a master; one must feel fear or inspire
it, citizens.
For what is left? What human things remain, or what divine things are
unpolluted? The Roman people, a little while ago the ruler of nations,
stripped of empire, glory, and right, without the means to act, despised, does
not even have a slave's rations left.
A great body of the allies and of Latium, given citizenship by you for many
distinguished services, are barred from it by one man; and a few followers
have seized the ancestral homes of the innocent commons as the wages of their
crimes.
Laws, courts, the treasury, the provinces, kings: all in the hands of one man;
and at last the license of death and life over citizens.
At the same time you have seen human victims and tombs stained with civil
blood.
Is anything left for men except to wipe out the wrong or to die with courage?
Since nature has set one end for all, even those fenced with steel, and no one
waits for the last necessity without daring anything, except with a woman's
spirit.

But I am a man of sedition, as Sulla says, because I complain of the rewards
of disorder; and I am eager for war, because I demand the rights of peace.
No doubt because you will not otherwise be safe and secure enough in your
empire unless Vettius the Picene and the clerk Cornelius squander what others
honestly acquired; unless you all approve the proscription of the innocent for
their wealth, the torture of distinguished men, a city emptied by flight and
slaughter, the goods of wretched citizens sold or given away like Cimbric
plunder.
But he throws in my face possessions from the goods of the proscribed. And
that indeed is among the greatest of his crimes: that neither I nor anyone at
all would have been safe enough, if we did what was right. And those things
which I then bought from fear, having paid the price according to law, I
nevertheless restore to their owners; and I do not mean to allow any plunder
from citizens.
Let those things be enough which we endured when they were brought on by
frenzy: Roman armies joining battle against each other, and arms turned from
foreigners against ourselves. Let there be an end of all crimes and insults;
of which Sulla so little repents that he counts what he did as glory, and, if
he were allowed, would do it more greedily.

I no longer fear what you think of him, but how much you dare. I fear that
while each of you waits for another to take the lead, you may be caught first,
not by his resources, which are frail and rotten, but by your own sloth, which
a man may go and seize, and seem as fortunate as he is bold.
For besides his stained followers, who wants the same things, or who does not
want everything changed except the victory? The soldiers, I suppose, with
whose blood riches have been won for Tarula and Scirtus, the worst of slaves!
Or those over whom Fufidius was preferred in seeking magistracies, that filthy
serving-girl, the disgrace of every office?
And so the victorious army gives me the greatest confidence: for them, through
so many wounds and toils, nothing has been gained except a tyrant.
Unless perhaps they set out to overthrow the tribunician power, established by
their ancestors through arms, and to wring from themselves their own rights
and courts: a splendid wage indeed, when, banished to marshes and forests,
they understand that the insult and the odium are theirs, the rewards in the
hands of a few. Why then does he march with so great a column and such spirit?
Because success is a marvelous cloak for vices; once it is shaken, he will be
as despised as he was feared. Unless perhaps under the show of concord and
peace, names he has given to his crime and parricide. And he says there can be
no republic and no end of war unless the commons remain driven from their
fields, the bitterest civil plunder; unless the right and judgment of all
things stay in his hands, which belonged to the Roman people.
If you understand these things as peace and settlement, then approve the
greatest disturbances and destructions of the republic; nod to the laws
imposed on you; accept leisure with slavery; and hand down to posterity the
example of trapping the republic at the price of its own blood.
For myself, although through this highest office enough had been won for my
ancestors' name, for my dignity, and even for my protection, yet it was not my
plan to make a private fortune, and dangerous liberty seemed better than quiet
slavery.
If you approve this, stand by me, citizens, and with the gods helping well,
follow Marcus Aemilius, consul, as leader and author, to recover liberty.

** Oratio Philippi in senatu
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2-h1
:END:

I should have wished most of all, conscript fathers, that the republic be at
peace, or that in dangers it be defended by every man of spirit, and last that
wicked designs bring harm to their advisers. But instead everything has been
thrown into confusion by seditions, and by those whom it better became to
prevent them. In the end, what the worst and most foolish have decreed, the
good and the wise must carry out.
For war and arms, though hateful to you, must still be taken up, because they
please Lepidus; unless perhaps anyone's plan is to furnish peace and suffer
war.
Good gods, who still protect this city though you have dropped your care:
Marcus Aemilius, last of all scoundrels, of whom one cannot decide whether he
is worse or more cowardly, has an army for crushing liberty, and has made
himself feared from being despised. You, muttering and hanging back, wish for
peace with words and with the songs of soothsayers more than you defend it,
and you do not see that by the softness of your decrees dignity is stripped
from you, fear from him.
And rightly so, since from plunder he got the consulship, and for sedition
a province with an army. What would he have gotten for good deeds, you who
have given such prizes to his crimes?
But no doubt those who to the last decreed envoys, peace, concord, and other
such things have won his gratitude. On the contrary, they are despised, held
unworthy of the republic, and counted as plunder, since they seek by fear the
peace they had lost by having it.

For my part, from the beginning, when I saw Etruria conspiring, the proscribed
being summoned, the republic torn by largesses, I thought we must make haste,
and with a few I followed the counsels of Catulus. But those who extolled the
good deeds of the Aemilian house and said that by pardoning the Roman people
had increased its greatness, who said even then that Lepidus had nowhere gone
too far, when he had taken up private arms to crush liberty, each seeking
wealth or patronage for himself, corrupted the public counsel.
But then Lepidus was a brigand with camp-followers and a few cutthroats, none
of whom would have traded his life for a day's wage. Now he is proconsul with
military command, not bought but given by you, with lieutenants still legally
subordinate, and to him flock the most corrupt men of every order, burning
with want and desires, driven by consciousness of crimes, for whom rest is in
sedition and in peace there is turmoil. These sow tumult from tumult, war from
war: once the followers of Saturninus, then of Sulpicius, then of Marius and
Damasippus, now of Lepidus.
Besides, Etruria and all the remnants of war have been stirred, the Spains are
troubled with arms, Mithridates on the flank of our revenues, by which we are
still sustained, is watching for a day for war. Indeed, nothing is wanting to
overthrow the empire except a fit leader.
This I beg and beseech you, conscript fathers: take heed, and do not let the
license of crimes, like a madness, pass by contact to the uninfected. For when
rewards follow the wicked, it is not easy for anyone to be good for nothing.

Or are you waiting until, with his army brought up again, he invades the city
with steel and flame? That is much nearer from the state in which he now stirs
than civil arms are from peace and concord. Those arms he took up against all
things divine and human, not for his own wrong or that of those whose injury
he pretends, but to overthrow the laws and liberty.
For he is driven and torn by passion and by fear of his crimes,
without counsel, restless, trying this and that. He fears leisure, he hates
war. He sees that he must go without luxury and license, and meanwhile he
abuses your sloth.
And I have not counsel enough whether to call it fear or cowardice or madness:
you seem to wish, each for himself, that such great evils, like a thunderbolt,
may not strike him, but you do not even try to keep them off.

And I ask you to consider how the nature of things has been reversed. Formerly
the public evil was prepared in secret, the defenses openly, and so the good
easily outstripped the wicked. Now peace and concord are disturbed openly,
defended in secret. Those who like those things are in arms; you are in fear.
What are you waiting for? Unless perhaps you are ashamed or reluctant to do
right. Or do Lepidus's demands move your minds? He says it pleases him that
each man have his own restored, and he holds what is another's. That the
rights of war be rescinded, while he himself compels with arms. That
citizenship be confirmed for those from whom he denies it was taken. That for
the sake of concord the tribunician power be restored, from which all discords
were kindled.
Worst and most shameless of all men, are the want and grief of citizens a care
to you? You who have nothing at home except what was won by arms or by wrong.
You seek a second consulship as if you had given back the first. You seek
concord by war, by which what was won is thrown into confusion: traitor to us,
faithless to them, enemy of all good men. Have you no shame before men or
gods, whom you have violated through trust or by perjury? Since you are such,
I urge you to stay in your purpose and keep your arms, and not, restless
yourself, hold us in anxiety by putting off the seditions. Neither the
provinces nor the laws nor the household gods allow you as a citizen. Go on as
you have begun, that you may find what you have earned as soon as possible.

But you, conscript fathers, how long will you suffer the republic to be unsafe
by delaying, and try arms with words? Levies have been held against you, money
wrung publicly and privately, garrisons withdrawn and imposed. Laws are
commanded from caprice, while you in the meantime prepare envoys and decrees.
By Hercules, the more greedily you seek peace, the sharper the war will be,
when he understands that he has been sustained by fear more than by what is
fair and good.
For he who says he hates tumults and the slaughter of citizens, and on that
account keeps you unarmed against an armed Lepidus, thinks that what the
conquered must endure you should rather suffer, when you could do those things
yourselves. So he urges peace from you for him, and war from him for you.
If these things please you, if such a numbness has crushed your spirits that,
forgetful of the crimes of Cinna, at whose return to the city the honor of
this order perished, you are nevertheless going to give yourselves and your
wives and children over to Lepidus, what need of decrees, what need of
Catulus's help? Why, he and other good men care for the republic in vain.
Go as you please; get yourselves the patronage of a Cethegus and other
traitors, who long to renew rapine and fire and again to arm their hands
against the household gods. But if liberty and true things please you more,
decree what is worthy of the name, and increase the spirit in brave men.
A new army is at hand, besides the colonies of old soldiers, all the nobility,
the best commanders. Fortune follows the better men. Already those things
which have been gathered by our sloth will fall apart.

Therefore I move as follows: since Marcus Lepidus is leading toward the city
an army raised by private counsel, with the worst men and enemies of the
republic, against the authority of this order, that Appius Claudius the
interrex, with Quintus Catulus the proconsul and the rest who hold military
command, be a guard to the city and take care that the republic suffer no
harm.

** Oratio C. Cottae ad Populum Romanum
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3-h1
:END:

Citizens, many dangers have been mine at home and in the field, many
adversities, some of which I endured, some I drove back with the help of the
gods and my own courage. In all of them my spirit never failed the task, nor
did labor fail my decrees. Bad fortune and good changed my resources, not my
nature.
But on the contrary, in these miseries everything has deserted me along with
fortune. Besides, old age, heavy in itself, doubles the care: for me,
wretched, with my life already spent, not even an honorable death may be hoped
for.
For if I am a parricide of yours, and twice born in this city I hold cheap my
household gods, my country, and the highest office, what torment is enough for
me alive, or what punishment when dead? Indeed I have outdone by my crime all
the punishments told of among the dead below.

From first youth I lived before your eyes, as a private man and in
magistracies. Those who wanted my tongue, my counsel, my money, used them. I
did not practice a cunning eloquence, nor my talent for doing harm. Most
greedy of private favor, I took on the greatest enmities for the republic.
Beaten in those enmities together with the republic, when, needing another's
help, I looked for more evils, you, citizens, gave me back my country and
household gods with huge dignity.
For these kindnesses I would scarcely seem grateful enough if I granted to
each of you the life I cannot. For life and death are the rights of nature. To
live among citizens without disgrace, whole in fame and fortunes: that is
given and received as a gift.

You made us consuls, citizens, when the republic was most hampered at home and
in war. For the generals in Spain demand pay, soldiers, arms, grain, and the
case forces it, since by the defection of the allies and Sertorius's flight
through the mountains they can neither fight hand to hand nor get what they
need.
Armies are kept in Asia and Cilicia because of Mithridates's excessive power.
Macedonia is full of enemies, and no less the coasts of Italy and of the
provinces; while in the meantime the revenues, small and uncertain from the
wars, scarcely bear a part of the costs. So with a fleet smaller than before,
which used to protect the supplies, we sail.
If these things were brought on by our deceit or sloth, come, as anger
advises, take punishment. But if our common fortune is harsher, why do you
begin what is unworthy of you, of us, and of the republic?

And I, whose age death is nearer, do not beg off, if by it any inconvenience
is taken from you. Nor could I soon more honorably, by the nature of my body,
make an end of life than for your safety.
Here I am, Gaius Cotta, consul. I do what our ancestors often did in hard
wars: I vow and give myself for the republic.
Then look around to whom you will commit it. For no good man will want such an
honor, when account must be rendered of fortune and of sea and of a war waged
by others, or one must die shamefully.
Only keep in your minds that I was not slain for crime or greed, but that I
willingly gave my life as a gift for the greatest kindnesses.
By yourselves, citizens, and by the glory of your ancestors, bear adversity
and take counsel for the republic.
Much care is in the highest office, many huge labors, which you refuse in
vain, seeking the wealth of peace, when all the provinces, kingdoms, seas and
lands are harsh or worn out with wars.

** Epistula Cn. Pompei ad senatum
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4-h1
:END:

If I had taken on so many labors and dangers against you, my country, and the
household gods, as often as from first youth under my command the most wicked
enemies have been routed and safety won for you, you would have decreed
nothing worse against me in my absence than you are doing even now, conscript
fathers: me, thrown contrary to my age into the fiercest war with an army that
has deserved most highly, you have, so far as in you lies, finished off with
hunger, the most wretched of all deaths.
Was it in this hope that the Roman people sent their sons to war? Are these
the rewards for wounds and for blood so often shed for the republic? Worn out
with writing and sending envoys, I have spent all my private resources and
hopes, while in the meantime from you in three years scarcely a year's costs
have been given.
By the immortal gods, do you think I can stand in place of the treasury, or
keep an army without grain and pay?

I confess I set out for this war with more zeal than judgment: I who, having
received from you only the name of command, in forty days raised an army and
drove from the Alps into Spain the enemy already hanging on Italy's neck.
Through those mountains I opened a way other than Hannibal's, more convenient
for us.
I recovered Gaul, the Pyrenees, Lacetania, the Indigetes; and I met the first
onset of Sertorius in his victory with new soldiers and far fewer, and I spent
the winter in camp among the fiercest enemies, not through towns, nor from any
ambition of mine.
Why then should I list battles or winter expeditions, towns razed or
recovered, when deeds speak louder than words? The enemy's camp taken at the
Sucro, the battle at the river Durius, and Gaius Herennius, the enemy's
leader, destroyed with the city of Valentia and his army, are clear enough to
you. For these, O grateful fathers, you pay with want and hunger.

And so my army and the enemy's are on equal terms: for pay is given to
neither, and either victor can come into Italy.
This I warn and beg you to heed, and not force me by necessities to take
private counsel for myself.
Hither Spain, so far as it is not held by the enemy, we or Sertorius have laid
waste to extermination, except the maritime cities: of themselves they are a
cost and a burden to us. Gaul last year fed Metellus's army with pay and
grain, and now with bad harvests can scarcely keep itself going. I have spent
not only my private fortune but even my credit.
You are what is left. If you do not help, against my will and with me warning
you, the army from here, and with it the whole war of Spain, will pass into
Italy.

** Oratio Macri trib. pleb. ad plebem
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5-h1
:END:

If, citizens, you too little understood what difference there is between the
right left you by your ancestors and this slavery prepared by Sulla, I should
have had to speak at length and teach for what wrongs and how often the armed
commons seceded from the fathers, and how they provided themselves with
tribunes of the commons as champions of all their right.
Now it remains only to exhort, and to go first on the road by which I think
liberty must be taken up.
Nor does it escape me how great the resources of the nobility are, against
which I, alone, powerless, with the empty show of a magistracy, am beginning
to drive their dominion; and how much more safely the faction of the guilty
acts than the innocent do alone.
But besides the good hope from you, which has conquered fear, I have resolved
that the adversities of the struggle for liberty are better for a brave man
than not to have struggled at all.

Although all others, created for your right, have turned all their force and
their commands against you by favor or hope or rewards, and hold it better to
do wrong for a wage than to do right for nothing.
And so all have already yielded to the dominion of a few, who under a military
name have seized the treasury, the armies, the kingdoms, the provinces, and
hold a citadel from your spoils; while in the meantime, like cattle, you, the
multitude, offer yourselves to individuals to be held and enjoyed, stripped of
everything your ancestors left you; except that by your votes you appoint for
yourselves, as once guardians, now masters.
And so all have gone over there; but soon, if you take back what is yours,
most will come to you. For few have the spirit to defend what they like; the
rest belong to the stronger.

Or do you doubt whether anything can stand in your way if you go forward with
one mind, you whom they feared when you were faint and slothful? Unless
perhaps Gaius Cotta, consul from the middle of the faction, restored certain
rights to the tribunes of the commons otherwise than from fear. And although
Lucius Sicinius, the first who dared speak of tribunician power, was trapped
while you muttered, still they feared odium before you grew weary of the
wrong. I cannot wonder enough at this, citizens; for you understood that your
hope had been in vain.
When Sulla died, who had imposed a wicked slavery, you believed there was an
end of the evil. There arose Catulus, far more savage.
A tumult intervened in the consulship of Brutus and Mamercus. Then Gaius
Curio lorded it to the very destruction of an innocent tribune.
You saw with what spirit Lucullus went against Lucius Quintius last year. How
great, last of all, are the tumults now stirred against me! Which were surely
being raised in vain, if they were going to make an end of dominion before you
made an end of slavery: especially since in these civil arms other things were
said, but on both sides the struggle was for dominion over you.
And so the rest, from license or hatred or greed, burned for a time. One thing
only has remained, which was sought on both sides and snatched away for the
future: the tribunician power, a weapon prepared by our ancestors for liberty.
This I warn and beg you to heed, and not, changing the names of things toward
cowardice, call leisure what is slavery. To enjoy even that, if crime has
overcome what is true and honorable, is not now a condition that can be had.
It would have been, if you had been wholly quiet. Now they are taking notice,
and unless you win, since every wrong is safer by its gravity, they will hold
you more tightly.

/What then do you advise?/ someone among you will put in. First of all, this
habit you practice must be dropped: of a ready tongue and a coward's spirit,
mindful of liberty no further than the place of the assembly.
Next, I will not call you to those manly deeds by which your ancestors won
tribunes of the commons, a magistracy previously patrician, and votes free
from patrician sanction. Since all force, citizens, is in you, and what
commands you now endure for others you could certainly carry out or refuse for
yourselves, are you waiting for Jupiter or some other god as adviser?
Those great commands of the consuls and decrees of the fathers you make valid
by executing them, citizens, and of your own accord you hasten to increase and
aid the license over you.
I do not urge you to avenge your wrongs, but rather that you desire rest; nor
do I want discords, as they charge, but an end of them, and by the law of
nations I demand restitution; and if they stubbornly keep it,
I shall not advise arms or secession, only that you no longer offer your
blood. Let them hold and administer their commands in their own way, let them
seek triumphs, let them hunt Mithridates, Sertorius, and what is left of the
exiles with their ancestral portraits: let danger and toil be far
from those who have no share in the fruit; unless perhaps that hastily made
grain law pays for your services. By it, even so, they have valued the liberty
of all at five pecks a man, which is certainly no more than prison rations.
For as in prison that scanty supply keeps off death while strength wastes, so
so small a thing does not free you from household care, and it cheats the
slenderest hopes of every idle man.
Yet even if it were ample, since it was held out as the price of slavery, what
numbness it would be to be deceived by it and actually to owe gratitude to the
wrong for what is your own. Craft must be guarded against.
For by no other means can they prevail against you as a whole, nor will they
try. And so at the same time they prepare soothings and put you off until the
coming of Gnaeus Pompey, the very man whom, when they feared him, they lifted
onto their own necks, and presently, their fear gone, they tear to pieces.
Nor are they ashamed, these self-styled defenders of liberty, so many men,
that without one man they either dare not remit a wrong or cannot defend a
right.
For my part I am fully convinced that Pompey, a young man of such glory,
prefers to be first with your consent rather than a partner in their dominion,
and that he will be among the first authors of tribunician power.
But, citizens, formerly each of you found protection in the many, not all of
you in one man. Nor could any one mortal give or take away such things.

And so enough of words has been said, for it is not ignorance that blocks the
way.
But some numbness has seized you, by which you are moved neither by glory nor
by disgrace, and you have exchanged everything for present sloth, thinking you
have liberty enough, no doubt because your backs are spared and you may go
here and there, gifts of rich masters.
And even these things are not for the country people, who are cut down in the
quarrels of the powerful and given as gifts to magistrates for the provinces.
So the fighting and the winning are for a few: the commons, whatever happens,
are treated as the vanquished, and will be more so every day, if those men
keep their dominion with greater care than you reclaim liberty.

** Epistula Mithridatis
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6-h1
:END:

King Mithridates to King Arsaces, greeting. All who, in their own prosperity,
are asked to join a war must consider whether they may then keep the peace,
and then whether what is asked is pious enough, safe, glorious, or shameful.
If lasting peace were yours to enjoy, if there were not enemies at hand and
most wicked, if outstanding fame would not follow from crushing the Romans, I
would not dare ask alliance, and I would hope in vain to mix my ills with your
goods.
And those things that seem able to hold you back, anger at Tigranes for the
late war and my own less than prosperous fortunes, if you will judge truly,
will most of all urge you on.
For he, being at your mercy, will accept whatever alliance you wish. Fortune,
having stripped me of many things, has given me the use of good counsel; and
what men in prosperity desire, I, not the strongest, offer as an example by
which you may more rightly order your own affairs.

For the Romans have one cause, and that an old one, of making war on all
nations, peoples, and kings: a deep lust for empire and riches. With that they
first took up war with Philip, king of the Macedonians,
while they were hard pressed by the Carthaginians, pretending friendship.
Antiochus, coming to his aid, they turned aside by the cunning grant of Asia;
and soon, when Philip was broken, Antiochus was stripped of all the land this
side of Taurus and of ten thousand talents.
Then Perseus, Philip's son, after many and various struggles, received in
faith among the gods of Samothrace: those cunning men, inventors of treachery,
because they had given him life by the pact, killed him by sleeplessness.
Eumenes, whose friendship they display as a glory, they first betrayed to
Antiochus as the price of peace; afterward, kept as a watchman of captured
land, they made of a king, by costs and insults, the most wretched of slaves;
and with a feigned impious will they led his son Aristonicus in triumph in the
manner of enemies, because he had sought his father's kingdom. Asia was
besieged by them.
Last they plundered Bithynia when Nicomedes died, though a son born of Nysa,

For why should I mention myself? Me, cut off on every side by kingdoms and
tetrarchies from their empire, because the report was that I was rich and
would not be a slave, they provoked through Nicomedes to war; I was not
ignorant of their crime, and I had already borne witness of what would happen
to the Cretans, then the only free people of all, and to king Ptolemy.
And I, avenging the wrongs, drove Nicomedes from Bithynia, recovered Asia, the
spoil of king Antiochus, and took from Greece a heavy slavery.
Archelaus, last of slaves, hindered my beginnings by betraying the army.
And those whom cowardice or crooked cunning kept from arms, so that they might
be safe by my labors, are paying the bitterest penalties: Ptolemy putting off
the war day by day with money, the Cretans already attacked once and destined
to have no end except destruction.
For my part, when I understood that because of their internal ills battles had
been put off rather than peace given, with Tigranes refusing (who now too late
approves my words), you far away, all others at their mercy, I nevertheless
began war again, and Marcus Cotta, a Roman commander, I routed by land at
Calchedon and stripped of a most beautiful fleet at sea.
At Cyzicus, lingering in a siege with a great army, grain failed me, with no
one around helping; at the same time winter barred the sea. So, trying to go
back to my father's kingdom without the enemy's force, I lost by shipwrecks at
Parium and Heraclea the best of my soldiers with the fleets.
Then, when the army was restored at Cabira and after various battles between
me and Lucullus, want again came upon us both. Under him lay the kingdom of
Ariobarzanes, untouched by war; I, with all the country around laid waste,
withdrew into Armenia. And the Romans, following not me but their custom of
overthrowing all kingdoms, because they kept a multitude from fighting in
close places, display Tigranes's want of foresight as a victory.

Now I ask you to consider: if we are crushed, do you think you will be
stronger to resist, or that there will be an end of the war? I know you have
great resources of men, arms, and gold; and for that reason you are sought by
us for alliance, by them for plunder. But the plan is this: with Tigranes's
kingdom whole, with my soldiers skilled in war, far from home, with small
labor, to finish the war through our bodies, since we can neither conquer nor
be conquered without danger to you.
Or do you not know that the Romans, after Ocean set a limit to them as they
pressed west, turned their arms here? And that from the beginning they have
had nothing except what they seized: home, wives, fields, empire? Once a
gathering of men without country or parents, founded as a plague of the world;
whom nothing human or divine stops from dragging and destroying allies and
friends, far and near, poor and powerful, and from counting everything not
enslaved, and kingdoms most of all, as hostile?
For few want liberty; the greater part want just masters. We are suspected as
rivals, and as avengers who will be at hand in time.
But you, who have Seleucea, greatest of cities, and the kingdom of Persis with
its famous riches, what do you expect from them except guile for the present
and war afterward?
The Romans have arms against all, the sharpest against those whose defeat
yields the greatest spoils. By daring, by deceiving, and by sowing wars from
wars they have become great.
By this custom they will destroy everything or perish. That will not be
hard, if you from Mesopotamia and we from Armenia surround an army without
grain, without auxiliaries, still unharmed by fortune or by our faults.
And that fame will follow you: that you set out to help great kings and
crushed the robbers of the nations.
This I warn and urge you to do, and not to prefer by our ruin to put off your
own, rather than by alliance to become the victor.
