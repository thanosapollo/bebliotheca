These gifts we offer you according to our vow, Zeus king and savior; receive
them, and as you saved us graciously, come also to the thank-offerings, supply
the speech and send it on as far as a human speech can reach, as far as
possible, so that we may not become wholly ridiculous, nor fall away from the
whole. And yet as I come to the very thing I do not know what to make of
myself, but now I think I also learn very well how completely I was in danger,
and I was not myself because of the sea, praying for such things as it is
neither easy to fulfill nor perhaps does the attempt even have the credit of
prudence, promising to speak a hymn of Zeus, and that without meter. Great
indeed was the sea then and it made everything

* 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:

both to do and to speak. Nevertheless, for nothing that has been vowed must be
left out, they say, but anything whatsoever paid in any way is better than
left out once for all, let us try somehow to discharge our duty toward the
god; and whether it is finer or more deficient will be his concern. And indeed
to leave it out altogether will bring the reputation of idleness, which it is
not lawful to have in things toward the gods, but for not reaching it the
nature of the matter itself will provide pardon. For we shall not seem to have
left it out willingly, but of necessity to come later. And so to speak, it is
better, if one must, to incur laughter than blame from the gods. Since then we
have vowed and these things could not be otherwise, we must, as athletes who
have entered themselves do not withdraw, but go to meet it and attempt the
contest. Come then, Muses, children of Zeus, for I at least do not see when
one would call you better than now, whether you on Olympus with Apollo Leader
of the Muses sing the divine song, hymning your father and the father of all
things, or whether Pieria is a dear dwelling to you, or whether you dance on
Helicon in Boeotia, come, you who know all things, from where shall we begin
of the works and gifts of Zeus, whatever is of use? What shall we dare to say
about Zeus? Grant that the speech become mad, as the subject is, so also in
the things fitting for him, and do not leave me in the midst of heaven and
earth. Zeus made all things and all things that are are works of Zeus, both
rivers and earth and sea and heaven and whatever is between these above and
whatever is under these, and gods and humans and whatever has soul and
whatever comes to sight and whatever one must grasp by thought. And he first
made himself, not reared in the fragrant caves of Crete, nor did Cronus mean
to swallow him, nor did he swallow a stone instead of him, nor was Zeus in
danger nor

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:

may he ever be in danger, nor is anything older than Zeus, no more than sons
would become older than fathers and the things that come to be than those that
make them, but this one is first and oldest and founder of all things, himself
come to be from himself. And when he came to be it is not possible to say, but
he was from the beginning and will be forever, self-father and greater than to
have come to be from another. And just as he brought forth Athena from his
head and needed no marriage for her, so still earlier he made himself from
himself and needed nothing else for being, but quite the contrary all being
began from him and there is no time to name. For time was not yet then,
because nothing else was either. For no work of a craftsman is older than he.
Thus then the beginning of all things is Zeus and from Zeus all things. And
being stronger than time and having no one to oppose him, he himself and the
cosmos were together. So quickly did he make all things. And he made them
thus: and if we slip anywhere, let him be called the Gentle for us; he begins
from foundations and makes earth, so that all things might stand upon it for
him, having rooted the depth with infinite roots and compacted it, with rocks
instead of bolts and with the rest of that which nature has received for
compacting, and having raised up mountains, defenses against rains and waves,
and having mixed plains in the middle, seats of mountains and of earth,
fastening whatever power the one has to the other. And whatever had second
place in weight he brought on, setting the sea upon the earth in the midst of
it; and as if arranging friendship and partnership of neighbors he mixed in
rivers, which were to flow from earth into the sea and again from the sea
through other unseen places were to flow under into the earth and join each to
the other. And he wove them together so that each should be in the middle of
the other,

islands and straits and isthmuses, the one always coming to be in the middle
of the other. And just as he fortified the earth through each of the two
shapes, so again earth and sea, making both safe through each other; and he
set air over both, the breath of earth and sea. And having set fire from
above, which they name aether, with this fourth he occupied all things, and
here he brought in the strongest of his craft, having adorned all heaven with
stars, as the sea with islands, and having made it transparent through them,
and having sent down the efflux of fire as far as earth, not completing all
these things in as much time as we have now gone through about them, but
quicker than it is possible to think; and together with the making and
crafting he divided each according to likelihood and assigned lots, making the
animals fitting to each place, and assigning the proper births, dwellings and
allotments to those born; for gods to dwell in heaven as in the acropolis of
the whole, which is the fairest and brightest, the purest of dwellings; nor
does old age enter into it; and to humans upon earth, and to the tribes of the
sea he gave the sea, and to winged things the air, so that each would most of
all obtain their due, finding the allotments according to their own nature and
power and taking the kindred dwellings; the earth-born and earthy tribe the
earth, the wetter the sea, the lighter and drier the air, and again the humid
and fiery and stretched over all these the aether and heaven. Thus then as if
making it a city or having built it, then he brought in the men. And when he
had distinguished the matter and constructed the cosmos, he filled all things
with kinds, making them in succession with harmony and foresight that nothing
be left out, that all things be fitting and complete for one another, himself
craftsman and founder of the whole, having substance and power.

And as also all the tribes of gods each have an efflux of the power of Zeus
the father of all, and quite simply according to Homer's chain all things hang
upon him and all things are fastened from him, a chain much fairer than a
golden one or any other one might invent. And Love and Necessity, these two
most uniting and strongest, he generated among the first things, so that they
might hold all things together for him. And the mortal tribe he did not think
worthy of one and the same portion throughout, nor did he refuse to be father
of humans as well as of gods, but from the same stock, as those who now
prepare perfumes, whatever was as it were the sediment and had remained below,
this he left to be the seed of man, having neither equal honor nor like power,
nor pure of evils and without share of them, as that former race was, yet more
honored than the rest of the mortal race, as having shared in reason and not
altogether released from the divine portion, but as having the last of a
channel or spring. Thus then he gives the front seat and offices and
protectorates to gods, and second, much later, he gives also to humans, and
the middle things, both the nurslings of air and sea and whatever remains of
earth, he made under both, as those in armies set the best in front, the
second behind, and the bad, as the saying goes, in the middle. And to the gods
he assigned the four regions, so that nowhere should there be anything empty
of gods, but they should be present everywhere to all things that are and come
to be, the first fatherland the heavenly one and that in the air and that in
the sea and that upon earth, as it were some governors and satraps divided.
And of the providence from them he made nothing share more than man,
everywhere preserving for the races their due and thinking the order not to be
useless. Humans, knowing these things and owing great thanks for great things,
as later in time

:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:

gathering one another they settled cities, they set apart the acropolises for
Zeus, both looking to the pattern of the whole, that he himself was dweller of
the heights of the whole, and at the same time willingly withdrawing from the
most important places, as to the only one of tyrants who knows how to rule.
And that kinship of race is advantageous even to the ruled he showed us here
too from the second things: for violence does not touch gods; for this reason
they need no laws; but for humans alone of the other animals he established
law, both that they not be destroyed by murder and that robberies and
violences not come to be, but that justice be before violence. And Zeus
legislated these things when he sent Shame and Justice among humans together
with the other gods, to care for humans and guard their life for them. So both
our generation from the beginning is from Zeus, as also for this whole, and
the preservation of life and arts and laws and that our race being mortal is
in part immortal by succession. And the reasoning about these very things is a
part of Zeus. But Homer was mad and far from the mark, who made Zeus in the
assembly of gods forbid the gods to care for humans; but he did not forbid
this, nor will he forbid it, respecting himself. For it is not lawful for him
to turn about nor to have another mind, neither by forgetfulness nor by
repentance, than that which he had from the beginning, when he made gods
caretakers of humans, and humans attendants and servants of gods; which was
going to be most fitting and advantageous to both. Zeus then is cause and
craftsman of all things that are, and through him all things come to be, both
heavenly and earthly, as the name itself also indicates, spoken not far from
the cause, whenever we say Dia. For Zeus he has been called by us because he
is cause of life and being to each, and again inasmuch as we name some by
assigning cause, according to this

place of the voice we made him homonymous with the name of cause, addressing
him as Dia, since through him all things come to be and have come to be. And
the unceasing motion of the sun above earth and under earth is a command of
Zeus spoken beforehand to the sun for the brightness of the whole cosmos, and
the courses of the moon and the dances of all stars are Zeus's ordering; and
Ocean of the surrounding water, as he came to be from Zeus from the beginning,
so also remains, guarding his own bounds. And the Seasons each year in turn go
over all the earth. And all time divided into day and night has the lengths of
each fitting to the season, providing rest and labors, as is fitting, more or
less; and the union of heaven and rain is Zeus's; and Apollo gives oracles to
humans of Zeus's unerring counsel, and Asclepius heals, and Athena Worker has
received this rank by Zeus's mind, and Hera of marriage, and Artemis of
childbirth and huntress, benefit humans, preserving the mind of the great
benefactor of all. And Pans dwelling in mountains and Nymphs overseers of
streams have their allotment with Zeus. And Poseidon and the Dioscuri save
those who sail, obeying Zeus, and the Muses found music and showed it, Zeus
having willed that there be Muses and music, for the sake of gods and humans
together. And all things everywhere are full of Zeus and he is set beside all
at each action, as teachers beside children and the co-drivers beside the
charioteers. And the benefits of all gods are a work of Zeus, and all care for
humans, guarding the rank handed down by him, as of a general of all in an
army. And the portion which he may assign he knows, as is reasonable. For he
himself gives it and this is inescapable

fate, whatever Zeus assigns to each, whose offspring all things that are. The
peaks of mountains do not escape him, nor springs of rivers, nor cities, nor
the sand below the sea, nor stars; nor does night stand in front, nor sleep,
of these great and only eyes of his that have seen the truth. And of rivers
the fairest and most notable, the Nile himself then was Zeus-fallen, as
imitating the father, and as it were a governor appointed by him over the
things in Egypt, instead of Zeus's rains he himself comes upon and fills the
earth. Zeus is father of all, of heaven and earth and gods and humans and
rivers and plants, and through him we see and have whatever we have. He is
benefactor and overseer and protector of all, he is president and leader and
steward of all things that are and come to be, he is giver of all, he is
maker, he in assemblies and courts giving victory has been called of the
marketplace, in battles Tropaeus, in diseases and all occasions helping,
Savior, he Liberator, he Gentle; reasonably, as being also father. He is king,
of the city, descending, of rain, heavenly, of the peak, all the great names
that he himself found fitting for himself. He having beginnings and ends and
measures and lots of all things, ruling all equally everywhere, he himself
alone could say what must be said about himself, as a god having been allotted
something more; for this then Pindar has said more finely than anything else
by anyone about Zeus. One must begin from him and end at him, calling him
leader and helper of every speech and every action, as is fitting for the one
who rules all, founder and himself the only complete one of all things.
