* Chapter 1
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Eumenes of Cardia. If fortune equal to his virtue had been given to this
man, he would not indeed have been greater (for we measure great men by
virtue, not by fortune), but far more illustrious and even more honored.
For since his lifetime fell in those times when the Macedonians
flourished, it much diminished him, living among them, that he was of a
foreign city, and nothing else was lacking to him except high birth.
For although he was of the highest family in his own country, still the
Macedonians took it ill that he should ever be set above them, and yet
they did not refuse to endure it: for he surpassed them all in care,
vigilance, endurance, shrewdness, and quickness of mind.

While still very young he came into the friendship of Philip, son of
Amyntas, and in a short time reached the closest intimacy: for already
in the youth the native quality of virtue shone. And so Philip kept him
at hand in the place of a secretary, which among the Greeks is far more
honorable than among the Romans. For among us secretaries are in truth
regarded, as they are, as hired men; but among them, on the contrary, no
one is admitted to that office unless of honorable station, and known
for loyalty and industry, because it is necessary that he share in all
counsels. He held this place of friendship with Philip for seven years.
After Philip was killed he held the same rank with Alexander for
thirteen years. In the last period he even commanded one of the two
wings of cavalry, which was called the Companion cavalry. To both,
moreover, he was always present in council and was held a partner in all
affairs.

* Chapter 2
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h2
:END:
When Alexander died at Babylon, and the kingdoms were being distributed
to the several friends, and the supreme power to be guarded was handed
over to the same man to whom Alexander, dying, had given his ring,
Perdiccas (from which all had inferred that he had entrusted the kingdom
to him until his children should come under their own guardianship: for
Craterus and Antipater were absent, who seemed to outrank him;
Hephaestion was dead, whom alone Alexander, as could easily be
understood, had valued most), at this time Cappadocia was given to
Eumenes, or rather assigned: for it was then in the enemy's power.
Perdiccas had attached this man to himself with great zeal, because he
saw in him great loyalty and industry, not doubting that if he won him
over he would be of great use in the affairs he was preparing. For he
was thinking, as almost all do who desire great empires, of seizing and
embracing the shares of all. Nor indeed did he alone do this, but all
the rest as well who had been Alexander's friends. First Leonnatus
resolved to occupy Macedonia beforehand. He tried with many great
promises to persuade Eumenes to desert Perdiccas and make alliance with
him. When he could not win him over, he tried to kill him, and would
have done it, had Eumenes not escaped secretly by night from his posts.

* Chapter 3
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h3
:END:
Meanwhile those wars were kindled which after Alexander's death were
waged to mutual destruction, and all rushed together to crush Perdiccas.
Although Eumenes saw him weak, because he was forced as one man to
resist all, still he did not desert his friend, nor was he more eager
for safety than for loyalty. Perdiccas had set him over that part of
Asia which lies between Mount Taurus and the Hellespont, and had opposed
him alone to the European adversaries; he himself had set out to attack
Egypt against Ptolemy. Eumenes, since he had neither large nor reliable
forces, because they were both untrained and had been gathered not long
before, and since Antipater and Craterus were said to be approaching and
to have crossed the Hellespont with a great army of Macedonians, men
outstanding both in fame and in experience of war (the Macedonian
soldiers, indeed, then had that reputation which the Romans now enjoy:
for those who hold the supreme empire have always been counted the
bravest): Eumenes understood that if his troops learned against whom
they were being led, they would not only refuse to go, but would melt
away at the very news. And so this seemed to him most prudent: to lead
the soldiers by out-of-the-way routes, on which they could not hear the
truth, and to persuade them that he was setting out against certain
barbarians. And he held to this plan and led his army into line and
joined battle before his soldiers knew with whom they were matching
arms. He also brought it about, by occupying the ground first, that he
fought rather with cavalry, in which he was stronger, than with
infantry, in which he was weaker.

* Chapter 4
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h4
:END:
In their fiercest clash, when they had fought a great part of the day,
the general Craterus fell, and Neoptolemus, who held the second place of
command. With this man Eumenes himself closed. When they had grappled
and fallen from their horses to the ground, so that it could easily be
understood that they had contended with hostile spirit and had fought
even more with the mind than with the body, they were not pulled apart
until the breath left the one. From this man Eumenes was wounded with
several blows, and none the more did he leave the battle, but pressed
the enemy more fiercely. Here, when the cavalry had been routed, the
general Craterus killed, and many besides, especially nobles, taken, the
infantry army, because it had been led into such ground that it could
not slip away against Eumenes' will, asked peace of him. When it had
obtained it, it did not remain in faith, and as soon as it could it
withdrew to Antipater. Eumenes tried to revive Craterus, carried
half-alive from the field; when he could not, in keeping with the man's
dignity and with their former friendship (for he had been intimate with
him while Alexander lived), he buried him with a splendid funeral and
sent the bones to Macedonia to his wife and children.

* Chapter 5
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h5
:END:
While these things were being done at the Hellespont, Perdiccas was
killed at the river Nile by Seleucus and Antigenes, and the supreme
power was conferred on Antipater. Those who had not deserted were, by
vote of the army, condemned to death in absence; among them Eumenes.
Struck by this blow he did not yield, nor did he conduct the war any the
less. But his slender resources, though they did not break the greatness
of his spirit, still lessened it. Antigonus, pursuing him, though he
abounded in every kind of force, was often harassed on the marches, and
was never allowed to come to close quarters except in those places where
few could resist many. But at last, when he could not be taken by
counsel, he was surrounded by numbers. From this, however, having lost
many of his own, he extricated himself and fled to a fort of Phrygia
called Nora. While he was being besieged there and feared that,
remaining in one place, he would lose his war-horses, because there was
no space for exercising them, it was a shrewd invention of his, how a
standing beast might be warmed and exercised, so that it might more
willingly take food and not be kept from bodily motion. He bound up its
head with a strap higher than it could fully touch the ground with its
forefeet, then from behind forced it with blows to leap and kick: which
motion struck out sweat no less than if it were running on a course. By
this it came about, which seemed wonderful to all, that he led the
beasts from the fort equally sleek, after he had been several months
under siege, as if he had kept them in open country. In this
confinement, as often as he wished, he either burned or scattered
Antigonus' works and fortifications. He held himself in one place as
long as it was winter, because he could not keep camp in the open.
Spring was approaching: by a pretended surrender, while he treated of
terms, he imposed on Antigonus' officers and drew himself and all his
men out unharmed.

* Chapter 6
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h6
:END:
To this man Olympias, who had been Alexander's mother, when she had sent
letters and messengers into Asia to consult whether she should go back
to Macedonia (for she was then living in Epirus) and seize her own
affairs, he first advised her not to stir and to wait until Alexander's
son obtained the kingdom; but if she were carried by some desire into
Macedonia, she should forget all injuries and use command against no one
more harshly. She did none of these things: for she both set out into
Macedonia and there conducted herself most cruelly. She asked of
Eumenes, though he was absent, that he not allow the bitterest enemies
of Philip's house and family to destroy the stock as well, and that he
bring aid to Alexander's children. If he granted this request, he should
as soon as possible prepare armies to bring to her as support. And that
he might do this more easily, she had sent letters to all the governors
who remained in their duty, that they should obey him and use his
counsels. Moved by these things Eumenes judged it better, if fortune so
brought it, to perish while returning thanks to those who had deserved
well, than to live ungrateful.

* Chapter 7
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h7
:END:
And so he gathered forces and prepared war against Antigonus. Because
there were together many noble Macedonians, among them Peucestas, who
had been Alexander's bodyguard and then held Persis, and Antigenes,
under whose command was the Macedonian phalanx, fearing envy (which
nevertheless he could not escape) if he himself, a foreigner, should
obtain the supreme command rather than some one of the Macedonians, of
whom there was a multitude there, he set up in the headquarters a tent
in Alexander's name, and ordered a golden chair with scepter and diadem
to be placed in it, and all to meet there daily so that counsel on the
highest matters might be taken there, believing he would be in less envy
if, under the appearance of command and the pretence of Alexander's
name, he seemed to conduct the war. Which he accomplished. For since
they met not at Eumenes' headquarters but at the royal ones, and
deliberated there on affairs, he was in a manner hidden, although
nevertheless all things were done through him alone.

* Chapter 8
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h8
:END:
Here in Paraetacene he clashed with Antigonus, not with a drawn-up
battle line, but on the march, and, having handled him badly, forced him
to return to Media to winter. He himself divided his forces to winter in
the neighboring region of Persis, not as he wished, but as the will of
the soldiers compelled. For that phalanx of Alexander the Great, which
had traversed Asia and conquered the Persians, grown old in glory and
also in license, demanded not to obey its leaders but to command them,
as our veterans now do. And so there is a danger that they may do what
those did, by their own lack of restraint and excessive license ruin
everything, and no less those with whom they have served than those
against whom they have stood. But if anyone reads the deeds of those
veterans, he will recognize them as equal to these, and will judge that
nothing differs except the time. But I will return to those men. They
had taken winter quarters not for the use of war but for their own
luxury, and had gone far apart from one another. When Antigonus learned
this and understood that he was not equal to his opponents if they were
ready, he resolved that some new plan must be taken. There were two ways
by which from Media, where he was wintering, one could reach the enemy's
winter quarters. Of these the shorter was through desert places, which
no one inhabited for want of water, but was of about ten days; the
other, by which all traveled, had a circuit twice as long, but was well
supplied and abounding in all things. If he set out by this, he
understood the enemy would learn of his coming before he himself had
finished a third of the journey; but if he pressed through the empty
country, he hoped to crush the enemy unaware. To accomplish this he
ordered as many skins and even leather sacks as possible to be got
ready, then fodder, besides cooked food for ten days, so that as little
fire as possible should be made in camp. He concealed from all what
route he meant to take. Thus prepared, he set out by the way he had
determined.

* Chapter 9
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h9
:END:
He had finished about half the distance when, from the smoke of his
camp, suspicion was brought to Eumenes that the enemy was approaching.
The leaders assemble; it is asked what must be done. All understood that
their own forces could not be gathered as quickly as Antigonus seemed
about to be at hand. Here, while all were wavering and despairing of the
highest matters, Eumenes said that if they were willing to use speed and
do what they were ordered, which they had not done before, he would set
the matter right. For the journey which the enemy could cross in five
days, he would bring it about that he should be delayed no less than the
same number of days: therefore they should go about and each gather his
own forces. To check Antigonus' onset he took this plan. He sent certain
men to the lowest mountains that lay in the way of the enemy's march,
and instructed them that in the first part of the night they should make
the largest fires they could, as widely as possible, and lessen these at
the second watch, and in the third make them very small, so that, by
imitating the custom of a camp, they might plant in the enemy the
suspicion that there was a camp in those places and that word of their
coming had been given beforehand; and they should do the same the next
night. Those who had been ordered carefully carried out the instruction.
Antigonus, when darkness came on, saw the fires: he believed that his
coming had been heard of and that the enemy had gathered their forces
there. He changed his plan and, since he could not attack them unaware,
turned his route and took that longer circuit of the well-supplied road,
and there waited one day to rest the soldiers' weariness and refresh the
beasts, so that he might fight with a fresher army.

* Chapter 10
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h10
:END:
Thus Eumenes by counsel defeated a shrewd commander and checked his
speed, and yet he gained little. For by the envy of the leaders with
whom he was, and by the treachery of the Macedonian veterans, although
he had come off superior in the battle, he was handed over to Antigonus,
though the army had three times before, at separate times, sworn that it
would defend him and never desert him. But so great was the jealousy of
some against his virtue that they preferred to lose their faith rather
than not destroy him. And Antigonus, though he had been most hostile to
him, would have saved him, if it had been allowed by his own men,
because he understood that he could be helped by no one more in those
affairs which already appeared to all to be hanging over them. For
Seleucus, Lysimachus, and Ptolemy were threatening, already strong in
resources, with whom he would have to contend for the highest matters.
But those around him did not allow it, because they saw that if Eumenes
were received all would be of little account beside him. Antigonus
himself, however, was so inflamed that he could not be soothed except by
great hope of the greatest things.

* Chapter 11
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h11
:END:
And so when he had put him in custody and the captain of the guards
asked how he wished him to be kept, he said, "As the fiercest lion or
the most savage elephant": for he had not yet decided whether to save
him or not. Both kinds of men came to Eumenes, both those who from
hatred wished to take fruit for their eyes from his fall, and those who
from old friendship desired to speak with him and console him, many also
who were eager to know his form, what manner of man he was whom they had
so long and so greatly feared, in whose destruction they had placed
their hope of victory. But Eumenes, when he had been longer in bonds,
said to Onomarchus, who held the chief command of the guard, that he
wondered why he was already being held thus for the third day: for this
did not suit Antigonus' prudence, to use a conquered man so; rather he
should order him either to be killed or to be set free. When he seemed
to Onomarchus to speak too fiercely, Onomarchus said, "What? If you were
of that spirit, why did you not fall in battle rather than come into an
enemy's power?" To this Eumenes: "Would that that had indeed happened!
But it did not come about because I never met with a braver man: for I
never matched arms with anyone who did not yield to me." Nor was that
false: for he fell not by the enemy's valor but by the treachery of
friends. [omitted] For he was of honorable presence and of strength firm
for bearing labor, and not of so great a body as of a graceful figure.

* Chapter 12
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h12
:END:
Concerning this man, since Antigonus did not dare to decide alone, he
referred it to his council. Here, when at first all were disturbed and
wondered that punishment had not already been taken of him by whom they
had been so badly handled for so many years that they had often been
brought to despair, and who had killed the greatest leaders, in whom
finally there was so much that, as long as he lived, they themselves
could not be secure, and if he were killed they would have no trouble:
lastly, if he restored him to safety, they asked what friends he would
use: for they themselves would not be with him if Eumenes were there.
Having learned the will of the council, he nevertheless left himself
space for deliberating until the seventh day. Then, however, when he
already feared that some sedition of the army might arise, he forbade
anyone to be admitted to him and ordered his daily food to be removed:
for he said he would not bring force against one who had once been his
friend. He, nevertheless, worn by hunger no more than three days, when
the camp was being moved, was strangled by the guards without Antigonus'
knowledge.

* Chapter 13
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h13
:END:
Thus Eumenes, at forty-five years of age, after from his twentieth year,
as we have shown above, he had served Philip seven years, had held the
same place with Alexander thirteen years, in those years had commanded a
wing of cavalry for one, and after the death of Alexander the Great had
led an army as general and had partly driven back, partly killed, the
greatest leaders, captured not by Antigonus' valor but by the perjury of
the Macedonians, had such an end of life. In whom how great was the
opinion of all those who after Alexander the Great were called kings can
most easily be judged from this: that of them no one while Eumenes lived
was called king, but prefect; the same men after his fall at once took
the royal array and name, and did not wish to make good what they had at
first proclaimed, that they were keeping the kingdom for Alexander's
children, and, this one champion having been removed, they revealed what
they felt. The leaders of this crime were Antigonus, Ptolemy, Seleucus,
Lysimachus, Cassander. Antigonus, however, handed over Eumenes dead to
his kinsmen to be buried. They buried him with a military and honorable
funeral, the whole army attending, and took care that his bones should
be carried to Cappadocia to his mother and wife and children.
