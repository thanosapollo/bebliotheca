HOMILY ON THE RESURRECTION OF THE DEAD

αʹ. We discoursed with you before concerning doctrines and the glory of the
only-begotten Son of God, stopping the mouths of those who bring down his
dignity, and alienate him from the one who begot him; today I wish to exercise
the more ethical discourse, and to make the whole exhortation concerning life
and conduct; or rather this discourse will not be ethical only, but also
dogmatic; for I am preparing to enter upon the subject of the resurrection.
And this subject is a varied one, both setting us right in doctrines at once,
and ordering our life, and freeing the providence of God from every
accusation. And just as, when it is disbelieved, it overturns our life, and
fills it with countless evils, and turns things upside down; so, when it is
believed, it brings in for us the arguments concerning Providence, and
prepares us to take much care of 50.418ter virtue, and to flee wickedness with
much zeal, and fills all things with calm and peace. For he who does not
expect to rise, nor to give account of the things done by him here, but thinks
that our affairs will stand only as far as the present life, and that there is
nothing further beyond, will neither take care of virtue; for how, when he
expects no reward of his labors to be laid up for him? nor will he hold off
from wickedness, expecting to undergo no punishment of the things badly done
by him; but having given himself over to unseemly desires, he will go after
every form of villainy.

But he who has persuaded himself concerning the coming judgment, and has that
fearful tribunal before his eyes, and the inexorable accounts and the vote
that cannot be cheated, will try in every way to hold to temperance =50.419=
and fairness and the rest of virtue, and to flee incontinence and insolence
and all the rest of villainy; and this man will also be able from this with
much abundance to stop the mouths of those who accuse the providence of God.
When some, seeing the fair and temperate and those who live in righteousness
being choked by poverty, being abused, being slandered, scarcely furnished
with necessary food, and often being pickled in a long disease and grievous
illnesses, and obtaining no protection, but sorcerers and polluted men and men
full of many evils clothed in wealth, living in luxury, wearing bright
garments, dragging after them a great swarm of servants, being admired,
enjoying power, possessing much boldness of speech with the king; then some
accuse the providence of God over these things, and say: Where is this of
providence? and where of a just judgment? the temperate and fair man in evils,
the incontinent and corrupted man in goods; and this one is admired, but that
one is despised; this one enjoys much luxury, that one is in poverty and the
last of evils; he who doubts concerning the things to come will be silenced,
and will say nothing at all; but he who knows how to philosophize concerning
resurrection will easily check the blasphemy, and will say to those who are so
impatient: Cease whetting your own tongue against the God who made you. For
our affairs have not stood only as far as the present life, but we are
hastening toward another life much longer, or rather having no end; and there
in every way this poor man who lives with righteousness will receive the
rewards of these toils, but the incontinent and sorcerer, of this villainy and
of this

unjust luxury will pay the penalty. Let us not then vote concerning the
providence of God from the present things only, but also from the things to
come. For the present things are a contest and a wrestling-ground and a
stadium, but the things to come are prizes and crowns and awards.

Just as therefore the athlete must fight in the wrestling-ground and in sweat
and dust and much heat and toils and hardships, so also the righteous man must
endure many things here and bear all things nobly, if indeed he is going to
receive the bright crowns there. And if the prosperities of the wicked trouble
some, let them reckon that again also upon these, that, just as robbers and
tomb-breakers and murderers and pirates who sail the sea, before they enter
the court, enjoy much luxury, making others' misfortunes their own plenty, and
acquiring unjust wealth, and getting drunk every day; but when they come under
the votes of the judge, they pay the penalty of all those things; so also
these who buy harlots, and set Sybaritic tables, and lift their eyebrows, and
swagger in the marketplace, and tear the poor, when the only-begotten Son of
God comes with his own angels, and sits upon that judgment-seat, and brings
the world into the midst, being brought in naked and stripped of all the show,
and having no advocate nor protector, will be carried off without any pardon
into the rivers of fire.

Do not then either bless these because of the =50.420= luxury here, but weep
because of the coming punishment; nor count the righteous man miserable
because of the poverty here, but bless him because of the coming wealth of
good things, and root the word concerning the resurrection in your own mind,
so that, even if you are good and it tries you, you may become more proven,
taking on greater eagerness from the coming hopes; and if you are bad, you may
stand off from villainy, making yourself more temperate by the fear of the
coming punishment.

βʹ. For this reason Paul also continually moves for us the word concerning
resurrection, just as indeed today you heard him crying and saying: For we
know that if our earthly house of the tabernacle be dissolved, we have a house
from God, not made with hands, eternal, in the heavens. Or rather let us lead
the word higher up, and let us see from where he fell into the word concerning
resurrection. For it is not simply, nor as it happened, that he continually
moves this teaching; but both wishing to teach the things to come at once, and
wanting to strengthen the athletes of piety. For now, through the grace of
God, we enjoy much peace; for kings live in piety, and rulers have come to
know the truth, and peoples and cities and nations, having been freed from
error, all worship Christ; but then, at the beginning of the preaching, when
the seeds of piety were just being cast, the war was great and diverse and
varied. For rulers and kings and household members and kinsmen and all were at
war with the faithful, and the war was raised even through nature itself. For
a father often delivered up a child, and a mother a daughter, and a master a
household servant. For not only the cities, nor the countries, but even houses
often fought against themselves; and the tumult that then held them was more
grievous than any civil war. For both money was snatched away, and freedom was
betrayed, and the danger hung over life itself, not barbarians running upon
them and abusing them, but those who seemed to preside and to rule treating
the ruled more grievously than all enemies. And making this clear Paul said
that You endured a great contest of sufferings; this, being made a spectacle
with reproaches both


and afflictions, and this, having become partners of those who were so
treated. For you both suffered with my bonds, and accepted the plunder of your
goods with joy. And to the Galatians he says: Did you suffer so many things in
vain, if it be indeed in vain? And to the Thessalonians, and to the
Philippians, and simply to all to whom he writes, he witnesses many such
things.

And this was not the only grievous thing, that a great and continuous war had
been moved from without, but that even among the faithful themselves it
happened that there were some scandals and strifes and contentions and
jealousies; and making these very things clear Paul said: Without, fightings,
within, fears. And this war was more grievous than that, both for the ruled
and for the teachers. Paul at any rate did not so fear the plots of the
enemies as the falls within, and the lawlessness of his own. When at any rate
someone committed fornication among the Corinthians, he continued the whole of
that time mourning, tearing his own inward parts, and wailing bitterly. There
was also a third thing not less than what has been said, bringing much toil
upon the believers, the very nature of the affa50.421 irs being full of many
sweats and toils. For the road on which the apostles were leading them was not
some easy and ready one, but rough and hard to accomplish and needing some
philosophic and sleepless soul and one accurately finished from every side.
For this reason Christ also called it narrow and compressed. For it was not
permitted to live without fear, as among the Greeks, in shamefulness and
drunkenness and gluttony and luxury and extravagance; but it was necessary
both to bridle anger, and to master unseemly desire, and to look down on
money, and to trample on glory, and to be above envy and grudging. And how
great a toil these things furnish, they know who box with them every day. For
what is more grievous than unseemly desire, tell me, which, like a rabid dog,
continually leaps upon us, and troubles us every day, and needs a soul
continually awake? And what is more bitter than wrath? For it is sweet to
avenge oneself on the one who has wronged, but this was not permitted. And why
do I say, it was not permitted to avenge oneself on the one who has wronged?
It was necessary to do good to those who grieve us, and to bless those who
revile, and never to throw out even a bitter word; and it was necessary to
display temperance, not that as far as the deeds only, but also that as far as
the thought. For one must hold off not only from the incontinent act itself,
but also from such a look, and not even luxuriate in the looks of well-formed
women, as being about to undergo the last penalty even for this viewing. Since
therefore the war from those without was great, the fear from those within was
great, the toil toward the working of virtue was great. And there was also a
fourth thing besides, the inexperience of those who were about to contend in
this great contest. For the apostles did not take men who had received piety
from ancestors, but men reared in slackness and luxury and drunkenness and
shamefulness and incontinence. And this was no small thing toward the
difficulty of the contests, that they had not been trained in this philosophy
from above, nor from ancestors, but were now for the first time stripping for
these contests.

γʹ. Since therefore the difficulty for those then contending was so great,
consoling their toil he continually moved the word concerning resurrection.
And he does not only by this exhort and anoint the athletes, but also by the
narration of his own sufferings. Therefore before falling into the words
concerning resurrection, he narrates his own sufferings, saying thus: In
everything afflicted, but not straitened; at a loss, but not utterly at a
loss; persecuted, but not forsaken; cast down, but not destroyed; through
these making clear the daily deaths, that like living corpses


they went about being delivered over to death every day. When therefore the
things that were being brought against them, then he moves the word concerning
the resurrection: For we believe, he says, that he who raised our Lord Jesus
Christ will also raise us through Jesus and will present us with you.
Therefore we do not faint, having a very great consolation in the contests,
the hope of the things to come. And he did not say to them: Therefore do not
faint; but what? Therefore we do not faint, showing himself also to be in the
contests continually. For in the Olympic contests, the athlete within =50.422=
contends, but the trainer sitting far off brings in the help from words,
contributing to the wrestler only so much as to shout and to speak; but no law
permits him to stand near and to bring in the alliance from the hands.

But in the contests of piety it is not so, but the same man is both trainer
and athlete at once. For this reason, not sitting outside the
wrestling-ground, but having thrown himself into the contests themselves, he
anoints those boxing with him, saying: Therefore we do not faint. And he did
not say, Therefore I do not faint, but, Therefore we do not faint, wishing to
raise them by the praises. But even if our outer man is being corrupted, yet
the inner is being renewed day by day. See apostolic understanding. He
exhorted them from the things they were suffering, that In everything
afflicted, but not straitened; he exhorted from the resurrection of Jesus,
saying, That he who raised Jesus will also raise us. Then he also moves some
other manner of consolation. For since the many of men, being some
small-souled and weak and wretched, even if they have been persuaded
concerning the resurrection, grow faint toward the length of time and grow
dizzy and fall back, he also gives them another wage and recompense before the
resurrection. What then is this? Even if our outer man is being corrupted, yet
the inner is being renewed day by day; calling the body the outer man, saying
the soul is the inner man. And what he means is of this sort: Even before
rising, and before enjoying the coming glory, you have already from here
received no small recompense of the toils, in the very being afflicted our
soul being renewed for us, becoming wiser and more devout, acquiring more
patience, being made more vigorous and stronger. For just as those who contend
in the bodily contest, even before the crowns, and before the awards, take a
great wage from the exercise itself and from the wrestling, making their own
bodies healthier and stronger through the exercise, and fleeing every
infirmity; so also in the contests of virtue, even before heaven is opened,
and before the Son of God comes, and before we receive the recompenses, we
reap a great wage from here, our soul being made more philosophic. For those
who have sailed a great sea, and have borne countless waves, and have boxed
with many beasts, and have endured many storms, even before the trade itself
reap no small gain from this long sojourn, by daring and being bold against
the deep, and making these overseas sojourns without fear and with pleasure;
so also in the present life, he who has borne many afflictions for Christ, he
who has suffered many terrible things, even before the kingdom of the heavens
has received a great recompense, having acquired boldness of speech toward God
from here =50.423= already, and having made his own soul higher, so as
henceforth to laugh at all the terrible things. And so that what is said may
also become clearer, I wish to make it manifest upon an example. For this same
Paul, having borne countless terrible things, received no small recompenses
even from here, laughing at tyrants, rising up against


raging peoples, looking down on every punishment, and remaining unstruck
toward beasts, and toward iron, and toward seas, and toward cliffs, and toward
uprisings, and toward plots, and toward all the terrible things, of whom what
could become equal? For the unexercised man who has suffered nothing terrible
is accustomed to be troubled even by chance things; or rather not the things
themselves only, but even mere expectations; and why do I say mere
expectations? the shadows frighten and terrify such a one; but he who has
stripped for all things, and has entered the contests, and has suffered
countless terrible things, is henceforth above all, laughing at those who
threaten as at jackdaws crying. And this is no small crown, nor the chance
wage, when nothing of human things can grieve him, when the things fearful to
others become contemptible to this man, when over the things at which others
shudder and are beside themselves, he himself is about to laugh, having
transferred himself by the excess of patience into the philosophy of the
angelic powers. For if indeed we bless the body that is able to bear without
pain both cold and heat, and famine, and want, and journeying, and other
hardships; much more must one bless the soul that is able to bear stoutly and
nobly all the assaults of the terrible things, and to keep its own mind
unenslaved through all. Such a one is more kingly than the kings themselves.
For that one both bodyguards and friends and enemies could wrong much by
plotting and doing evil but him who has such a soul as I have now said,
neither king, nor bodyguard, nor household servant, nor friend, nor enemy, nor
the devil himself will be able to harm. For how, him who has practiced to
think none of the things supposed to be terrible to be terrible?

δʹ. Such was the blessed Paul; for this reason he said: Who shall separate us
from the love of Christ? tribulation, or straitness, or persecution, or
famine, or nakedness, or danger, or sword? as it is written, that for your
sake we are put to death all the day, we were reckoned as sheep of slaughter;
but in all these we more than conquer through him who loved us. This then
hinting also here he said, that Even if our outer man is being corrupted, yet
the inner is being renewed day by day; the body becomes weaker, he says, but
the soul more powerful and stronger, and is more winged. And just as a soldier
having arms that weigh him down, even if he be exceedingly noble and warlike,
would not become fearful to the enemies, the heaviness of the arms spoiling
the speed of the feet and the experience of warlike things; but if he should
take light and easy-to-handle ones, he comes upon the opponents like every
winged thing; so also he who does not fatten his own flesh with drunkenness,
nor with slackness and luxury, but making it lighter and finer by fasts and
prayers, and by the much patience of afflictions, just as =50.424= some winged
one coming down from above, so with much rush falling upon the phalanxes of
the demons, easily brings down the opposing powers, and makes them subject. So
also Paul, having received many blows, and having fallen into the prison, and
having been bound in the stocks, had the body weaker, stretched by toils, but
the soul strong and vigorous; and so strong was the bound man, as by voice
alone to shake the foundations of the prison, and to bring the loosed jailer
bound to his own feet, and to open the doors that had been shut. Paul
therefore gave us this no small consolation even before the resurrection, that
we become better and more philosophic by the trials.

For this reason he says: Tribulation works patience, and patience proof, and


proof hope, and hope does not put to shame And again someone else says: An
untried man is unproven, and the unproven is worthy of no account. So that we
reap no small things from the affliction even before the resurrection, having
the soul more proven and wiser and more intelligent, and having been freed
from all cowardice. For this reason he says: Even if our outer man is being
corrupted, yet the inner is being renewed day by day. How is it renewed, tell?
All unmanliness being driven out, unseemly desire being quenched, love of
money, vainglory, simply all the other corrupted reasonings being destroyed.
Just as therefore the soul that enjoys idleness and fearlessness is easily
caught by these passions; so the one that has continually been occupied in the
contests on behalf of piety does not even have leisure ever to think these
things, the care concerning the wrestlings leading it away from all those. For
this reason he said: It is renewed day by day. Then again consoling the souls
that are pained at the terrible things being brought on, and that do not know
how to philosophize, he raises them through the hope of the things to come,
saying thus: For the momentary lightness of the affliction works for us
according to excess unto excess an eternal weight of glory, we not looking at
the things seen, but at the things not seen. For the things seen are
temporary, but the things not seen, eternal. And what he means is of this
sort: Most of all then even here the affliction benefits us, making our soul
wiser and more philosophic; and after this it also procures for us countless
good things according to the future, not balancing the toils, but much greater
than the wrestlings both according to quantity and according to quality.

Paul, making clear both these things, I mean, both the superiority of the
dangers, and making the comparison of the prizes, also sets the eternal
against the momentary, the weight against the light, the glory against the
affliction. For the affliction is temporary, he says, and light, but the ease
rather he did not say, ease, but, glory, which is much greater than the ease
eternal and continuous and great. And weight here, he did not say the toilsome
and burdensome, but the costly and honorable, according to the custom of the
many, who are accustomed so to ca50.425 ll the things of much price also of
much weight. When therefore he says weight of glory, he means greatness of
glory. Do not then reckon this only, he says, that you are scourged and
driven, but also the crowns and the recompenses, that those are much greater
and brighter than the present things, and have no end, nor limit. But these,
he says, are in experience, and those in hopes; and these are manifest, but
those do not appear, but are hanging and in mid-air; but even if they are
unmanifest, yet they are more manifest than the things that appear. Why do I
say more manifest? You are able to see those more than these. For these pass
by, but those remain. Therefore he added, saying: We not looking at the things
seen, but at the things not seen; for the things seen are temporary, but the
things not seen eternal.

εʹ. And if you should say: And how shall I be able to see the unmanifest
things, and not to see the present things? I will try to lead you into this
faith from the things of life. For not even of the affairs in the world, of
these perishable ones, would anyone easily take hold, unless he has seen the
unmanifest things before the manifest. What I mean is of this sort: The
merchant endures many storms and uprisings of waves and shipwrecks and
countless difficulties, and he also enjoys wealth after the storms, disposing
of the cargoes, and working much business. But the storms are earlier, and the
cargoes later; and the seas and the waves appear when he has gone out of the
harbor, but the trade does not appear; for it is still in hopes. But
nevertheless if he does not first look toward that unmanifest and not present
one, nor being in hands, but in hopes, he would not take hold of these present
and appearing things.


So also a farmer yokes oxen, and draws a plow, and cuts a deep furrow, and
casts seeds, and spends all the things that are, and endures frost and ice and
rains and many other hardships, and after this toil expects to see the
standing grain waving, and the threshing-floor filled. Do you see how here
also the toil is earlier, and after these the wage; and the wage is unclear,
but the toil is clear and manifest; and that is in hopes, this in hands? But
nevertheless this man also, if he does not first look toward that unclear and
unmanifest wage not seen with the eyes of the body, will not only not yoke
oxen, nor draw a plow, nor cast seeds, but will not even be moved from home
toward this work ever.

How then is it absurd in the affairs of life to see the unmanifest things
before the manifest, and to endure the toils before the wages, and first
bearing all the difficult things, then to wait for the good things from them,
and being exhorted by the hopes of the unmanifest things thus to take hold of
the manifest; but in the case of God to hesitate and to doubt, and to demand
the recompenses before the toils, and to appear more small-souled than farmers
and sailors? For we shall not only appear worse than them according to this,
being vexed toward the future, but also according to another thing not less
than this. What then is this? That those men, not even being able to be
confident altogether concerning the end, not even so stand off from the toils;
but you, having a trustworthy surety of the crowns, not even so imitate their
endurance. For the farmer often after casting the seeds, and tending the
earth, and seeing the standing grain waving, either hail, or rust, or locust.
or some other such blow having been brought on, has fallen away from the toils
=50.426= recompense, and after the many sweats returns home with empty hands.
And the merchant again, after running through a great sea bringing the
merchant-ship filled, often at the very mouth of the harbor, winds having
fallen, having dashed against some reef, has scarcely come out having saved
the body naked. And in simply all the things of life it is likely that many
such misfortunes often happen toward the end. But in your contests this is not
so; but it is altogether necessary that the one who has contended, the one who
has sown piety, and has borne many toils, attain the end. For God did not
commit the recompenses of these toils to unevenness of airs, nor to rushes of
winds; but they are laid up in the heavens, in the inviolable treasuries.

For this reason Paul also said: Tribulation works patience, and patience
proof, and proof hope, and hope does not put to shame. Do not then say that
the things to come are unmanifest; for if you should wish to examine with
accuracy, they are much more manifest than the things in hands. And showing
this very thing to us again Paul calls the one eternal, and the other
temporary, making clear their corruptible nature by the temporary. For before
they appear they fly away, and they run off before they have arrived, and
their changes are swift-turning, and the possession is untrustworthy. This
both upon wealth and glory and power, and upon the well-formedness of bodies,
this both upon strength and upon simply the other things of life one might see
happening. For this reason the prophet also, mocking those who live in luxury
and are mad about money and all the other show, They reckoned these things, he
says, as standing, and not as fleeing. For just as it is not possible to take
hold of a shadow, so neither of the affairs of life; but some are dissolved by
the end, and some even before the end flow past more swiftly-falling than
every torrent. But the things to come are not such; for they do not know
change, they do not receive turning, they do not understand old age, they have
no alteration, but they flourish continually. and remain upon the same
comeliness. So that if one must unmanifest and unclear


say and untrustworthy, one must say the present affairs, and not remaining
with those who have acquired them, but changing masters, and every day leaping
from this one to another, and again from that one to another. Paul then,
having made all these things clear to us, and for this reason having called
the present things temporary, and the things to come eternal, moves the word
concerning the resurrection, saying thus: For we know that if our earthly
house of the tabernacle be dissolved, we have a house from God, not made with
hands, eternal in the heavens.

στʹ. See again also here how he has used the words properly, showing through
the names themselves the power of the thoughts. For he did not simply call the
body a tabernacle, but showing to us the temporary character of the present
life, and making clear the change toward the better, all but saying: Why do
you groan and weep, beloved, that you are struck and driven and fall into
prison? why do you lament these partial ill-treatments, when you ought to
receive the complete dissolution of the body; or rather not simply of the
=50.427= body, but of the corruption in the body? For in order to show that
these partial ill-treatments ought not only not to grieve us, but even to
gladden us, he shows that the catholic and last taking-down is a work of
prayer for us, so calling the dissolution through death. And again: For indeed
in this tabernacle we groan, longing to put on over us our dwelling from
heaven. And house of a tabernacle he either calls the body through both of the
names; or house of a tabernacle the houses in which we dwell, the cities, the
shape of the present life. And he did not simply say, I know, but, We know,
taking on also the mind of the hearers.

I am not discoursing, he says, concerning things that are doubted, nor
concerning things that are unknown, but on behalf of the things for which you
have already received the faith, having believed in the resurrection of the
Lord. For this reason we also call the bodies of those who have departed
tabernacles. And see how he has used the word properly also. He did not say,
It was taken away and vanished, but, It was dissolved, showing that it is
dissolved, in order that it may rise more cheerful, in order that it may rise
more bright. Then just as upon the toils and the prizes he made a comparison,
from the quality, from the time and the quantity; so also he does here,
calling the body that falls a tabernacle, and the one that rises a house; and
not simply, a house, but eternal; and not simply, eternal, but heavenly,
showing the superiority both from the time and from the place. For this is
earthly, that heavenly; this temporary, that eternal. And now we need both a
body and dwellings because of the weakness of the flesh; but then the same
will be both body and dwelling, not needing a roof, nor any coverings, the
incorruption sufficing for it in place of all. Then showing the superiority of
the good things stored up in it, he says: For indeed in this tabernacle we
groan. He did not say, I groan, but he makes the mind common. For indeed in
this, he says, we groan, wishing to draw them into his own philosophy, and to
make them partners of his own judgment. For indeed in this we groan, longing
to put on over us our dwelling from the heavens. He did not say simply, To put
on, but To put on over; and he adds: If indeed also having put on, we shall
not be found naked. And what has been said seems to be unclear, but it becomes
clearer through the things next added: For indeed we who are in the tabernacle
groan being burdened, inasmuch as we do not wish to put off, but to put on
over. Do you see how he does not forget himself, nor does he call this body a
house, but a tabernacle again; Inasmuch as we do not wish to put off, but to
put on over? Here he has given a vital blow to those who slander the nature of
the body, and accuse our flesh.


For since he said that we groan and we do not wish to put off, lest you think
that he flees the body as an evil and a cause of villainy and an enemy and a
foe, hear how he corrects the suspicion; first by saying, that We groan
longing to put on over us our dwelling from heaven; for he who puts on over in
truth puts another on over; then by saying, We groan being burdened, inasmuch
as we do not wish to put off, but longing to put on over. And what he means is
of this sort: We do not wish to put off the flesh, he says, =50.428= but the
corruption; not the body, but the death. Body is one thing, and death another;
body is one thing, and corruption another; neither is the body corruption, nor
is the corruption a body. And the body is corruptible, but the body is not
corruption; and the body is mortal, but the body is not death; but the body
became a work of God, and the corruption and the death were brought in by sin.
I wish then, he says, to put off the alien, not the own; and the alien is not
the body, but the corruption. For this reason he says: Inasmuch as we do not
wish to put off, that is, the body, but to put on over the body the
incorruption.

The body is in the middle of corruption and incorruption. It puts off then
corruption, and puts on over incorruption; it casts away what it received from
sin, and acquires what the grace of God gave. And that you may learn that the
putting off he says not concerning the body, but concerning the corruption and
the death, hear the things immediately added. For having said, We do not wish
to put off, but to put on over, he did not say: In order that the body may be
swallowed by bodilessness; but what? In order that the mortal may be swallowed
by the life, that is, in order that it may vanish, in order that it may
perish; so that he speaks of a perishing not of the body, but of death and
corruption. For the life coming upon does not vanish and consume the body, but
the corruption that has come upon it and the death. The groaning then is not
through the body, but through the overlying corruption; for indeed the body is
heavy and burdensome and grievous, not from its own nature, but from the
mortality that later accrued to it. But it is not a corruptible body, but it
is incorruptible. For so great is its nobility, as even in the corruption
itself to display its own dignity. At any rate the shadows of the apostles
drove away the bodiless powers, and the dust and the ash prevailed over
demons, and the garments that had company with their bodies took away
diseases, and brought back health.

ζʹ. For do not tell me of phlegm and bile, and sweat, and filth, and the other
things, which those who accuse the body say; for these were not of the nature
of the body, but of the corruption that later came upon it. And if you wish to
learn thoroughly its virtue, go over the formation of all the members, the
shape, the operations, the agreement with one another; and you will see the
constitution of these members with one another more accurate than every
well-lawed city that has all the citizens philosophic. And if running past
these things up and down, you should put forward only its corruptible and
mortal character, not even from this shall we be at a loss for a defense. For
that not only no harm has come from this, but even much gain for the race of
men, is clear from that. For all the saints living with this displayed that
angelic constitution, and no harm at all came to them from this toward the
course of virtue; and those looking toward impiety had no small hindrance, the
corruption of the body, toward not going further of their own lawlessness.

For if many =50.429= of men, wearing this corruptible and passible body,
imagined equality with God, and transacted many things in order to put such a
glory around themselves; if they did not have the body passible and
corruptible, convicting their weakness, whom of the more mindless would they
not have deceived? When therefore even of impiety, which


is the last limit of wickedness, it appears to become a hindrance, and
furnishes to the saints to display the manliness of the soul, of what pardon
would those who slander it and speak evil of it obtain? For we would not have
these things only to say concerning it, but that it has also become a cause of
knowledge of God for us. For if his invisible things from the creation of the
world are clearly seen, being understood by the things made, and faith is from
hearing, it is quite clear that the soul, being led by the hand through eyes
and ears, came to know the God who made it. For this reason Paul loves it, and
cries saying, that We do not wish to put it off, but to put on over it the
immortality. And do not tell me: How can the body rise again and become
incorruptible? For when the power of God is at work, let the how not be laid
beside. And why do I say in the case of God? He made you yourself a craftsman
of resurrection to become, this upon the seeds, and this upon the arts, and
this upon the metallic materials. For indeed the seeds, if they do not first
die and rot and be corrupted, do not bear the ear. Just as therefore seeing
there the grain rotting and being dissolved, you do not doubt concerning the
resurrection, but you make for yourself a most clear demonstration; for if it
does not remain rotting nor being corrupted, it would never rise; so also
reckon upon your own body; when you see the corruption, then most of all
philosophize concerning the resurrection. For death is nothing other than a
taking-away of the corruption henceforth; for death does not consume the body
simply, but its corruption. This one may see happening also upon the metallic
materials.

At any rate those experienced in these things take the gold-bearing earth, and
handing it over to the furnace they work gold, and mixing sand and other
material, they have made pure glass. Then what the power of fire, this the
grace of God will not be able, tell me? and who of those who have mind in any
way would say these things? How he made you at the beginning, consider, and no
longer doubt concerning the resurrection. Did he not take earth and form it?
and yet what is more difficult, to make from earth fleshes and veins and skin
and bones and fibers and sinews and arteries, and the organic, and the simple
bodies, eyes, ears, noses, feet and hands, and to put into each of these both
a private and a common operation, or to work things that have become
corruptible immortal? Do you not see how the earth is of one form, but the
body is varied and of many forms, both according to the operations, and
according to the colors, and according to the formation, and according to the
substances, and according to all the other things? How then do you doubt
concerning the things to come, tell me? and what need is there to speak
concerning bodies? For the infinite powers, and the peoples of the angels, the
archangels, the ranks above these, how did he make them, tell me? For I would
not be able to say the manner, but that to will only sufficed for him. Then he
who has worked so many bodiless armies, is he not able to renew again a man's
body that has been corrupted, and to bring it to a greater dignity? And who is
so without feeling, as to doubt concerning these things, =50.430= and to take
away the resurrection? For if a body does not rise, a man does not rise; for
the man is not soul only, but soul and body. If then soul only rises, the
living being rises from a half, but not complete; and otherwise resurrection
would not be said properly upon a soul. For resurrection is of that which has
fallen and been dissolved, and a soul is not dissolved, but a body. And what
is the, If indeed also having put on, we shall not be found naked? He hints to
us here a great and unspeakable mystery. What then is this? Which he also says
in the Letter to the Corinthians; that We shall all rise, but each in his own
order. And what is what is said? that both a Greek and a Jew and a heretic and
every man who has come into this world, according to


that day will rise. And making this very thing clear he said: We shall not all
sleep, but we shall all be changed in an atom, in the twinkling of an eye, at
the last trumpet.

ηʹ. Since therefore the resurrection is catholic for all, and common both of
pious and of impious, and of wicked and of good men, lest from this you think
that some unjust judgment comes to be, nor say to yourself: What in the world
is this? I the earnest man who have toiled so many things and been distressed
rise, and the Greek, and he who has been impious, and has worshiped idols, and
has not known Christ, he also rises likewise, and enjoys the same honor? lest
saying these things you be troubled, hear what he says: If indeed also having
put on, we shall not be found naked. And how is it, he says, for one who has
put on incorruption and immortality to be found naked? how? When we are
stripped of glory, and deprived of the boldness of speech toward God. For
indeed the bodies of the sinners rise incorruptible and immortal; but this
honor becomes for them a provision of punishment and of vengeance; for they
rise incorruptible, in order that they may burn through all. For since that
fire is unquenchable, it also needs such bodies never being consumed.

For this reason he says: If indeed also having put on, we shall not be found
naked. For this is not the only thing sought, that we rise and put on
immortality, but that having risen and having put on the immortality, we may
not be found naked of glory, and of the boldness of speech toward God; that we
may not be delivered over to the fire. For this reason he says: If indeed also
having put on, we shall not be found naked. Then again making the word
concerning the resurrection more trustworthy, having said that the mortal is
about to be swallowed by the life, he added: And he who has worked us for this
very thing is God. And what he means is this: From the beginning he formed the
man for this, not in order that he perish, but in order that he travel toward
incorruption. So that even when he permitted the death, he permitted it with
such a mind, in order that you may be made temperate from the punishment, and
having become better, you may be able to be brought to the immortality again.
And from above and from the beginning this counsel and this mind prevailed
with God, and with this vote he formed the first man; this he hinted to us
immediately even from the very prefaces. For if he had not wished from the
beginning to open for us the doors of the resurrection, he would not have
allowed Abel, who had displayed all virtue, and had become a friend to him, to
suffer =50.431= the things which he suffered. But now showing to us that we
travel toward another life, and some other age of the righteous has been left
remaining, having the recompenses and the crowns laid up, he allowed the first
who became righteous, not having received here the wages of the things for
which he toiled, to go away crying, through the things which he suffered, to
all and saying, that after the life from here there is some retribution and a
wage and a recompense. For this reason he also translated Enoch, and snatched
Elijah up, hinting to us the doctrine concerning the resurrection. The power
of the one who does it is sufficient then for full assurance; but if someone
is weaker, and wishes to take another demonstration and a pledge of the coming
resurrection, God has given this also with much abundance, sending down the
grace of the Spirit with much lavishness. For this reason Paul also, having
made the word concerning the resurrection trustworthy from the resurrection of
Christ, from God being the one who crafts, adds also this saying, saying that
he has also given us an earnest, not of money nor of gold and silver, but an
earnest of Spirit; and the earnest is a part of the whole, and trustworthy on
behalf of the whole. For just as in contracts he who has received the earnest
is henceforth confident on behalf of the whole and has believed, so also you,
having taken an earnest, I mean the gifts of the Spirit, no longer


doubt concerning the good things laid up. For he who raises the dead, he who
heals the blind, he who drives away demons, he who cleanses lepers, he who
sets right illnesses, he who looses death, he who is able to do so many and so
great things in a perishable and mortal body, of what pardon will you obtain
doubting concerning the resurrection? For if, the time of the recompense not
yet having come, but the wrestlings still standing, God has repaid us with so
many crowns, consider how many good things he will give you at the time of the
awards.

And if someone should say: But we do not see these signs happening now, nor do
we have so great a power; I would say that, that it makes no difference,
whether now, or formerly =50.432= these things have come to be. For that they
came to be before by the apostles, the Churches everywhere of the world bear
witness, and peoples, and cities, and nations that ran to the unlearned and
fishermen. For the unlettered and beggars and poor and cheap would not have
mastered the world, if they had not had the help of the signs. But
nevertheless you have not become stripped of the grace of the Spirit, but many
symbols of that gift still remain even now, much greater and more wonderful
than these very things that have been enumerated; for it is not equal to raise
a body that has died, and to free a soul deadened by the sins from that
destruction, which comes to be through baptism; it is not equal to drive away
illnesses of flesh, and to put off a burden of sins; it is not equal to set
right a maimed eye, and to shine upon a darkened soul with light. For if there
were not an earnest of the Spirit even now, baptism would not have been
constituted, there would not have been a remission of sins. there would not
have been righteousness and sanctification, we would not have received
adoption, we would not have enjoyed mysteries; for a mystical body and blood
would never come to be without the grace of the Spirit; we would not have had
priests; for it is not possible for these ordinations to come to be without
that visitation. And one might say many other symbols of the grace of the
Spirit. So that you also have the earnest of the Spirit, and you have put off
a deadening of soul, and a maiming of reasonings, and you have put off the
unclean life. Let us not then be vexed concerning the things to come, having
taken such pledges; but having gathered the word concerning the resurrection
from every side, let us also display a life worthy of these doctrines, in
order that we may also attain the unmoved good things, which surpass every
word and human mind; of which may it come to be that we all attain, by the
grace and love of mankind of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom and with whom
the glory to the Father, together with the Holy Spirit, unto the ages of the
ages. Amen.
