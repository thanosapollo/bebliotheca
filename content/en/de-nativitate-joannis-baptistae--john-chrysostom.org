* On the Nativity of John the Baptist
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: h1
:END:

Of our father among the saints, John Chrysostom, archbishop of
Constantinople, on the birthday of Saint John the Forerunner and Baptist.
Today the word of teaching cannot be put off, for the exposition has John
the Baptist, the unsilenced voice of the wilderness. "I am," he says, "the
voice of one crying in the wilderness." And wherever the memory of a voice
is brought forward, the honor of words is of necessity owed.
But what honor shall we pay this righteous man? We are hemmed in on every
side by his achievements. For he proclaims good news while still carried in
the womb; he works wonders the moment he is born; he advances as he is
trained in the deserts; he is nourished as he is led forward by the Spirit;
he stands between law and grace; he preaches the baptism of repentance; he
baptizes the Master when he arrives; as he baptizes he also beholds the
grace of the Trinity; he bears witness to the truth by crying out; he
points out the Master who had been prophesied; he confesses the one
stronger than himself; he leads those who believe to him; he preaches that
that One grants the fulfillment; he brings forward peoples thirsting to
know him; running, he announces the Master; he urges them to run to him; he
teaches the crowds what is profitable; he admonishes even tax-collectors
who come to be baptized; he rebukes even Herod for his lawlessness; and he
receives a blessed end as well. With what words shall we marvel at these
things? What tongue shall we set moving among them? Which of them shall we
pass over, and what shall we even have strength to say about them? What
hope can stir us toward them? Great is the breadth of the praises, great
the sea of the achievements. The mere memory of the deeds strikes those who
speak with astonishment, and it plants fear in the soul of the one who
marvels.
But let Zechariah also come now, the father of the righteous man, who was
both condemned to silence and through this child found speech. Let him
narrate to us the things that concern himself. Let him say how he spoke
when his tongue was held fast, how he talked when his mouth was bridled,
how John is born and the angel's sentence is loosed, how the punishment is
brought on and the healing is granted. And if you please, let us come at
last to the exposition of the wonder itself, which holds the conception of
the righteous man. For the evangelist, introducing a brief proclamation
concerning both Zechariah himself and Elizabeth his wife, first indicates
their names and their nature and their rank, then adds praise of their way
of life and character, in which he also shows the condemnation of silence
and makes known the grace of fluent speech.
And hear the words of the Gospel: "They had no child," it says. Now I not
only see their childlessness, but I also recognize the ancient image of the
righteous, I mean Abraham and Sarah. But I also learn of barrenness giving
birth, another childbearing of grace and of nature, a strange mystery of
bringing forth children. For this is also the manner of this promise: "An
angel appeared," it says, "standing at the right of the altar of incense."
The place of the vision is worthy of trust, showing the sight to be truth
and not error. "But Zechariah was troubled," it says, "when he saw, and
fear fell upon him." O cowardice, the prelude of unbelief, the rottenness
of the mind, the slipperiness of the soul! Which the angel himself,
correcting, said: "Do not fear, Zechariah, because your petition has been
heard." He casts out the fear and brings in longing in its place. For
having said "Do not fear," he did not fall silent, but also announced the
gain of the prayer. "Your petition has been heard," he says. And what was
the petition? The increase of his Levitical tribe. "For your wife
Elizabeth," he says, "will bear you a son, and you shall call his name
John."
Fair is the possession of the promise, great the gift of the vision. Truly
Abrahamic is the boast. For as the conception of Sarah was foretold to that
man, so also the pregnancy of Elizabeth has been announced beforehand to
this one. But the prediction is equal; the order of the events is not
equal. For Abraham suffered nothing terrible from the promise, whereas
Zechariah, after the good news, is found speechless, not having believed
the angel and having received silence as his punishment.
"For behold," he says, "you shall be silent and not able to speak until the
day these things come to pass, because you did not believe my words." The
judgment is unyielding toward the one who has offended; the wrath of the
punishment admits no delay. For truly the angel ought not to have been
disbelieved, nor the altar held cheap, nor the hour of incense reckoned a
time of deceit (for the angel appeared to him as he was offering incense),
nor the history of Abraham forgotten. Both then a barren woman, and at
another time old age, bring forth children; everywhere what was said has
been shown easy to believe. For the wonder of such childbearing held not
only a barren woman but also a virgin giving birth contrary to expectation.
And the unbelievable was being trained beforehand into faith, the
impossible being accustomed as possible. Hence unbelief is now punished in
the case of the barren woman, so that the wonder-working in the case of the
Virgin may not be doubted.
"You shall be silent," he says, "and not able to speak." O the punishment
that loves mankind, which worked the chastisement rather as a correction!
For the angel chastens the very voice that slipped; he bridles the very
tongue that dared. For he was, it says, making signs, and he remained mute.
Well did the evangelist also write "he remained." For the silence was
waiting for the voice that was being born, Zechariah for John, the old man
for the child, the priest for the prophet. And indeed the infant is born
and the father's voice rises again. For when Zechariah was asked, it says,
what he wished the child to be called, he asked for a writing-tablet and
wrote saying: "John is his name." He who did not believe the angel's words
was now compelled to proclaim the vision in writing. He who did not then
receive by hearing what was spoken now also legislates by the hand what is
coming to pass. Hence, it says, all marveled. For his mouth was opened
immediately, and his tongue, and he spoke blessing God.
O the strange wonder! A child's name is written and a father's mute mouth
is opened; John is named and the tongue of the one naming him is set in
order. You see that we did not miss the mark when we too called the present
exposition unavoidable. Behold, the very name of the righteous man both
opens a silent mouth and raises a tongue hard to move. For thus the voice
of John is shown crying out, because even the name itself is shown to be
the working of a voice. "I am," he says, "the voice of one crying in the
wilderness: Prepare the way of the Lord, make his paths straight."
O the wonder! The Word sojourns and a voice proclaims beforehand; the
Master comes and a servant is sent ahead; a king arrives and a soldier is
made ready in advance. But why do I now, running ahead, speak of the
preaching in the wilderness, when I have another strange and great voice of
the beginnings? Works come first, higher than words. For while the Baptist
still dwelt in his mother's womb, still held by the bonds of nature, still
wrapped in his mother's swaddling-bands, he cries out the Master's presence
by a leap. For it came to pass, it says, that when Elizabeth heard Mary's
greeting, the infant leaped in her womb. See the strangeness of the
mystery: the infant is not yet born, and in her womb he speaks by the leap;
he is not yet allowed to cry out, and he is heard through deeds; he has not
yet learned life, and he proclaims God beforehand; he does not yet know
creation, and he announces the Creator in advance; he does not yet see the
light, and he recognizes the Sun; he is not yet born, and he is eager to
run ahead.
For he cannot bear to be held while the Master is present; he does not wait
for the bounds of birth, nor for the time of preaching, but he contends
even to burst the prison of the womb, and he is eager to announce
beforehand the Master who is coming. "He who looses the bonds," he says,
"has arrived, and why am I held bound? He who by the Word constructed all
things has come, and why do I still wait for the laws of nature?" "Let me
go out," he says, "let me run ahead, let me preach, let me cry: Behold the
Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world."
But why is our discourse today also stretching itself out with the infant's
hot zeal? Let us hold back now for a while, if you please, even the tongue
that has leaped enough with the infant. For it is good to measure the
exposition also to the time, and already to take delight in what has been
said in any way, and with God to wait also for the contemplation of what
follows. For neither did the Baptist force the bounds of birth by his leap,
but he too remained the fitting time, knowing that the Master after him
would fulfill this also for our sake; to whom belongs the glory unto the
ages of ages. Amen.
