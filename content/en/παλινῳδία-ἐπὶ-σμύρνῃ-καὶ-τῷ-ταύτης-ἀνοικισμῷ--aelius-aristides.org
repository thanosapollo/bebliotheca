But for me, O men of Greece, the same thing has happened as to those in the
tragedies who keep silent for a long time, and then, when the moment has
offered, speak either to a chorus or to anyone at all. For all the time before
I restrained myself, from when those things which you know of came to pass,
and I neither set out to console the citizens nor did anything else except
write to the protectors what the occasion required and watch for what would
come from there; since the gods, doing well, and the emperors who are steered
by the gods' counsel and who guide the affairs of men, have driven off the
fortune that was alien to the city and have brought in instead the good
fortune that long belonged to it, I think that I myself too may happen to
speak something in season. If then it were possible for me to move wherever I
wish, I would have been present and spoken with you; but since the Savior
holds me, converse through letters was left. And if this can be referred to a
precedent, there is no need to wonder; but if it has never yet been done by
anyone else to this extent, yet it belongs to us, it is not

right to take offense. When I learned what had happened (for I happened not to
be present, but I had already gone out beforehand, by whose counsel you may
reckon), I sang certain monodies, until unawares I was saved by the disaster
into certain speeches. For there was no other way to hold out, unless I gave
myself over to these. But now it is time for me to imitate Stesichorus with
the palinode, and not, after singing then things I did not wish, now keep
silent about the things that have come from the prayers. And I think I shall
differ from Stesichorus in this much: for he, in place of the things he said
ill of Helen, hymned the second things instead; but I shall now adorn in joy
the very things which I then adorned while lamenting, offering praise clean of
wailing. For it befell us, O men of Greece, to stumble according to the common
fortune of men, which it is not possible to pass through to the end without a
share of evils, but to recover by the fortune of the city, through which it
has never yet been without a share of any of the fair things. And one would
know this even by judging from the time. For that it slipped at the very
moment when it was not going to lie fallen, nor to fare like the death of a
man, as certain other cities have already done before, but to rise again on
stronger foundations: how would a man of good sense not count this as part of
good fortune?

And the divine power, as it seems, devised a pairing. For the emperors who are
best in all things and hold all cities under themselves had the ambition to
found the fairest city, and for the city, to be raised through them and to
inscribe such men as founders turned the disaster into prosperity. For
formerly we sang of Theseus and Alexander, the one, I think, the most humane
among the Greeks, the other of all kings the most admired for daring in wars.
But now we have taken on still more splendid and greater founders, to whom all
earth and

sea is subject, both from their fathers and from what they themselves added;
and of wisdom and justice and truth and kindness they have no less, if not
even more, than of strength in arms. For who is so far beyond the Pillars of
Heracles, or who beyond the Caucasus, as to be unhearing of their humanity and
greatness of soul? They did not let the name of Smyrna become a myth, but sent
some outflow of their own fortune into her, and granted not to lament the
fall, but to celebrate a resettlement. Alexander's sleep is sung, having been
the prelude of the settlement; but they showed such wakefulness that they were
beforehand in raising us up, before the one who would tell them what we had
suffered had gone forth. And they used instruments at once most divine and
most splendid, consoling with words and showing what Hesiod once prophesied,
how great a thing music is when joined to kingship, and furnishing every
provision both for the healing of what had happened and besides for the
putting on of other ornaments. And not even this sufficed, but as if they were
themselves citizens in the city they pointed out sources of money and charmed
with hopes those who would be ambitious and called them to the help of their
hands, and they promised the willing, and that they would not trouble the
unwilling, and if we should at once want something else besides these things,
they bade us speak, as they would gladly grant it. So that to me not even all
the wealth from men seems equal in worth to this continuity; for it has made,
if it is lawful to say, the earthquake beneficial to the city. For before it
came to this fortune it was not yet clear to what degree it is honored, nor
how the others ought to stand toward it; but now it is possible to say the
saying of Themistocles, if indeed this at least is to be believed of him, that
upon the gifts that came to him from the Persian he uttered something of this
sort to his children, that

now then they had been saved, when they had been ruined. But he, at any rate,
is said to have spoken this when deprived of his fatherland; for us, together
with the recovery of the fatherland, money, ornaments, ambitious gifts,
emperors as founders and counselors, all the fairest things have come
together. Must we not say that these things belong to their unconquered
fortune and to the city's good fortune, furnishing immortal memory to them
from the things by which they found and act, and to her from the things by
which she is founded? They had such excess that they did not even think it
enough to set out of their own motion toward the founding, but instead of
waiting for ambassadors from us, they themselves became ambassadors for us to
the council at home, deeming it right to vote the things which none of us
would have dared to ask. Has not every dirge been wiped out by these things,
and every memory of both the private hardships of each and the common ones,
and has there not been brought in instead gladness with praise, and desire to
live now, if ever, upon the hopes to come, and due thanks to the gods, to the
emperors, to the chorus that received the policies of the leaders, glad and
mindful of Smyrna as was fitting? It is worth, then, O men of Greece, to
mention also now the part that belongs to you who are of the same race, how
you were disposed in accord with these things and showed most clearly the
goodwill which you had from all time toward the city. And one would learn this
by examining the affairs of the first and oldest of the Greek cities, I mean
that of the Athenians, how it fared in the face of its disasters. And I do not
say this as one who thinks it right to pull that city down (may I never become
so extreme a lover of my own city), but wishing at once to console the
citizens and to show those present that it is native to our city through all
seasons to fare well, and of the

beloved portion it is as it were the allotted share. Those men, then, deprived
of their ships, deprived of their walls and of the whole empire, and having
come to this, that the people was forced to become an exile from the
fatherland (for I will keep silent about so many things in between): yet when
they had fared so, except for Pharsalus and Argos and Thebes, none remembered
either greater or lesser thanks for the benefactions of all time; nor even
that the city was sung as the stay of the race. But those whom I named,
receiving the exiles and standing against those who demanded them, laid up
this thanks to the city that surpassed in all things, with whom the oracles
say good fortune is established.

Come then, let us also examine the affairs of our city, lest it should in any
way be fitting for any of her lovers to be ashamed: which until these times
stood as it were a pattern of a city's beauty, subduing at once from the sight
those who put in both from land and from sea, and to those who passed within
showing and furnishing the fairest things, of which there was no satiety of
eyes nor of spending of time: which it would now be endless to recount. But
when it was necessary also to take experience of the other portion, what the
rulers were moved to be eager about we have just gone through; but among you
who are of equal rank, what of all things did not happen? Were there not
lamentations city by city and in the common councils of those crying the
greatest things and longing for the city in the place of a fatherland, and of
those thinking it right to dissolve festivals and making speeches about her,
as each had power? For as if a common fall of Asia had occurred, so you held
your minds. And as for the help in the works themselves, who has yet
remembered things that have happened among the Greeks up to this time? Markets
from everywhere for those remaining in the land, arriving from earth and sea,
and rivalry and eagerness of the greatest cities on either side, calling them
to themselves and

sending vehicles and journeys, and besides preparing dwellings and share of
councils and all resources, as for their own parents or children, and similar
things from those falling short in size, but lacking nothing in eagerness and
honor. For who did not make it a godsend for himself? For who did not think
this favor was rather found than conferred, to receive as fellow-dwellers
those who had so far held first place? As for contribution of money, then, and
promises for the future from either continent, and many other kindnesses that
came according to the power of each, who could finish telling them? And not
for this reason did the city think it right to receive all things in
succession, nor as the givers wished, but as it was noble for her to take. And
why must one recount these things in part? For all the nations that fill Asia
for us contend in a common ambition toward the city, raising the strongest of
the ancient things. And upon this city alone the old saying was proved to be
false, that there is forgetfulness among friends of those who have fared ill;
so much of distinction and graces and glory was kept for her even beside the
disaster by the divine power. But now all that belongs to a dirge-like harmony
and ungracious figures and that has its praise from grief has gone out of the
way, and the continent wears white, and Greece holds festival upon a good
theme, and the city, as in a drama, even now grows up again from the
beginning, changing its age, the same become old and new, as the tale is that
the phoenix comes back to life from itself; for it was not, I suppose, the
god's will that another phoenix should come to be instead of that one and
after it. And all things seem to have gone as by a road. For Theseus having
founded it from the beginning, Alexander later brought it to this form. And a
third hand, of those who conquer in all things, raises the same city and puts
it together; for they did not think it right to move it, from love of the

existing one, but they raise it upon the traces. And the nature of the city,
it seems, longed for double founders, allotting two foundress-goddesses. And
already harbors recover the arms of the dearest city and it is adorned again,
and for the Meles there is nothing to hinder it from having its neighbors, and
gates of spring and of summer are opened under crowns. And choruses of Nymphs
and Muses dance in her and about her, and west winds will not pain as they
blow. O blessed among the older men those who will arrive at this day, on
which they will see Smyrna having her own ornament; and children who will
suffer no loss, but will see the fatherland such as their parents inhabited;
and those in their prime, having both shared in the work and the toil, and
also sharing in the sacrifices and festivals, for themselves and for those who
come in; since the hopes of things to come are still fairer than the present
goods. But Zeus of the city, and goddesses who hold the allotment of this
city, and Poseidon, who received both lots, to shake and to save, and gods of
kings and of the Greeks, may this second founding of ours come to be better
and more secure, upon much gladness and glory of the founders, and with good
things meeting the city through all future time, and may there be something of
mine too among the Greeks now and hereafter.
