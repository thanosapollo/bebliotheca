PHILOSOPHICAL
On voice
Since our aim is to treat of every philosophical voice simply, and we must
first know about which voices philosophy is concerned, making the beginning of
the discourse from voice itself we say that voice is either meaningless or
significant. And the meaningless is that which signifies nothing, while the
significant is that which signifies something. Then again the meaningless
voice is either inarticulate or articulate. Inarticulate, then, is that which
cannot be written, while articulate is that which can be written. There is,
then, a voice both meaningless and inarticulate, as the noise or the crash
that comes from a stone perhaps or wood, for it is neither written nor
signifies anything; but meaningless and articulate, as goat-stag, skindapsos;
for these are written, but signify nothing, for neither a goat-stag nor a
skindapsos ever came to be nor is. Concerning the meaningless voice, then,
both the inarticulate and the articulate, philosophy has no account. The
significant voice again is either inarticulate or articulate. An inarticulate
significant voice, then, is as the barking of dogs; for this signifies the
dog, because it is a dog's voice, and it also signifies someone's presence.
But it is inarticulate, because it is not written. Therefore philosophy has no
account of this either. But the significant and articulate is either
particular or universal; particular as Peter, Paul, universal as man.
Therefore philosophy has no account of the particular either, but of the
significant and articulate and universal, that is, common and said of many.
And this again is either essential or non-essential. Essential, then, is that
which makes clear the substance, that is, the nature of things. Non-essential
is that which makes clear the accidents, as: man is a rational mortal animal.
All these are essential. For if you take away any of these from man, he will
not be a man. For if you say that he is not an animal, he is no longer a man.
And if you say "he is not rational", he is not a man. Likewise also, if you
say "he is not mortal", he is not a man; for every man is both an animal and
rational and mortal. For this reason, then, they are called essential, because
they complete the nature of man and it is impossible for man to be constituted
apart from them. Likewise also in the case of each thing those that constitute
its nature are called essential, while non-essential are the accidents, which
it is possible both to be in the man and not to be, as the white; for even if
someone is not white, he has not lost being a man. Likewise also the black and
such things. The essential voice, then, either makes clear the what-it-is or
the what-sort-it-is, as when asked "what is man" we say "animal", then when
asked "what sort of animal" we say "rational mortal". The essential voice,
then, that makes clear the what-sort-it-is is called difference. And that
which makes clear the what-it-is either signifies many species and makes the
genus, as substance (for substance signifies both man and horse and ox; for
each of them is both said to be and is a substance, but is another species) or
makes clear many that differ from one another in number, but in species not at
all, and makes the species; as man signifies many, that is all particular men,
who differ from one another in number (for Peter is one and Paul another and
they are not one but two) but they do not differ in species; for all are both
said to be and are men. The more particular, then, as Peter, is individual and
person and hypostasis, which signifies the someone. For when asked "who is
this", we say

"Peter", which also subsists by itself. And that which contains all the
individuals is called species, and is more universal than the individual,
because it contains many individuals, as man. For it contains both Peter and
Paul and all particular men, which is called nature and form and substance
according to the Fathers. And that which contains many species is called
genus, as animal; for it contains man, ox, horse and is more universal than
the species. And the Fathers called the species and the genus nature and form
and substance. And the non-essential voice either belongs to one species or to
many. And if it belongs to one species, it is called a property, as the
capable-of-laughter, for the capable-of-laughter belongs to man alone, and the
capable-of-neighing to the horse. But if it belongs to many species, it makes
the accident, as the white; for this belongs both to man and to horse and to
dog and to other species, which are many and various in species. And these are
the five voices, under which every philosophical voice is brought. These,
then, we must know, what each signifies and in what they share with one
another and in what they differ. And they are these: genus, species,
difference, property, accident. Genus is that which is predicated of several
that differ in species in the what-it-is, species that which is predicated of
several that differ in number in the what-it-is, difference that which is
predicated of several that differ in species in the what-sort-it-is and is
taken in the definition as essential. One must know that this is called
essential and natural and constitutive and divisive and specific and most
proper difference and quality. And property is that which belongs to one
species and to it alone and to the whole species and always. And accident is
that which is predicated of several that differ in species in the
what-sort-it-is and is not taken, but is able both to belong to something and
not to belong. And this is also called non-essential difference and quality;
concerning which we shall learn more accurately with God in what follows, in
which the practice is. An individual is that which, from substance and
accidents, subsists by itself, separated in number from those of the same
species, making clear not the something, but the someone. 2 On the ten most
generic genera, which they name categories One must know that of things said
some are said simply and without combination, as substance, accident and such
things, and some with combination, as a horse runs, as Socrates philosophizes.
And our aim here is to discuss simple expressions through the medium of simple
concepts that make clear simple things. And each of the things said simply is,
itself by itself, in no affirmation, much less in a negation. But by the
combination of these with one another affirmation and negation come to be; for
affirmation is composed of at least two expressions, as "Plato walks". And
negation comes to be by addition of the negative particle, that is "Plato does
not walk". And the combination either comes to be according to force, as "I
live"; for it has the force of two expressions; for the one who says "I live"
makes clear "I live," and the one who says "you live" makes clear "you live";
or according to voice, as "a man walks". And of things said simply and without
combination, one signifies substance, as man, horse; one quantity, as two,
three, two-cubit, three-cubit; one relatives, as father, son; one quality, as
white, black; one where, as in a temple, in the marketplace; one when, as last
year, yesterday, today; one being-situated, as standing, sitting; one having,
as being-armed, being-shod; one doing, as burning, cutting; one
being-affected, as being-burned, being-cut. These ten, then, are called
categories as from agoreuo, that is, I say; for they are expressions
predicated of simple things, and these making them clear through simple
concepts: 1. substance, 2. quantity, 3. relatives, 4. quality, 5. where, 6.
when, 7.


being-situated, 8. having, 9. doing, 10. being-affected. Substance is divided
into body and incorporeal, body into animate and inanimate, the animate some
into sensitive and insensitive, others into animal, zoophyte and plant; for
animal is nothing other than an animate sensitive substance. Again animal is
divided into rational and irrational, the rational into mortal and immortal,
the mortal into man, horse, dog and such things. Substance, then, is the most
generic genus because it has no other genus before it predicated of it
synonymously; and we said "synonymously" because of being. For being is before
it and is more universal than it; for being signifies not only substance but
also accident. And being is divided into substance and accident, since all
things are under being; for non-being is none of all things. But even if being
is before substance, still it is not a genus of it; for being is not
predicated of substance synonymously. And synonymous predication is when the
subject receives both the name and the definition of the predicate, that is,
of the one above. And substance receives the name of being, but cannot receive
its definition. Substance, then, is the most generic genus, while man and
horse and such things are most specific species because they are not cut into
other species; for even if man is cut into male and female, these are not
species; for they are consubstantial. And it is impossible for species to be
consubstantial with species. And those between the most specific species, that
is man, and the most generic genus, that is substance, are called both genera
and subaltern species, species of those before them, that is those above, and
genera of those after them, that is those below, as body is a species of
substance but a genus of the animate, the animate a species of body but a
genus of animal, animal a species of the animate but a genus of the rational,
the rational a species of animal but a genus of the mortal, the mortal a
species of the rational but a genus of man. And man is called both a species
of the mortal and of the individuals under it; again it is called a species
and no longer a genus. And substance is called a genus only, and no longer
also a species. For this reason, then, substance is called most generic genus
and man most specific species. And every genus that has no other genus before
it is called most generic, and every species that has no other species under
it and is not a genus is most specific. Everything, then, immediately before
the individuals, that is nearest, is a species only and no longer also a
genus. 3 On quantity Quantity is a heap of units and not a division but rather
a joining. And of the quantitative, one is continuous, one discrete. Discrete,
then, is that whose parts do not join to some common limit, as two palms; and
continuous is that whose parts join to some common limit, as one three-cubit
piece of wood. Under the discrete quantitative, then, are brought number and
speech; number as two, three and such things; for the parts of two, which are
one and one, do not have a common limit joining them. And speech measured by
syllables is also brought under the discrete quantitative; for the syllables
do not have a common limit joining them. And under the continuous are brought
line and surface and body and place and time. And line and surface and body
and place and time are brought under the continuous quantitative; for the
parts of each of them join to some common limit, those of a line to a point,
those of a surface to a line, those of a body to a surface. For a point is
without quantity. And the line has one dimension; for it is length without
breadth. And the surface has two dimensions, length and breadth. And the body
has three dimensions, length <and> breadth and depth. And place


is a limit of that which contains, inasmuch as a limit the contained is
contained, as the air contains the body. The end, then, of the air that
contains the body is called the place of the body, and not the whole air, so
that place is a surface of the air that contains the body and is thus brought
under the continuous quantitative as a surface. And the parts of place join to
a line as of a surface. And the parts of time join to the now; for the now,
being without quantity, is a limit of the past and a beginning of the future.
And one must know that a surface is the outer part of the body, from
appearing. This, then, is either equal and even and smooth and is called
plane, or curved and unequal and uneven and is called simply surface. And one
must know that body and surface and line are under one and together, while
time and place are about them. Place especially belongs to the quantitative;
one is positional, one non-positional. The positional, then, makes clear
being-situated somewhere and the parts remaining for one another; and it also
has a certain continuity and mutual holding toward one another. And
non-positional are those that are not such but have the contrary. Under the
positional, then, are brought line, surface, body, place; for the parts of
each of them remain and are situated somewhere and have continuity toward one
another, the parts of the line in the surface, of the surface in the body, of
the body in the place, of the place in the body as of a surface. And
non-positional are number, speech, time; number and speech, because their
parts neither have continuity nor are situated somewhere nor do their parts
remain. And the parts of time, even if they have continuity, still neither
remain nor are situated somewhere. And number is twofold, one in the numbers,
one in the numbered things, as a pint; one the measuring one, the bronze, and
one the measured, as either wine or some other such thing. The numbering,
then, is not positional, but the numbered is positional. And every
quantitative is both a measure and measurable. One must know that quantity is
the measure itself and the number that measures and that numbers. But speech
and time and place, body and surface and line are not quantity but
quantitatives as subjects of quantity and measured and numbered, likewise also
the numbered number, that is the wine and such things. And some are subjects
of continuous quantity as being numbered continuously, others of discrete as
being measured and numbered discretely; wherefore they are also called
quantitatives and not quantity. And these seven are called quantitatives
properly, but by accident we call quantitatives the things observed in them,
as action, motion, the white and such things; as, if the action and the motion
come to be in much time, we say much action and much motion, but if in little,
little; likewise also, if there should be whiteness in a large body, we say
much white, but if in a small, little. And Aristotle also brings motion by
itself under the quantitative. And inclination is also brought under the
quantitative because the most is observed in it. The property of the
quantitative is the equal and the unequal; and lightness and heaviness are
under the qualitative. 4 On relatives Relatives are said to be such things as
are said to be of others, the very things they are, or in some other way
whatever toward another, that is, such as are said to be of others or toward
another, either toward a genitive or toward another case; for this is what "in
some other way whatever" signifies, that is, according to whichever case; for
relatives are said not only toward a genitive, but there are times also toward
the same case, whether genitive or dative, both the giving-back and the
giving-back-in-return, as the double is double of a half and the half is half
of a double toward a genitive, and toward a dative the like is like to the
like, or the giving-back toward a genitive and the giving-back-in-return
toward a dative, the giving-back as knowledge is knowledge of a knowable, and
giving-back-in-return; the


knowable is knowable by knowledge; or toward a nominative, as the mountain
great toward small and much toward little; for no one says "great of small" or
"little of much". And of relatives some are named with the same name, as the
friend is friend of a friend and the like is like to the like and such things,
and some otherwise. And of these some according to excess and defect, under
which are referred the species of the multiple, as the greater is greater than
a lesser, some according to the judging and the judged, as knowledge is
knowledge of a knowable, habit is habit of a thing-had, perception is
perception of a perceptible, likewise also disposition and position and
reclining and sitting and standing; for of these the genus is position. And
under whichever category the genus is, under this the species of the genus are
also brought. But sitting and lying and having-stood, as paronyms and
infinitives, are not brought under relatives; for they are not position. And
some according to power and impotence, power as the heating and the
being-heated, and impotence that according to privation of the power, when we
say that sight has an impotence to see the starless sphere. And some according
to the cause and the caused, as a father is father of a son. And some
according to the reference toward the genus, as we say that grammar is under
relatives not insofar as it is grammar but insofar as it is knowledge; for
knowledge is the genus of grammar, and knowledge is knowledge of a knowable.
Likewise also reclining and sitting and standing, for of these the genus is
position, and virtue because of habit; for virtue is a habit. 5 On the
qualitative and quality Quality is that according to which certain ones are
named qualitative, or quality is that according to which those that
participate in it are said paronymously; for from prudence the one who has
prudence is called prudent and the one who participates in heat is called hot.
And one must know that the qualitative is more universal than quality; for the
qualitative signifies both the quality and that which participates in it,
while quality does not signify that which participates in it. Likewise also in
the case of the quantitative and quantity. And quality is among the things
said in many ways, not as homonymous but as synonymous. For the name dog, said
homonymously, is not a common genus of the land and sea and starry, but
quality, being a genus, is predicated synonymously of the species under it;
for its species receive both its name and its definition. And of qualities
some belong to animate bodies, as sciences and virtues, diseases and healths,
and are called habits and dispositions, and some to both animate and
inanimate, as heat, cold, shape, figure, power and impotence. And of these
some are in potency, some in act; and if in potency, they make power and
impotence; and if in act, they have either gone through in depth, as heat
through the whole of fire and whiteness through the whole of milk and of snow,
and make the third species, affection and affective quality, or on the surface
and make the fourth species, figure and shape. 6 On the difference of union
Union comes to be according to various modes; for it comes to be either
according to blending, as in the case of various flours being blended and
mixed, or according to glueing, as in the case of bronze and lead, or
according to fitting, as in the case of stones or woods, or according to
confusion, as in the case of things that melt and of the metallic, as in the
case of wax and pitch and as in the case of gold and silver, or according to
mixture, as in the case of liquids, wine perhaps and water or wine and honey.
For the union according to composition is the interpenetration of the parts
into one another without disappearance, as it is in the case of soul and body,
which union some have called mixture or growing-together; for the things that
are together have such a nature as both to be united to those that can


receive them, as things co-destroyed, and being united to remain unconfused
and incorrupt and unaltered, as things that lie beside. The soul, then, is not
in a body, but the body is in the soul. And one must know that some of the
Fathers did not accept the name of mixture in the case of the mystery
according to Christ, but all accepted the union according to composition.
There is also the union according to juxtaposition; and it resembles that
according to fitting. Again a union is called personal, when someone, putting
on the person of another, makes the words on his behalf in his place, and a
relative union as of a friend toward a friend. And Nestorius also invents
other unions according to dignity I mean and sameness of will and equal honor
and good pleasure and homonymy. 7 On division Division is a first cutting of
the thing, as animal is divided into rational and irrational. Superdivision is
the second cutting of the same thing, as animal is divided into mortal and
immortal. Subdivision is the cutting of the limb, as, animal having been
divided into rational and irrational, the rational is divided into mortal and
immortal. But division and superdivision do not come to be in all cases, but
when not all are contained under the first division, as, animal being divided
into rational and irrational, there are found also among the rational
herbivorous, that is eating herb, and among the irrational likewise
carnivorous. We therefore of necessity make a superdivision, that is a second
cutting of the same animal, saying: Animal is divided into herbivorous and
carnivorous. And there are 8 divisive modes: either as genus into species, as
when you divide animal into rational and irrational; or as species into
individuals, as man into Peter and Paul and the remaining particular men; or
as a whole into parts, and this in two ways, either into homoeomerous or into
anhomoeomerous. And homoeomerous is when the segments admit both the name of
the whole and of one another and the definition, as flesh is divided into many
fleshes, and each part of the flesh is called flesh and admits the definition
of flesh. And anhomoeomerous is the reverse, when what has been cut admits
neither the name nor the definition either of the whole or of one another, as
if you should divide Socrates into hands and feet and head; for neither is the
foot cut from Socrates called Socrates nor does it admit the definition of
Socrates. Or as a homonymous voice into various significations; and this again
in two ways; for it either signifies some whole or a part. And a whole, as the
voice dog; for it is applied to the whole of a land dog and a starry and a
sea, which are some whole and not a part of an animal. And as a part, when the
name of tongue is applied both to the tip of the shoe and of the pipe and of
the tasting part of animals, which are parts and not wholes. Or as substance
into accidents, as when I say: Of men some are white and some black. Or as
accident into substances, as when I say: Of whites some are animate and some
inanimate. Or as accident into accidents, as when I say: Of colds some are
white and some black. Or as things from one and toward one. From one, as from
medicine a medical book, a medical drug; for from one, medicine, they were
named medical. And toward one, as a healthful drug, a healthful food; for they
look toward one, health. And of things from one some are said from some cause,
as the image of the man is said from a true cause, the man, and some as things
named from something, as a medical book and such things. And one must know
that in no one mode of division is the by-nature prior and posterior observed
in the segments, nor the more and the less, except in the from-one and
toward-one and that from which the naming is. And what the by-nature prior is,
you will find by seeking in the teaching concerning the prior; for our soul
has two


powers. 8 <On philosophy> There are six definitions of philosophy. Philosophy
is knowledge of beings, insofar as they are beings, that is, of their nature,
and knowledge of both divine and human things and practice of death, and of
death the chosen as they say, but I say also of the natural, likeness to God
according to what is possible for man. And we are likened to God according to
the just and the holy; the just, then, is that which distributes the equal,
that is neither to do injustice nor to suffer injustice, and the holy is that
which is above the just, that is the good, that is rather to suffer injustice
than to do injustice. Art of arts and science of sciences; for philosophy is a
beginning of every art. Friendship of wisdom; and true wisdom is God; the
love, then, toward God, this is the true philosophy. And philosophy is divided
into theoretical and practical; the theoretical into theological,
mathematical, physiological; the mathematical into arithmetic, music,
geometric, astronomical; and the practical into ethical, economic, political.
Theoretical, then, is to contemplate both the incorporeal and the immaterial,
that is concerning God, who is primarily and properly immaterial; then also
concerning angels and demons and souls, which themselves also are called
immaterial so far as toward the body, even if toward the properly immaterial,
that is the divine, they are material, which is the theological. And to
contemplate also the nature of material things, that is of animals and plants
and stones and such things, which is the physiological. And to contemplate
also the intermediates of these, which are sometimes contemplated in matter,
sometimes outside matter, and are intermediates of the immaterial and the
material, which is the mathematical. For number by itself is immaterial, but
is also contemplated in matter, that is in wheat; for we say "ten measures of
wheat"; likewise also the rest. And the practical adorns character and teaches
how one must live as a citizen; and if it educates one man, it is called
ethical, and if a whole house, it is called economic, and if also a complete
city, it is called political. 9 <Various definitions> A dogma is an inquiry of
true piety, an unerring and unlying discernment or in words a deliverance from
error. A catholic dogma is a judgment set forth to all without force and a
contradiction of another and an overturning of the worse. A definition is a
concise account making clear the whole underlying substance of the thing. A
definition is an account, beyond which the thing, whose it is, is not
permitted to go forth from itself, because it is known in it and it alone. A
definition is a making-clear of a thing through an account, without which
neither what it is nor that it is is known. A definition is a concise account
making clear the nature of the underlying thing. A horos is, as far as
the chance thing comes and there is brought to an end. A description is a
concise account making clear accidents, that is that which from both accidents
and properties makes clear the subject. And definition differs from horos, in
that definition is more particular, while horos is more universal; for horos
signifies both the definition and the boundary-mark and that of which there is
a limit. Horos is also said of that into which the proposition is analyzed;
while the definition makes clear only the concise account that makes clear the
nature of the underlying thing. And description differs from definition, in
that definition is taken from essential things, while description from
non-essential, that is accidents, and the descriptive definition from both
essential and non-essential. And the health of a definition is to convert, and
the vice not to convert. Definable is that which is subject to the definition,
that is the thing defined, indefinable is only the uncreated


(for every creature is defined by the creator), hard-to-define that which
appears in transformation and not according to substance, which it is, as in
the case of angels and demons and the soul. The definition of definitions is
that which defines all things, but itself is defined by none. Union is a
running-together of various things. On the difference of logos. Logos is that
which is always essentially co-present with the Father. Logos is the immanent,
that which is spoken in the heart. Logos is a messenger of a thought and
without hypostasis. One, then, is essential and enhypostatic, one immanent,
one uttered. Only-begotten is he of whom neither before nor after another has
been begotten. Composition is an interpenetration of the parts through the
whole into one another. Composition is to make from one and another into one,
either dividedly or confusedly or thus and thus. Composition is that which is
constituted from various things and does not know the property of each.
Division is that which has come to be privately and part by part and in no way
toward another. Division is that which has made the single-formed into
many-formed by fencing off. Division is the first cutting of the thing, and
subdivision the cutting of one of the cut limbs, and superdivision the second
cutting of the same thing; as animal is divided into rational and irrational;
behold a division; the rational into mortal and immortal; behold a
subdivision; again animal is divided into carnivorous and herbivorous; behold
a superdivision. Divisible is that which is divided, indivisible is that which
is not divided. 10 <On the five voices> Genus is that which is predicated of
several that differ in species in the what-it-is. And genus also signifies the
fatherland, as when we say that Peter is a Galilean by genus. And it also
signifies the beginning of the coming-to-be of certain ones, as when we say
that those from Israel are Israelites by genus. Genus is the thing
contemplated by itself. Species is applied according to two significations;
for both the shape is called species, as "first a form worthy of tyranny".
Species is also called that which is divided from the genus, as when we call
man a species of animal. Species is that which is ranked under the genus. And
again species is that of which the genus is predicated in the what-it-is. And
again species is that which is predicated of several that differ in number in
the what-it-is. And this definition is of the most specific species. Most
generic genus is the first genus, which, being a genus, is not a species, that
is that which has no other genus above it, from which it is cut, as substance.
Most specific species is the last species, which, being a species, is not a
genus because it has no other species beneath it, but being immediately above
the individuals as being divided into them, whose definition is: That which is
predicated of several that differ in number in the what-it-is. And those
between the most generic genus and the most specific species are called both
genera and subaltern species. Difference is said in three ways, both commonly
and properly and most properly. Difference is that called common difference,
the separable accident, because it is commonly observed in the whole species,
as when so-and-so differs from so-and-so according to being-healthy and
being-ill, according to being-moved and being-at-rest, according to sitting
and standing. Difference is that called proper difference, the inseparable
accident, because it is not present to the whole species but to some, as when
so-and-so differs from so-and-so according to the snub or grey-eyed or bald.
Difference is that called most properly among the philosophers, which is also
an essential difference, by which the species exceeds the genus. And again
difference is that which is predicated of several that differ in species in
the what-sort-it-is.


Difference likewise is that which is of a nature to separate those under the
same genus essentially. Difference in the same way is that by which each
differs essentially. Difference is that which makes another species and that
which is taken in the definition. Difference is an account according to which
the otherness of the significations toward one another is of a nature to be
preserved. And identity is an unvaryingness, according to which the account of
the signified has acquired the wholly unique, no mode of difference being
known. And one must know that identity is taken according to three modes. For
there is the same in genus, as when we say that man and the horse are the
same, inasmuch as both are animals. There is also the same in species, as when
we say that Peter and John are the same in species, inasmuch as both are men.
Again there is the same in number, as when we say that Peter is the same as
himself. And difference also is taken in three ways. For there is difference
in genus and difference in species and again in number. Property is that which
fits one nature and converts toward the definable, as the capable-of-laughter
of man and the capable-of-neighing of the horse. The account among the
philosophers is of this, of that which belongs to all and to it alone and
always, as they call a property supervenient-on-substance. Property is that
which belongs to all and to the species alone and always. A peculiarity is
that which is known properly, in that in which it is, and alone, and in
another not. A peculiarity again is the significant of every substance, both
figure and thing. A peculiarity is that which signifies the existence of this,
whose it is. Accident is that which comes to be and passes away without the
destruction of the subject. Accident is that which it is possible for the same
both to belong and not to belong. Accident is that which, existing in
something not as a part, is impossible to be apart from that in which it is.
Accident is both the separable, as heat, cold, whiteness, and the inseparable,
as hookedness in a nose and grey-eyedness in an eye. Generic is that which
contains the genus in itself. Specific is that which contains the species in
itself. Specific is the substance, that is the nature. Containing is that
which gathers and fills up the particulars in itself. 11 <On substance and
form and nature> Substance is that which subsists through the whole and needs
no other for being. Substance is the existence of each. Substance is the
common nature of beings, according to which both the individuals and the
species share with one another, being referred under one and the same
definition of substance. And substance is a self-subsistent thing that has
hypostasis. Substance is everything that subsists according to its own
existence and does not have its being in another. Substance is primarily and
properly everything that is self-hypostatic and does not have its being in
another. Substance is properly said of being itself, that which needs no other
for existence. Substance is a common and indefinite name predicated with equal
honor of all the hypostases under it. Substance is that which exists not in
one only but in many and various in number and is not observed more in one and
less in another. Substance is that which is said of the underlying hypostases
and is observed in all of them equally and with equal honor. Substance is that
which is the same and one in number receptive of contraries by turns; this
definition those outside have for the most part set down of the particular
substance so called among them. Substance is that which is predicated of many
that differ in number in the what-it-is and is co-present with all and equally
imparts its own name and thing. Form is called and taken this seen species of
the body. But form was taken properly as substance by the Fathers, as also by
the apostle; for it is unlawful in the case of the invisible God and impious
to call the form a species, that is a figure. Form is that which is
contemplated according to its own contemplation and has no need of another for
its making-clear. Form is that which is by itself and does not need a sharing

the having-been-natured-thus and not in every way the substance itself. And
again it is customary to call nature, for the most part, the substance, and
especially for our teachers. Nature truly is this, in which the being of the
substance is taken. Nature according to the outside philosophers is a certain
power that has sunk into the matter, a beginning of rest and motion. Nature is
the truth of things. Nature and substance and form is the quality of beings
that is. Unsubstantial is the uncreated and non-existent through all.
Particular substance is an articulate and insignificant account. Essential is
that which, being present, preserves, and being absent, destroys, as in the
case of the rational. And non-essential is that which neither being present
preserves nor being absent destroys, as in the case of the white. The
philosophers call the inseparable accidents supervenient-on-substance. An
individual is that which is composed of peculiarities, the collection of which
would never be seen upon another. A person is that which, through its own
activities and peculiarities, furnishes us a manifestation of those of the
same nature that is quite clear and circumscribed. 12 <On hypostasis and the
enhypostatic and the anhypostatic> Hypostasis is a running-together of the
characterizing peculiarities, distinguishing the individual and the proper,
that is the someone-man, from the common. Hypostasis is a running-together of
the peculiarities about each. Hypostasis is that which, through the
characterizing peculiarities, presents and describes in the someone-thing the
common and uncircumscribed of the nature. Hypostasis is a substance with
peculiarities differing in number from those of the same genus. Enhypostatic
is that which in no way subsists by itself but is contemplated in others, as a
species in the individuals under it, or that which is composed with another
different according to substance into the coming-to-be of some whole. We call
hypostasis that which subsists by itself and is not co-contemplated with any
other, which some also call an individual; and this would be a collection of
accidents, which, all contemplated together, are present to no other except
the one to which they are present. Enhypostatic is that which is in truth and
is contemplated in existence. Anhypostatic is not the non-being but that which
has not come to be, as the darkness. Anhypostatic is everything that has not
come to be from God but stands in the absence of the better, as it were the
darkness or disease or sin or death. Consubstantial is that which is in no way
and without omission lacking of the other. According to hypostasis is a thing
that subsists from various natures. According to hypostasis is that from two
things, but in one person. Union according to hypostasis is the nature that
runs toward another in a hypostasis. <Mixture is> a juxtaposition of bodies,
an interchange of qualities. Mixture is a yoking of bodies that mix against
one another the qualities in them. Mixture is a running-together of substances
foreign to one another and an interchange of the qualities about them. 13
<Still on the same> According to nature is that which is commonly seen in the
genus as constitutive of the substance. Single-formed is the substance seen in
its species alone and not in transformation. Single-formed is that which can
be seen in a single mode. Single-formed is the substance without all change
and transformation. By itself is that which is present to something primarily
and according to substance and not according to accident, as the rational; for
this is present essentially to man. Homogeneous are those under the same
genus. Homospecific are those under the same species. Homohypostatic are those
that have one and the same hypostasis. Heterogeneous are those

Consubstantial are those that participate in the same substance. Heteroousian
are those that differ according to substance. Heterohypostatic are those that
are divided according to hypostasis. Homophyletic are the consubstantial,
heterophyletic are the heteroousian. Homogeneous and homophyletic and
homospecific and consubstantial are the same; heterogeneous and heterophyletic
and heterospecific and heteroousian are not the same. 14 <On homonyms and
heteronyms and such things> Homonyms are those of which the name only is
common, but the account of the substance according to the name is other, that
is those that have the same name, but the thing signified from the name is
other and other, as John the Baptist and John the apostle, both having one
name "John", but the thing signified from the name other and other; for the
one is Baptist and the other apostle, and the one son of Zachariah and the
other of Zebedee. And synonyms are those of which both the name is common and
the account of the substance according to the name is the same, that is those
that have the same name and the thing signified from the name, that is the
definition of this common name itself, as animal both man and the horse; for
both share in animal and in the definition, for each of them is called animal
and admits the same definition of animal, for it is an animate sensitive
substance. Polynyms are many names said of one thing, as aor, sword, knife,
spathe; for all these names make clear the same and have one definition, for
their definition is two-edged iron. And heteronyms are those that share
neither in the name nor in the definition, of which both the name and the
account of the substance is other. If, then, they have one subject, they are
called heteronyms, as ascent and descent; for they have one subject, the
ladder. And others are those of which both the name and the account of the
substance is other, and they do not have one subject, as substance and
accident. And paronyms are those that have the appellation from something, as
just from justice. 15 <On the common and the subject> Common is either that
which is contemplated in many or that which is predicated from many. And
common is said in four ways: either that which is divisible into the parts, as
the land being allotted, or that which is taken indivisibly in common use but
not at the same time, as one slave or one horse of two masters, or that which
is made proper in a pre-occupation, but is referred back to the common, as a
place in a theatre or that in a bath, or that which is put forward indivisibly
into a common and the same concept, as the voice of the herald was taken here
according to this mode; the logos is the <voice> that through itself makes
known concerning the whole, whose it is. The subject is said of the substance
itself. And of a subject is said when the universals are predicated of the
particulars, and in a subject as the accidents in the substances, as the white
in a swan. The subject is twofold: one toward existence, one toward
predication. And toward existence they say that substance underlies the
accidents, and toward predication the particulars underlie the universals. In
a subject is that which, existing in something not as a part, is impossible to
be apart from that in which it is, as color in a body; for color is not a part
of the substance of the body, but color cannot be outside the body. Universal
is that which signifies many, as man; for it signifies all men, who are many.
And particular is that which has been divided from the universal and is
subject to it, as Peter, Paul and each one of the men. Synonymous predication
is when both


the name and the definition of the predicate the subject receives. By itself
is that which is present to something primarily and according to substance. 16
<On the categories of habit and of power> Quantity is a limit of number, that
is a composition of units. Number is defined as a heap of units or a flood of
units, but not a division of units. Quality is that which shows in itself the
difference from the others. Habit is the activity according to nature of each
and completeness, as in the case of the soul temperance, courage, prudence,
justice, and in the case of the body the completeness of the limbs and the
proportionate and health. And the missing of these very things and the failure
and, wholly, the taking-away is called privation. Habit is a certain lasting
quality; and I call the activity the offspring of this. Habit is a certain
quality hard to move and hard to change, while the disposition that is
distinguished against habit is easy to change and easy to move. Habit, then,
is called in one mode the activity of the held and of the holder, as of the
weapon and of the armed and of a garment and of the one being clothed. And
second the adventitious activities that are lasting, whether they be natural
or psychic, natural as heat in bodies being heated, and psychic as science.
According to a third mode, that which it does not yet have, but has a fitness
to receive, which was the first signification of "in potency". And according
to a fourth mode the natural quality, that is the natural habit, as the heat
of fire and the sight of the sleeper and the cold of snow. And the fifth the
final activity, as seeing and heat; according to the fourth signification,
then, Aristotle took the opposition of the opposed as according to privation
and habit, that is of the power, whether the productive or the passive. And
disposition is the such-and-such quality of each according to accident. And
power is said one according to fitness, one according to habit; according to
fitness, as when we say that the child is a grammarian in potency, inasmuch as
he has a fitness toward becoming a grammarian; and according to habit, as when
we say that the grammarian at rest is a grammarian in potency, inasmuch as,
even if he does not use the art, he knows it. Power again is said according to
habit, as when we say that the child is seminal, inasmuch as he is of a nature
to be seminal and is in potency, even if not yet in act because of the time;
likewise also the grain of wheat is an ear in potency according to habit, for
it has a natural power to become an ear. Power is also said of strength. Power
is also said of the army, that is the expeditions. Relatives are those for
which being is the same as to be relatively somehow. Relatives are these, for
which being is the same as to be said toward another. And relatives are said
to be such as are of others, the very things they are; and at the same time
they also convert toward one another. Relatives are those for which being is
the same as to have somewhere relatively. And some have interpreted the
description more clearly, that these are relatives, for which being is the
same as to be somehow toward another. Where is a making-clear of place, as in
Rome, in Alexandria. When is a making-clear of time, as last year, today,
tomorrow. Being-situated is that which has a position somehow toward another,
as the body that is situated somehow toward such-and-such a position or toward
reclining or sitting. Having is a substance about a substance, as a ring about
the finger and a garment about the body. Doing is to have in itself the cause
of the activity. Being-affected is to have in itself and in another the cause
of being-affected. Doing is said in the case of the productive arts, carpentry
I mean and such things; but acting, in the case of those of which the end,
that is the result, does not remain, as in the case of flute-playing and
dancing; and contemplating is said in the case of astronomizing, geometrizing,
thinking and such


is said. Impassible is that which is without all matter and can in no way be
thought, which it is, by this. Impassible is that which is without affection
and above all touch. Impassible is that which has no need of another and
always remaining likewise upon itself. Affection is an alteration of this
something, of what it was, whether for good or also otherwise. Affectible is
that which can be altered into this, which it was not before. 17 <On the prior
and the together and motion> Prior is said of one than another in five ways.
Prior properly is that which is prior in time, as we say older or more elder.
Prior by nature is that which co-destroys and is not co-destroyed, and is
co-introduced but does not co-introduce, wherefore the simples have precedence
of the composites and the universals of the particulars. And prior is also
that according to order. Prior is also that according to power, that is
according to worth. Prior likewise is also the cause and the caused; and one
must understand cause as that which accomplishes, and caused as that which is
accomplished. Together are said those that co-exist with one another and are
not prior one than another, whether in time or by nature or by cause or by
order or by power, that is worth. And together are also said those that are
divided against each other, that is the two limbs from the same division, as
of animal the one rational the other irrational. Motion is an entelechy of
that in potency, inasmuch as it is such, as the bronze is a statue in potency;
for the bronze can receive the form of the statue. An entelechy, then, of the
bronze that is a statue in potency is the being-dissolved, the being-shaped,
the being-scraped, which are motions. In as many categories, then, as there is
the "in potency", reasonably in these motion will be contemplated; both in
substance and in the quantitative and in the qualitative and in the where
category, in substance coming-to-be and destruction, in the quantitative
increase and diminution, in the qualitative alteration, and in the where that
which is borne in a circle and that which is moved in a straight line, and the
former is called revolution, and the latter simply motion in a straight line.
And some are seven motions in the where category, that is the local and
transitional motions: the down, the up, the within, the without, the right,
the left, the circling. 18 <On assertion and syllogism> Conception the Fathers
and the true account have declared to be twofold; for one is as it were a
thinking-upon and a considering-upon, which unfolds and clarifies the rough
and inarticulate contemplation and knowledge of things, so that what seemed to
sense to be simple is shown by the busy work of the mind to be many-parted and
various. And the other happens to be a fiction of thought according to a
combination of sense and imagination, composing from beings the things that in
no way are and opining them to be; and such is the myth-fashioning of the
hippocentaurs and sirens; for taking the parts of wholes and composing from
parts some other thing, according to much license and ease, it fashioned in
the thought and in the accounts the things in no way contemplated in
hypostasis and substance, then also being shaped in the materials it made
idols. A beginning is a cause of all things that have come to be.
Without-beginning is that from which all things have been generated, but
itself ungenerated. An end is the hitting of the aim; an end according to
Aristotle is that for the sake of which the others, but itself for the sake of
none, or thus: An end is that for which one does the others, but itself
through none. Simple voice, name, verb, dictum, term; these five differ from
one another according to the subject in nothing, but the difference is only
according to the relation, as man as simply significant of something is called
a simple voice, as a subject is called a name, as holding the rank of a
predicate is called a verb, as a part of a proposition is called a dictum, as
a part of a syllogism is called a term. An assertion is an account signifying
what belongs to something or does not belong, as "Socrates <does not> walk"
makes clear that walking does not belong to Socrates. This assertion is
divided into affirmation and negation. An assertion is an account in which


there is being-true or being-false, as "Socrates is a man"; for see, it is
true; or "Socrates is not a man"; for see, it is false. Affirmation is an
account signifying what belongs to something, as "Socrates walks". Affirmation
again is an account asserting something of something. Negation is an account
signifying what <does not> belong to something, as "Socrates does not walk".
Contradiction is a fight of two propositions that divide the true and the
false upon every matter and upon every time, as "every man is just", "not
every man is just"; of these in every way the one is true and the other false.
A proposition is an account asserting something from something, which is a
negation, or something of something, which is an affirmation. A term is that
into which the proposition is analyzed, as a proposition is "Socrates walks";
this proposition is analyzed into "Socrates" and into "walks". Each of them,
then, as a part of a proposition is a term. A syllogism is an account in
which, certain things having been set down, something other than the things
laid down follows of necessity through the things laid down; and I said
"having been set down" instead of "having been agreed", and "through the
things laid down" I say, that is that through the propositions set forth the
conclusion comes to be, not needing from outside some other constitution or
term. A lemma is that which has been taken as agreed toward the construction
of something. And one must know that these five according to hypostasis have
differed from one another in nothing, as assertion, proposition, problem,
objection, conclusion; for when I simply assert that the soul is immortal, it
is called an assertion. And when it is taken as a part of a syllogism, then it
is a proposition to say "the soul is immortal". And when someone objects to
the proposition, seeking: From where is it clear that the soul is immortal?
then such a thing is named an objection. And when again we propose for
inquiry: Is the soul perhaps immortal? it is called a problem. And when it is
inferred from a proposition, it is called a conclusion, as: The soul is
ever-moving, the ever-moving is immortal, then from these propositions:
Therefore the soul is immortal; that is the conclusion. A problem is a theorem
stretching toward choice and flight toward contemplation and knowledge. A
question is an interrogation requiring an extended answer. One must know that
the interrogative account differs from the questioning, in that to the
interrogative a swift answer follows, that is through a little, but to the
questioning a long and through much answer follows, that is through many
accounts. An objection is that which from the first starting-line, that is
from the prefaces, overturns the account. And a counter-stand is that which
accepts the account as true, but shows that it in no way harms toward the
matter at hand, as when we wish to show. A heresy is an untrue opinion
concerning faith. A heresy is an opinion of several men agreeing with one
another but disagreeing with others. And a common concept is that which is
agreed by all, as that God is. And a thesis is a paradoxical supposition of
one of those known in philosophy, that is a strange concept, as the account of
Parmenides, that he said being is one, and as that of Heraclitus, that all
things are moved. 19 On the four dialectical methods One must know that there
are four dialectical methods, that is logical: 1. the divisive, which divides
the genus into species through the medium of the differences; 2. the
definitive, which from the genus and the differences divided, as the divisive
defines the subject; 3. the analytic, which analyzes the more composite into
the simpler, that is the body into the humors, the humors into the fruits, the
fruits into the four elements, the four elements into matter and form; 4. the
demonstrative, which through some middle shows the matter at hand; as it is
set before me to show that the soul is immortal; I take some middle, the
ever-moving, and I syllogize thus: The soul is ever-moving; the


ever-moving is immortal; the soul therefore is immortal. And one must know
that the syllogisms belong to the demonstrative. And one must know that the
analytic is a certain threefold; for there is a physical as aforesaid; and
there is also a logical analysis, when we analyze the syllogism set forth into
its own figure; and there is a mathematical, when we take the thing sought as
agreed and arrive at something agreed, from which the matter at hand is
hunted. As let the thing sought be, whether the soul is immortal. This thing
sought I take as agreed and I say: Since the soul is immortal, there are
recompenses of bad and good actions; and if there are recompenses, there are
the courts of justice under the earth; and if there are the courts of justice
under the earth, there is that which is judged and that which judges; and if
there is that which is judged and that which judges, there is a provider and
providence, which is agreed among all. From here then according to composition
I say: Since there is providence and a judge, there are the courts of justice
under the earth; and if these are, there are recompenses; and if there are
recompenses, there is that which is judged; and if there is that which is
judged, the soul therefore is immortal.
